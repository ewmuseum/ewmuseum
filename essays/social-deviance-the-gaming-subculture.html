Social Deviance: The Gaming Subculture

I had to do research on gaming addiction from a sociological stand point and seeing as how it covers e-fedding in general as a form of MMORPG, I felt I'd share it. --- Social Deviance: The Gaming Subculture By Ryan ______ Sociology 2020, Section 104 Professor [name withheld] April 7, 2006 Social Deviance: The Gaming Subculture Over the past thirty years, a subculture has developed from pass-time venues such as card-based games, console games, board games, computer games, and live action role-playing games (LARP); as a result, companies around the world cater exclusively to the needs of ‘gamers.’ Gaming, as defined here, is any excessive play of the above-mentioned game types. The gaming trend began in the 1970’s as video games were introduced on the first console systems; simultaneously, fantasy tabletop role-playing games were being introduced, including the time-worn Dungeons & Dragons which is considered by some to be the predecessor of the role-play genre as well as a major source of the gaming subculture. (Wikipedia1) Most persons who engage in the use of games as a hobby and pass-time are not what society would label ‘deviant’, there are some who take the game playing experience to excessive and obsessive levels, however. In some groups, game playing has become not merely a pass-time, but an aspect of daily living that holds such value, that it often supersedes other important aspect of the gamers’ life. While ‘gaming’ could be considered a victimless deviance, one has to look at the effects it has on the gamers’ life as well as the relationships he holds. Many times, gaming takes precedent over important social aspects in the lives of teenagers, severely lowering the number of face-to-face interactions to the bare necessity and damaging their social skills in general. In a modern era where the internet removes the need for the post office for the social aspects of mail and even, in some aspects, the telephone, we must wonder about the social interaction skills of these people who allow themselves to become obsessed with this world. Much like any ability or skill, one must practice in an effort to become good at conversation and social interaction. Instead of this, formerly essential, aspect of our daily lives, the gamer society is creating worlds of interaction based on nothing but pure fantasy through text messaging, graphically enhanced games, and even with friends in their lives with whom their role playing becomes bred into their dialogue. Regarding this phenomenon, some argue that due to poverty, physical limitations or the cut-throat world of teen politics, these gamers are merely finding an escape for their true personality which fail to surface in their non-gaming existence, creating an outlet and a training center for social interaction. John Suler (2002) states that, “Role theory in social psychology speaks about how a successful life is an efficient juggling of the various tasks and positions we accumulate and develop from childhood through adulthood. Cyberspace living is yet another manifestation of this shifting, juggling maneuver. It gives people the opportunity to focus on and develop a particular aspect of who they are. It may even give people the chance to express and explore facets of their identity that they do not express in their face-to-face world.” This subculture has gone beyond what most have done in the past by creating entire new worlds and types of interaction, severely limiting the gamer in many ways as well as extending them in others. The question of does this harm the gamer is still to be answered, however; yes would imply that the gamer misses out on interactions of normal life that a chat or fantasy world simply cannot duplicate nor can this world create the tactile aspects of human interaction: touch, smell, and taste. No would imply that even though the gamer is severely limited from interpersonal experiences, it extends their general ability to interact with others infinitely. This paper will argue that though this world offers a plethora of different abilities, it severely limits and even hinders the gamer from experiencing aspects of a healthy lifestyle. Arguably, different aspects of gaming produce different benefits and flaws; for example, console or computer gaming limits interaction even more than RPG (role-playing games) played in chat rooms and on forums while at the same time, these games are sometimes not as addictive as RPG platforms. Outside of interaction issues, addiction is another detrimental effect of gaming. Addiction to gaming of all sorts is similar to a substance addiction, as noted by Young (NG, Brian David & Weimer-Hasting, Peter), fostering traits such as withdrawal and cravings while Walker’s research indicates a similarity to obsessive compulsive gambling addiction. The gaming subculture is referred to as deviant; the reasons for this are taken from the conflict theory as well as the control theory for diverse reasons. Video or RP games are not illegal nor do they inhibit the lifestyle of others directly and yet, they are deviant in the eye of the majority because of the people who play them. Commonly the teenage male introvert is the stereotypical gamer and the imposing social majority would be the “ruling class” in high schools and eventually, colleges. Informal controls used are gossip and withholding of peer based relationships as well as negative expressions such as the common ‘nerd’ or ‘geek’. The Control theory would argue that, while not criminal, this deviance is sponsored by a lack of social control in general. However, to say that the average gamer is essentially ‘bad’ in nature is inane; instead, the gamer merely indulges – or over indulges – in this interest. Another possible explanation would be an application of Anomie theory; social expectations are sometimes difficult for introverted people who may suffer from low self esteem or low self worth, creating an uphill battle in the quest for social interaction on an in-person relationship level. Gaming offers a quick and somewhat easier path to interaction with persons who hold the same interests; the negative aspects of this short path are that the gamers are then somewhat alienated from the accepted populace around them, who as a result of their chosen associations or pass-time, may look down upon them, resulting in an application of conflict theory. Choose your poison: the types of gaming addiction While many gamers indulge in more than one type of addiction, there are those that specialize, even making careers out of their unnatural love for games. There are many different choices in the large and often convoluted world of gamers and every one has its pitfalls and subtle differences. In general, there are three types of games: card games, video games, and role-playing games. Card games are perhaps the least dangerous of the different types in regards to social interaction sacrifices as they require in-person game-play most of the time. The most popular examples of this currently are Magic: the Gathering and the ever-popular Pokemon. These games are battles between two or more players using a deck or several decks of cards, specifically made for the game. These decks can be bought and expanded; with this growing field, the game makers are constantly making new sets and series to enhance and change game-play, keeping it new and interesting with each series that is produced. Magic: The Gathering (colloquially, Magic or MTG) has reached world-wide status as one of the most popular card games in existence. Its popularity has culminated in the development of large – almost behemoth – tournaments played all over the world. Many Magic players compete for cash earnings, making the parallel with gambling even more noticeable (Wikipedia2). Pokemon has remained predominately a game for those of a younger age and thus limiting the possible addiction by parental intervention. Fortunes are spent on new cards as well as travel and entry into tournaments; gamers will put their relationships, careers, and obligations on hold when traveling cross-state or even cross-country to partake in a well known tournament or game. Card gaming is perhaps the most difficult aspect of the gaming subculture to invade as it requires supplies, a social group of (usually) local players and exposure. Video games, ranging from the console games (X Box, X Box 360, Play Station 1 & 2, PSP, Gamecube, and etc.) to computer based games have been the gateway into the gaming subculture for many years. A popular pass-time for younger children is the video game genre, possibly as a result of the popularity of Nintendo in the 1980’s. However, since the days when ‘defeating’ an enemy meant jumping upon its head, the video game industry has matured in many aspects creating engrossing games of all genres that venture from childlike (Pikman 1 & 2) to adult-genres (Vampire: The Masquerade – Blood Lines) that expose the player to strongly sexual themes as well as realistic violence. In recent years with the advent of online multi-player for computers as well as the now infamous X Box Live and Play Station equivalents, interacting with dozens – sometimes hundreds – of others while playing a video game in the privacy of one’s own living room has become common practice. Engaging in interaction with ‘friends’ to which one owes no commitment is extremely enticing; but where does this end and where does the interaction cease being enough? Also, does this interaction without commitment condition a gamer to desire the same type of relationships in-person? The answer to this question depends upon the impressionability of the person and the length of time committed to this type of interaction, it is entirely possible that a person could become temporarily or permanently expectant of this type of relationship, holding semblance to antisocial or, possibly, sociopathic personality disorder. Video game systems and computers capable of handling today’s mainstream games as well as the games are expensive, requiring a great deal of commitment from the buyer and thus even more encouragement to play excessively in an effort to ‘get your moneys’ worth.’ Similar to the culture surrounding the card game Magic: the Gathering, many games now feature tournaments where the final reward is a cash payment, driving gamers to even more extreme lengths. Recently, Counter-Strike has warranted such a competition, making it one of the most popular and addictive games on the market. Another aspect of the video game sector is the online poker fad. Texas Hold’em has garnered wide spread popularity in the last few years as a result of ESPN's World Series of Poker, the Travel Channel's World Poker Tour and Bravo's Celebrity Poker. Game sites such as Party Poker [www.partypoker.com] and Royal Vegas Poker [www.royalvegaspoker.com], in conjunction with College Poker [www.collegepoker.com], have become commonplace in dorm rooms and house holds, featuring both real money and play money tables, offering the chance to win fortunes for those who wish to bet and eliminating the risk for those who do not. Dr. Procopio (Procopio, M.) has noted a comorbidity amongst different types of addiction ranging from substance abuse to sexual addiction; it is possible that this comorbidity extends, also, to include gaming addiction. Role-playing games are arguably the most addictive aspect of the gamer subculture. When looking upon this genre, one must remember that an RPG can manifest itself as a video game for either console or computer systems or as text-based in the way of in real chat rooms (IRC) and forums. Another format of the RPG genre is the LARP format which requires in-person social interaction. The computer based variations made for online multiplayer participation, MMORPG (massive multiplayer online role-playing games) are the most addictive and are often referred to as Heroinware. Everquest, one of the most popular MMORPG’s currently available has been responsible for complete neglect of life outside of the game. Everquest, sometimes referred to as Evercrack, because of its never ending and ever growing story. Dr. Miller spoke of his own infatuation with the game Diablo II, "Each goal leads to another goal, and there are critical choices you make along the way," he said. "You invest a lot of time and thought into developing a character. You feel like you've wasted your time unless you reach the next goal." (Becker, D) While games like these warrant the most media attention, perhaps as a result of players actually attempting to sell digital property to one another or the deaths that resulted from the game – from over play, suicide, or homicide – they are not the only aspects of MMORPGs. The internet has allowed for new and interesting ways of interacting; from Fantasy Baseball and fantasy football to Vampire: the Masquerade and other such similar role-playing game based chat rooms and forums where hundreds of players congregate and act out their character’s lives in competition with one another and the challenges presented by the game. Obsession is common as different players in different time zones attempt to correlate their schedules, often eliminating sleep from one’s schedule entirely so that you may run across a particular person in hopes of completing a storyline or quest. This is particularly a danger of IRC based role-playing as there is no permanent manner with which different persons can interact without being online at the same time. Forums offer a separate problem; obsessive compulsive refreshing and loading of the page, reading every topic available and then returning shortly after to ensure that nothing has been missed or to see if anything new has arrived, interrupting other activities including homework and work. Forum based role-playing has dwindled in recent years, at first getting popular with role-playing games such as Vampire, Dungeons and Dragons, and etcetera but losing steam with the move to IRC for a more personal feel in regards to dialogue and relating to others. Forum’s have not been abandoned all together however, they have simply become an all new animal, the most popular of which is an internet hobby colloquially referred to as ‘e-fedding.’ E-fedding is a wrestling based game where handlers (players) develop a character and compete with other handler’s characters for accolades and titles using short story style role-plays. These role-plays are written by a single person and require little to no interaction with the other handlers; the role-plays are then judge by a panel or the owners of the websites. While this may seem to be rather innocent and in fact, somewhat educational as it boosts the writing and literacy skills of the players, role-plays are no laughing matter as they often reach and exceed 10,000 words, requiring weeks worth of free time and large expenditures of effort to complete. Large communities have developed, allowing handlers and website owners to interact, creating small societies within each site. The large amount of time necessary alienates the handlers and often distracts them from other obligations as well as school and work. Eric said, “My grades dropped at one point while I was competing for a World Championship. I had to take a hiatus from the game to get my grades back up.” Eric went on to explain that to most he knew, the game was embarrassing in their real life and they would try to hide their activities. Reno explained his experience with the addictive quality of the game, “I have tried to quit numerous times but I always end up being pulled back in by friends or due to boredom.” Eric continued, “I have managed to quit the game; however, I have filled the void with online poker.” (Reno, E; E-mail interview). Eric’s statement regarding online poker adds validity to the possibility of comorbidity with other types of gaming addiction. Eric Reno requested that I use his character’s name instead of his own in the interest of privacy. Gaming is a hobby for all ages, as it requires little physical mobility. However, the stereotypical gamer is also the most widely effected demographic. Male teenagers, of all races and ethnicities, often with social inhibitions, are the most likely suffering of gaming addiction. Gamer’s often show signs of low self esteem as well as a negative self image and often find social interaction in schools with local peers difficult and as a result, resort to finding other, easier methods of fitting in. Often, gaming provides a sense of belonging to a cast of people who have not found that same sense within their lives outside of the fantasy world. Members of the gaming subculture will often associate only with others within their comfort zone, limiting their exposure from a wide range of people and personalities greatly. The victims of gaming addiction, and indirectly the gaming subculture, are the gamers themselves who indirectly alienate themselves from the public at large as well as from opportunities and relationships not available within their gamer community. Other victims of the gaming addiction are the families, friends, spouses, and significant others of the gamer as they have to suffer with distance, obsessive behavior, withdrawal aspects and the loss of time with the individual as they devote it, instead, to their game of choice. Lea, an Everquest player spoke to C|Net regarding the game, "I have a friend who's in the process of getting a divorce because of 'EverQuest.' A guy I talk to has been through three girlfriends and even more jobs because of the game." (Baker, D.) So what is the harm in obsessive gaming? This paper lays out the possible situations and pitfalls that this addiction can lead to in some detail as well as the consequences of those pitfalls; ranging from failing grades to failing marriages, game addiction has very serious consequences. The Gaming Subculture, as a whole, is considered deviant by society as a result of issues with popularity and image; however, as the subculture grows and matures with time, that arguments for claiming this is deviant grows weaker. Gaming addiction is not uncommon in today’s society, sometimes associated with ADD and its semblances, and can happen to anyone who fails to exercise will power in regards to the amount of time they indulge in this hobby. There are no legal limitations regarding game play as it is in no way a crime, merely a social blemish, leaving the amount of play up to the gamer themselves. Provide this lack of external controls with the highly addictive atmosphere of the games and the absence of moral wrong doing in regards to this activity, and you have an essentially perfect dependence. Willpower is the only device a gamer can use to limit their time playing the games they love; rationalization is common and some will go to any length to find an excuse to play. The Gaming Subculture has passed the test of time, its roots based in the 1970’s, and is proving to grow even stronger with the onset of new games and technology. There seems to be no end in sight for the game addicts, no limit to the variety of games being produced every year, or to the online players with whom they can play with. Gaming addiction is a strong negative aspect of the gaming subculture and it will grow more common as gaming becomes even more accepted by the public majority. References Becker, D (2002).
