About the Museum

Forty years of cheap pops.

This is a museum of fantasy wrestling: play-by-mail, tabletop, the dial-up online services, Usenet, the web, and every other form the hobby took. Promotions run by post and by email and by forum and by podcast, characters invented for an audience of one or each other or thousands, whole federations kept alive by nothing but the people who loved them. This is where that history gets kept, one collection at a time. the Janitor · 8 Oct 2026

Fantasy wrestling here means the fiction-making hobby where participants build their own canons through characters, promotions and stories, not fantasy-sports-style pick-and-score games about real wrestling. See the essay All Fantasy Wrestling Is Beautiful for more information.

What this is, and isn't

This is a preservation project, not a highlight reel or a shrine. The project documents every award, but gives out none of its own. It treats everything, from the most celebrated parts of the hobby to its least known, with equal care. It aims to honor the memory of every member of the community who passed too early, whether we knew them or not. It attempts to find and serve everything that is publicly available by default, as well as personal donations when we're trusted with them. Where it is biased, it is (hopefully) biased structurally: personal archives are more complete than web archives, web archives are present where email archives are not, open platforms survive while closed gardens do not, and we piggy back on the Herculean efforts of other archivists: the Internet Archive, the Archive Team, Arquivo, Common Crawl, and others.

What gets shown, what stays sealed, and who decides are all governed by the constitution. The archive includes everything without asking permission; anyone with a stake in the material can ask for removal or de-identification, and it is honored without argument. Contact us at takedown[at]ewmuseum.org with the request and context.

At its simplest: the museum exclusively honors the dead. A living promotion's content may be collected, but it isn't served; a fed that died and came back to life more than a year later will serve the old content and not the new unless requested otherwise. No real name is shown for any living community member.

It is not an effort to make money. There are no advertisements, tracking codes, or cookies, and there never will be.

It is not ours. The entire site is available publicly on GitHub, GitLab, and as regular donations to the Internet Archive itself. The data is not ours to license; we provide it for research and archival purposes exclusively.

It is an effort to honor our community and its members, and provide a place for anyone to return to even for just a brief smile of nostalgia. The world feels like it needs more silly things to smile over without ulterior motives, and I hope this is taken as intended.

In memory

One of the things I always loved about this community is that we honored and remembered our fallen friends. The Museum seeks to honor all of our lost in its memorial hall. I hope you'll forgive my personal bias on its first two names: my friends Sean Simon Williams (1985–2018) and Jaymes Faulkner (1972–2022), who were the inspiration for creating this. They loved this hobby more than anyone I knew, and the bulk of this archive exists simply because I wanted to find their content and read it in their memory.

Jay in particular was the one who ensured the memory of Chris Williams (1999) was honored for nearly a decade with the Chris Williams Memorial Cup. I couldn't think of a better way to continue the example he provided me. This museum's priority will always be to find our lost community members and honor their participation in the game as comprehensively as we can. My friends are no more important than your own: the dedication at the front is all I ask for having built this, but every other part of the machinery honors all equally.

Who we are

This is a collaboration primarily between two people who long ago were known as Ryan-o and Argyle, two old (especially Argyle, sheesh) hands from Primetime Central (PTC). We'd love to have more people with more diverse perspectives involved eventually.

Our complementary sister-site, e-wrestling.org, aims to rebuild per-fed sites whole; it contains greater depth with less breadth, along with better visual presentation. We were also inspired by other parts of the community, to include E-W Archive, as well as the many parts of the community still active today: eW Place, Reddit's r/efeds, and everywhere else people still gather today. We hope this adds to and inspires the living community; replacing any part of it is not our goal.

Looking backwards, I'm blown away by how many lifelong friends I made in this game. How the people within it contributed to who I am today. How the skills I learned within it contributed to my professional success later in life. And I'm blown away by how many others I know feel the same way, or who noted how the hobby helped them work through their personal demons, or learn another language, or find community ... whatever it was, however it benefitted you, even as a simple distraction at some point in your past. It has made me smile hundreds of times in building it and reconnect with many old friends. I hope it does the same for you.

The forum

Our Discourse forum serves two primary purposes: 1) It provides the option to archive forum databases in the same way the site archives fed content. As of today this is exclusive to the former PrimetimeCentral forum database, but we welcome other former forums that might want to donate their data, in whole or in part. 2) It provides the ability to interface directly with the machinery of the archive to contribute your own data, correct errors, etc. Past that, it has no aspirations on becoming any major hub within the community, but you're welcome to use it to reminisce.

At the risk of getting on an old person soapbox, while I'm filled with nothing but inspiration from the active community, I do hate how the closed gardens of today have cannibalized and monetized our communities. It seems like the only value we received in this trade was reach and scale, and yet the community still feels most alive on small, private community forums. It strikes me all as reminiscent of the early 1990s era, where our community lived on Usenet, Prodigy, Compuserve, and AOL. Today, nearly everything from the walled gardens is lost to time while Usenet's open standard content survives, and the ever-present enshittification of these services mirrors the bait-and-switch practices of that era. History likes to rhyme. Discourse was chosen specifically because it is an open-source community project.

The usage of AI

AI was used to build the site and archive. The overwhelming majority of the content is faithfully rendered as the human authors wrote it at the time, with modifications only for the sake of privacy and to cut sexual depictions of minors. For any content that was authored later, we explicitly attribute authorship, including anything generated by AI. The semantic search offered for documents is powered by Cloudflare AI Search; a local, AI-free advanced search is provided alongside it. You can use the Discourse forum to interact with AI bots to contribute materials or updates to the museum; you can also just email us at contributions[at]ewmuseum.org.

How to read the mark: a sentence the Museum wrote itself — a caption, an epitaph, a memorial note — carries a small green mark reading “Written by AI, 2026”. A sentence a person wrote and put their name to carries that name instead. The documents themselves are never written here: they are the words of the people who wrote them at the time.

AI's a complicated subject and norms are still being decided on; we find value in its use as a tool for operating with data at this scale. We aim to disclose its use transparently, and provide options to avoid its use when possible, to meet people wherever they are on this topic. If you choose not to use this because of AI at all, we understand; we've provided you with the raw data as well.
