The Constitution

How the Museum decides.

A museum is only as trustworthy as its rules for what goes behind the glass. These are ours. the Janitor · 8 Oct 2026, revised 9 Oct 2026

Seven rules

One: All* content is served, verbatim

A lot of this is twenty-five to thirty-five years old and was made by teenagers, and some of it aged poorly. Okay, a lot of it did. There's only two real ways to handle this: censor the past and pretend it didn't happen, or acknowledge and own it. We've chosen to do the latter. The documents here (cards, promos, ledgers, origin scripts, character names, etc.) are served whole and unedited. The protection is not omission but an honest content notice on every document:

The content notice, in full A period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. The Museum serves it whole because pretending it didn't happen would be a worse dishonesty than being embarrassed by it.

Pages published before this wording was tightened may still carry its earlier phrasing verbatim, dated to when they were written, not silently rewritten after the fact.

Preservation is not endorsement: the Museum's own writing does not adopt a past era's cruelty or perform its caricatures.

* One specific content-based exception exists: depictions of sexual acts with minors. Across tens of thousands of processed documents, this has very rarely been exercised; when it has, it was mostly small lines or scenes removed from served content. We've only encountered one egregious and repetitive case; we banned all of that handler's RPs from ever being served.

"Whole" means the fiction. A few things we hold are never served, wherever they turn up: private messages and email between members, passwords and account details, credit and guestbook lists of real names, and off-topic personal chatter. The rare roleplay or site document built on harassing a real person isn't served at all.

Two: We Honor the Past

The Museum's subjects are former projects that are no longer active and, where welcomed, memorial subjects.

For projects, this means nothing will ever be served until a project has gone at least one year from its last update. That time frame may get longer, but it will never get shorter.

For individuals, we only name memorial subjects, and only when the community acknowledges them under their real names. We will also honor memorial subjects under aliases when requested or when the community only knew them under those aliases. If the community mourns someone, we honor that person in their words.

The memorial verification bar. You won't be surprised to know the community has seen more than a few faked real deaths over its history, so we make a good faith effort to verify a memorial before we grant it: the community mourning out of character, next-of-kin testimony, a public record, or our own verification. Outside confirmation isn't required, since some of the people we remember lived where obituaries and gravesites are hard to find. Where the community is uncertain, or its accounts conflict, we'll adjudicate. The lean is toward inclusion: verification, not suspicion.

The memorial register. A memorial page carries facts, links, images, and dedication. Never process, provenance narration, curation machinery, or a quote from a working conversation. Dedication lines the Janitor writes are placed verbatim, unattributed. The test: would a grieving friend reading this page want this sentence? If it's about how the Museum knows a fact rather than about who the person was, it goes.

Three: Privacy

The Museum never identifies a living, private community member by their real name or any other form of PII, and never connects a character to the real name of the person who played them. When living community members are acknowledged by name, we redact that information. Again, much of this content was written in another time, and privacy standards were very different then. We chose to err on the side of caution rather than creating a doxing database. We also don't write about the people behind the characters: no ages, life stories, or circumstances of anyone living.

When something comes out of a page, the page says so: [name withheld] where a real name was, [detail withheld] for anything else. The marker never says what kind of thing it was, and only the words that need to go, go.

One exception to this is where community members participated in the fiction under their own names. As long as this was witting participation, we keep those names as established, unless a request is made to modify or rename the character. If your name is John Doe, and you created a character named "Dangerous" John Doe, or created John Doe Wrestling Federation, or hosted your project at johndoe[dot]com, those points will stand in the record; the detail that John Doe is actually your name or that your email was johndoe[at]example.com never will.

"Witting" is critical here: a real name stands only where the record shows its owner took part: wrote themselves in, played along, ran the fed. Where it doesn't, we treat the name as used without consent and redact it. In a few rare cases a project was built almost entirely around unwitting community members' real names, and we serve nothing of it but the fact that it existed.

The other exception is that public individuals can be named. Much of this community was built on playing as real wrestlers; as public individuals, they can be named, even under their real names, though we would still redact other PII attributed to them. Similarly, politicians, celebrities, etc. can be named, but they will be marked as depictions of the real person.

Four: Nothing is backdated

Anything written today is marked as written today. Recovered primary material is labeled as recovered, with its source, and dated as much as possible to its original date. Any newly written framing is attributed to its author, AI or otherwise, and labeled as written in 2026. We never disguise a new sentence as an old one. Essays are written by people, under their own names; AI never writes one.

Five: Takedown & Privacy Policy

The Museum's default is characters, never people (rule three), but a default isn't a guarantee by itself. A ring name can coincide with a real one; a detail can slip past a curator's read; using your real name might have seemed like a good idea at 20 and not at 40. Standing to ask is broad, on purpose: the person themselves, a handler or contributor, family, or a friend asking on behalf of someone who no longer can. No burden of proof beyond involvement: the Museum doesn't adjudicate whether a request is "deserved," doesn't require a reason, and doesn't argue the point.

Ask for removal or for de-identification. Say what you want changed, and it changes. Reach us on the forum or at takedown[at]ewmuseum.org.

Every change lands as a dated commit that says it's a takedown without repeating what was taken down. This is a git repository, so its history keeps a trace that something changed. We'll never point anyone at old history to get around your request, and if the history itself is your concern, we'll talk through what's reasonable.

The Museum can't license what it doesn't own: nothing here may be used to train AI or for commercial purposes, and AI-training crawlers are blocked.

Six: How something gets in

Three rules govern anything the Museum takes in from outside its own curator. Review before publish, always: no anonymous or auto-publishing path, ever; a curator stands between every contributor and the public site. Text first: a contributor affirms submitted writing is theirs, or their scene's, to give. Images are curated memorial exhibits only: hand-picked and minimal, never bulk-imported or contributor-direct; takedown honored without argument.

If you have material (your own fed, a friend's, a hard drive from 1999), bring it. A single page is enough to open a wing. What counts is fantasy wrestling in whatever form it took: websites, newsgroups, forums, by mail, including fan feds of real promotions. Backyard wrestling doesn't; we count it as independent wrestling.

The dead-only rule. Nothing goes in unless the promotion is dead. This is a memorial institution, not a directory of the living. An active promotion's material may be collected and held, but it is never mirrored or served while the promotion lives. A dead promotion whose old website happens to still be online is still dead, and still collectible: the organization ended, and an unattended site is a ruin, not a residence. If a collected promotion is later revived, the Museum's holdings stand as the record of the era that ended. The living revival isn't tracked, and nothing new is served unless and until that era, too, is over.

The line is serve, not collect. What the rule forbids absolutely is serving a living promotion: a living promotion or community is never served while it lives, and never mirrored, and the invitation path is unchanged: a living promotion brings its own material when its own era ends, and nothing of it is published before then. Collecting one is a different act. A living site may be read and collected (where it is, what it is, what it descends from, and the pages themselves) politely, serially, robots honored, never in bulk; what is collected from a living promotion is held in the workshop, and none of it becomes a served document while that promotion lives. Robots honored means a site whose robots.txt disallows us, specifically or generally, is never accessed or routed around.

The wing/frontier boundary. This is an unfinished project, and the frontier is our window into how unfinished it is. It explicitly states what we know of that we still intend to find, and in the priority order we intend to do so. That priority order is always biased towards honoring the past: memorial subjects are the top priority, followed by an oldest-first priority. The exception to this is that collection often depends on the Internet Archive, a non-profit organization with its own limited resources; we collect steadily, transparently, and politely, which means slowly. Donated collections require no collection and will immediately jump the line. Collections that can come from other avenues (including more recent projects through Common Crawl, Portuguese-language projects through Arquivo.pt, etc.) can be collected parallel to the IA queue, and can jump the line. And the frontier may never be completed; every collection may return many new frontier entries that will reorder the priorities. If you believe we should prioritize a particular collection, tell us why on the forum or by email.

Seven: The registry

Behind the documents, everything we can turn into a simple fact, we do: who wrestled whom, who held which belt and when, who ran with which group, which fed a character came from. Those facts are kept the same way everywhere, so a match from a 1996 newsgroup fed reads like a match from a 2004 website fed. The written parts are kept short: a fed’s epitaph, a character’s caption, a storyline recap, and the memorial pages. Each is labelled with who wrote it, AI or a person.

Two characters with the same name stay two characters until there’s real evidence they’re the same, and we say what that evidence is: the source itself, the person who played them telling us, or a match strong enough that we call it presumed. We never quietly merge two records, and we never quietly decide they’re different.

When the old sources disagree, we keep both and say so rather than picking a winner. Every document says where it came from. A character’s record holds who they are; anything that changed from fed to fed (a hometown, a finisher, a height that grew two inches) stays with the fed that said it.

Every fed carries three labels: how much of it survived, how far we’ve got with it, and where it lived: a website, a newsgroup, a forum, by mail. None of them is a ranking. A fed with three surviving pages mattered as much to the people in it as one with three thousand.

Curators, human and AI, propose; the active curator makes the final call.
