Storm Chaser

Date: Dec.16/02 Inch after inch of snow melted as the warm sun beat down on the white blanket that covered the ground. The warming rays of sun also poured through an open window in my apartment. As I sit in my Lay-Z-Boy, trying to sleep, the light in my eyes made it difficult to do so. As I managed to wake myself up, I slowly got up out of the chair, and wandered over to the computer. I had to check the PCW website, and see who I was to face at Turmoil. To my surprise . . . It was a . . . ROOKIE . . . Chris “The Perfect” Storm . . . ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ I had to laugh as I read the name of my opponent . . . what kind of middle name is “The Perfect”? My middle name is Eugene and you don’t see me making a fuss about it, and putting it in quotation marks! What a loser . . . And he thinks he’s all big cuz his last name is Storm . . . oooOOOooo . . . scarey! Am I supposed to be worried about his gale force winds, and pouring rain? Hell, I’ve dealt with worse storms than him! Don’t believe me? Well here’s a little story about my fishing adventures . . . The true story of “The Perfect Storm” . . . ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ The year after I had finished university, I really had no direction in life. It was hard finding a teaching position . . . so I decided I’d take the year and do something I had always wanted to do. I traveled to Norway, and signed on as a crew member aboard a Norwegian whaling ship. So in the spring of 1990, I flew to Oslo, Norway and boarded the good ship Ørjan. We set sail on February 25th, 1990 . . . and that was the last solid ground I saw for the next six-seven months. No matter how think a jacket you wore, the winds of the Arctic Ocean sliced to bone with chilling velocity. Water that sprayed up over the side of the ship froze on contact, creating deadly spikes just waiting to pierce the flesh of an unsuspecting fisherman who slips on the ice. We had not seen the sun for many days, and I think the horrible weather was beginning to wear thin the nerves of the sailors. The dreary, grey sky brought the worst out in everyone, including yours truly. The whole whaling experience had not turned out as I had expected. They had me stuck in the galley for the first few months . . . and that was the lowest job on the ship. Even the guys who removed the blubber from the whales got more respect than me. The Captain, Arne Bernt, was a very paranoid man, and did not appreciate having an American on board his ship. That is why I was treated like dirt . . . But the events that were to come would change all that . . . I would earn the respect I deserved . . . Like I will earn the respect I deserve in the PCW . . . ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ It was May 9th, 1990, and our ship was twenty or thirty miles off the northern coast of Alaska and the sky was sunny and blue. The warmth of the sunlight felt good on the skin, and for the first time in several weeks, we could go out on the deck without a jacket. However, our fun in the sun would not last for long . . . For the horizon brought with it, the savage rage of an arctic storm . . . The waves began to grow larger and larger, but no one took any notice to them. We had seen bigger and badder waves than these before, so we felt we had nothing to fear. As the dark storm clouds crawled along the sky, ever ready to pounce upon our tiny whaling vessel. Within a few, short hours, our vessel was surrounded by forty or fifty foot waves, and the driving rain had enough force to bring us to our knees. The sun had been shut out completely by the black clouds, and the once calm, blue sea was now a churning stew of grey-green foam. As the waves used our boat as a ping-pong ball, we were tossed and swept across the deck like rag dolls. Suddenly, a wave that had to be at least seventy feet tall rose to starboard and picked up our boat like a child picks up a micro-machine. As it dropped us back to sea level, Captain Bernt was thrown overboard, but managed to grab hold of the railing. With the rest of the crew seeking shelter, or being pinned down by the torrential downpour, it was up to me to save our beloved Captain. Wave after wave pounded me back, but I couldn’t give up . . . not with the Captain’s life on the line. I dodged and avoided several waves, and finally made my way to the Captain. As I reach my hand outward, the Captain would not attempt to grab hold. He was to proud to be saved by some American. I begged and pleaded to him . . . I tried to make him realize, without his wisdom and experience, we would never make it home again . . . thankfully he began listening to reason. As the ship began rising up another swell of water, the Captain reached out to grab my hand, but the motion of the boat dropping off the other side caused the Captain to lose his grip and fall toward the water. Without thinking, I dove over the railing and grabbed the Captain with one hand and the railing with the other hand. With all my strength, I pulled the Captain and myself to safety. The next day, the skies had cleared up and the storm was now behind us. We managed to sail our mangled boat into a small port in northern Alaska. The crew and myself finally had a chance to relax, as we sipped some coffee at a tiny café. They all gathered around me, thanking me for my courage and strength to save the Captain. Even the captain managed to set aside his embarrassment to actually appreciate having me around. ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ So, if you think I’m considering Chris Storm a threat . . . then you’re dead wrong! I’ve gone through too much, for too long to backdown from some little punk like Chris Storm! And as for Mark Amulet and this matter with the ‘Golden Robe’ . . . Well, I’ll take care of that little piece of business at Decimation . . .
