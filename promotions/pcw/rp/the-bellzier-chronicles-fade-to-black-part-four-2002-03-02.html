The Bellzier Chronicles: Fade to Black [Part Four]

2 March 2002
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: March 2, 2002 Good Luck SupermarketOakland, CA What is the past without a demons from which to hide from? Tom Lexian had his own demons, from which he drew his passions and desires from. What person on this Earth didn’t have secrets or feelings bottled within them that they didn’t wish was buried down even further so it could never be unearthed again? It was a sad, gut wrenching feeling to discard a single memory, hoping that one would never have to feel the pain that it brought again. Most of Tom’s memories were ones of decked out in either ecstatic rapture, or pains of adolescence. His childhood had been so short, and so stressful, that he could hardly recall a time that he was truly carefree. In his mind, a child is purely innocent, devoid of such inherent sins that the bible of Catholicism had denoted as morality. He, himself, had been isolated at such a young age. He would stay at his mother’s side, never realizing the potential of himself or the outside world. Television was an outlet to the reality which permeated most of the United States’ pop culture, but even that had been taken away from Tom. His family had been too poor to afford such luxuries, and the family would not receive a television set until he was at least six or seven years old, opening up a new chapter of experiences through such shows as “Sesame Street” and other popular PBS telecasts. He wouldn’t realize the true harsh realities of “street” life until he began going to school. School was a new concept to Tom, whose only experience with other children had been through a few years at Day Care, being taken care of while his parents worked, and a brief stint in several different kindergartens. His parents hadn’t been entirely certain why he hadn’t enjoyed his stay at any of the kindergartens he visited – in reality, he didn’t either. There was always something about the school that made him uncomfortable, and that equate into a very convincing complaint by Tom to his parents at the end of the day. Whether it was justified or not, it was reason enough to pull him out of the school and into another, and another, and another… “Are you ok?” Mai Le asked, a concerned look splattered onto her face. “You look a bit…dazed…” Tom quickly shook his head, trying to dust away the cobwebs from his daydream. He soon realized that he was no longer in the intricate mazes of thoughts in his mind, but back in the Good Luck supermarket, face to face with Mai Le. She still had that innocence to her that Tom remembered as a child. Her eyes were not the stereotypical asian eyes that were often ridiculed for being too slanted or too closed – in fact, they were just as bright and eager as any other eyes that Tom had seen in his life. She was so full of energy and spontaneity, it was a pleasure to even be in her presence. It wasn’t until Tom and Mai began going to elementary school that the hard nosed Mai was finally revealed upon the world. The former Mai was gone, and the new focused, concentrated Mai appeared to have been just finished out of the factory. “I’m fine,” Tom finally revealed, still rubbing the temples on his head. His head felt like it was throbbing, back and forth, up and down, but Tom tried to fight through the agonizing pain. He felt a similar pain after a particularly rough wrestling match. There were those stiff professionals, mostly second or third generation wrestlers, who put as much effort into delivering the most pain per blow as they did on their promos. Promos were always important - the most charismatic wrestlers (those who got the fans attention, and who were absolutely adored by the fans) often got the best matches. That meant higher profile events, bigger dressing rooms, and most importantly, a larger pay check at the end of the night. “Are you sure?” Mai said, still concerned. She placed her hand on Tom’s back, but he quickly brushed the hand aside, soon bowing his head down as he realized what a rude move he had just made. Mai looked at Tom for a moment, and bit her lower lip, as if conceding that she had made a mistake. “Well, if you feel light headed again, just tell me. We have some special herbs you can take just for that ailment. Just got it in last week.” “Thanks…” Tom said, beginning to trail off. He sighed, knowing how asian medicines were boasted to have incredible results, but the bitter taste often didn’t result in a better lifestyle. He followed Mai back through the store, over to the cashiering area located on the back wall. It was odd placement to have the register located in the back of a store, but he remembered how nothing in the Good Luck supermarket could be considered normal. Tom looked forward, seeing the register directly in front of him. It was one table with a several chairs on the far side of the table. Several cashiers working the table would share the same register, which made for a wonderful game of musical chairs during peak hours. Although Tom and his friends would often joke about the funny sight that the cashier table brought, no one could dispute how efficient the workers were. All of them had been working together for so long, they moved like a well oiled machine, never faltering. Tom saw Mai’s mother, sorting through receipts at the table. She looked tired; her hair was completely white, her eyes seemed to tell a thousand tales. Tom realized how much Mai’s mother had been through, and he respected that aspect of her life. It was a respect that he also had for his own mother, and his father for that matter. Tom said what he would about his own childhood and adolescence, but he knew that nothing would compare to the hells those people had been through. Mai walked up to her mother, watching her work with the receipts. They poured on from the register, seemingly overflowing from where it began. One stack was a mess of unorganized papers no more than a few inches long, while the second stack was a nice, ordered file filled with the receipts. Mai’s mother looked up, a bit annoyed. “What’s wrong?” “You look busy,” Mai replied, suddenly becoming much smaller than she was before. Her entire demeanor changed as she spoke to her mother. “Are you organizing the receipts?” “Of course I am,” Mai’s mother snapped back. “Don’t I organize them every night?” She looked at Mai, as if she was asking a rhetorical question, but quickly became suspicious. “What do you need?” Tom smiled to himself, seeing a bit of his own mother in Mai’s mom. He didn’t know if it was a common intuition among mothers or what not, but a mother could always seem to pinpoint a time when their children wanted or needing something. Deception was futile. “Well, there’s some one here I want you to meet,” Mai finally announced. She looked over at Tom, who was still standing distant away from the other two women. A small lump in his throat formed as he remembered the look that he had gotten from Mai’s mother as she looked at him and the crashed aquarium that he had tipped over. It was the look that most children dreaded, and would probably even cause a few adults to quiver and shake with bad memories. To see such fury and anger unleashed, it was no wonder that Tom, as a young child, would be so spooked. He wouldn’t even stay to hear the lecture that she would have given him – he biked out of the store as quickly as he could. That was the last time he would ever step foot into the supermarket, and would ever come face to face with Mai’s mother. What Tom didn’t realize was that a meeting would come eventually… “Who is there?” Mai’s mother asked, looking around, finally spotting Tom. She looked him over carefully, not recognizing Tom, who was all grown up now. “This is Tom…Tom Lexian,” Mai replied, realizing that her mother did not recognize Tom. “He’s Nyoc’s nephew…” Her eyes widened a bit in disbelief, as she continued to scrutinize Tom, who felt more like the little boy who had just tipped over the aquarium then the adult that he was. “Tom?” She asked to herself. She took off the glasses she was wearing, placing them onto the table. “I haven’t seen you since you were…since you were…” “It has been quite awhile,” Tom said, finally getting the courage to speak up for himself. He moved from the stance he was standing at, and moved towards the table, extending his hand out to Mai’s mother. She looked at it for a moment, shaking his head. Tom realized that shaking hands really was a pop culture custom…Mai’s mother, being a bit old fashioned, probably didn’t shake hands very often. “You look so old now,” Mai’s mother said, looking deeply into his eyes. “Not the boy I remember anymore.” Tom was still quiet, not knowing what to say next. Mai’s mother had a way about her that was different from any other woman he had met. She was still, quiet…yet oh so vocal with her actions and gestures. Although she was just standing there, watching him, he knew that she was analyzing every bit of him. Every detail, every last curve in his face, was being placed under intense scrutiny. He found that his own mother did the same thing – she would run her hand down Tom’s arm, which used to be just a thin piece of skin and bone, nothing more. Now it was a bristling mass of muscle and substance, and Tom’s mother often didn’t know what to make of it. Tom supposed that it was a Vietnamese custom to compare the present with the past, but such customs often weren’t spoken of. It was most likely passed on from generation to generation, with Mai’s mother doing it to her, Mai’s mother seeing that and doing it on her daughter, and so on. “That boy is still inside of me,” Tom said, a sly smile coming onto his face. “I doubt he’ll ever go away.” “You were too serious when you were young,” Mai’s mother replied, placing her hands inside her apron. “I remember when you’d play around the store with your little friends…you’d ride your bikes through the entire store, scaring the customers and knocking over food. I’d yell at you, and you’d cry and cry…” It was an eerie feeling to be able to hear someone recall your past. Just as Mai’s mother was describing his actions, he was actually envisioning it in his mind. It became so crystal clear to him, almost like a vision. He was suddenly snapped out of the dream, reacting to the feeling of someone touching his shoulder. He looked over to his left, seeing Mai with a worried look on her face. Her hand was draped onto his shoulder, as if pulling him back into reality. Tom could only close his eyes, gathering his thoughts once more. “Are you sure you’re alright?” Mai asked. Tom nodded his head, vigorously this time, hoping that the strength that he denied his affliction would reassure Mai, but he doubted it. “I’m fine…I’m just a bit tired. I’ve been daydreaming a lot lately for some reason.” “Ok…” Mai replied, still watching Tom’s face. Tom was almost certain that she didn’t believe him – the tone of her voice gave away her concern. “How come you never returned to the store?” Mai’s mother asked. It was a sudden question, that all but startled Tom, who was still trying to poise himself. “Mrs. Le…” “Call me Doan,” she replied. “You are old enough to be able to call me by my first name. As you said before, you are not a child.” Tom may not be a child, but in the precense of Doan, he might as well have been a scared eight year old who didn’t know how to hold his own against an adult. There was a prejudice against children, one that said that they could not speak to adults as true adults. Politeness, although valued, would never be respected. There was a secondary force at work when at conversation – it was about pride and respect. If you spoke without dignity, whom ever you are conversing with will pick it up, and see you as less than him or her. Prejudice? Yes. Stereotypical? Yes. Judgmental? Absolutely. But it was inherent and Tom was already losing his war with confidence. “Of course he’s not,” Mai interrupted. She had noticed that Tom had become markedly stoic, not responding to Doan’s comments quickly. It was a sign of weakness. “I think he was scared after he destroyed that aquarium of yours. Remember? The one that held the crabs?” Doan nodded slowly, the memory returning to her. “Ah yes, I do remember that. As I recall, Tom and his other friends ran immediately away from the store. The only difference was that the other boys came back.”
