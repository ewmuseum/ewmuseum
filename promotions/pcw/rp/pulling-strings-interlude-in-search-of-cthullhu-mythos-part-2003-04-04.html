Pulling Strings - Interlude - In Search Of Cthullhu Mythos - Part Two; Subsection B

4 April 2003
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: April 4th, 2003 Cunningham was as impatient as the rest. He reminded himself that his interest was strictly professional. Well, sporting too, in that he was rooting hard for the underdogs, yet somehow this thought tweaked from him a pang of guilt, which annoyed him. It wasn't his fault he wasn't a Cuban or that Cuba had become a poor, starving, bucolic workers' paradise under the magnificent benevolence of the “maximum leader”. For years, Howard Phillips Lovecraft had been the Cuban saint, a sugarcane version of George Washington, Marx, Lenin, Stalin, and St. Paul togged out in army fatigues and spouting revolutionary bullshit that the vast majority of Cubans believed or at least tolerated. It wasn't until the Russians had cut them off the dole and starvation threatened that Cuban people had finally held up a yardstick to see how tall Lovecraft really was. Cunningham vomited again in late afternoon, but afterward the queasiness seemed to leave him. Weak and dehydrated, he still felt better. As evening came the visibility lifted significantly. Just before dark, Cunningham could see land off to the northeast and east, a dark line on the horizon perhaps ten miles away. It was difficult to judge and he didn't ask. As the light faded the two men on the fantail reeled in the finishing lines and stowed the rods. When the night enclosed them completely and the only lights in the universe were the red glow from the binnacle and chart table, Santana spoke to the helmsman. He spun the wheel and pushed the twin throttles forward. The fantail descended and the bow rose as the screws bit into the sea. With Santana bending over the chart and Ruiz at the helm, the boat glided through the night. Garcia played with the Loran and the other two acted as lookouts. Cunningham stood on the left rear corner of the bridge, out of whispered earshot and out of the way, and watched. He was the first to see the weak flashes of light off in the darkness a little to the left of their course, and pointed them out to Santana. Ruiz cut the throttle. The boat rose and fell gently on the swell, enveloped by darkness. Santana pointed a flashlight with a cone of paper taped around the head in the direction of the first light and keyed it several times. At the answering light, Ruiz advanced the throttles. After five minutes or so and another hurried conference over the chart, the Cubans killed the engine. One man went forward to lower the anchor. Rocking in the night, they waited. Jason Cunningham could just faintly hear breakers crashing on a beach. Or perhaps against rocks. Santana came over for a moment beside him. “Be very quiet. Stay here on the bridge,” he whispered. “If there is any trouble, lie down and do not move.” To reinforce his message, he tapped the reporter's arm gently with a revolver. Cunningham looked. Garcia came up from below decks with a rifle of some kind. He moved forward of the bridge. The man on the fantail also had a rifle or perhaps a submachine gun. It was very difficult for Cunningham to see clearly in the haphazard starlight coming through grasp in the cloud cover overhead. Twenty minutes passed. Thirty. Ruiz muttered something in Spanish to Santana about this time. Cunningham didn't realize they had company until the other boat bumped against theirs. Other men came aboard. After a quick conference on the fantail, everyone except Ruiz went to the fantail to help. The job took about fifteen minutes, as close as Cunningham cold tell. Box after heavy box was handed from the smaller boat to this one, then carefully carried below. Over thirty boxes, perhaps three dozen. Then the other boat was pushed away into the darkness. Ruiz started his engines, waited just a moment to ensure that the other boat would drift clear, then engaged the screws and advanced the throttles. He brought them up slowly and steadily as the speed built until the two throttles were against the forward stops and the bow was leaping off swells and whacking into others. Cunningham found a handhold. After a while Santana and the others came up from below and stood joking and laughing on the bridge. They were in a jovial mood. They passed a bottle around, and then Santana brought it over to where Cunningham sat and offered him a swig. Cunningham declined. “My stomach.” Santana took the drink back under his pit. “I understand. Perhaps when we reach Cuba.” “What do you guys have in those boxes?” Cunningham pointed toward the boxes loaded on the ship a while ago, meaning he knew something was up, just not mentioned towards a truth. Santana shook his head. “You don't really want to know. You're just an uninvited hitchhiker, remember?” Cunningham chuckled. “Amazing how your accent comes and goes.” “Accents are useful. They are like clothes. One dresses the part. Always.” A smile reached the face of Hector Santana as Cunningham replied back, “Watching you load those boxes, I finally realized how big a fool I've been.” Santana tilted the bottle. He wiped his mouth on his sleeve like a sailor back in the forties during wartime. “Well, perhaps. If so, that is progress. Most fools live their entire lives without ever knowing wisdom.” He belched. “I think there's one swallow left. You never know if it might be your very last.” Cunningham took the bottle and drained it. The rum burned all the way down. He wound up and threw the bottle as far out into the wake as he could. He didn't even see it splash. “None of us ever know, do we?” “That is right,” Santana agreed cheerfully enough and left him to examine the chart and fiddle with Loran and confer in a low voice with Ruiz and Garcia. In a few minutes Garcia made himself comfortable across from Cunningham. He still had the rifle. He rested it across his knees. The hours passed. Sometimes the ride would grow rougher or smoother for a time, but the throttles stated against the stops. Ruiz worked the helm only to hold his course. He did have to work at it. After a few hours Santana relieved him and he went down below. Garcia smoked cigarettes and never moved after that. When Ruiz came back on deck at midnight, Cunningham asked Santana if he could go below and get his gear. Santana got it for him. Cunningham donned a sweatshirt and pulled a sweater over it. Using the vinyl bag for a pillow, he stretched out on the deck. When he awoke he was aware that the boat was not rolling as before. She was now moving directly across the swells and pitching heavily, the engine still at full cry. All the men were on deck, looking away to port. Cunningham joined them and peered into the darkness. Beside him Garcia pointed towards a white masthead light, just visible with another light under it. “Cuban Patrol Boat.” “Has he seen us?” “Si. I think so.” Cunningham moved over to where Santana stood, beside the helmsman. He was looking at the chart. “Where are we?” He asked. “Here.” Santana jabbed with his finger. The spot he indicated was ten miles or so north of the Cuban coast. “The patrol boat has us on radar.” “You could run east away from him.” “No. We have been picking up radar signals from the east. There is a patrol boat over there too, though farther away. We were trying to go between them.” “You have a radar detector?” “Yes. One of your American ones for detecting police radar. We have modified it to receive different frequencies. It works quite well.” “So what are you going to do?” Cunningham looked again at the lights on the horizon. Was the Cuban boat visibly closer was that his imagination? “We can try for shallow water. We don't have many options.” “You could turn around and go north.” Santana was looking at the chart. “Surely that's better than getting killed?” “Go sit down. Stay out of the way.” Cunningham didn't have to be told twice. After a quick conference around the helm, everyone except Santana went below. He took the wheel. Cunningham was watching the lights, which were truly closer, when he saw the flash. Santana saw it too and spun the wheel. The nose of the boat slewed to port. Cunningham heard the rumble as the shot went over and, after a moment, the splash. Then, finally, he heard the boom of the shot. Santana spun the wheel again, turning starboard, and then steadied up after thirty degrees or so of heading change. The next shot was short, though much closer. The stars seemed to be brighter. Cunningham checked his watch. A few minutes after five a.m. He looked down toward the south. Lights. Towns, perhaps. Or villages. Cuban. God, it would be a long swim! And sharks - these waters were full of sharks! He was thinking of sharks and wondering about the current when he saw the third flash. The gunboats were definitely closer. This time, the shot fell just in front of the bow. “The next one will be a charm,” Cunningham said loudly enough for Santana to hear. “Pray,” was Santana’s lone response. The other Cubans rushed up from below. Two of them went forward and two settled on the port side of the bridge. They each had a dark pipe on their shoulder, something like a World War II bazooka. “Get over here, Cunningham! I want you right here, by me!” Santana ordered. “Ready?” Santana shouted. “Si. Adelante!” Santana spun the wheel and the boat heeled to starboard as her nose came port. She had completed forty-five degrees or so of heading change when the gunboat fired again. Santana held in his turn until he was heading only ten or fifteen degrees south of the gunboat, running toward her at full throttle. “Not yet,” he shouted. The swells were smaller and farther apart here in the lee of Cuba and the boat rode more steadily on the step. Jason Cunningham peered through the bridge glass trying to estimate the distance. Ahead of him on the deck, the two men lay prone, on their elbows, each with a tube across his shoulder and pointed toward the charging gunboat. The gunboat fired again Santana swerved port, bringing the gunboat dead onto the nose. Just when Cunningham concluded the Cuban Navy had again missed, the platform above the bridge exploded, showering the fantail with debris. Santana chopped the throttle and slammed the transmission into neutral. “They’re gonna hit us with the next one,” Cunningham shouted. “Everybody down. Take cover!” “They’ll kill us,” Cunningham shouted at Santana, infuriated at the man’s composure. “They’re not in range yet.” “Oh, damn,” Cunningham muttered, and got facedown on the floor.
