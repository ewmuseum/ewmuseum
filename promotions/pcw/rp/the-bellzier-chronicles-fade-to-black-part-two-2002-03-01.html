The Bellzier Chronicles: Fade to Black [Part Two]

1 March 2002
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: March 1, 2002 Oakland, CaliforniaOutside of Goodluck Supermarket Tom’s mind began to swirl as Fred’s words began to sink in. Suddenly it became apparent to him. “You were in the war…weren’t you?” “22nd division under Colonel Jesop,” Fred replied, nodding as if he was remembering his time there vividly. “That war was nothing more than a blanket attempt to stop communism – something that could not be done. It should have been apparent to us that communism would not survive, after all, people want to make decisions for themselves. Free will is something that all humans desire – communism just snuffs that out. But off we went, brave soldiers fighting for their country and everything it stood for. We were so justified in our actions, that it didn’t even matter that we were invading an independent country which was struggling for their own identity and dwelling in their own problems. When I stepped foot onto Vietnam, just air lifted from the U.S.S. Birmingham, what I felt was the utter despair that we did not belong there.” “Vietnam was fiercely divided, north and south,” Tom said, remembering the stories that his mother had told him about the war. “The north were mostly the rich land owners, while the south was made up of rice farmers and poor peasants. The north had all of the power.” “You know your history,” Fred said, smiling. “That’s good. My company was rooted in the peasant camps of South Vietnam. There were some brigades that took advantage of the situation, pillaging villages and only seeing the enemy as an asian face. I knew better, and so did my company. We grew attached to the village we stayed in, helping them rebuild what the north had stolen from them, and I even fell in love with a young Vietnamese woman myself. Her name was Le Trong.” Tom smirked, looking down at the ground once more. “That was my grand mother’s name.” “It’s beautiful name, isn’t it?” “It most certainly is.” Fred leaned his head back onto the concrete wall behind him and sighed. “She was like a ray of sunlight for me, every time I saw her. I picked up the language fairly quickly…well, I had to. It was the only way I could figure out whether she was saying, ‘I like you,’ or ‘Screw off!’ Oh, we had some great times together. I remember on days where I didn’t have to patrol, I’d sneak her out of her room, and we’d just walk in the jungle, making our way to this rock I had always passed on my patrols. It had a great view of the moon. I could swear that when we laid next to each other, the moon would be staring directly at us, telling us that we were right to be together.” “I suppose she never introduced you to her parents…” Fred laughed loudly. “I’d like to see the look on her parents face when they saw me strolling through the door. Hell, I’d bet they’d beat me down just for trying to court their daughter. No, I never met her parents. She knew, just as I did, that they would never approve. Still, that didn’t stop me from trying.” “And then the war ended…” Tom began to say. He knew how the story would end. It was a story that many veterans from war told, meeting a love of their life overseas, yet not being able to see them after the war was over, thus missing their chance for true love. Some were reminiscent of their experiences, while others were ultimately depressed. Tom wasn’t sure how Fred viewed the situation. “And then…I lost her…” Fred said, trailing off. “I didn’t even get a chance to say good bye or anything. Fighting had broken out about three miles up from the village. A Viet Cong convoy had been spotted by our scouts, so we were ordered to engage and entrap the convoy. We just picked up and left. I have nothing from her…not a note…not a letter…” “I’m sure it was a very trying time for you…” Fred shrugged, trying to appear as if he didn’t care, but wasn’t fooling Tom. “This is a big world. People are always losing touch, even with themselves. Maybe it wasn’t meant to be…” “Or maybe it was…” “I suppose we’ll never know.” Tom had no response for Fred’s utter disregard for his feelings. Tom had a feeling that Fred was hiding the hurt that resided within him about his flame, but didn’t wish to push the subject any further. He decided that it might be the best to just leave now. “Well, I appreciate the time you had to speak with me. I wish you the best.” Tom took out his wallet, pulled out the twenty dollar bill, and handed it to Fred. He looked at the bill, not knowing whether to take it or not. He finally took the bill in his hands, stared at it for a moment, and then looked back at Tom. “What is this for?” Tom shrugged, huddling into his coat a bit more. “I’m not sure. Maybe you’ll find better use for it then I would. I have a feeling I’d spend it on something stupid like gum or a movie rental.” Fred chuckled, placing the bill in his pocket along with his change. “You know, I haven’t held a bill larger than a one dollar bill in my hand in…god, I don’t even know when. Are you sure you don’t need this money?” “Go ahead and take it.” Fred smiled, standing up and heading over to the shopping cart he owned, which was sitting silently next to the doorway. He began digging under the endless number of aluminum cans which were collected inside, and finally pulled out a small brown box. Tom looked at it curiously, as it read, “Whitman’s Works,” on the cover of the box. Fred opened the box, revealing a small treasure trove of items. They ranged from small sea shells to marbles, but of course, the smallest tokens could mean the world to some people. Fred revealed a small bag of dirt, which sat at the back of the wooden box. He held it in the palm of his hand, looking at it longingly. “Tell Mr. Tom,” Fred said, staring away from the bag and at Tom. “Have you ever been to Vietnam?” “No,” he replied, with a stutter in his voice. “Unfortunately I haven’t.” “That’s a shame,” Fred said, shaking his head. “It’s such a beautiful country. A good buddy of mine used to tell me that the he thought the jungles itself could come alive and watch your every move as you made your way around it. I think I can help you experience it. I want you to have this…” Fred took the bag of dirt from his hand, and placed it into Tom’s hand. Tom looked at the bag curiously, wondering what the significance behind a bag dirt could be. He stared back up at Fred, looking for answers. “That’s dirt I took from Vietnam, before I was shipped back home,” Fred said, noticing Tom’s confusion. “I want you to have it. Maybe it’ll convince you to take a trip back there some day.” “I can’t accept this. I’m sure it means a lot to you.” “That it does,” he replied, watching Tom hold the bag of precious dirt in his hands. “But I think it’ll mean more to you. I’ve had that bag in possession for quite some time now. I think it should help one of its own find their way back to its home, don’t you think?” Tom shook his head in disbelief. “I don’t what to say. You know, when I gave you that twenty dollar bill, I didn’t expect anything in return.” Fred smiled. “I know. That’s why I want you to have that bag.” Tom placed the bag in his coat pocket, making sure that it wouldn’t spill. Fred made his way back to his doorway bedroom, waving at Tom from a distance. Tom waved back, and began walking away with a smile on his face. Who says that strangers were all hostile? The night was turning cold now, and the cold puff of frigid air that came out from Tom’s mouth as he breathed was a testament to that statement. He pulled the dark overcoat he was wearing closer in to his body, wishing that he had a scarf to place around his chilled, exposed neck. At this time of the year, the Bay Area was beginning to warm up from the unusually wet winter season, and the evenings would be comfortable. This night seemed to beckon back for the stormy nights of December, bringing in dipping temperatures and a forever, bone shattering chilled wind. Tom looked around him quickly, watching a small number of people exiting restaurants and stores, making their way back home. It was times like these that made Tom wish that he had someone at his side. Tom knew that the empty streets could be construed as giving off a haunting feeling, but he felt at home here. His auntie, bigger sister to his mother, often took him to Chinatown to buy food and to run different errands. She was the baby sitter that was always available to take care of Tom whenever his parents. To Tom, she was like a surrogate friend to him in his dark days of trying to fit in. Even through high school and college, she was always willing to lend a ear to Tom who felt that his dad was too strict to understand, and his mom was too unwilling to adjust to changing times to relate to him. It was at this time that he felt that his auntie would be able to aid him in his ultimate quest… Finally, after walking for seemingly hours down the streets of Chinatown, he came upon a small grocery store. Tom stopped at the store’s entrance and glanced up, looking up at the store’s sign which read, “Good Luck Supermarket.” He remembered being taken here as a child and running up and down the aisles like a run away dog. He’d play tag with the other kids of the neighborhood, who didn’t view Tom as different or an outcast, but as another kid to have fun with. Sure, they’d have too much fun at times, but what kids didn’t? Whenever a jar dropped or an aisle was dirtied by their foot prints, his auntie would come down at them and offer them “White rabbits” to behave themselves. “White rabbits” were vanilla flavored candies, with a little rabbit picture on the center of the candy. Although a bit lame, Tom enjoyed the taste of the sweet and would gladly trade running around the store to a candy. Tom walked into the store, looking around briefly to see if anything had changed. The aisles were still in the same placement, and even the fruits had the same smell as they did before. He looked down at the door entrance floor, seeing little skid marks run alongside the floor. Those marks were made by him and his friends in their younger days, when they’d ride their bikes into the store for the challenge. As a consequence for their ambition, they’d make unruly marks on the floor and get a broom beating by the workers. He looked over at a fruit cart next to him, advertising apples for only twenty-nine cents a pound. Tom took a ripe, red apple from the cart, and took a bite out of it. The sweet juices of the apple dripped down his chin, causing him to wipe it quickly away with the back of his hand. The apple was just as juicy and as wonderful as any apple he had tasted before, and he suddenly realized that it was a taste he had almost forgotten with the busy supermarket chain produce he had been conforming to. Suddenly, a loud shout could be heard from his left. “You! Thief! Put that apple down!” Tom turned around, already beginning to babble about how he was going to pay for the apple anyways. As he saw where the shout was originating, he saw a tall asian woman coming out from the breads section. She had on a white t-shirt and light blue jeans, anchored with a red apron. She had a scowl on her face as she came closer to Tom, carrying a dough roller in her hand. “I…I was going to pay for it…” Tom stammered, putting the apple on the ground as if it was a loaded gun. “Of course you were,” the woman said sarcastically. “Next you’re going to tell me that our produce is so good that it deserves to sit on the dirty ground.” “Oh…I’m so sorry…” Tom replied quickly, picking up the apple from the ground. He wiped off some of the dirt which was now on the apple, and gave it a quick blow of air to clean it off. “There now…it’s uhm, well, it’s almost clean now…” “I should call the police,” the woman replied angrily. She seemed to be gripping the dough roller in such a fashion as to be able to swing it with great ferocity. Tom stepped back a few steps, hopefully stepping out of harms way, but the woman was approaching him quickly. Just as Tom was about to try to beg for forgiveness, he got a closer look at the woman’s face. The curves, the shape…the eyes…he suddenly realized who she was. A smile crossed his face and he exclaimed, “Mai! Mai, it’s me. Don’t you remember? Le petit garcon….” The woman stopped, remembering the name, and stared at Tom carefully. Suddenly her eyes bulged wide and she dropped the dough roller on the ground, placing her hand over her mouth. “Oh my god…Tom, is that you?” Tom nodded slowly. “It’s me alright. Tom Lexian – all grown up now. It’s been awhile Mai.” “More than awhile…” Tom sighed, closing his eyes. “A very long time…”
