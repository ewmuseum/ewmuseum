Momento Mori (Part 1: Preludes & Nervosas)

15 December 2002
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: December 15th, 2002 My name is Alexandria Tomas, and I am a student of psychiatry at Miskatonic University in Arkam, Massachusetts. For my student thesis I have been asked to compose a comprehensive psychiatric profile of anyone of moderate renown, preferably with a history of mental instability. At first, I had trouble locating someone with adequate material for an interesting paper, until I came upon the story of one Marvin Harris. Now while not my first choice as far as media exposure went, Marvin’s extensive history of abuse, repeated institutionalization, and numerous mental breakdowns were far too enticing for me to pass up. I was worried at first that his profession as a pro-wrestler would make him hard to track down and interview, but I was morbidly lucky to find out he was currently confined to a hospital bed in Daytona Beach, Florida. He had just awoken from a severe five month coma, and immediately went off to settle a score with the man who put him there. When he had returned his hands were covered with blood and he was on the verge of collapsing. The paramedics, after consulting with the police, agreed to let him recuperate in their recovery ward until charges had been filed. They never were. I immediately caught a flight out to Daytona to meet with him, and while I was nervous as to what kind of reaction I would get from such an unexpected encounter, I knew this may be my one chance to catch Marvin off his guard. On the flight there I looked the copies of his criminal, medical, and biographical records that I’d had faxed to the university. Apparently he had a fairly mediocre childhood, raised in a white lower-middle class family in Panama City, Florida. A report filed by his elementary school consoler mentions constant bruises, possibly due to abuse, but has no mention of a follow-up investigation. The medical reports show no signs of chemical imbalance as the cause of his mental instability, and even note him being in exceptional health. His criminal record is by the far his longest and most comprehensive record. Marvin was arrested numerous times on ‘mutual fighting’ charges, but apparently none of them went to court. His luck finally ran out when he was seventeen, however, and he was convicted of assault. He spent the rest of age seventeen in juvenile detention and the rest of the five year sentence on probation. After getting out of juvie he seemed to have had a change of attitude, he cleaned up his act and earned a G.E.D. within a three month period. He applied for college, but his significantly low SAT scores prevented him from getting any financial aid or scholarship and he regretfully had to put his college ambitions on hold. It was then that he was introduced to the wrestling business. He was only a passing spectator at first, but quickly fell in love with concept and eagerly sought out a trainer. His ‘employment records’ are filled with numerous little memorandums of praise from his many teachers, many complimenting his adaptability and endurance. He ‘graduated’ to the status of wrestler upon reaching his twenty-first birthday. These were not as happy times as you might think, for while Marvin’s pro-wrestling career had just begun to bloom, his younger brother’s sports career was impaled on a drug possession charge. Their father, Jacob Harris, set the full blame on Marvin for his brother’s drug habit, and promptly disowned him from the family. My records make no mention of him contacting his parents since then. I touched down in Daytona a little after noon, and though I felt jetlagged from my journey down the eastern seaboard I was determined to learn more about this man. I took a cab from the airport to the Our Lady of Perpetual Mercy Hospital nestled in the center of busy Daytona Beach. A short time later I stood beneath the massive, polished structure of glass and metal and knew this was my last chance to turn back. I held the door handle of the cab for a long moment, debating in my mind what I was about to do, but finally closed it with a resounding slam and began the walk toward the entrance. * * * The recovery ward seemed like a rather jovial place, for a hospital anyway. Everywhere I looked I saw families and friends gathered around members of their flock who were pleasantly recovering from whatever ailment befell them. You could see the relief in their smiles, and the way they muted and understated the wellspring of emotions was touching. By the time I reached the room the orderlies said contained Marvin Harris I was shining brightly and overcome with the optimistic spirit. That all faded when I walked inside. Now, I knew from seeing brief footage of some of his more publicized matches that he was far from a handsome man, and the gasmask was a little unnerving to say the least, but what I saw upon that hospital bed filled me a sense of pity and disgust one usually reserves for a mutt that’s been run down on the highway. He was a large, stocky man covered under the thin sheen of a hospital gown, and a wide assortment of bandages. His meaty hands were strapped down to the sides of the bed, and would periodically jerk hard against them in futile protest. His eyes alternated from being dropped and glazed to being suddenly wide and alert, as if he was fighting off some onslaught of fatigue. I must have stood in the doorway and stared for in awe for about five minutes, but it didn’t matter because he didn’t even seem to acknowledge my presence the entire time. Finally, I composed myself and knocked against the open door beside me; he immediately spun around and narrowed those muddled eyes on me. “What the (expletive) do you want?” he said, “I told you I wasn’t going to take anymore goddamned meds and I meant it.” He studied me for a long moment. “Mr. Harris, I’m student doing my college thesis, and I know this must seem sudden and awkward, but I really need to talk to you for a second. If it’s alright, that is.” “What the hell kind of thesis is it?” “Well, actually, to tell the truth, it’s a thesis on you.” “A thesis on me?” He snorted out a laugh. “What exactly are you studying?” “Psychiatry.” “Psychiatry?” he repeated flatly, and under his steely gaze I bowed my head and nodded quickly. After a long pause of silence, he turned his head toward the window and stared out at the skyline, eyes darting after the birds that would fly past. “Ask your questions then.” I had already begun to turn and be on my way, anticipating his refusal, so when he replied in the opposite I was momentarily taken aback. I didn’t want to linger though, and quickly I made my way over to the small table set aside for visitors and began unloading my interviewing supplies; a small tape recorder, notepad, tapes, and pencils from my backpack. I could feel him watching my every movement from the bed, feel those eyes moving over me, and I tried not to seem too afraid as I turned the tape recorder on and set it on the small platter set over his chest where they’d put his meals. As I sat it down a blast of hot breath from the ventilator of his mask hit my hand and caused me to jerk it back suddenly. He only chuckled at this, seeming almost accustomed to it. I made a small quick note of it before beginning the interview. “Interview with Marvin Harris, date of October 16, 2002, the time is roughly half-past noon. Mr. Harris, could you tell me when you had your first psychological episode?” “Call me Marvin. I insist.” “Alright, Marvin. When did you have your first psychological episode?” “That’s a tough question, doc. See, according to you and yours I don’t actually consider myself insane, I live in a delusional world where my way of thinking is no different from the norm, and it’s everyone else who is dysfunctional. So, what you mean to ask is when did I notice that the rest of the world had gone mad, yes?” “If you prefer to see it that way, Mr. Har-..Marvin. Please, continue.” His expression seemed to soften and his features didn’t seem as tense, almost like I’d passed some sort of test. “I was twenty-one, and I’d just been kicked out of my father’s house. I went on the road with the local wrestling promotion, but a combination of frustration and nagging depression soured my ring performance and I was fired. There I was, homeless, without a family, a job, or anything worth living for. So, like anyone pushed to such extremes I tried to kill myself.” “How?” He shook his head and chuckled, like he was more embarrassed than anything about it. “I stabbed myself in the gutty-whats with a steak knife and then stumbled all the way to the emergency room with it still sticking out my side. After they stitched me up I had to see a shrink on a mandatory basis for at least six months. That’s how I met Fraud.” “Fraud who?” His head bowed, his breathing began to grow shallow, and when he finally looked up I could clearly see the pain dredging up such memories had brought him. “Professor Sickman Bartholomew Fraud. Yes, that is his real name. I was lucky; he didn’t really give a (expletive) about me at that point. That didn’t come till later.” I had heard of the rather, unorthodox, Prof. Fraud while I was still in my freshman year at Miskatonic. He advocated a more ‘traditional’ approach to the treatment of mental illness, often considered cruel and excessive in our modern times. He had powerful friends though, mostly found in the diehard conservative and religious sector that used him to help ‘cure’ homosexuals and brainwash misbehaving youths into zombies, and had gained a sort of bitter fame among the psychiatric community. I knew if I pressed him here I could be putting us both in danger, so I quickly made the decision to shift topics. “I’ll certainly have to look into that. So, Marvin, what are you involved in currently, and what effect does it have on your mental state?” He tilted his head to the other side, eyes still glued to me, and I could tell he’d picked up on my hesitation to talk about Fraud. Thankfully, he decided not to pry. “The doctor’s say there’s still a possibility I suffered brain damage they don’t know about yet, and naturally they want to do more tests. Between you and me, though, I’m getting the (expletive) out of dodge and getting back on the road, on the wrestling circuit.” “Does your career really mean that much that you’d put your life in jeopardy like that?” He shrugged and motioned around the room. “Look around; what else have I got in this world, eh? My family avoids me, or exploits me; the people I trust always turn on me. There’s no way in hell I could get a nine-to-five job anymore, not with my past. I’ve come too far down this road to ever be able to turn back. It may cost me everything, including my life, but I’d rather die in the ring than out on streets. It’s a matter of survival, of dignity, of honor. These things are all I have left. Those people out there, who enjoy watching me get torn apart, are my only family; they’re sick and morbid, like me. It’s them who I work hard for, not a belt or a paycheck.” I can’t say for certain I understood his motivations, but I did admire his dedication. I made the decision to end our session for that day, with the explicit promise that we’d continue it as soon he had gotten settled on the road. After that I gathered my things and made for the door, I had a lot of editing work to do, but as I was walking out I heard Marvin say to me over my shoulder; “If you want something interesting for your report, go find my old taped therapy sessions at Fraud’s asylum in Virginia. They can explain everything.” I nodded, and left. (Some of you craftier gents may remember this RP from the failed debackle known as the PURE Deathmatch Tournament. Well, since that fell through I see no need in letting the RP series going to waste, so here it is, in all its repost glory!)
