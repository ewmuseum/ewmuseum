Date: Um... Now? An Analysis of Urban Hip Hop & Rap CultureStudy Subject: Dante Jackson Acknowledgements -- Sandy Wong, thank you for being my eyes and providing me with visual information to support my thesis. Rush Mason, thank you for being utterly unimportant to me and leaving me alone for a few days so I could do this. Duke Williams, thank you for tagging with me on Tuesday Turmoil. Our win over Xavier Kannon and Rush Mason was cut from programming, but we know what went down. You powerbombed Rush Mason, gave that Whiskey Bent and Hellbound to Kannon to drop him on top of Mason, I sandwiched them both with a Shooting Star Double Stomp, you pinned Kannon for the win. Rush Mason, no thanks given because he is so freaking annoying. Dante Jackson, thank you for being you and giving me a thesis study to go off of. Rush Mason, God I hate him so much. Urban Hip Hop & Rap culture has come out of nowhere in the past twenty years, growing from an underground following merely in urban clubs, to part of the larger pop culture. Urban minorities, suburban white children, Mexicans, Japanese, Europeans... peoples around the world have embraced this form of music and its artists. Dante Jackson, though given a 'A' for effort, seems to have trouble catching on. He's a good wrestler, but the hip hop industry and fans have yet to take him into their arms and homes with as much enthusiasm or zeal as Snoop Dogg, Eminem, Xhibit, Sean 'Puff Diddy' Combs(he's in line for another name change, by publication this one will probably be outdated), Ludacris, Nas, Jay-Z, or even Jimmy Fallon. That's right, Jimmy Fallon has a larger cult following than Dante Jackson. Jimmy Fallon was nominated for a Grammy and Dante Jackson wasn't. The relative insignificance of the Grammy aside (especially when they didn't give one to India.Arie, those bastards; Alicia Keys sings about a black man in prison and then he gets out and still can't get things together, they give her like 10... India.Arie sings about self-confidence, the power of faith, and believing you can do anything, and they give it to some 300-year-old country singer!) it's still a mark of respect by the music industry as a whole for CEO's, producers, and other executives to nominate someone for a Grammy. Then again, the Neptunes didn't get nominated either, but they're producers and get paid $200k a track anyway. Let us look at exactly what makes a rapper: the criteria for being considered the best and why Dante Jackson apparently does not fit that billing. After much research, there seems to be certain guidelines set that must be met in order for a rapper to be considered the best. By revealing these, I may in fact be aiding Dante Jackson in his quest to be a great rapper. According to the industry and the fans, he's only mediocre at best; and since they would be paying his check, it's their opinions that matter. It can't be otherwise, because if he was only doing this for himself and not for pop culture validation, then he would be a street performer and rap for free instead of charging $15.99 for a 7 track CD that only lasts 28.7 minutes. That in itself is a bit of a joykill; who releases CDs that last less than 40 minutes anymore? Oh, right, Andrew W.K. And I know that Dante Jackson wouldn't want to be counted in any of the same categories as Andy over there. But without further ado, here is the list of what makes a rapper great. Rap about street life Dante Jackson actually misses this point completely. His target audience has to be urban consumers. From there, his work will move out to everyone else. But pop culture fame aside, you must still gain acceptance in the urban circuit. And that means rapping about what they know. Dante Jackson has the background to fall back on, he is a product of the urban environment as well. But Dante Jackson turns everything he does into a battle-rap. People like him for wrestling, some people like him for rapping. But he can be both without combining the two (it would actually be better since his split concentration tends to lead to failed endeavors in both industries). The rap fans don't want to hear him talk about Chet King or Rent-A-Hero. They want to hear him talk about street life, things they know and care about. Dante Jackson forgot this very simple rule, and thus has yet to become a successful rapper. Battle-Rap must at some point be directed to others in the recording industry Eminem seems like he talks about everybody, but everything he says is directed at critics and other industry insiders, and especially recording artists. All recording artists who speak ill of him are fair game. It's a sort of rapper's etiquette, you only battle rap against those in the recording industry, or otherwise those who can release on a track or in a music publication something to get you back. Dante Jackson breaks this etiquette. Every rap on his entire CD (I WANT MY MONEY BACK, Dante had better bring $16.95 to the ring with him) always deals with wrestling opponents. The rapping fans don't care about his opponents. He could at least say something about another rapper, but he doesn't. Why? My hypothesis is that he recognizes that his skills in rapping are average at best, and that he would be lyrically torn to shreds by his industry superiors. In wrestling he manages to hold his own; in the imagery and representation of war, the spiritual killing fields of battle-rap, he would rather stay in the rear and fight the civilians than go into armed combat against the highly trained and experienced soldiers. Such a rap philosophy violates the rap etiquette and further makes a case against Dante Jackson being a credible rapper. Rap about women. All male rappers incorporate women into their lyrics in some way. Some go ahead and talk about the women whom are hoes, but it can't end there. The rappers need to give some (to the ratio of 30-50% of all lyrical content to avoid going overboard and totally alienating males *coughJARULEcough*) lyrical acknowledgement to women. Specifically, what type of women they like, what type of women they have, what they like women to do for and to them. Rapping about the companionship (however ephemeral or long-lasting) of a woman is not a foreign concept in the world of rap, in fact, it's required. Now, hardcore rap always has the males grabbing their testicles and talking about making people their 'bitch,' using phrases like 'Deez nutz in yo mouf!' They need to balance that with some dedication of lyrics to the women they like, lest they be confused for homophiliacs instead of the standard homophobes that all rappers are. LL Cool J manages to dedicate much of his music to women while still keeping both genders for a fanbase. P. Diddy needed a girl twice last year. And even Scarface gave women a mention in The Fix. Dante Jackson only raps about men, and opponents at that. This simple fact leads me to three hypotheses. 1) Dante Jackson forgets to rap about women. 2) Dante Jackson expects the women to come to him whether he speaks about them or not. 3) Dante Jackson has a crush on his opponents. While Dante Jackson's honest answer to the question of which of these is true is required, this isn't a search for the truth, merely a thesis discussion of why Dante Jackson isn't the best rapper he could be. Rappers must 'bling' The most successful rappers are those who rap about the good life: the cars, helicopters, mansions, jewelry... all collectively known as 'bling.' Rappers may not have this at the beginning, but they need only rap about it and the fans will pay them to live that lifestyle. Sandy and other eye-witness accounts confirm that Dante Jackson is lacking bling. He drives a 1995 turqouise Mazda Miata, wears old clothes and Puma sneakers, pays on his credit card instead of taking out a big roll of cash... no bling, in other words. The most identifiable symbol of bling, the $50 thousand platinum chain around the neck is also missing from Dante's wardrobe. At most, he'll wear a normal herring-bone chain with a silver cross on it. Low-tech, no bling. It can't be a security matter; many other rappers have it and flaunt it, and walk around as if no one will try anything. Dante Jackson is physically capable of defending himself, and he does have an entourage of friends to protect him. And given the requirement of a rapper to be 'hood rich' (have wealth and extravagantly flaunt it to the point of being the black version of the Great Gatsby) and bling, one can only assume that Dante Jackson hasn't yet reached that upper echelon of rappers. One's teeth must be covered with fillings. The more fillings, the better. These jobs run anywhere from $30 thousand to $100 thousand depending on if gold or platinum are used for the fillings and if diamonds are used (and the quality and weight of the diamonds). Baby of the Big Tymers/Cash Money Millionaires has a mouth full of fillings. Lil Wayne, Trick Daddy likewise, but not to such an extent. The reasoning behind this? To have a mouth filled with diamonds and precious metal gives a symbol of a person who's words are worth much. That rapper is an expensive speaker. Dante Jackson has all his own teeth with no fillings and diamonds on them. Another missed requirement necessary to be at the top. Signature wardrobe Certain rappers have a signature style like Outcast or Nappy Roots, but Dante Jackson is just 'old clothes from 5-10 years ago,' which doesn't work. Not even that, but he doesn't have his own clothing line like all the best rappers do. *Shakes head* No respect for those that came before him. Dante Jackson considers himself the beginning of the rap industry. It is career suicide not to give credit and homage to those who came before you and set the foundations of the industry. Indeed, ask Dante Jackson who Rakim or Eric B are, Bizmarkee, Grandmaster Flash, Public Enemy, NWA, Run DMC, MC Lyte; ask him about any of the old school rappers that created the industry that he tries to get by in and he'll answer in some sort of gibberish as though you asked him what's the capital of Albania in Latin. A rapper must acknowledge and respect those who came before him. Dante Jackson doesn't. Not in the wrestling industry, and certainly not in the rap & hip hop industry. I could actually go on for a good while longer, but I wish not to take up too much of your time. But certainly you can see how Dante Jackson is missing the tools necessary to be a great rapper. Or at the very least, he refuses to use them. Only Dante can decide for himself if he's going to drudge forever in raps low-card, or if he'll put the total package together and rise to fame. And losing battle raps to Chet King (though he is good at it) isn't the right way to start.
