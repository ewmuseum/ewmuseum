The Bellzier Chronicles: Fade to Black [Part One]

27 February 2002
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: February 27, 2002 Oakland Chinatown in front of Goodluck SupermarketOakland, California 4:59 am I woke up startled in my car. That’s when I remembered. I was not in some dream world anymore, but reality in which I was a marked man. But I was also an angry man. They had Nat, and that was something that I would not stand for. But what could I do? I could go on some crusade and attempt to save her, but it would do nothing but make the situation worse. I was only an individual, but in my warped mind, I believed that I could do more than the common joe off the street. I guess that makes me special in a way. Out of the corner of my eye, I could see an individual walking up towards the car. I leaned up in my seat to get a better view of the person. It was the man from the stall at Denny’s. He was back. He wiped away some of the shattered glass from the drivers seat window and laughed. “A spy should always invest in some bullet proof glass, my friend.” “Don’t call me your friend,” I retorted. “And I’m not a spy.” “Tom, I have news for you that will probably make you very happy,” He answered back. “We have Nat back.” “What?!” I replied with a startled look on my face. “When did this happen? How? Where?” “Calm down,” he said, motioning for me to sit back down. “Our men picked her up from Bellzier goons. Seems like they were driving her around, keeping us from tracking her. But she’s safe now, so you don’t have to worry. We’ll arrange for you two to meet in an hour.” I was relieved. All of a sudden, my world seemed to come back together like a puzzle that needed to be solved. The man knelt down towards me and smiled. “You love her, don’t you?” I stared over at the store in front of us. “That’s the Good Luck market. I used to go there as a child, you know? And there was this girl whose parents worked there. We grew up as children, and as we got older, I think we became closer than just friends. I can still remember the day I returned here. I was so much older, yet still the same person.” “You met with her again?” “Yes. I can remember it like it was yesterday…” Flashback “Hey man, spare a brother a dime would ya? I’m awfully hungry.” Tom Lexian stared down at the homeless man who sat in between the doorway of an apartment complex. He was Caucasian, who wore a dirty, black overcoat and ripped blue jeans. On his head, he had a smudged New York Yankees cap, which appeared moldy from many long, wet walks in the a Bay Area rainy season. His face appeared to be tired; wrinkles strewn down from his eyes down to his chin, being markedly hidden by his long beard. The hair that could be seen on his head was a mix of gray and black, and fell down mid neck on his body. His beard resembled the same color, except it appeared to be well groomed, which was a stark contrast to his hair. The sight of the man was a common one for Tom, who came to Oakland from his San Francisco apartment to visit his parents occasional. Tom actually grew up in his parents’ home which was in Frisco, but after Tom graduated from college, his parents decided to move back to Oakland. At first, Tom thought that they might be heading for the suburbs of Piedmont or perhaps the busy streets of Downtown Oakland where much of the commercial stores were located. To his surprise, his parents moved to Chinatown a year after Tom decided to skip Graduate school altogether and pursue his dream into professional wrestling. Their home was now located in a small apartment complex called, “Seaside Homes.” It had probably been almost six months since Tom had last visited his parents, but he planned on seeing them soon. At the moment he was more pressed with other concerns. He turned his attention back to the homeless man who continued to look up at him from his conclave within the doorway. He was covering himself with an olive green colored blanket, which he used to cover himself up to his chest. It was too small to cover his entire body, however, so his feet stuck out from the bottom of the covers. As a child, Tom remembered walking with his mother down to the store fronts in San Francisco, and seeing pan handlers running alongside the sidewalks. His mother would just turn the other cheek to their pleas for extra change, and walk forward without taking a second glance at them. Tom noticed that most other San Francisco residents followed suit, using an ignorance moniker to respond to any pan handlers. Tom, as a child, didn’t understand what the problem was with giving them a few coins of change. After all, they were the ones with clean clothes on their backs and a stable roof over their heads. One incident Tom remembered avidly was one day in Golden Gate Park when he was playing in the park with a few of his friends, under the supervision of his mother, of course. While they were tossing around a Frisbee, Tom was stopped by a homeless man whom had been watching them from afar. He was African-american, and wore dirty white shorts, coupled with a Hawaiian shirt that looked out of place in today’s times. He came up to Tom with a broad smile, and asked if he could play as well. Tom agreed happily, and they set out to throw the Frisbee a bit more. It was an extraordinarily warm day, and it was thankful that he and his friends had on loose, cool clothing as well as a large stock of juice and water in his mother’s cooler. After they had played a bit, they stopped in order to rest from the unbearable sun and to take a quick drink. As they enjoyed a nice kool aid break, the homeless man asked if he could have a sip of the drink as well. He was sweating profusely, and appeared to be a bit out of breath. Tom did him one better and gave him an entire canister of juice that he could drink. The homeless man replied with a nice, “Thank you,” and went on his way. A few moments later, his mother came back from her short walk around the park, and scolded Tom. When they came back home, now separated from all of Tom’s friends, she told Tom never to give homeless anything, whether it be money or food. She explained that they had to take care of themselves, and giving to strangers will only prompt them to ask for more. It was similar to the saying, “Give someone an inch, and they’ll take a foot.” Tom, being as young as he was, could not respond and just stood there, taking the tongue lashing that his mother gave him. It was that day that would forever tarnish Tom’s view on the homeless. He would not become as stingy as his mother, however. Throughout high school he would continue to help as he could through community service and worked with people less fortunate than most. He thought that if roles were reversed, he would hope that people would be kind enough to aid him as well. Tom pulled out some loose change from his pocket, shuffling it in his hand. He had two quarters, a dime, an assorted amount of pennies. It wasn’t a lot of change, but it was better than nothing. He thought about reaching into his wallet and perhaps finding some bills to give to the man, but he remembered that he had stuffed one twenty-dollar bill in there before he left. He doubted somewhat that the homeless man would have change. “There you go,” Tom said, giving the man a reassuring smile. As the man took the change into his hand, Tom watched his face carefully. He envisioned the man doing one of two things: a) Either taking the money graciously, thanking Tom for his gratitude or b) Looking at the meager change and asking for more. If the latter occurred, Tom was unsure how he would respond to the man. Thankfully, he took the change in his hand, counting it in his head, and looked back up to Tom with a smile. The smile showed Tom the man’s rapidly decaying teeth, as he obviously didn’t have the resources to keep them clean. “Why, thank you sir,” He replied, placing the change in his pocket carefully. As he unzipped the pocket, some other change fell out, leaving pennies and nickels flailing over the ground. The man sighed slowly, and got up to pick up the change. Tom bent down to catch some of the fleeing coins before they ended up in the street, and collected at least thirty cents worth of pennies to give back to the man. The man laughed as he shook his head at what had just happened. He accepted the coinage and went back to his conclave. “Thank you again. You certainly are a kind one.” “No problem,” Tom replied. “I would give you more, but that’s the only change I have. I mean, I would give you some bills but all I have is a twenty dollar bill and…” “Hey, hey, hey…” The man said, waving his hands in the air. “Don’t worry about it, man. I’m cool. You actually gave me something, which is more than I can say about most people around here.” “It was only a little bit of change…” “No, I’m talking about kindness and generosity.” Tom looked down at his feet after the compliment. He had never been good with accepting praise, especially from someone that was supposed to be under him. After all, this man was homeless – no job, no home…no life. Tom didn’t like to think about people in that way. He always figured that we were all equal, and nothing can change that fundamental belief. Still, he had no response for the man who looked at him with such gracious eyes. “My name is Fred Johnson,” the homeless man said, extending his hand out to Tom. He now had a sincere look on his face and appeared to be interested in beginning a conversation with Tom. Tom shook the man’s hand. It felt like rubbing his skin against sand paper, as Fred’s hand was rough and gritty. “Tom Lexian,” He replied, smiling back at him. “I wonder how many Toms there are in this world,” Fred asked playfully. “Probably a lot, I’d say.” “I wouldn’t doubt it,” Tom replied, “It’s a rather common name.” “And it’s not quite your native language now, is it?” Tom stared at the man, trying to decipher if there was any hint of an alternative motive with his question. Tom’s appearance was anything but foreign to some people. He had the asian face, the asian eyes, and he has been told many times that he would never be viewed as a true American. That bothered him throughout grade school and high school, but in college, he realized that it didn’t matter anymore. The United States was built on immigrants from its very foundations, so in reality, every American was a foreigner. Of course, that was a stark contrast to the racial prejudice he had been subjected to in his lifetime. The stereotypes about his ability to drive, the assumption that he would be a scientist, and his assumed excellence in the sciences, were all unfair judgments. He had also heard his share of racial slurs as a child. He’d be walking alongside the benches in the playground, head down and hands dug deeply into his pockets, and he’d be walking all by his lonesome. It was a lonely childhood, some could suppose, but Tom was still a happy child. He tried to get involved with sports such as kick ball or basketball, but it always appeared that he was the odd man out. He would be virtually ignored on the court, never getting a chance to touch the ball. It was a disparaging situation, and Tom found himself thinking to himself, using his imagination to keep him busy. Of course, he couldn’t keep to himself for very long. Some of the other kids began to notice the solitary lifestyle that Tom had begun to live during recess hours, and decided that it would be a better time spent efforting to break Tom down. It would all begin by a small of small boys that had been expelled from the basketball courts by upper division kids who wanted the courts by themselves. The boys, now bored and with nothing to do, noticed the strolling young boy and made contact. Tom wasn’t much for confrontations, and would only give the boys one worded answers and kept his head down. Tom didn’t know when the name calling began. He was one of the only kids at the school of an asian descent, so he was automatically singled out. Tom wasn’t sure what the names meant or how they were supposed to be derogatory towards him, but he was aware of enough to realize that they were not compliments. He took the insults tongue in cheek and went on his way, never letting his emotions get a hold of his judgment. He would eventually make some very good friends in school, taking those friendships all the way through grade school to high school. While that was a great turning point in Tom’s confidence, what continued to stick with him was the prejudice that he had incurred as a young child and which he incurred as a young adult. Tom licked his bottom lip, moistening it since it felt a bit dry. “Actually it is. I learned English before I learned Vietnamese.” Fred’s eyes widened a bit, and he nodded slowly at Tom’s answers. “Really? That’s interesting. Most foreign parents teach their children their native language first. I guess it’s just pride.” “I would suppose that it is culture.” “If you’re an American, you know English,” Fred rebutted, now beginning to take a stand on the issue. “It’s that simple.” Tom watched the man carefully, noticing that he was defending a view that seemed quite popular in the United States at the moment. However, he had his own take on the so called values of the U.S., and was not afraid to share them. “English is a language,” Tom replied, placing his hands in his pockets. “It’s not representative of the entire country or it’s culture.” Fred stared back at Tom, seemingly analyzing his entire face, his posture, and his stance. Tom felt like a hot light was being held to his face and wanted to flinch away from the glaring light, but refused to shift nervously. Any quick movements or hints towards being uncomfortable would only make him appear weak to the man. Conversation and debate was not very far off from physical combat. It takes precision and accuracy, as well as strategy. “You don’t stand like them,” Fred finally commented. “You just don’t have their secretive nature…” “Would you mind elaborating on that a bit?” Tom asked, a bit of annoyance being hinted to in his voice. “Who exactly is ‘them?’ ” Fred shook his head, laughing a bit to himself. Tom stood there, hands still in his pockets, although they were balled into fists. Tom was beginning to be angered by Fred’s obvious disregard for discretion and tact at the discussion of racial matters. “Don’t tell me you’re going to get all sensitive and up in arms about race,” Fred replied, with a sly grin on his face. “I have experience with this topic. I know what I’m talking about.” “So that justifies you being a racist?” “Now let’s get something straight here,” Fred refuted. “I am a lot of things. I’m homeless, I have no money, I have no family, and I’m probably a jerk…but I am not a racist.” “Well what do you expect to me say?” Tom said, beginning to get upset. “You told me yourself that I don’t ‘stand like them.’ What was that supposed to mean? I’m an American with a Vietnamese and Irish heritage.” “Irish?” Tom’s hands exited his pockets and were now crossed across his chest. “That’s right, irish. My father was irish.” “And your mother was a beautiful young woman…who happened to be Vietnamese.” “Don’t patronize me old man…” Fred chuckled to himself. He sighed slowly and looked up at Tom with seemingly innocent eyes. “It’s people like you that make this world go round and round, ya know? You fight for injustice and prejudice…you hope for a better world. You understand who you are, yet you don’t know who you are. I’ll tell you something right now – I’m glad you know what your heritage is, because most screw balls on this fine planet don’t. It’s a privilege to be able to trace your roots back to your ancestors – to be able to see what they accomplished and the sacrifices they had to make. I said what I said because I’m ignorant I suppose, but I had spent so much time around them, I guess I feel like I am a part of them as well.” Tom’s mind began to swirl as Fred’s words began to sink in.
