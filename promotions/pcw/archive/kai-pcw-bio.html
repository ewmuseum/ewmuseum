Kai Biography Real Name Kai Xao Kua Nicknames KXK, The Crunk Monk Hometown Jinan, Shandong Province, China Birthday 18th January 1980 Height 5'9' Weight 193 lbs Biography Introduction into Wushu Among China's well-kept secrets, one caught the imagination of many people - Chinese wushu. Wushu is an important component of the cultural heritage of China, with a rich content that has remained untarnished over the centuries. Literally translated, 'wu' is military, 'shu' is art. Wushu therefore means the art of fighting, or martial arts. Previously, wushu figured significantly in the simple matter of survival through China's many wars and political upheaval. Today, wushu has been organized and systematized into a formal branch of study in the performance arts by the Chinese. It reigns as the most popular national sport in the country of 1.1 billion people, practiced by the young and old alike. Its emphasis has shifted from combat to performance, and it is practiced for its method of achieving heath, self-defence skills, mental discipline, recreational pursuit and competition. To describe wushu, it is best to understand the philosophy of its teaching. Every movement must exhibit sensible combat application and aestheticism. The wealth of wushu's content, the beauty of wushu movements, the difficulty factor, and the scientific training methods are the song of the elements that set wushu apart from martial arts. Routines are performed solo, paired or in groups, either barehanded or armed with traditional Chinese weaponry. In short, wushu is the most exciting martial art to be seen, felt, and ultimately practiced. How is wushu related to kung fu and taijiquan? 'Wushu' is the correct term for all Chinese martial arts therefore kung fu and wushu were originally the same. During the last thirty years, wushu in Mainland China was modernized so that there could be a universal standard for training and competing. In essence, much emphasis has been placed on speed, difficulty, and presentation. Consequently, wushu has become an athletic and aesthetic performance and competitive sport, while 'kung fu' or traditional wushu remains the traditional fighting practice. Taijiquan is a major division of wushu, utilizing the body’s internal energy or 'chi' and following the simple principle of 'subduing the vigorous by the soft.' Kai Kua has followed this for as long as he could remember, never knowing his parents; he sought comfort in the pain of the Shaolin life, and where others could see pain Kai would see a challenge to overcome. The grand master of the monastery was and still is a very wise and knowledgeable man and from an early age, Kai wanted to be like him in every way possible. -::Birth::- Kai was born in Jinan, China on the 18th of January 1980 to unknown parents. He was given to the monastery when he was only two days old, as his mother, who had been raped, could not support or cherish the child. The grand master and his wife took the child and raised it as their own. -::Age 4-10::- From the age of four onwards Kai became heavily involved with the training of the Shaolin, under strict guidance of the grand master, his father figure would teach him all he knew about the way of taijiquan and its teachings. Kai was a superb pupil, he was often able to compete with children three times his size, using his speed and natural agility to his advantage. -::Age 10-15::- Perhaps the toughest times in Kai’s life; he was told that he was adopted by the monastery and that his mother did not want him as her child. This made Kai become a recluse, putting all his aggression and strength in to training. Furthermore, Kai locked him self in the rock chamber by the monastery, only making contact with the outside world to eat. Here he stayed from age 14-15, honing his skills in the darkness, and bringing himself to optimum fitness levels. -::Age 15-21::- Unknown illegal fighting syndicate challenges monastery, war between two for six years. Not much is accounted for in this time. -::Age 21::- Kai Kua finally addresses the question that has been eating him up ever since the revelation of it; he decides he wants to leave the monastery… the tests: Before the Qing dynasty in China, disciples of the Shaolin temple who wished to leave had to pass through five tests in order to be permitted departure. The first was to defeat the Lohan Tang. A Lohan is a kind of bodyguard and the Lohan Tang were the 18 best fighters from the 5 Shaolin Ch'an families. The departing disciple would have to face and defeat each of these 18 fighters. Upon defeating the Lohan Tang, the departee would then face the Da Mo Yuan. These were four fighting monks charged with protecting the abbot. After defeating the Da Mo Yuan, the departee would face the San Jing Gung. These were three fighting monks chosen to defend the library of manuscripts preserved at Shaolin. After defeating the San Jing Gung, the departee would face the Mu Yi Xiang. This was a hall of wooden dummies, which were set to react to hair triggers. If triggered, each dummy would strike with enough force to knock a man unconscious. If the departee could navigate to the end of the hallway of wooden dummies, his last trial was to carry a cauldron full of hot coals outside by pressing his forearms against the sides of the cauldron. The cauldron had a dragon on one side and a tiger on the other. These images would be branded onto the forearms of the Shaolin disciple, who then was free to go. Kai completed all these tasks in a record; the time taken was a strenuous one day three hours and thirteen minutes. His eagerness to leave could only be matched by his passion for China. He left the monastery with no regrets and set on his way to the port town near Jinan. Here he decided to stow away on a ship and see where it would take his life. The boat was a small fishing boat heading for America; he stowed away for the duration of the journey and survived on the scraps of food left over from the chef’s cooking. On his arrival to America, he jumped off the ship and scooted to the nearest place of refuge. Here, under a bridge, a black male was contesting an illegal fight, and the local champion had just defeated another chump. Kai was interested and could speak good English; he enquired how he could make money; the reply was a rough “We got a challenger here!” Kai was thrust into the middle of the circle to a sadist smile from his opponent. The bets were placed and BANG! Kai had won with one calculated punch to the nose. The black male, Marcus, saw Kai’s talent and offered to put him up at his house, only if he could take a large percentage of Kai’s winnings. Kai agreed and the two have been partners for a few months. Marcus, who was killed last week in a gunfight in a club, left all his belongings to Kai. After his early stint, Kai recognised a fellow taijiquan student, known as Matsushima. He was an aged Japanese male, who like Kai, was trained in the art of taijiquan in the monastery. Matsushima, a realist, would teach Kai that Buddhism had its limits, and that his way was the way forward. Kai would listen and began to be sucked into Matsushima’s life of crime. Moreover, Kai thought that he could change the way that Chinese people thought in America.
