All For The Community

3 March 2003
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 03-03-03 The apartment was recovering from the sunshine that had blinded the interior at the break of dawn. Shadows were subsiding as the clock that hung precariously on the wall told me that it was almost time to leave. I packed one final, old t-shirt that had a strange spiral design on the centre. I pulled the zip to meet the other and thus, closed the backpack. I rested the backpack around my broad shoulders as I flicked my long, blonde dreadlocks behind my head. I glanced over at Jose who was reading the daily newspaper and not paying any attention at all to me. “You sure you don’t want to come with me?” I asked him, hoping that he would change his mind at the last moment. “No, no, no. You’ll be fine by yourself, plus you know those places bring my down, son” he replied. His words were reluctantly making sense to me. I only half understood his reason for avoiding my destination and considered him somewhat selfish for not attending. “Well, I’ll have to make tracks. I’ll be back soon” I told him before exiting the pale coloured apartment. I continued my stride down the dusty and run-down hallway in the apartment block and made my way outside through a pair of old sliding doors that didn’t function correctly. ‘This is my palace’ I laughed into myself. It was hardly an attractive area to live in but my job made the sacrifices all worthwhile. My eyes spun upwards, showing the New Jersey sky. It was blue with very little cloud cover and the sun added an extra shine to the picturesque sky. The same could not have been said about my long walk. Along the roadsides lay a vast amount of litter and empty cardboard boxes. A while later, I noticed a lot of boarded-up windows and doors alongside derelict housing and factories. Rugged graffiti made for the only paint on the exterior of these buildings and I suddenly realised that I was vast approaching my destination. The light blue pupils of my eyes recognised the eyesore of this area and the low-class sticker that it was designated with. Crossing the bumpy, low-quality tarmac that created the road, I spotted a sign that read ‘East Atlantic City Shelter’. The outside of the building emitted a ray of desperation with ‘Help Needed!’ flyers occupying a lot of the old, white paint on the walls. My boots collided with the ground with every footstep, my heart racing at ten times its normal rate. I was nervous, I had never visited a homeless shelter and was worried about stepping in. However, I knew that good would come out of the trip. I pushed a cold, metal handle down and swung the door open. A previously unknown yet pungent smell immediately crawled its way up my nostrils as my face cringed. Huge parts of the red fabric, which lined the floorboards, was covered with dirt and the dark wooden seats were entirely occupied. Each person that was seated wore torn clothes, old shoes and boasted a scruffy appearance. A few people dressed in yellow plastic aprons, mixed soup with a ladle while others lined white, ceramic bowls along the length of the serving area. I was early. I flung my arm high into the air and waved my hand in their direction. The three helpers nodded their heads towards me as I remained standing. I took a look around the shelter – my heart went out to these homeless people. I was standing beside an area where four or five plastic chairs and an extremely shoddy television set were situated. Located three feet from this was the dining area. A few six seated table, each chair connected to one table, were dotted close by the makeshift wooden table that was connected to a cooking area. The homeless seemingly drooled over the pot of soup that was currently being cooked. One of the generous helpers finally approached while I viewed the scene. “Are you Mr. Salvador from the phone?” he asked politely, slightly struggling to pronounce my surname. “This is he. When do I start?” I asked him. “Well, after the food is served. By the way, I can’t thank you enough for doing this, Mr. Salvador.” I smiled, my cheeks slowly burning a shade of red. “Call me Tota and don’t worry about the help” I explained. After a strong handshake, his small, thin frame headed back to mixing the soup. I continued to watch him as he poured helpfuls of vegetable soup into each of the bowls. The steam slowly rised, momentarily hiding his short, wide face and elegant short black hair. My eyes shifted to the hungry homeless. Watching them be given not only a home but food made me thankful to the voluntary workers. My eyes began to well up with emotion but I fought the tears back and looked out of the window into the sky. Several minutes passed by and the smell of vegetable soup began to diffuse into nothing as I felt a tiny finger gently poke me in the back. I carefully turned around but could see nothing within my eye-level. I motioned my head further downwards and saw a small child, about ten years old, wearing a dirty and torn t-shirt, one black shoe and one open-toed sandal and old, faded jeans. His dark hair was long and scruffy and he was missing several teeth. “You’re that wrestler guy!” he repeated several times, jumping each time with excitement. I shone a smile at the young child before I heard a voice from further into the shelter. “Tota, over here!” the voice echoed. I looked past the child and saw the voluntary worker pointing over to a vacant chair. I gave him the thumbs up and explained to the child that I had to do something. He was slightly upset so I told him that I would talk to him in a short while. I made my through the mass of homeless people, of all ages and all walks of life, to sit down on the chair. I removed the backpack from my two shoulders, immediately releasing a lot of pressure from that area. I leaned my bag against the leg of the plastic chair and sat down. A queue of the thirty strong crowd lined up in front of me. The younger ones seemed to be nervous, excited, happy or a mixture of all three. I unzipped the bag beside me and removed the first t-shirt. It was completely plain but the first receiver of the gift was thankful nonetheless. I donated the items of clothing to all people in attendance and autographed the shirts for those that recognised me from television. Each one showed excellent manners as they thanked me for my generosity. The final person in the line was the small child that I had encountered previously. “Can you write your name on it?” he asked me. “Of course I can, kid. Do you watch PWC a lot?” I asked him, trying to find out how popular the indy federation actually is. “Well some of us kids watch it the Saturdays we can. We have to use that small, broken TV and it doesn’t always work” he explained to me, his poor grammar shining through his missing teeth. As the other volunteer workers concentrated on working with the other homeless people, I was left to deal with the enthusiastic youngster. I didn’t mind his company, in fact it was raising my spirits. I handed him the signed t-shirt with my name printed on it as he raised his arm into the air and released a loud “Yes!” I was glad to see the joy in his face but was quite caught off guard by what he said after that. “That’s 2 PWC t-shirts I got now! Yours and 2GVCs” he said, laughing at the same time through bliss. Obviously curious as to why he possessed a 2GVC t-shirt, my mind cycled through various answers, ending up focusing on the fact that 2GVC weren’t that bad and had visited the shelter before. “I didn’t know they did volunteer work here…” I said, trying to get the child to inform me of who had given him the t-shirts. The kid finally snapped out of his joyful mood and spun around to face me. His greasy black hair swung in the same direction moments later. “No, I got a 2GVC t-shirt from that guy who does all the talking on the TV” he said, confusing my further. For some reason, the slight mention of 2GVC made me fill up with fury inside. However, the fact that I was trying to keep everyone in the shelter at high spirits, I contained the anger and manifested it into kindness. “What guy that does all the speaking?” I was still trying to find out. “The one that doesn’t do all the moves but speaks about them. He was called Mark, I think” he added. I knew it! I knew it wouldn’t have been 2nd Generation Viet Cong. For once I had actually secretly accused them of doing a good deed but I knew it was too good to be true. It had turned out to be donated by PWC commentator, Mark Malloy. “I don’t like Jimmy or Chandler! I like Tota and Jim. Will you tell Jim I hope he’ll be okay? Oh, oh, oh, and will you beat Jimmy and Chandler this weekend…” he paused, and seeing no agreement from my expression added, “for me?”. What could I tell him? “I can try to beat them… for you. I will tell Jim you say hello and wish him well too,” I said to him, immediately making him ecstatic. The child was now smiling, a smile stretching from one ear to the other. I was proud that I had brought so much happiness to a misfortuned child. The child stretched his arm out, motioning for a handshake to thank me for the donation and the time I had spent with him. I slowly reached my hand out but halfway there, I withdrew it. The child was stunned that I had pulled out of the handshake but I reaffirmed his belief in me by wrapping my arms around his body in the form of a hug. His small, thin arms struggled to fit around my large chest as I ruffled his hair playfully. I released the hug and once again zipped up my backpack. I had to leave; I didn’t want to get so attached that it would upset me to see how they live. I gave the child a high-five before informing the volunteer workers of my departure. “Well thanks a lot. You certainly have had made a difference today” the talkative one said. “No, thank you. This is an unforgettable experience and I was glad to help the community” I said before once again shaking their hands and exiting. I turned the door handle and swung the door open before turning to wave goodbye to them all, especially the young child I had conversed with earlier. I left the place, a small tear slowly trickling down my cheek. I wiped it away and began to walk home. I had never felt so fulfilled in my entire life as I did on that day. I had mentally added something to the small child’s life. The walk home seemed like a blur, as my mind was so high on helping those homeless people that I was delighted with myself. ‘Just for that kid,’ I thought, ‘just for that kid, I’m going to try extra hard to overcome 2GVC this Saturday night.’ Not only had I metaphorically touched that child but he had also touched me on a mental level.
