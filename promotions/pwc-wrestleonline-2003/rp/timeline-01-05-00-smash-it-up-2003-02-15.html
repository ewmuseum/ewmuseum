Timeline - 01/05/00 : Smash It Up

15 February 2003
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 15/02/03 The bright green eyes of Karina Wolfenden, features that look like they should be mounted on a ring to make jewellery of amazing beauty, stare with rare disgust dead ahead. As if she has blinkers on, her gaze does not wander, despite all the chaos around her. Against the glistening backdrop of her eyes, the arches that symbolise McDonalds reflect, as if being projected from her optic nerve. All that can be seen of her admittedly plain face are the green orbs and the bridge of her small nose, a dust mask barring access to the lower half of her face, shielding it from both view and identification. Over her mane is pulled a brightly coloured ski-hat, just a tuft of hair protruding from under it where her mirrored sunglasses rest. ‘The pigs blocked off Trafalgar!’ an angry voice bellows from the wilderness, those five words alone still managing to exude every emotion of hate and disgust that exists. The news of the Metropolitan Police’s tactics to control the rioters spreads, their idea of control being many’s idea of wrongful imprisonment. Leaving the obscured face of Karina, it is shown she is one of the crowd, part of a body of protesters all there for the same reason. Like a tissue cell, each person amassed there having the same purpose and function. They detest the planet’s desire for wealth, and are sickened by it’s methods obtaining it. Before them, is the chain they have chosen to make an example of, the yellow arches nothing more than a target, a beacon. Every other day that year, their trademark symbol was used to attract customers, a logo known the world round but on May 1st, 2000 it attracted a very different clientele. It’s hard to notice Karina in the rioters, strange given the way she stands out on the Pro Wrestling Circuit, but the hat’s pattern and tuft of bright hair allow her to be found. But once seen again, she’s more recognisable for what she has in her right hand. Leaving the terracotta coloured dust in the stray fibres of her black glove, a broken brick rests in her palm. More people notice the rubble that weighs heavy on her wrist, and begin to egg her on. Not that she needs it. ‘Smash it up!’ they all chant, pumping their fists in their air. Slowly, she tosses the bit of brick around in her hand, letting the moment sink in, psyching herself up. In a group of thousands, one girl from New York has become the focus of attention. Would they still be cheering for her if they knew she flew over on First Class? However, in the May Day Riot, everyone there has a common bond, regardless of race, background and privileges. Looking out the very top of her eyes, the black border of her eyelids slowly infringing on her view, Karina draws her arm back. The weighting of the brick sends an extra jolt down her arm before it’s propelled from her glove to a raucous, resounding cheer. As if it is locked on to the arches, the brick does not waver from it’s course, the eyes of hundreds following it’s flight before it hits the glass. The first contact sends minute cracks through the window’s crystalline structure, cracks they extend out like roots of a plant as the body of the brick forces it’s way through the pane. ‘Wreck it!’ comes the rallying call from the mob, the crashing of the glass barely audible above their collective voice acting as a start gun for their race to ransack. The form of Karina Wolfenden, eyes still transfixed on the now flawed and cracked visage of the yellow arches, is the small island of calm in the raging sea of rioters, the currents sending them swarming into the establishment. Only the occasional knock from the onrushing rioter moves Karina, her feet rooted to the ground and the jolts just making her sway like a flower as the breeze hits it’s leave. She has no interest in joining in with the ransacking, those in the restaurant who’re hurling chairs around and wrecking machinery are not who she wants to surround herself with anymore. Looking at the brick dust on her glove, she pulls her mask down, showing her pink lips, smiling as the yellow arches no longer burn their reflection into her eyes. Then, amidst the crashing, smashing and fevered chanting, she disappears back into the crowd. Another cell in the tissue, and other face in the crowd, just another girl.
