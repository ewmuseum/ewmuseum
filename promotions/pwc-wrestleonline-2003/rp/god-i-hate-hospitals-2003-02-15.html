God I hate hospitals.

15 February 2003
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 02/15/03 “God I hate hospitals.” The thought keeps running through Jim Axtell’s head as he walks the halls of the Atlantic City Medical Center. The brightness of hospitals always seemed to intrigue Jim, considering the dark and gloomy atmosphere they inhibit. Jim wears a pair of faded blue jeans, tan work boots, and a tan Carhart coat to shield him from the Jersey winter. He carries something under his arm, a gift of some sort. Glancing down at a piece of paper in his hand, Axtell meanders his way down the corridor looking for a room. As he walks by several rooms with the doors open, he can’t help but peek inside. Injury, sadness, and death seem to ooze from each and every one he walks past. It sends a shiver down his spine, causing him to visibly shudder. He carries on down the hallway, arriving at the last door on the right. “Room 414,” he says to himself, double checking the piece of paper in his hand. “Guess this is it. I don’t know why they had to switch rooms again. This is getting ridiculous.” As he approaches the door, Jim glances at the chart hanging on the wall. It reads ‘Porky Jack Bunson’ at the top. The reality of everything slaps Jim in the face, halting him just short of the door. No matter how many times he comes to see his friend, the routine is still the same. He takes a deep breath, then knocks softly on the wooden door. “Come on in,” calls Porky from inside the room. His voice is empty, seemingly devoid of life. Jim pushes the door open, the hinges creaking like something off of some horror movie. As he steps into the room, Jim forces a smile on his face. “Porky! How are ya, man?” The excitement in Jim’s voice is feigned, and Porky knows it. Nevertheless, he plays along. “Damn fine, Jim! Damn fine!” Bunson extends his hand, which Jim takes and the two greet each other with a firm handshake. “You haven’t lost the grip in your hands, that’s for sure,” Jim says, buckling his knees and grimacing playfully. A smile crosses Porky’s lips, a genuine smile which has become a rarity for him lately. The 6’6”, 271 pound man from Chicken Lick, Kentucky, hasn’t had much to smile about these days. As Jim grabs a chair from the side of the room, Porky tries to push himself up in the bed with his arms. He struggles mightily, the strain showing on his face. “You need some help?” asks Jim as he rushes over to Porky’s bedside. Porky shakes his head emphatically. “Nosir, I reckon I’ll have ta be able ta do this on ma own from now on.” Jim watches somberly as Porky struggles and strains just to move his body a few inches. Finally Porky collapses back in the bed, a defeated look on his face. “That outta do ‘er fer now, Jimmy. Take a seat, take a seat,” Porky demands as he motions to the chair behind Jim. Jim takes a seat in the chair he brought over. He takes the gift he was carrying and hands it over to Porky. “Here’s a little something from some of the guys,” Jim says. Porky takes the gift from Axtell, looking at it tenderly. “Aw shucks, Jim, y’all didn’t hafta do that.” “Just open it,” orders Axtell. Porky smiles and rips the gift wrap off the present, revealing a brown cardboard box. Porky fumbles around with the package and finds the lid, taking it off the top. He looks inside the box and his eyes start to well up with tears. “Some of the guys signed that for you, a little get well wish.” Jim’s eyes fill with emotion as well, but he fights back any tears so he can try to keep the mood light. “They want to see you back in there, ramming your head against one of those.” Both men chuckle somewhat as Porky pulls the gift from inside the box. It’s a padded turnbuckle from the PWC ring, with get will wishes scribbled all over it. Porky takes the time to read all the notes written on the pad, a single tear running down his cheek. He quickly wipes it away, hoping to prevent Jim from seeing it. “We know you’ll be back someday, Porky. We’re all rooting for you,” Jim says encouragingly. Porky sets the turnbuckle pad back into the box, replacing the lid neatly. He then looks down at his legs. “I can’t move ‘em, Jim. The docs say I’ll never be able to move ‘em again. There’s a full break in tha spinal cord.” Porky hangs his head sadly. Jim rises from his chair and moves in close to his friend. “Who cares what those doctors say? They’re wrong all the time, that’s why they have malpractice insurance.” Jim’s attempt at a joke falls flat. “Look, you just keep praying and working those legs out. Hope is your best friend right now, don’t lose it.” Porky looks up at Jim, anger in his eyes. “I want ta make that monster pay fer what he done, Jim! How can I do that from a wheelchair?” “Don’t you worry about him, Porky. Don’t worry about any of them right now. Just concentrate on you. Let me take care of them,” Jim says with conviction. “I promise that I’ll make things right.” “It’s not fer you ta do, Jimmy,” says Porky. “I should have been out there to stop him, but I wasn’t. You had my back when people were beating me down, and I wasn’t there for you.” Jim looks right into Porky’s eyes. “I’m sorry.” Porky shakes his head and pats Jim on the shoulder. “It ain’t yer fault, Jim. You was busy in back with them two Chow Mein’s. I’m a man, I can say that I just got ma clocked cleaned.” There is an uneasy pause for a few moments, then Jim prepares to leave. “I can’t stay too long today, Porky. I have to get home to Kami. She’s still not doing too well with the move and all.” Jim shakes Porky’s hand once again. “I’m sure everthin’ll work out fer ya, Jim. Just take care of that perty lady an everthin’ll be just fine.” Porky smiles his first real smile in a long time. Jim waves good-bye and takes his leave of the room. Porky opens the box on his lap once again and pulls out the turnbuckle pad from inside. He just stares at it as we fade out.
