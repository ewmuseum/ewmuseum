(with the character), creative roleplaying ("RPs", "flashes") helps bring your character wins. We used to have a mega "RP Guide" to close every single loophole some of our more disruptive members would squirrel out like porch rats to get away with.

The Happy Clause - I can make some of the people happy all of the time, but I cannot make everyone happy all of the time. Keep that in mind at all times, please. c. The Spunky Clause - From here on out, we're shooting for realism. If I don't think something you write would get aired on national TV, then don't send it to me. d. I don't care how many titles you have won outside of AWMC, or how many people you have defeated outside of the federation -- if you're coming into AWMC, don't expect an
insta-push. e. Character Assassination - The basic premise of this rule is that a person is not allowed to attack another character in a way that the other character has potential to be 'ruined', without getting the permission of that character's handler first. This includes any and all attacks that would leave a character injured, overly humiliated, or permanently scarred.

The Diggs Clause - AWMC administration reserves the right to refuse flashes of inappropriate material (i.e. racism, personal shots, various retardedness) as well as the right to refuse to count the same inappropriate material towards a match. If it's refused, that's the end of it. Anyone who tries to talk me into running an inappropriate roleplay will be canned REAL
quick-like. This is just a game. It's not life. If you lost, you lost because I thought you should've. If you wonder if you were out-rp'ed, you were. If you think you need some more heat, you do. If you take it out on me, don't expect to get any reaction, except reference to this rule. If you're seriously ticked, then you're seriously fired. If you've ever been a fedhead, you know that it can be a hard job, and the less aggravation I have to suffer, the better.
g. The Brain Clause - Just because you write a strat that covers all the grandest moves, every counter imaginable, provisions for interference and the rest, don't have a fit if it isn't used or the match goes contrary to your plans for world domination. The whole concept of pro wrestling is that the wrestlers vouluntarily submit themselves to being hit with the moves that are tried against them. We all know that any wrestler could realistically get out of any hold, or avoid any move if he really wanted to. But he CHOOSES not to, because that's the way the show is done. Just because you have a counter or protection doesn't automatically mean it'll be used. Sometimes an angle is better furthered (while accomplishing mutual goals) by a different outcome.
If you can't sell something that turns out contrary to your master plan and decide to take it out in roleplays, you'll have plenty of practice applying to other feds, guaranteed.

Are sneak attacks allowed? Attacks and interference are allowed in moderation and administration has the right to refuse a sneak attack. Try to keep it somewhat realistic in the context of wrestling. 'sell' attacks and incorporate them into flashes, strats, etc. However, if you do have problems with the way your character was portrayed in an attack

New recruits are issued 90-day contracts with the federation. During that period, a new signee is evaluated for her/his compatibility with the fed and the new signee can get a feel for the organization. During the 90-day contract period, new signees are obligated to stay with AWMC and will complete their duties as assigned. New signees can work with handlers, develop angles and make themselves known in the fed. At the end of the 90-day contract, the
AWMC or recruit has the option to terminate the contract or make an "extended" contractual agreement, which is basically says the handler can stay as long as s/he wants and either party can drop from the AWMC at any time for any reason.
6. How many wrestlers may I handle? Members are permitted one wrestler or one tag team. Tag team members may wrestle singles bouts but are ineligible to earn singles rankings.

SOME BASICS Some basics (adapted from the rec.sport.pro-wrestling (RSPW) newsgroup FAQ): 1. A face, short for babyface, is the "good guy". He acts in the best interests of the fans, will save his friends (and innocents occasionally) from being attacked, and will not attempt to purposely hurt another wrestler. 2. A heel is the "bad guy". He acts in his own interests, he will insult the fans, turn on his friends, and will often maliciously attempt to injure other wrestlers. 3. A tweener is a term invented in 1996 to describe Diesel's final run in the WWF, as he was acting like a heel while getting a face reaction from the fans. Generally "tweeners" are actually heels. 4. A wrestler will "turn" from one to another. A face will do a "heel turn" and a heel will do a "face turn". 5. A "pop" is a loud and instantaneous reaction from the fans for anything. This can include a ring entrance, big move, pinfall, interview segment, chairshot or whatever.
6. A "push" could be described as winning a lot, but today it's more like winning + airtime + quality of wins + quality of opponent + other intangibles = push. 7. "AWMC administration" and "bookers" interchangeably refers to the AWMC President and Vice President(s), who determine match outcomes and fed direction. II.) MATCHES A.) HOW ARE MATCH RESULTS DECIDED? AWMC is a booked fed. Here's how we will decide each match, for the most part. 40% Roleplaying Two flashes and one in-ring is the limit on roleplaying, though one great roleplay can sometimes beat out two average ones. Numerous RPs sent in for one match will be frowned upon. Several RPs, whether weak or strong in quality, inconvenience bookers and break the rules. Focus instead on producing the best roleplays you can within the limit. Descriptive, consistent (with the character), creative roleplaying ("RPs", "flashes") helps bring your character wins. AWMC has some guidelines for RPing here. Spelling and grammar count here as well.
30% Angles What is an angle? An angle can be best summed up as "Why or how is this wrestler fighting that wrestler?" An angle is basically wrinkle in a storyline, having to do with the character or having to do with build up for the match at hand. Contrary to popular belief, an angle is NOT a feud.
The RSPW FAQ differentiates between gimmick (who is wrestling), feud (who they're fighting) and an angle (why/how they're fighting). Examples are: changes of your wrestlers allegiance or personality; change of gimmick; or something your wrestler needs to fight back from or overcome. How you make it unfold is what makes it interesting.
Pro wrestling's outcomes are often heavily determined by angles and wrestlers will often lose to benefit a given angle. On occasion, it's within the realm of possibility that your match may hinge on your opponent's stronger storyline winning out. This is part of the game.
You can consult with your opponent or the booker on an angle, but that isn't always necessary. Put yourself in your wrestler or team's place and analyze the situation. Be creative and have a reason for your angle. Good angles and feuds create an interest for your wrestler among handlers and bookers, and helps you get a push. And even if you lose at the conclusion of your angle, you will move up in the rankings.
15% Strats Strats are match strategies that help determine how your wrestler will approach an opponent in particular and a match as a whole. AWMC has some guidelines for strats here.
10% Heat What is heat? The RSPW FAQ defines heat as: "If a wrestler does his job correctly, he will draw a strong reaction from the fans, which is heat.
If both wrestlers are on their game, the fans will sustain the 'heat' throughout the entire match. Heat also refers to the reaction that a certain wrestler draws from the fans outside of the ring. A wrestler who the fans particularly hate is said to be drawing 'heel heat' and will need to be beaten by a babyface to 'take the heat off him.' It is best for a wrestler to draw heat the same as their orientation (i.e., a heel should draw heel heat). If a heel is drawing face heat, then he will usually be made into a babyface to sustain that heat."
In the context of e-wrestling, heat is earned by (among other things) the number of other characters who discuss your character, the amount of interest your work garners among other members and bookers, and the effectiveness of your character in drawing attention and defining the character. You don't need to be a heel to get heat, but you need to be consistent with your character.
Faces don't pull heel stunts (i.e. attacks unless someone's being attacked, etc.) and heels don't do what faces do (i.e. cater to fans, etc.).
Anyone who brings out the qualities of the character effectively can draw heat. Of course, there is also such a thing as 'bad heat.' The RSPW FAQ defines it as:
"unintended negative heat. When either a face or a heel draws bad heat, it is generally not because of any actions done by the character, but simply because the fans can no longer tolerate the person." Violating guidelines, being unprofessional as a member or generally offensive can draw bad heat. You can also draw bad heat by making moves inconsistent with the character you have developed (heels doing face stuff, faces doing heel stuff), unless you're turning the character.
5% Effort Basically, the effort you put into the fed. Both roleplays and strats are required in AWMC so it is important to send in both for your match. Failure to send in one or the other (or both) will be an influential factor in the outcome of your match.

SNEAK ATTACKS/INTERFERENCE Attacks and interference are allowed in moderation and administration has the right to refuse a sneak attack. Try to keep it somewhat realistic in the context of wrestling. Another word on attacks -- many people look on being attacked as a bad thing. In fact, attacks can help extend a feud and give the contestants more to talk about, plan retaliation for, etc. Use an attack to your advantage.
D.) WINNING AND LOSING Everyone wins. Everyone loses. The most established franchise wrestler can lose to even the greenest newcomer, and the hot, anticipated newcomer can lose to even an average wrestler. New wrestlers in real and fantasy wrestling
'pay dues' and can lose a few matches, and major stars can slump at times. However, anyone can recover from even the most devastating defeat to rise above it all and become a winner. How you handle it is up to you.
How do you handle losing? There are a few things you should NOT do. Sending angry, expletive-laden letters to the President is your ticket out of the fed. Attacking writers will earn the same result. Similar letters to the
President about how you can never recover from a loss and how it's killed your character and the heat for that character is also pointless; the loss happened already. Gossiping on IRC or elsewhere about being 'screwed', 'cheated', etc.
will receive sanction; talk to the President before damaging fed morale by running it down. Whining in your flashes about being 'jobbed' is unacceptable.
Refusing to 'sell' (making moves and matches believable) a loss is also unacceptable, though you can usually get away with 'you got lucky that time'
sorts of comments. Approach losing by being somewhat objective. You won't lose for no good reason. If you're still at a loss, e-mail the President and ask about the match, your work and why you lost. No need to take a smug tone or nasty attitude -- the only person getting hurt by it is you. If you're going to ask, however, don't be afraid, upset and nasty if you get an honest answer.
Sometimes a match is decided very closely. Sometimes there are angles going on. And sometimes a decision just has to be made and someone has to lose.

REPLACEMENTS If two people are booked for a match and one or both don't roleplay, one competitor may be pulled and another wrestler who has been active but may be underexposed or looking for an 'in' to things may be used. The new person may or may not win, but they do get the exposure and push their efforts earn them.
This is one way for AWMC to reward the people who RP regularly, 'encourage' people who think they're in for an easy win to work, as well as one way to add a little unexpectedness and spontaneity to the cards. If your wrestler will be used to replace someone, you will be emailed first to develop a hook to getting you in the match.
III.) ROLEPLAYING: A.) WHAT AND WHEN Roleplaying is done via Cheap Shots, a Tuesday and Saturday flash packet show, or an in-ring flash that the handler is responsible for writing. Your roleplay should consist of some form of playlet - describe the scene, your character, give his/her dialog. An in-ring flash generally consists of your wrestler doing one of: (a) coming to the ring or (b) being backstage at the match card event or (c) having some form of TV hook-up to the card, to state their case to the fans with the intention of building heat, making a challenge known, or to respond to another wrestler.
Roleplays should be relevant and interesting, they don't have to consist of 'I am the greatest, I'm gonna beat you up' dialog and should not be repetitive or harass anyone. They can show your character to be sensitive, emotional or even weak and cowardly, they don't have to be "tough". If they are good
(interesting, entertaining) they count in your favor. B.) THE GUIDELINES There are some basic guidelines for RPs, and, in particular, doing a good one. The AEWF puts it best in "The 6 Major No-no's When Writing Flashes": 1. Spamming/Being pointless -- "Huge interviews/flashes that just goes on and on. In many things size matters. Bigger is often better but that isn't the case in e-wrestling. Writing long interviews/flashes is not a guarantee that you will win. If you write a huge rant with only three or four lines that makes any sense and your opponent writes a short compact interview without dead moments and the same amount of meaningful lines he will probably win.
So remember size has nothing to do with success in e-wrestling. Quality does." 2. Because Austin 3:16 said so! -- "If you're not in a fed with real wrestlers, never ever quote a real wrestler. In a small fed with inexperienced handlers you might get away. But if there are veteran handlers in the fed they will never get off your case. Expect to be called a wannabe, a copycat and chased and flamed until you either stop doing it, lose so many matches you get tired of it or hightail out of the fed. It isn't excellent to talk about being the Excellence of Execution if you're not Bret Hart. It's a sign of you being unimaginative and a poor roleplayer. Using a "real" wrestler's catchphrase won't help you, it won't develop you as a roleplayer and it won't win you a title in any federation. So don't! Stone Cold didn't say so."
3. Excessive use of four-letter words -- "No I'm not talking about words like: glad, like, have etc. I'm talking about swearing. Immature teenagers think it's cool to use four-letter words. The rest of the e-wrestling world doesn't.
Writing interviews/flashes with every second word being 'fuck' won't make you sound older. It will only make your wrestler/wrestlers sound like kids and if it does then you don't need it do you? A good rule that applies on many areas is less is more. If you have your wrestler come out time after time in flashes/interviews only consisting of obscenities the wanted tough/angry/bad effect wears of very quick. People will read it and think, 'OK he knows a lot of four-letter words, so what?' Your character becomes tiresome and uninteresting. However if you wrestler speaks 'normally' most of the time in a flash/interview when he is angry starts swearing. A thing he usually doesn't do then you will get power behind the swearing. Creative, original, catchy, eloquently put and thought-through interviews/flashes will always prevail against obscenity flashes."
4. 'Smart' Comments -- "Wrestlers work very hard on creating the illusion that wrestling is real. I have never ever seen a wrestler use the term 'jobber' in a interview and there's a reason for it. If they did, they would destroy the illusion of realism that they are trying to create. However in the world of e-wrestling, a roleplaying world trying to simulate the world of professional wrestling, there are tons of wrestlers accusing others of being jobbers.
That simply looks stupid. If you want to say that another wrestler is a talentless bum, don't use the word jobber. Never talk about things your wrestler isn't supposed to know about. In wrestling they call that breaking kayfabe.
Roleplay your wrestler like he was a real wrestler and don't say things real wrestlers wouldn't." 5. Don't be unrealistic -- "Don't have your wrestler benchpress 2,000 pounds. Don't have him fly. You get the picture. Those things will only make your wrestler look pathetic and suggest you have a limited imagination. It makes your wrestler look like a comic book character instead of the wrestler he is supposed to be. So try to be realistic. Before you send out a interview/flash look at it and see if you think it's believable."
6. Don't use other handlers' wrestlers -- "One of the best way of infuriating other handlers and fedheads for that matter is if you include other handlers' wrestlers in your flashes/interviews without the handler's permission. Sure you might think it would be cool to write a flash in which your wrestler beats the crap out of another wrestler but it isn't. Using another handler's wrestler is both disrespectful to the handler and the federation. If you want to set up an angle in which your wrestler attacks another wrestler in a parking lot or somewhere else not included on a card, contact the handler and set it up. Feuds are very good for wrestlers to be in as they often makes the wrestlers more showcased in the federation."
Then there are some AWMC ground rules we've developed over the life of the fed. 1. Not only is common sense advised, but respect for unknown factors (who you're playing with and their sensibilities) are a must for AWMC to continue to be as successful as it has been. A fed is only what their members make it, and the fed as a whole reflects what type of people their members are.
2. Racist references -- racial slurs, attacking certain racial/ethnic groups, etc. -- are a no-no. Period. This includes implying or inferring stereotypes of different ethnic groups or making stereotypes the butt of jokes or whatever. Examples: stereotypical Black characters, Mexicans with huge families or all illegal immigrants, referring to characters by race, etc.
This is common sense. 3. Gay references are not acceptable in any form. This includes insinuating opponents are homosexual, that given rivals engage in gay sex acts, or use of various gay epithets. Also, using gay people in your flashes to make some point about your character's good- or badness is unacceptable. You don't see it in pro wrestling, and won't do it in RPs. Again, common sense rule.
4. Out-of-character comments or in-character comments that can be perceived as being directed in an out-of-character context are not acceptable under any circumstances. If you have a problem with someone, talk to him or her or drop it. If you have booking problems or personal conflicts outside of wrestling with some people, do not bring it in flashes.
5. Sexual references can be alluded to tastefully if you really need them (i.e. a wrestler is a ladies' man, it's fine to have him in the company of women, discussing what a stud he may be, etc.) but should be treaded upon lightly. Sex scenes -- intercourse, etc. -- may not be used. Putting outright inappropriate stuff in flashes, including rape, child molestation, bestiality and sodomy, among others, results in immediate dismissal, no exceptions.
6. If it sounds too stupid or tasteless for words, you're probably going down for using it. This includes, but is not limited to, urinating or defecating on people; using anything even remotely related to Nazism; attempts to be clever in regard to guidelines or commenting/alluding to guidelines in RPs; and a mix of other stuff you can guess.
7. Spelling and format as well as staying in character count quite a bit. The management has far too much to do each week without editing misspellings and unintelligible sentences.

IV.) STRATS A.) WHAT IT IS A strat should describe your wrestler's behavior/attitude/intentions for a match. It should contain information on how your wrestler will be dressed, any special actions at the start of the match, e.g. will they push or shove their opponent during the instructions, attack before the bell etc. It should contain a list of the moves that they'll try and use during the match, any special combination of moves they'll use to try to get a win. Any special areas of attack they'll try, e.g. they attack their opponents knee because they know it was injured in an earlier match. Aspects of their own or opponents character/style that they'll try and exploit (e.g. they're bigger than their opponent so they'll concentrate on power moves, or they're smaller but faster so they'll concentrate on high flying moves). You may even consider a series of moves in response to what you know about your opponent and their ring style for example.
Also, a nice touch and the sign of a good worker/seller is to describe some possible counters to your own moves, that your opponent may or may not have thought of. This helps make for a great match and it helps the writers write the cards. This type of attitude also shows me that you are not doing this only out of ego, but that you care about the show.
B.) THE GUIDELINES Some ground rules 1. Strats should be no larger than 12K in size. If you write beyond 12K, don't expect everything to be used. 2. Writers are instructed to use as much of a strat as they can. However, two or more strats sometimes come in for a single match, so it's a balancing act. Don't be upset if some stuff gets put aside or countered. 3. Do put some insight in how your character will act in the ring and what they'll do to get the opponent. If there has been a bitter series of flashes between characters, maybe your character will attack before the bell and brawl, or whatever. Giving writers insight on your character's mindset helps in writing.
4. If particular parts of the strat are 'must use', label them. Don't assume the writer will guess them. In line with that, if by chance the 'must use' stuff is not used, you can guess there was a reason. Ask and don't get angry first. 5. Do NOT put together strats where you try to counter every single move by your opponents. Some move counters and direction is great, but 20 counters to 20 moves are a waste of time -- you can't have a fun match where every move is countered. 6. Keep consistent with your character. If he's a brawler, he won't be doing lots of aerial moves. If he's a technical guy, he's not going to break into a street fight immediately. Developing your strat around your wrestler's abilities and shortcomings goes a long way in your favor and toward creating a realistic match.
7. No reused/recycled/reworded strats are allowed or counted. 8. Do NOT come up with a bunch of scenarios in case you're attacked post-match. One or even two things are appropriate. Some suggestions if you're clueless about strats 1. Research your opponent on the AWMC page (How does your wrestler match up size- and strengthwise? How much does he weigh compared to your wrestler? What kind of a wrestler is he, and how does that mesh with your wrestler's style? What are his signature moves compared to yours? How can you compensate for those differences?) or read old matches and RPs so your wrestler's RPs come off informed and sharp. How will you start the match?
2. Do you have a backup plan in case your opponent has started against your own moves? If your original plans are fouled up, a backup plan assures you are prepared. 3. When will your wrestler go for his finisher and what will you do to try and counter or avoid your opponent's finisher? 4. How will the crowd react throughout this match? Will they chant the taunt or name you called your opponent this week? Will they wave your signs if your character is a face? Will your character taunt them if he's a heel? Will they bring your character weapons as you requested in an in-ring? The fans in the arena make it entertaining too, so feel free to use them.
5. How will the broadcast team sell your attitude, moves offense/defense in this match? 6. How will you be prepared for attacks by outside rivals? 7. One VERY good way to assure particular spots/attacks get in is by writing them you. You can write it in play-by-play style (or in a summary format for dark matches), using the card's announcers to sell your moves. When writing announcer comments, always use the announcer's initials (followed by a colon), try to keep them in character, and -- for god's sakes -- don't make your opponent look completely inept or we'll get complaints. :) Write as many as you want and there's an excellent chance they might all get used.
Note there is no promise that any or all of the strat will be used, although we will do our best to use as much as is possible in the circumstances.
Please don't get frustrated if it is the case that your angle/roleplay/strat/or challenge doesn't seem to get used. For a variety of reasons each is constrained by the 'big picture' and what others want as well.
