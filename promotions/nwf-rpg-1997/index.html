A BBS-native modem wrestling role-playing game with a self-published, dated, four-era operational history running from March 1994 to a 23 September 1997 relaunch, whose entire surviving record is its own rules -- no roster, no champion and no card ever reached Usenet. Written by AI, 2026

Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

The Network Wrestling Federation was “re-established” — its own history post uses that word, implying a still-earlier version nothing else survives to confirm — in March 1994, run through a California BBS under the on-air handle Stevie Cheesecake as a modem-based wrestling role-playing game. Its own history calls it “the first of its kind,” a roster of about twenty-five wrestlers building to a first pay-per-view, The Wheel of Torture, that July.

The game changed hands three times over the next three years, each transition recorded plainly in its own later history post: its founder had to take the board down entirely before the year was out, handing the game to an operator known as the Expert, who ran it until a time-driven hiatus that November; the Expert passed it on in February 1995 to a third operator, Raging Bull, who kept it going with help until August 1996, then shut it down again; a brief February 1997 revival by Raging Bull alone folded after a single card for lack of time. That August, Raging Bull and the Expert relaunched it together as co-promoters, resuming competition on 23 September 1997 across BBS nodes reaching as far as Montreal.

Its own house rules barred real-wrestler impressions outright — “copycats of real wrestler will not be allowed due to possible copyright problems,” though a borrowed nickname or finisher was permitted — and ran two weekly TV tapings (Monday in Manhattan from a New York nightclub, the Weekend Warriors from touring venues) plus a named annual calendar of seven pay-per-views running Waikiki Wipeout in January through Christmas Carnage in December. Results were “determined randomly with dice or a computer by the promoters,” the rules stated outright, adding: “you will have to take our word that we are being honest.”

Four championships anchored the roster, including an International title explicitly modeled, by the promotion's own admission, on Ric Flair's old “real world champion” ploy against a rival promotion's own world title — a deliberate feud device rather than a genuine second lineage.

No roster, champion or card of the NWF's own ever reached the newsgroup itself; its entire surviving record is its own rules, history and show architecture, and no closure notice of any kind survives beyond them.
