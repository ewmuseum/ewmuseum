Portrait of a Teenage Society : Family

26 November 2002
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: November 26th, 2002 My family and I hardly ever talk, baring the freak holiday conversation that doesn't end in a tantrum by my father and a peptic ulcer from my mother. Those relaxation tapes I bought them last christmas are still in their original shrink wrap. I can pretty much tell you in advance with a two percent error how my family will interact thanksgiving eve. That night I'll take a bus to get back to my home from center city all because my parents don't feel the desire to just drive the couple of miles and pick me up. Feel the love people. I'll get home and they'll be waiting at the bus stop, arms outstretched. At least my mother will, my father's never been a hugging kind of guy. I take after him in that respect. I depart the bus and place my feet on solid ground, cracking a smile that's been painted by numbers by a six year old with spasms. There's an awkward moment of tension before my mother hugs me, and my father just nods his head. His arms are crossed, and to uncross them to shake my hand would be sacrilegious. And he's not an anti-christ kind of guy. We don't have a lot of eye contact, myself and my father. It's mostly my mother hogging me like how a six year old in play school steals the Flintstones' phone and doesn't let anyone else use it. Not like I'm being used, but that's another story for another time. We quickly catch up on the 'Days of our lives' until, before long, more moments of awkward silence. And what better way to fill awkward silence than my father's innate desire to show his angst toward my clothing and my hair style. 3... 2... 1... Breathe. My mother becomes the voice of reason, the referee in our war of words. She desperately tries to quell this argument so she can possibly save this perfect moment of me exiting the bus forever in some sort of locked box in her mind, or maybe a picture clear photograph. Too bad the lock has lost it's key. Too bad the photograph became blurry. My dad rants about 'How could you dress from a thrift shop,' or my personal favorite, 'How could you spend more on hair care than shirts.' It's not my fault I'm Mr. Bargain and won't buy anything unless it's on sale. I got that from my mother. I got my poorness from YOU dad, so it's not like I really have a choice. 3... 2... 1... Breathe. The eternal power struggle between father and son. The father, an ex-military general, never actually retired. He's still the same man, still the same power and dispenses it like an iron fist to those around him. Like me. A man who's defiance drives my father insane to the point of internal destruction. I'm surprised he's not the one with the ulcer. I used to tell him all the time that I wanted to become a professional wrestler and he just shot my dreams to shit. He'd tell me that it's pointless, it's degrading, and it's a spectacle to the family name. He'd tell me he'd rather be caught dead than seeing me fulfilling my dream. Yes, he'd rather die than see me, his son, happy. 'It's not a job son, it's just grown men faking their hurt in tights.' he'd say. 'Join the Army,' he'd say. 'It'll give you discipline, which you sorely lack, and it's real. It'll make you a better man.' And yet, I'm making more money now than he ever did in his army. I'm famous to a crowd of people that don't have to respect me because I'm in charge, and I'm doing it by my own rules. And as for becoming a better man? Didn't a wise person once say that to be able to love, you have to first love yourself? Hi Dad, I'm Noah Hawkins. You might see me every Monday night on the WB on a show called Pressure Point. And I'm not an actor, I'm a professional wrestler. 3-2-1 Dad. Breathe. And I used to steal your cigarettes when I was 15.
