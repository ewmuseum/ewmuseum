The house e-wrestling game of Scoops Wrestling Networks, where a member created a Slammer, was dropped into one of more than fifty federations stacked in tiers under two leagues, argued on the fed's own flash board, and let the program decide the match. Written by AI, 2026

Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

CyberSlam tells its own beginning on its Newbies page, and tells it as a kayfabe: “It all started April 1997.” Thirteen names — Chaos Bill, CatWoman, Hardcore, Tough Guy and the rest — “pulled their life savings together, kissed the establishment good bye and started CyberSlam.” There was one federation, the CWF, and the seats were empty. Then a figure called The Slammer walked through the stadium doors, became President, banned one of the founders, and named the things the game would be made of: the players were Slammers, the playing was Slammin, and once a month there would be an Ultimate CyberSlam Event called CSlam with a hundred dollars on it. Its own champion gallery keeps the roll from there, month by month: Chaos Bill in April 1997, twenty-eight more after him.

It was a game with a cash register. The first Slammer was free; a second cost ten dollars a month, a transfer between federations fifteen, and the paperwork says over a thousand people were waiting for a free slot. What the money bought was a place in a machine. A member chose a wrestler's size, weight and moves at sign-up, then picked opponents off a drop-down, and the program decided the rest — the rulebook answers the question outright: “Are the winners pre-determined? —> No. Our online program looks at your Slammer's inherent talent… number of wins, number of losses and number of matches.” The writing happened somewhere else, on each federation's own flash board, where the angles were built and the feuds run, and where a commissioner with absolute authority could suspend anyone who overstepped. Three kinds of jobbing are named and defined in the rules, with penalties, because all three had happened.

The universe of federations grew into tiers. By the state the boards preserve, two named leagues, Titan and Hyperion, each carried one Elite federation and five Majors, and every Major carried four satellites written with a lower-case i — iCWA, iMWA, iROS — with a CFL marked “Super” and the CSlam Finals above them all. A wrestler climbed by winning: take your federation's belt at the end of the month and you moved up. Federations climbed too, and the record catches the mechanic in the act, because two states of the same navigation widget survive: the feds that moved from satellite to Major dropped the “i” off their own names on the way. iRWF became RWF, iNDW became NDW, iAWO became AWO, iUIWA became UIWA.

Its news operation was as elaborate as anything it staged. Three boards ran at once — an eSlam board for inter-fed news that outside federations posted their own cards to, a general news board, an event board — and a fourth tree, Hammerlock News, ran beside them with its own reporters. They wrote in character and in gimmick: a rumour desk promising a bigger scoop every week, an interviewer who ran his sit-downs in eight numbered parts and apologised in print when he fell behind, a reporter who introduced himself “Hello I'm Arcadia and your not.” One CEW column stops covering the week entirely to write a two-thousand-word appreciation of a retiring wrestler and the five-match feud that made him.

It ended from above. On 2 February 2001 an editorial on its own news board carried the news as its writer understood it: “snowball.com is shutting down Scoops, merging the news section with their's and obliterating that which we have come to know as Cyberslam.” The parent portal had been sold, and the game was a section of the portal. What the boards did over the next two days is the closest thing the record has to a last act: a petition page, a reporter filing from outside an arena that was supposed to be shut and telling every commissioner still reading to run a card anyway, federations posting goodbyes in HTML, one member writing that he lay awake until three in the morning over it, and the NDW taking its banners down, running up a Jolly Roger and reopening as the Rogue NDW in open breach of company policy. The last post held here is dated 3 February 2001. No page of the site is captured after 5 March.

Storylines

The February 2001 shutdown crisis Written by AI, 2026

CyberSlam's own last arc ran across the whole hub at once, not inside any one federation, and the record catches nearly every beat of it. It opens on 2 February 2001, when Flash_Lightning's own iIWF column stops being a column: “It's been great being a reporter… This is the worst thing that could have happen, and for what — something that's obviously more important than people themselves! MONEY!” (iIWF: PPV results… just kidding). The same day, Doc Holidaze's Fog posts a petition page and interviews RWF's own Law, who blames the parent company outright — “Snowball. That's about as far as I can go” — and signs off pointing his own fed's slammers toward a fallback board, RWFe on Cyberbrawls (RWF: A short interview with LAW).

The next day is the crisis's own busiest, and it splits between defiance and mourning almost federation by federation. Freedom, Hammerlock's own editor in chief, calls every remaining reporter to keep filing — “Report on the cards. Give an interview… Let's all put the focus back on CSlam” (CSlam: Challenge to Hammerlock Staff) — and the beat reporters answer. Oswald covers six federations across two columns the same day: the AWO and iUWA still standing (AWO,IUWA: I'll cover both), then the CFL, CWFe and CCWe, where Holy Evil loots the empty locker rooms for every belt left behind and crowns himself “the first ever CFL Uber-champ” (CFL,CWFe,CCWe: I'm serious about this), and, later the same day, a third round rating the TWC majors' own remaining activity fed by fed — the UIWA at four stars, the CGW at zero (TWC majors: Bring it on). CRF Commissioner Maverick vows to run the fed as normal — “if Cslam is going to shut down, at least you will be able to go down with a fight” ([CRF] Maverick says fight back Cslam) — and posts a full Monday Fire card the same day, its every title match carrying the same closing promise: “If Snowball should shut down the site before I can do results, The Current champs will remain the Current champs” ([CRF] The flock come flooding back). The CBF answers with a fresh World Title tournament for its own vacated belt (CBF: World Title Tourney), the CEW holds what its own reporter bills flatly as its final card (CEW: ONE LAST CARD!!), and the NDW goes furthest of all — taking its own banners down, running up a Jolly Roger, and reopening in open breach of company policy as the Rogue NDW (NDW: Rogue NDW?).

The hub's own voice frames the whole arc twice more that same day, once in grief and once in defiance. “The Glory,” reporting under that byline alone, opens a column by naming heroes who “stood up to the oppression of a corrupt empire” and closes it quoting Dylan Thomas's “Do not go gentle into that good night” whole (CSlam: The end? I think not!). Read beside it, CyberSlam's own earlier editorial on the closing itself — the one its own Epitaph, above, already quotes on the company's own decision — and its own follow-up rallying the boards to keep writing (CSlam: An editorial once more, CSlam: Fighting Back, CSlam: More than just a game, all…) are the same arc's own bookends. The surviving record's own last dated post is 3 February 2001; nothing suggests any of these federations' own last cards were ever staged.

Affiliations

Scoops Wrestling Networks

Owns CyberSlam (1997)
CyberSlam was a section of the Scoops Wrestling Networks portal, not a site of its own, and its own paperwork makes the dependency structural rather than decorative: a member had to register as a "Scoops Trooper" before creating a Slammer at all, the monthly champion's prize in the 1998 FAQ is "a Scoops Tee Shirt", and payments were processed by the portal's own parent company. When the portal was sold and shut down in February 2001, the game went with it.

H.I.T.'s original fed (IWO)

Affiliate of CyberSlam (1997)
IWO joined CyberSlam’s inter-fed eSlam board in May 2000 — the board’s own post announces it — and then cross-posted its own news there: a World Title change, a scaffold “Mayhem Match”, and a full press release for its May Mayhem pay-per-view of 28 May 2000. A posting relationship on CyberSlam’s board, not a CyberSlam-hosted federation.

FWLnet

Affiliate of CyberSlam (1997)
Announced as joining CyberSlam's eSlam board alongside IWO Online in May 2000, in a post that also names the FWF as already there. Its own address is given as fwlnet.com. What FWLnet was — a federation, or a network carrying several — is not settled by the one served post.

UCW-Wrestling

Affiliate of CyberSlam (1997)
“UCW-Wrestling joins Eslam, and the Monday Mayhem card for 6/12” — 9 June 2000, on the board’s own headline index.

WCW (West Coast Wrestling)

Affiliate of CyberSlam (1997)
Posted its own show — "West Coast Wrestling: Insanity for 6/6" — to CyberSlam's eSlam board on 7 June 2000, spelling its name out in full beside the acronym.

DIWA

Affiliate of CyberSlam (1997)
A regular poster to CyberSlam's eSlam board through May and June 2000: a Friday Night Fury card, the opening of a Middle League "with a minor league to follow", and an Owen Hart memorial show its own reporter says went well.

1998 (SWF)

Affiliate of CyberSlam (1997)
The Steelramp Wrestling Federation introduced itself on CyberSlam's eSlam board on 31 May 2000, announcing a bought-out domain, a site under construction and a full roster from 64 applications in four days, and saying it looked forward to posting there again.

ICF

Affiliate of CyberSlam (1997)
The International Combat Federation ran a bracket-tagged news column on CyberSlam's eSlam board through May 2000 — show recaps, a new Sunday Night Xtasi for new talent, and the rescheduling of its Bloodfest pay-per-view from 27 May to 1 June 2000.

GFWA

Affiliate of CyberSlam (1997)
Five GFWA posts on CyberSlam’s eSlam board across May and June 2000, covering a Tuesday BackDraft show and a BackDraft card of 13 June 2000.
