Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

XUW ran a full slate under its own name for at least five years, with the production values to match: three weekly shows, a monthly-ish Pay-Per-View calendar, a developmental territory with its own belts and TV tapings, and a wrestler-database engine that gave nearly every name on the roster a complete bio card — real name, hometown, finisher, other-federation credits, the works. It began life as “UCW,” developed by founder Fluid, before an outside independent promotion objected the name sat too close to its own; it settled into “XUW,” running out of the ULTIMOwrestling.com community (“if pro wrestling is your religion, this is your church”), with Fluid, diablos26 (D26), HBK, and MC Blitzwing named among its early staff.

Its own weekly slate ran three shows deep — Mayhem, the flagship, recovered into the 120s across 2001–2004; Vortex, a second weekly, recovered into the 110s; and XPosure, a third weekly that folded partway through the run, “due to lack of writers,” per a contributor's own account — plus house shows and its own developmental territory's weekly TV tapings. A full Pay-Per-View calendar survives for 2001–2004: four PPVs in 2001 (Road Rage, Copyright Infringement, Double Trouble, December 2 Dismember), stepping up to a dozen a year by 2002–2003 under recurring banners — Reborn chief among them, the fed's own self-described Wrestlemania-equivalent, run every spring from 2002 on. A 2003 Anniversary Show (the fed's second) confirms an August 2001 launch.

The community ran deep enough to support its own annual awards ceremony — 2001 and 2002 were held in-character, a fully-recovered 2003 nominee ballot survives as a ballot alone, with categories running from Feud of the Year down to a straight-faced “Most Lovable Jobber.” The same wrestler-database engine that issued every bio card kept a real win-loss record for nearly every name that stepped through the curtain, not just the champions. See Storylines below for the arcs those same ceremonies themselves crowned, recapped from the shows they actually ran across.

XUW also belonged, for at least part of its run, to the interfed alliance PTC. The clearest staged evidence: its own PTC Global Championship was defended in the main event of a Reborn PPV by a champion representing a separate PTC member fed, Rush Pro Wrestling (RPW), in a full worked outsider-invasion angle. See Storylines below for the angle in full, and Championships, filtered to XUW for the fed's own eight-belt lineage, transcribed with exact dates from its own recovered ledger.

Its own ending isn't dated in any surviving document — the belt ledger's own exact dates run only to the fed's last full snapshot (September 2002, with the Television Championship's own retirement extending to December 2002), and the 2001–2006 run on the placard above is the outer bound the corpus supports, not a documented closure. Its own trail simply goes quiet rather than announcing itself.

Storylines

XUW ran its own in-character Annual Awards Ceremony in 2001 and 2002, plus a fully-recovered 2003 nominee ballot — a fed naming its own Feud, Match, and Moment of the Year is telling you, in its own voice, exactly what it valued. The arcs below are led by those own categories and recapped from the served shows they actually ran across, every beat linked to the card it happened on.

The Ripper and the Phoenix — Feud of the Year, 2002 Written by AI, 2026

XUW's own 2002 Annual Awards Ceremony crowned HBK — billed there as "The Phoenix" HBK — and Jack "The Ripper" Nobbledy Best Feud of the year, over four other nominated pairings, on the strength of a Hell in a Cell match at that year's Big Brawl PPV. The ceremony's own retrospective, read live by the broadcast team as the two men shook hands on stage to accept the award, puts a shape on the whole run: "After they spent the best part of six months kicking the hell out of one another, there has to be some form of bond established. Especially considering their hellish match to end it all, off the top of the cell and all that."

The go-home go-away build survives on Mayhem #36 (27 May 2002), where HBK — framed throughout in the Phoenix persona, speaking by candlelight — calls the match by name for the first time on record: "I may not see you until Sunday, at the PPV, but when I do, I will see you in hell… Hell in a Cell…" The Cell match itself was fought at Big Brawl One, 2 June 2002 — a show Wayback never crawled under its own site-listed URL, any short-form alias, or a results page, a permanent gap in the surviving record rather than a fetching failure. What survives either side of it is the build captured on Mayhem #36 and the ceremony's own retrospective naming the result: HBK's arm raised, the two men shaking hands afterward to close the book on it.

“Get Out, Go Up, Pin, and Win” — Match of the Year, 2002 Written by AI, 2026

The same ceremony's Match of the Year went to a three-way cage match from More Double Trouble (Nov 2002) pitting three XUDF wrestlers — Doughboy (the developmental territory's own North American Champion), Latch Key Kid, and DarkSeid — against one another for a single XUW contract, run in two falls inside one locked cage. The first fall ("Get Out, Go Up") handed the contract to whoever escaped the cage first; the stakes were plain from the open — the broadcast called it before the bell, "in just a matter of minutes, one of these three men will join the ranks of XUW." Doughboy had a clean shot at escaping alone and chose instead to help Latch Key Kid lock in a sleeperhold on DarkSeid rather than climb out himself — the beat the broadcast flagged in the moment ("he's a man of his word") as the real character work of the match. DarkSeid broke free, LKK escaped the cage, and the callup was his.

The second fall ("Pin and Win," Doughboy's own North American Title on the line against DarkSeid) went another two stolen-key finishes before it ended: DarkSeid locked the cage door on the referee with the actual key, was caught reaching for it a second time but grabbed Latch Key Kid's wooden novelty "Key to Victory" by mistake, and Doughboy — recovered, and having fished the real key out of DarkSeid's own tights to let an official back in — finished him with the Cookie Cutter for the pin. Three wrestlers, one contract, one title, and (per the ceremony's own vote) all three nominated together the following year for having put on the match that mattered most.

The Ripper's death — Moment of the Year (nominated), 2003 Written by AI, 2026

The 2003 nominee ballot names, among its Moment of the Year field and without elaborating further, "Jack Nobbledy's (kayfabe) death at Reborn." The staging survives whole on the served Reborn (2003) card, and it is worth recapping in full rather than left as the namedrop the ballot alone gives it.

Nobbledy entered the show's main event, a triple threat for the World Heavyweight Championship against defending champion Samurai Bob and challenger Henrik the King — three men the broadcast framed as carrying bad blood back to the fed's own founding. Henrik won the title, pinning Nobbledy clean off a Kingpin. Nobbledy did not accept the loss: he attacked Bob at the top of the ramp, then turned on Bob's valet, Naron, chasing her up the scaffolding supporting the arena's own video screen. Bob and Naron jumped clear, landing safely through a stack of tables below; Nobbledy, still tearing at the scaffold's supports in pursuit, brought the structure down on top of himself as the screen and rigging collapsed onto the stage. The broadcast closed the segment on the wreckage, not on him: "I honestly don't see how anyone could have survived that."

Nobbledy does not appear again in the served record after this card — the collection's own holdings run to October 2004, and his own last appearance in them is this night. Per the standing practice for a documented death angle: the fact of the staging is reported, not re-performed at length, and the character's own arc is what the paragraph is about, not the mechanics of how the collection found it. The title he lost changing hands is itself the other half of the story — Henrik the King's win here is his own career highlight, not a footnote to Nobbledy's; see Henrik's own page.

The Deadly Draw Written by AI, 2026

A recurring in-house talent-shuffle gimmick, named across multiple 2003 awards categories: Doughboy's own callup from XUDF "via the Deadly Draw" is nominated Moment of the Year; Henrik the King's own "rise and fall from grace after winning the Deadly Draw" is nominated Storyline of the Year the same ballot — the same Henrik whose own Reborn 2003 title win closes the Ripper's arc above. Exactly what the Draw's own format was isn't recovered beyond those namedrops; nothing is invented to fill the gap.

XUW's own out-of-character hangout thread, "XUDF Thread of OOCness!!!!11", is served whole — not a roleplay, but the board's own day-to-day voice.

XUW's developmental territory, XUDF, ran its own North American and US Championships — confirmed active via the 2003 awards ballot's own match listings (Doughboy/Shadow/Darkseid NA Title matches; Joey Vendetti/Longhorn US Title matches) — no title-history page or lineage survives for either belt beyond those namedrops.

Affiliations

Global Internet Wrestling Federation (GIWF, 1999–2001)

Shared continuity with XUW (2001)
XUW’s own wrestler-database bio cards list “UWF” among more than one wrestler’s other-federation credits; XUW ran the same roster-and-ranking engine (“I.M. Productions”) as the UWF; XUW’s forum carried the same community culture already familiar from the UWF and GIWF; and XUW’s own “The Bastard” Al Crud shares an exact name, aka, and — per the same handler’s own account — the same handler as the GIWF/UWF’s own Al Crud. The two are read as two houses on one line, though the character connection is treated as likely rather than certain pending stronger evidence.

Primetime Central (PTC)

Member of XUW (2001)
Confirmed repeatedly from XUW’s own side: DJ’s interview states outright “We’re the only PTC fed to have a developmental fed at this stage,” treating “PTC fed” as a category XUW belongs to; the PTC Global Championship was defended at an XUW PPV (Reborn); Sidhe Nimh’s interview references entering a PTC Cruiserweight Tournament. Independently corroborated from PTC’s own side: a 2008 PTCPedia wiki carries live XUW articles.

Xtreme Ultimo Developmental Federation (XUDF)

Academy — feeds into XUW (2001)
XUW's own developmental/feeder fed, run at xudf.ultimoxuw.com with its own roster, titles, TV tapings, and full three-board forum substructure mirroring XUW's own. Per DJ's interview, XUDF made XUW "the only PTC fed to have a developmental fed at this stage" — i.e. structurally a first among PTC's member feds, not just an internal XUW program.

RPW (Rush Pro Wrestling)

Crossover booking with XUW (2001)
The PTC Global Championship, held by RPW's own "Chris Storm" ("the Last Great Technical Wrestler"), was defended in the main event of XUW's own Reborn PPV against Apocalypse, Patti Anderson, and Jesse Jamester — an outsider-invasion angle staged between two separate PTC member feds, not an XUW-internal storyline.

Universal Wrestling Federation (UWF, 2001–07)

Affiliate of XUW (2001)
Named on UWF’s own affiliates page among its contemporary partner feds, listed by its full name, Xtreme Ultimo Wrestling, alongside Primetime Central, WWA, and others — a public partner listing separate from the title lineage the two feds also share through GIWF.

Global Internet Wrestling Federation (GIWF, 1999–2001)

Predecessor to XUW (2001)
A second line running off GIWF alongside the UWF, sharing engine and cast rather than one single chain.

4 Winds Wrestling (4WW)

Crossover booking with XUW (2001) (March 2004)
