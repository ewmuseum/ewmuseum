documented on its own terms

A message-board federation whose Wednesday Wreckage broadcast and two pay-per-views, MotorCity Madness and Clash of the Immortals, carried a hardcore title feud and a leadership battle into the last months of 2000, while its recruiting bulletins hopped from one free host to the next all year long. Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

The federation's own record dates its opening to 1999, launched with a pay-per-view it called Total Anarchy; the promotion returned to the same city for that show every year after, marking it as a birthday for the business itself. It already had a working website by early February 2000, when a visiting community reviewer compared its front page to the opening screen of an old home-computer wrestling simulator and admired how thoroughly its wrestler tools were standardized; the promotion described itself, in bulletin after bulletin that year, as a booked message-board league running two hand-written cards a week from a private, password-protected board, open only to made-up wrestlers.

Its own recruiting drive never let up and never quite settled down either. A vice president for personnel gave way to a president by midyear, and a new vice president was still knocking on doors in December; the promotion's own front door moved with him, from one free host to the next, without ever losing its seven-belt, then six-belt, championship lineup.

Wednesday Wreckage, its weekly broadcast, staged an international tournament decided by straight jacket, a handcuffed grudge match built around a ladder and a suspended gavel, and a hardcore battle royal that spilled out of the arena entirely, chasing its own combatants through a children's hospital ward, a bar, and a loading dock before a masked man in a rival stable's shirt turned the finish upside down.

MotorCity Madness, staged at the Pontiac Silverdome that September, built an entire card toward a hardcore challenge between its reigning hardcore champion and its own heavyweight titleholder. The challenger dropped his opponent, looked down at him, and simply walked away rather than make the cover, leaving his rival counted out instead of pinned.

Clash of the Immortals returned to the Sky Dome in Ontario that December for what its own broadcast called its second running, deciding the promotion's tag titles, its world championship, and control of the promotion itself under the eye of an on-screen authority figure with rivalries of his own playing out at ringside. The last surviving 2000 bulletin, dated 15 December, still promised the promotion's first openings in months under its newly introduced vice president.

Its 2001 record picks up without a gap: a weekly Saturday Storm broadcast on 6 January defended five championships in one night and closed on an unfinished handicap main event, and a one-night, multi-man Meltdown tournament for the promotion's own top title later that month crowned no one outright, its two finalists fighting to a draw the reigning champion called off himself and carried over to a Wednesday Evening Confessions broadcast that resolved into a three-way no contest instead, on a night that also saw one of the promotion's own knights turn on his fellow knights to join a rival faction calling itself the Corporation. By British Brawl in March, control of the promotion's own tag and United States titles had both changed hands, one of them in a four-way collision that ended in a simultaneous double pinfall and a ruling of joint champions; and the promotion's third annual running of Total Anarchy, that same month, opened with its longtime champion voluntarily giving up the title to set an eight-man tournament in its place, closing with a new champion, a reshuffled Corporation faction, and two more championships changing hands inside one broadcast.
