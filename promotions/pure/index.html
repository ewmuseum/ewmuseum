A Japan-set puroresu promotion that argued its own case in a twelve-hundred-word manifesto against American sports entertainment, ran stadium cards called Ikusa through the spring of 2002, and crowned exactly one champion before its record runs out. Written by AI, 2026

Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

PURE put its argument on the site before it put on a show. Its Concept page spends twelve hundred words explaining what puroresu is and is not — “first and foremost a sport,” a contest whose matches are “more about telling a story, than picking a winner and a loser” — and works the claim through the 1994 Super J Cup final and the three stories it reads into that one match. It closes on a Do’s and Don’ts list: wrestle twenty minutes on nearly every show, expect drops on the head and stiff kicks, don’t wander around the ring pointing at yourself after a cool move, don’t cut twenty minutes of promo about how the people in the arena are stupid. Then it thanks two men from two other federations for helping write the guide.

What it staged followed from that. Four divisions — a World Heavyweight title, an Asian National title, a Jr. Heavyweight title and Tag Team titles — were ranked in print by a house columnist, Yuri “Quest” Takayama, who worked through all four in a single May 2002 piece and then ran twelve rumours past them. Inphino Blitz led the Asian National division and was told in the same breath that a gaijin holding an Asian National Championship was a bit of an oxymoron. The End led the Jr. Heavyweights, billed as a blind man who claimed to be half Japanese. The H.I.T. was ranked in two divisions at once and named among the finalists of the Jr. Heavyweight tournament, an “IWO superstar known for his extreme love of dangerous matches and mathematics.”

Its cards were called Ikusa and they ran in stadiums: Expo ’70 in Osaka on 14 April 2002, Matsumoto Stadium in Nagano on 28 April, Yamagata Park Stadium in Tendo for a fourth show whose card survives and whose result does not. Around them the newsdesk kept a running account of the promotion’s own week — its owner, Alexander Miyagawa, releasing four wrestlers by memo addressed to “Friends... Romans... Japanese Men...”; Charlie Loc’s cut tendon and fractured arm reported out of an Osaka hospital; Ali Khadaffi kidnapping a visiting Reverend and beating him badly enough at Ikusa III to cost him a month in a Japanese hospital.

It kept promising a larger version of itself. A first pay-per-view was announced for the Tokyo Dome, named “Future Destinies” one morning and updated to “Final Destinies” that afternoon, and it was to crown the first World Champion and the first Asian Champion on the same night. The World Heavyweight Championship history page went up ahead of it carrying a single line: “#1 - To be determined at ‘Future Destinies’ (8/14/02).” Only one PURE belt is ever recorded on anybody, and The End is still carrying it at the record’s own last word.

By October 2002 the promotion had changed shape. Its shows were called Gekito, its roster was other people — Phreak, Jason Marah, Vilas Lavagna, Vito Wynn — and its newsdesk was arguing for a Deathmatch title of its own on the strength of a Darkstar against Joey Flash match nothing else in the record describes. The last thing PURE left is a headline, “Gekito 4 Live Results,” posted on 18 October 2002. How it ended isn’t in the surviving record.

Storylines

PURE’s own record is a newsdesk’s, not a show’s: no segment or promo script survives anywhere in the seven held documents, and the one card the fed itself published carries no result. Most of what the newsdesk narrates is a single dated bulletin with no return visit — a release memo, an injury report, a signing. One thread alone gets told across more than one post, with an escalation and a named consequence, and it is the only arc this crawl can honestly call a storyline rather than a headline.

Ali Khadaffi, the Reverend, and the justice PURE never got to see Written by AI, 2026

Ali Khadaffi opened his PURE run the way the promotion opened its own account — a street-style attack on Inphino Blitz at the very first Ikusa, 14 April 2002. By mid-May a second man had been drawn into his orbit: the Reverend Jesse Kintei, confirmed for a guest appearance at Ikusa III as a guest of Jordan X Shabazz, a fan, the newsdesk said, of “minorities in sports and sports entertainment” who wanted to shake Shabazz’s hand. What actually happened between them at that show isn’t itself held — only its aftermath is. By 22 May, Kintei was in an Osaka hospital, facial injuries possibly requiring plastic surgery, a doctor’s carefully hedged “I’m sure the Reverend will make a full recovery” standing in for a fight nobody wrote up; Shabazz couldn’t be reached for comment. By 17 June, Shabazz and the corporation he headed announced they would not file a missing-persons report at all, and Shabazz went on the record instead: “I’m going to have Ali pay by my justice, the only real justice in this world, BLACK JUSTICE!” The newsdesk promised “More as it comes to us.” Nothing more came. None of Khadaffi, Kintei, or Shabazz appears anywhere in the Gekito-era record that closes PURE out four months later; whatever justice Shabazz meant is not in the surviving corpus.

Old School Wrestling (OSW, Easton, 2002)

Crossover booking with PURE (2002)
PURE’s own rumour headline “PURE To OSW To PURE?” (17 April 2002); its own column reporting “PCW/OSW additions to the JR Heavyweight division of Great Dragon, El Drago, and El Diablo” and naming “OSW Queen of Extreme competitors Bethany Yamoto and Brandi Wilson” for a proposed PURE women’s division; and, from OSW’s own side, a storyline asking “WHAT is the correlation between Inphino Blitz, Aki-yankah, Rent a Hero, and Rent a Villian?”, naming PURE’s own Asian National number-one contender.

Primetime Championship Wrestling (PCW, Primetime, 2000–2004)

Crossover booking with PURE (2002)
Roster flow both ways in the fed’s own words: “Jordan X Shabazz (from PCW)” signed 6 May 2002; The End’s “excellent record in EW … sending him to PCW and eventually PURE”; “PCW and OCW super-heavyweights Akiyama and Kimbusa are expected to sign”; a rumour that “PCW former champion Chris Sloboda, AKA Boda” would appear to punk out PURE’s owner; and the Reverend Jesse Kintei praising Shabazz’s work “here in PURE and the PCW.” The H.I.T.’s own move from PURE to PCW is the same crossover, seen from the character side.

Primetime Championship Wrestling (PCW, Primetime, 2000–2004)

Crossover booking with PURE (2002) (Sep – Dec 2002)
PCW's own newswire stages PURE as its "Japanese affiliate, and rival": PCW's CEO challenged PURE in September 2002, PURE accepted a 10-man tag team match at Red October (6 October 2002, the Tokyo Dome), where "Primetime Championship Wrestling sets foot on the turf of PURE", and after PURE's re-opening that December PCW loaned it three talents.

Primetime Wrestling Academy (PWA, Primetime, 2000–2003)
