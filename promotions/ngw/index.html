Next Generation Wrestling ran 65 shows in its first year, copyrighted its own gimmick matches, kept a public blacklist of people it would not work with again, and defended its top belt in buried alive matches for five years. Written by AI, 2026

Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

Next Generation Wrestling opened on 25 January 2000 and ran a Tuesday Night Storm the same night. Sixty-five more shows followed before the year was out, and its own transcript archive counts every one of them. The first World Champion, Excalibur, was crowned at Out Cold I inside a steel cage a month later and had lost the belt inside a Hell in a Cell four days after that, which is roughly the register the fed kept for the next five years.

Its Xtreme Title ledger is the clearest statement of what it was: fifty numbered reigns won in mega mall brawls, graveyard matches, anarchy matches, elimination scaffold matches and buried alive matches. It catalogued them all in a Match Encyclopedia and marked the ones it claimed as its own — “(c) = copyrighted by NGW staff. May not be used without approval” — and published a trademark notice claiming its shows, its gimmicks and “the names of its superstars” as protected marks. It also kept a public Blacklist page naming the people and federations it would not work with again, and printed the reason under each one.

Scottywood, one of the two men its own information page names as having opened it, is first on its all-time wins list with 61 and first on its singles list with 47, and ran the company on screen for most of the record. He held both the World and the Xtreme Titles in the spring of 2000, and got a chair to the skull from Robb Hardy a fortnight before Border Line 2004 for booking John Hitchin into two matches in one night and then entering him into the Battle Dome at number one.

It was a Primetime Central fed — its own news ran the headline “NGW the Top Rated PTC Cable Fed” in January 2003 — and it worked outside the alliance too: an eight-man interpromotional tag against Furiously Addictive Wrestling, announced on a radio show the two federations ran together at three in the afternoon, four wrestlers a side. Two names on its own retired roster carry the status “in FAW.”

How it ended isn't in the surviving record. Its own news stopped on 3 February 2005; its next Tuesday Night Storm card went up reading Date: TBA, Location: TBA, Deadline: TBA; and the last show it wrote is a Storm its own summary dates 22 February 2005, from the Hartford Civic Center. What survives of it is nearly everything it wrote down about itself — five title ledgers running to 211 reigns, a hall of fame, a record book, an archive of every show it ever ran — and eighteen of the shows themselves.

Storylines

The 2004 Battle Dome and the Year of the Hitch Written by AI, 2026

John Hitchin drew the number-one entry into the 2004 Battle Dome and, by his own words backstage beforehand, wasn't worried about the other twenty-nine men in it: “What does matter is this…is that I will be winning the Battle Dome tonight!” (results-bl177.html). He did — a new promotion record, ten eliminations, breaking Revolution X's own prior mark of nine and a half — but the last fall turned on a swerve. Scott King ran in expecting Scottywood to finish Hitchin off with a barbed-wire hockey stick; Scottywood cracked it over King's own skull instead, and the two men who'd spent the whole match trying to eliminate each other shook hands as security dragged King out. The prize was a World Title shot at Xtrememania V.

It didn't come easy. Two nights before Xtrememania V, King and his partner The Charm beat Hitchin and Dallas Reeves by disqualification in the Storm main event — the broadcast's own count had it as the second time King laid Hitchin out on that show alone (results-tns182.html). Xtrememania V itself isn't among the shows held, but its outcome survives secondhand: the following night's Storm opens on Hitchin addressing the crowd as the new champion, recounting the win in his own words — “Last night, in front of millions of people worldwide, I defeated the NGW Champion…EX-Champion Scott King for the World Title” — a Triple Crown night, the World Title joining the Xtreme and Battle Dome titles already on his shoulders (results-tns185.html). He held all three into the following year: results-tns197.html, the held corpus's own last dated show (22 February 2005), still has him defending the World Title.

Revolution X and Scottywood, one more round Written by AI, 2026

Revolution X and Scottywood's own rivalry predates every show this wing holds — Blood Fest 2004's own pre-match package cuts to Xtrememania I, years earlier, to Ice and Prime Time turning on Revolution X in a segment the held corpus doesn't otherwise carry. What it does hold is the payoff: a Falls Count Anywhere match at Blood Fest 2004, four years of history behind it by the broadcast's own count, that turns into the show's bloodiest brawl — a PlayStation 2 broken under a DDT, a barbed-wire-wrapped hockey stick used first as a weapon and then as a chokehold, and a finish that drives Revolution X face-first onto that same board of barbed wire for the pin. Scottywood wins clean (results-bf180.html).

What he doesn't get is a clean ending. As Revolution X climbs back to his feet with a chair, looking for one more swing, “Keep Away” by Godsmack — not Scottywood's own music — cuts in, and a second man who looks exactly like Revolution X walks out onto the stage beside Cindy Thomas-Riley. She says one line into the microphone — “Xtrememania 5!” — drops it, and both leave. Nothing in the held corpus explains who the second man is or what the line means; results-bf180.html is the last word on it.
