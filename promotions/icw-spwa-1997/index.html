A July 1997 Usenet fed founded under the name ICW by a former GCWF co-president, publicly told by a rival fed's own member that the name already belonged to someone else, and renamed Supreme Pro Wrestling Alliance one week later with the same titles, staff and first pay-per-view intact. Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

International Championship Wrestling opened on rec.sport.pro-wrestling.fantasy on 1 July 1997, its founding package signed by a self-described former GCWF Co-President starting fresh. It announced six titles, a Monday-night flagship show, and a first pay-per-view for late July at the United Center in Chicago, built around a thirty-two-man tournament for its World Heavyweight Championship.

Six days later it had to change its own name over a federal copyright complaint, relaunching on 8 July 1997 as the Supreme Pro Wrestling Alliance (SPWA), “the e-wrestling fed of the future,” with the same Monday-night format and a new inaugural pay-per-view, Dog Days of Summer, again built around a thirty-two-man tournament for a new World Championship. The federation's own roster reputation reached the wider newsgroup: SPWA carried a wrestler, Alex Extreme, ranked in the RSPWF 500's own top fifteen singles wrestlers.

That standing made its next move controversial. Its own operator closed the SPWA down that September to co-found a new e-fed, Frontier Japan Pro Wrestling (FJPW), announced 27 September 1997 as the work of three self-described creative minds, including SPWA's own former owner and the owner of a separate promotion, New Japan Internet Wrestling. One newsgroup critic, a former SPWA Vice-President by his own account, wrote publicly that SPWA “had an excellent thing going” and called shutting it down “a stupid decision.”

FJPW rebuilt the promotion overseas, based in Japan with occasional cards in Canada and Mexico — “Won't see us in the Garden folks!” — and switched from a simmed to a fully booked format: “No scripts, no pre-outcomes, just hardcore action.” It ran two weekly shows and a quarterly pay-per-view slate, and crowned its first Icon Heavyweight Champion through a thirty-two-man E-1 Invitational tournament ending in a “Chamber of Horrors” match fought inside a chain-link cage, its stipulation an electric chair built into the ring.

Its November 1997 pay-per-view, “Blood, Sweat, and Tears,” drew an approximate crowd of ten thousand in Nagoya for an Icon Championship final inside a two-ring, weapons-filled steel “Deathdome,” an inverted-cage battle royal for the tag titles, and a one-time attraction match pitting “The Iron Man” Mike Madland against “Mr. Hall of Fame” Tommy Van Dam.

The last document this lineage's own record carries is a 9 November 1997 FAQ restating FJPW's rules and staff. A later restatement is known to have renamed the fed again, to Frontier Japan Pro, but no closing document survives anywhere in the record under any of this promotion's names — International Championship Wrestling, the Supreme Pro Wrestling Alliance, or either Frontier Japan name.
