A weekly show called Courage, staged in named arenas with a television rating printed beside its early cards, that died in the autumn of 2003, came back in 2004, stopped in July 2006, returned in 2009 for a one-off in Montreal that turned into two more years and a world tour, and closed on its own fan board in August 2013. Written by AI, 2026

Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

All-Star Championship Wrestling ran a weekly show called Courage and, in its first years, printed a television rating beside every episode of it. Its own event archive keeps the figures to one decimal place — a 2.2 for Courage 67, a 3.2 for breakOUT, a 3.0 buyrate for Legends — alongside a real arena for each card, and the arenas are the tell: the Conseco Fieldhouse, the Staples Center, the Bell Centre, General Motors Place, the Palace at Auburn Hills, a converted university gymnasium in Eagar, Arizona. The fed toured North America on paper and kept the books in public.

It died more than once. Its own account of 2003 is that “success was also mixed in with many tragic moments, the last of which caused the federation to lose it's TV deal, and in turn, close to it's US audience”; a later show puts it more plainly still — “ACW, however, goes under in the fall of 2003.” It came back in 2004 “resurrected and brought back to US TV screens, promising a mix of the glory days and new talent,” and spent the next two years being fought over: five ownership changes, a match contested for the sole ownership of the company, and a newsdesk that blamed the network out loud for cutting Courage to two telecasts in October.

Its cards ran long — twenty thousand words is an ordinary one, and End Game 2010 runs past a hundred thousand — and they held comedy and continuity at the same weight: a bracket openly called the Jobber Tournament, a self-billed Jimmy Cain Jobber Slaughter, and a rivalry it dated back to 1993 given a handshake and a flash-forward to a funeral in 2056 at Legends II. Its belts were taken apart and put back together the same way. The Television and Scorpion Fighting titles were merged onto one holder in October 2004 and split again the following March by the champion who inherited both, on air, at his own discretion; on 26 January 2006 the promotion retired the United States and Television championships from the stage at once and gave their history back to the Spirit of ACW, a belt its own fiction dates to 2 April 1993 and whose holder picks the stipulation of every match it is defended under.

The weekly run stops at Courage 100, at Nationwide Arena in Columbus on 6 July 2006, and the fed's own belt pages vacate the titles still in play four days later. Its World Champion that last spring was Seymour Almasy, and the last match of the run is the one he lost the belt in, strapping it around Andy Sharp's waist afterwards with “Hold down the fort, Andy. I'm done.” It came back on 15 March 2009 for what it billed as one night in Montreal, a dead federation's one-off paid for, in the show's own telling, by “allowing paying fans a share in the federation for them to own for the rest of their mortal lives” — and it kept going. Courage ran weekly out of Montreal again, an All-Star Academy put on Showcase episodes of its own from October 2009, and End Game that December inducted the first class of an ACW Hall of Fame on the card itself.

In 2010 Courage went abroad and stayed there, a season at a time: Glasgow, Cardiff and Wembley Arena in the winter; Madrid and Barcelona in the spring, with Legends IV at the Nou Camp; Ukraine, Belarus, Poland and Russia in the early summer; Helsinki, Oslo, Copenhagen and Stockholm after it; Greece and the Acropolis in the autumn; and Italy for the winter, ending at the Stadio Olimpico in Rome on the last night of the year. A company of its own fiction, SlySports Entertainment, had bought the promotion through an executive named Jeremy Hunt, and the run's last card, Dia de los Muertos at the Estadio Azul in Mexico City on 27 February 2011, ends not in the ring but in a London office: SlySports' president learns that Hunt has cancelled every date of a South American tour and orders him found, and the write-up signs off “F I N.”

The fed's own fan board carried a third run from that summer — web shows, a card at Traeger Park in Alice Springs and two house shows in Perth in November 2011, two nights at the Nippon Budokan in early 2012, and a Legends Kick-Off Tour that reached Sydney, Nova Scotia, in June 2013. On 9 August 2013 its own closing post said: “Four and a half years ago, I got together with a few old ACW handlers and decided to do a one-off show to see how it would go. Four and a half years later, we are closing our doors.”

Storylines

Seymour Almasy, and the belts he would not merge Written by AI, 2026

Seymour Almasy arrives in ACW in the late summer of 2004 and loses. His first recorded match, on the Courage of 26 August 2004 in San Diego, is ruled a no contest against Kasper Sky; on Courage 65 he loses to Jamar Gordo. The card that made him champion says so itself, in his own head, walking to the curtain: “This was his first chance at a title in All-Star Championship Wrestling... Tonight would be the end of Seymour Almasy, ACW newcomer.”

That card is breakOUT, in February 2005 at the Palace at Auburn Hills, and the match is a three-way for the ACW Television Championship against the champion Fejona Min and Kelly Flawless. Almasy comes out to “Fight With Seymour” by The Black Mages, puts Flawless off the top rope to the concrete, and submits Fejona Min with an ankle lock. The official fetches the belt from the timekeeper and hands it to him on the mat. The show gives him one line of interior monologue afterwards: “he had succeeded in climbing his own personal Mount Gagazet.”

The belt he won was carrying another one. breakOUT's own text explains that the Television and Scorpion Fighting championships “had been unified. There simply weren't enough fighters in ACW to warrant the latter” — so the fed's owner handed Almasy the Scorpion title too and left the decision to him. He announced it on Courage 72, in Chicago: “SilverHAWK announced a few weeks ago that I was the Scorpion Fighting Champion, as well as the Television Champion. I'd pretty much figured the belts were unified…but as it turns out, the boss man's left it up to me to decide what I do with them.” His answer was that the Scorpion Fighting Championship had a history worth keeping: “my decision is thus. The two titles will REMAIN SEPARATE!” — the Scorpion in any match with a stipulation, the Television title in a straight one. He made the first defence the same night, in a kendo stick match against Lowell Dot Com, and kept it.

He was still carrying both in June. The fed's own fan board posts the End Game card on 8 June 2005 and bills him “Seymour Almasy[c][c]” in a five-way elimination match for the pair of them. End Game is held: in the five-way elimination match for both belts, Lowell Dot Com pinned him first for the Scorpion Fighting Championship, he kept the Television Championship by pinning Max Danger, and he won the End Game match itself when Alias quit.

Andy Sharp pinned him for the Television Championship on Courage 79; he did not move after the pin and was carried out. What the record does hold is the night he took the top one. At Legends II on 26 January 2006, in a three-way against champion Max Danger and Khristain Keller, he pinned to become ACW World Heavyweight Champion. He defended it against Brandon Youngblood on Courage 88, against LLB on Courage 92, and twice in one night at Honor & Glory, where the fed made its own World Champion qualify for his own main event and he won that too. Andy Sharp pinned him for it at Courage 100 on 6 July 2006, and he strapped the belt around Sharp's waist and said “Hold down the fort, Andy. I'm done.”

Alias and “Superstar” Vince Jacobs, thirteen matches Written by AI, 2026

Alias — the Original Pulp Hero, billed out of San Francisco — and “Superstar” Vince Jacobs fought each other on five of the cards the archive holds, and the fed dates the rivalry to 1993. Its own retelling at Legends II runs year by year: a chair to the knee in 1996 that cost Jacobs a World Championship, a Best of Se7en against Jason Kain, the promotion going under in 2003 and coming back.

Jacobs took the World Heavyweight Championship from Alias at King of Ages in August 2004, in a match billed Pounded and Fused III. He lost it four months later without being beaten: at breakOUT, in a three-way, he and Keller pinned Alias at the same moment, and the belt was vacated out of it. The card's own result line reads simply “Winner > Draw.”

The last of the thirteen was booked for Legends II as a two-consecutive-falls match after ACW retired the United States Championship out from under it. Falls go both ways and no third fall lands. The show closes the rivalry on a handshake across the top rope, a flash-forward to Jacobs eulogising Alias at a funeral in 2056, and the flat statement that neither man would wrestle in ACW again.

Five owners, one company Written by AI, 2026

ACW's own history segment counts the ownerships out: the co-founders Charles Dunn and James Boyd; a coalition of five Guardians Dunn hand-picked, headed by SilverHAWK, Joe Bishop and Jimmy Gonz; sole financial backing from Brian Carter, who bought the founders out and was then written off as dead; William Laguna, who bought the company while Carter was gone; and SilverHAWK, who was the last man standing. The Courage of 23 September 2004 stages Laguna against Carter for the sole ownership of ACW and never records who won it.

SilverHAWK's own run as owner ends the way the fed liked to end things. Joe Bishop opens Legends II by announcing that he “was attacked by unknown assailants last week after Courage” and would not be back, in front of a camera or behind it, for six months. The pay-per-view he had set up went ahead without him.

The night ACW buried two belts to dig up a third Written by AI, 2026

Legends II opens with a title-by-title history and two retirements. The United States Championship goes first, with Alias named its last champion; then the Television Championship, with Andy Sharp holding it as the announcement is made. The show gives him the reaction: “He was looking down at his now defunct TV title. He would be the last in a long storied list of champions.”

What replaces them is the Spirit of ACW, carried to the ring by the man the fed bills as its first champion. Its history, as Gonz tells it, is that it was introduced on 2 April 1993, held by Gonz and then by a wrestler called Ironsides, buried on two failed reigns, and split into the United States and Television titles. Its rule survives the burial: “with this title, whomever holds it…picks the stipulations that each match that it's involved in, is governed under. Either match-to-match, or over an entire reign.” The Television Championship match already booked to open the show becomes the match that crowns it, and Andy Sharp — who had spent 2005 being beaten by the people he is now above — wins it and holds it through every card the record still has.
