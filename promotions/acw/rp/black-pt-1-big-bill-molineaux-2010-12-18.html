Black, pt. 1: 'Big' Bill Molineaux

18 December 2010
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 18 December 2010 The same tragedies strike men down, generation after generation. The people may change but the stories remain the same. The stories have a being of their own, gossamer as the wings of a butterfly but tangible as a million men lying dead on history's battlefields. We are but their puppets, ushered into our places in their grand narrative, each of us single stitches in their immortal tapestries. Ours is an old story. A story woven from integral elements of the human condition; pride, lust, avarice, ignorance and brutality. Many pawns have moved by Our fingertips and many kings have fallen in Our timeless game. Now, We shall share some of these stories with you. We begin with the story of Our First. **** Out in South Carolina back in the 1830s, there was a cotton plantation bought up by an Irishman named Mahon. Mahon had some eighty slaves working out in his fields, business was good and, for a time, his slaves were the best kept in the state. When the scarlet fever claimed his wife, Jesus nor whiskey could plug that hole in him. His children, Alice and Lester, saw their old man waste away to a spleen-fuelled skeleton, spitting and swearing at his slaves, flogging them for no good reason. He went as far to demolish half of his slaves' homes, making them live three families to one shack, huddled up on the dirt floor with no food nor modesty. In their darkest times, folks regress. They revert back to what they know best, their simple pleasures and vices. For a man like Mahon, it was bareknuckle boxing. He got a taste for it back in Ireland, watching the fights with his Da in bars, even fighting a few fights himself when he came of age. So, for his amusement, to pass his wretched time, he started pitting his slaves against each other out in the barn late at night. By torchlight they would punch, kick, bite and bleed just for a few hours rest, a few extra morsels of food or shoes for their children. Lester was a noble youth of his mother's mould, but spineless. The condition of the slaves repulsed his good nature but his father's word was law. He just hoped that when the plantation became his own the task of amending for his father's sins would not be too great. The fights became the highlight of Mahon's bleak existence. He built them a ramshackle ring from old floorboards and hockled rope and every Saturday night as many as ten slaves at a time would ruthlessly beat their kinsmen, their brothers and nephews, while Mahon watched, mummified with consumption but made to feel alive by his blood lust. One slave stood tall above the others. Freakishly large, with hands like shovels and slabs of muscle bound to his mighty frame with veins thick as rope. 'Big' Bill Molineaux. In only a couple of punches he would crush any man he faced at the scratch-line. Mahon lavished him with gifts, giving he, his wife Hettie and his son Charles their very own shack. He gave them ample food, clothes and time to rest between their long shifts out in the fields. His Mighty Oak Tree, he called him. His Black Terror. He even made Bill his own title belt, old black hide adorned with a hammered-out frying pan and decorated with some of his wife's finest jewellery. The Black Bareknuckle Boxing Championship of South Carolina, or, as it would come to be known, the Black Title. Some of the slaves admired Bill, but many reviled him for the ruthlessness he showed his fellow men. Bill just wanted to ease the suffering of his young boy, who had never tasted anything but stale bread, never slept on anything better than rags, never had time to sit back and listen to the songbirds or count the clouds. And, while it would have brought him great shame to admit it, Big Bill was proud, and yearned to elevate himself above his sub-human existence any way he could. After an especially brutal brawl one Saturday night, the bloody and ogrish spectacle of which greatly pleased Mahon, he offered Bill anything in the world as reward – anything besides his freedom. So Bill asked for the next best thing. “Massa, I sure would like to learn to read.” Mahon, after some silent contemplation, decided there was no harm in the request. So it was arranged that Bill would be taught to read for a few hours every Tuesday and Friday afternoon by Mahon's own daughter, Alice, who had a fine collection of literature that would make for an excellent starting point. At first, the presence of Big Bill, with his poor manners and filthy clothes, upset the delicate constitution of Mahon's princess. With time, though, she came to appreciate his voracious appetite for learning, his developing talent for introspection. She came to admire his simple sense of honour and integrity, and discovered a gentleness within him that surprised her. If there was a distinct moment when she fell in love with him, it was when she read him part of Shelley's Frankenstein. “But I am a blasted tree; the bolt has entered my soul; and I felt then that I should survive to exhibit what I shall soon cease to be--a miserable spectacle of wrecked humanity, pitiable to others and intolerable to myself.” In those words Bill found expression for his wretched condition, his savage life, and he wept. Alice's instinctive rush to comfort him surprised her, and, with his head in her arms, she felt an inexplicable, uncanny sensation that she, maybe foolishly, came to label as love. Their relationship became intimate and soon, Alice became pregnant with Bill's child. When Alice could no longer conceal her pregnancy from her father, his instincts alone directed him to Bill. He confronted him in front of his family, slapping him and berating him. Bill, for the sake of Hettie, denied the charges levied against him but Mahon already had Bill's punishment in mind and could not be swayed. He decreed that the coming Saturday, he would fight Bill himself. Should Bill win, he would be rewarded with the freedom he foolishly longed for. Should Mahon win, Bill's son Charles would be flogged until sunrise and if he dared to survive, he would be sold as far away from his mother as the state's boundaries would allow. Word of the fight spread amongst the slaves and come Saturday night, nearly all of them were packed into the barn around that ramshackle ring. Mahon was drunk and cheery, dancing and throwing shadow punches, making as ridiculous a scene as possible. He had locked his daughter Alice in the attic and it had taken over a quart of whiskey to numb him to the woeful screams that still rang in his ears. Bill remained stoic, that piece-of-junk Black Title strapped around his mighty waist. The bell rang, and Mahon immediately ceased his antics. He simply drew his .48 calibre duelling pistol from his pants and shot Bill straight through the left lung. Hettie dived between the ropes, falling over herself, so Mahon shot her too, cutting a chunk of meat from her shoulder. Bill, with blood streaming from his mouth and pain eating into his chest like a bonesaw, struggled to stand, collapsing and getting to one knee only to collapse again, his hands slipping around in his own blood as he tried to push himself up. Mahon's laughter pierced the slaves' dead silence. “You think I'm an idiot, Bill? You think I would give a fair chance to an adulterer, a betrayer of my trust, a lowly god-damned ungrateful nigger?” Bill dragged himself half-way up only to meet Mahon's hard fists. His every breath was like a baptism in fire and with every blow he found it harder to hold back the spinning dark that threatened to consume him. In the distance, like echoes from another world, he heard Charles' tortured screams and Hettie's hysterical sobbing. Mahon danced around Bill, stamping on his fingers, kicking him in the temple with his steel toe-caps, dropping lefts and rights into Bill's ribcage. Finally, Bill could take no more and toppled, falling flat on his back. Mahon stood over Bill, plucked the belt from his waist and held it in the air, mocking him. “Look at me, I'm the nigger champion of South Carolina! I've got a frying pan stuck on a cut-up horse-saddle, that must make me important enough to fornicate my master's daughter!” As Mahon leered over him, Big Bill mustered all of the strength he had left and shot his arms out, sinking his fingers deep into the flesh of Mahon's throat. Mahon struggled and gasped, tried to claw at Bill's face but his arms just wouldn't reach, and, turning purple as a ripe plum, tears slicking his cheeks and eyes swelling shut, he fell down alongside Bill, who kept that death grip locked in like a constrictor. Bill, his eyes not blinking but fluttering, his skin alive with death's tiny spasms and tics, his body slowly shutting down, doing all he could to keep those muscles tight just long enough to choke the life out of the bastard that made him brutalise his fellow man for a half-decent meal. He stared hard into the near-bursting eyes of Mahon, whose mouth was locked in a toothy grimace, whose nostrils were flared, whose veins bulged out of his forehead like worms beneath his skin. Locked in this hopeless position, they watched the world fade away together. And maybe if Bill could have held that grip for just one second longer, could have held back the creeping dark just one more god-damned second, then he would have died satisfied that justice had been served and the chaos and misery the Black Title has caused would never have come to be. But his grip did slacken, and his body stopped twitching, and Mahon drew breath again. Bill never did. Mahon kept to his word. As soon as he could force air back into his pus-filled lungs, he grabbed the eight-year-old Charles Molineaux by his arm and dragged him out into the fields, warning off the angry slaves with wide arcs of his pistol. There, as the Spring rain beat down around them, he lashed the boy with all the mad ferocity left in his wasted frame for what could have been hours, until the skin of his back hung in rags and he looked no more alive than a piece of meat. Mahon, when he could flay the boy no more, left him face-down in the mud. He staggered inside his mansion and placed the Black Title on his mantelpiece, a hollow trophy, a feeble attempt at consoling himself for his ruined daughter and disgraced name. But outside in the field, beneath the mud and through the pain, Charles Molineaux's heart still beat as steady as his half-brother's, sequestered and unknowing in Alice Mahon's womb. And so, with these players, the next chapter of Our story was to be written.
