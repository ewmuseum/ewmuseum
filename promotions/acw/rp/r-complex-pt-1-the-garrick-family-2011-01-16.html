R-Complex, Pt. 1: The Garrick Family

16 January 2011
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 16 January 2011 26th November, 2010. The rental car smells of leather, eucalyptus and a faint trace of vomit that catches in my nose, and the lights of the motorway cut streaks of yellow through the night sky. It's 3:00 AM and while the other boys are partying the night away in the trendy, ethnically ambiguous bars of Turin, I'm heading on straight to Florence to find myself a new hotel. When I had got back to my room in Turin after Courage 128, I found my belongings waiting for me in the lobby; there were my two suitcases, my snakeskin jacket draped over the top, and whatever they found lying around they had stuffed into a black refuse bag. “You can stay here not any more,” the receptionist had told me in struggling English. “We have complaints, many complaints.” Times like these, I wonder whether I should have just finished my Philosophy degree and given up on my dreams of competing in the schoolboy fantasy world of professional wrestling. *** 2002. “No, no, you're not doing that quite right,” Brandon said, rolling under the bottom rope. I was sparring with Walter Lake, an Italian kid of a similar age to me who had travelled from Chicago to train here in the Garrick Family Compound. I'd rustled up the money for a one-way flight to Winnipeg, Manitoba, Canada and was paying for my training by tending bar and cleaning toilets, a decision I was starting to regret. Brandon put a hand on the back of Walter's neck and socked him in the jaw with a forearm smash, putting enough force behind it to take the kid's head clean off his shoulders, but Walter shook it off like it was nothing. And it was nothing. “When you're striking, you're being too careful. As long as you protect the guy's neck, make sure you're not rocking his head back too far, you can hit him as hard as you like and you're not going to do any lasting damage.” Brandon was the youngest of the Garrick family, maybe five years older than me but with a lifetime of experience in the ring. He was studying for a Law degree but still helped out his brother, Lance, and his father, Ray, in training the students. He was a hopeless perfectionist, blunt and serious, with the world-weary eyes of an old warrior set into an otherwise youthful and handsome face. While at first I despised the rigorous and intense attitude he had towards his family's craft, I came to admire his patience with his students and his dedication to their development. Sometimes he'd spend every night of the week with me in that dusty ring in the Compound, showing me how to incorporate my mixed martial arts training into my style. We'd go for a supper of dry chicken breast and egg whites at the all-night café and have discussions about ring psychology until the early hours, a subject he had tremendous passion for, a passion that he effortlessly transferred to me. One night, him bleary-eyed from studying hard and me broken in half after all day at work and all night at the Compound, he shared his family's history with me over a half-dozen cups of tar-black coffee. “The Garrick legacy in professional wrestling spans back four generations,” he said. “My great grandfather, Frank Garrick, invented the dog-collar half crab in a match with Stanislaus Zbyszko back in 1925. He was an expert shooter and took down many a strongman with his hooks and holds. He had an ideal for professional wrestling, an ideal that's been drummed into me since I was big enough to hit the ropes. That wrestling shouldn't be about storylines or personalities, shouldn't be about mummies and vampires and all of that bunk. It has to be about two men trying to out-smart each other in the ring. The Garrick family way is to go out there and work your ass off, give the fans out there a compelling story to sink their teeth into. A match they can really think about. And if you do that, you don't need the promos, the steroids, the flashy ring attire and gimmicks.” Then his pride was suddenly deflated by some errant train of thought, and his blood-shot eyes drowned in the bottom of his coffee cup. “Which is probably why my family haven't ever got anywhere. You've heard of the Harts, the Von Erichs, the Garvins... but never the Garricks. Because of this damn ideal, this old family motto holding us back.” When Brandon thought I was ready, he got me booked on a few independent shows in Manitoba. We tagged together, him wearing the traditional blue attire of his family and me decked out in a pair of disgusting second-hand Union Jack trunks with '100% British Beef' embarrassingly emblazoned across the arse-cheeks in faded gold. We'd walk out there to dead silence, no gimmicks, no music, no playing to the crowd. But the moment the bell rang, we were Gods. Every night, we tore down the house. Every night, we had the fans in the palms of our hands. The guys backstage would be talking about how they hated Brandon, hated his perfectionism, hated how he pushed them to their limits week-in, week-out – but if you asked any one of them what the best match of their careers was, they'd say it was with him. The problem was, every morning after, we were forgotten again. We never moved up the card, never won a championship and, in the words of the big-bellied cigar-smoking promoters in the area, never drew a dime. “That's okay,” Brandon said, unwinding his wrist-tape in the back one night, “I'm only doing this until I get my degree, anyway.” But it wasn't just a stop-gap for Brandon. As much as he tried to grow away from his family's legacy, it was always in his blood. There was a strange, tragic air about him, a duality of sorts; he resented that which he loved the most, hid his greatest talent, was ashamed by that which inspired in him the most pride. Brandon was a born wrestler, no matter how hard he struggled against it. Lance was much more easy-going. A good worker and a tremendous physical specimen, standing six foot three and built like the front-end of a truck, Lance had experienced the most success out of the three Garrick brothers, working predominantly in Japan as a Gaijin monster-heel. He took to me immediately when he realised I could hold my ale, and promptly made me his chief drinking buddy. We'd hit the local bars as soon as I finished my training, playing Pool or bragging to women. He regaled me with stories of his time in the land of the rising sun, promising to take me there one day. “My grandfather, Lou Garrick,” he'd say, “first went to Japan with Karl Gotch back in 1965. He always used to say that Gotch's German suplex looked like shit until he showed him how to fucking do it right. Grandpa always loved the Japanese way of doing things... I guess it was the closest to his dad's vision he could find. That's why I fucking love working there, I feel like I'm carrying on their legacy. That, and Japanese women are fucking TIGHT.” Other times, though, he didn't have the energy for his tales of sex and partying or his profanity-laced history lessons, and we'd drink in silence for hours. At the tail-end of one of these sullen nights, a night when Lance was so drunk he was swaying on his barstool and had to shut one eye to co-ordinate his glass to his mouth, I tried to fill the dead air by talking about what an impressive wrestler Brandon is. “I can't believe he's giving all of that up to be a lawyer,” I said. “He's the best I've ever seen.” “Brandon ain't a patch on what Archie was,” Lance slurred. Archie – the eldest Garrick brother. Walter told me he had died five or six years ago, but nobody seemed to know much more than that. “Archie was the best. Lemme tell you, man, Brandon worshipped Archie when he was little. He was his role model. Brandon dreamed of the day he'd be old enough to tag with him. That never happened. Never fucking happened.” There was a long silence. Lance belched, flopped across the bar, and let out a long, drawn-out groan. Ten minutes must have passed before I plucked up the courage to ask him what happened to Archie, and maybe another ten minutes passed before he was cognizant enough to respond. “Died on tour in Puerto Rico. Overdosed on painkillers proscribed for his trick knee.” It suddenly became clear to me why Brandon so desperately wanted to leave the world of professional wrestling. The sport that made his brother a superhero and then so cruelly took him away. *** After I couldn't make the rent on my roach-infested shit-hole flat, the Garricks offered me their sofa. The move eventually became permanent, and I spent a year waking up every morning on that lumpy sofa-bed to the sound of Lindsey, the Garrick family matriarch, singing out-of-key renditions of old country songs in the kitchen. She was once a teenage ring-rat, sleeping with any baby-oiled brute she could get her hands on, but when her fading youth signalled the end of her wild phase, Ray was still waiting for her. With them, I had my first experience of what it's like to be a member of a family. I would spend my Saturday mornings fishing with Lance, then help Lindsey make dinner, spot Ray while he fixed and mended the always slowly-collapsing family home and then wax philosophical with Brandon until the early hours after he got home from the library. I met a girl, Charlotte, on a drunken spree with Lance, and we got along really well. She was a Psychology undergraduate with a soft and delicate face, red hair, and an unusual toughness about her demeanour. Even our first disastrous attempt at having sex on that fucking sofa-bed didn't deter her from me and soon, we were talking about baby names in the way that besotted couples do. Lance finally did show me Japan. We tagged together for the entire duration of a three-month tour. The Japanese fans have incredible aptitude for understanding the subtleties of wrestling, and my MMA style, adapted seamlessly for the wrestling ring by trainers of the utmost wrestling pedigree, went down a storm. And, in three months, I learned more Japanese than Lance had in three years. I had a life in Winnipeg. A family that treated me as one of their own, a girl whose beautiful flaws drew me deeper into her cocoon each day, and every weekend I donned the trunks to pursue the profession that I loved. It was a far cry from my isolated life in the vicious Dagenham landscape. For the first time... I was happy. But, as I now know, happiness is a transient mental state governed mostly by levels of chemical saturation in the brain. It is illusory, and it is fleeting.
