A booked, non-simulated Usenet federation running from 1993 into December 1998 under a rotating writers' committee, holding four title lineages across 108 shows and 54 wrestlers, and staging gimmick-heavy stipulation matches — an Oatmeal Death Match, a Falls-Count-Anywhere-in-the-Mall match — under its own self-aware, comedy-first house style. Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

The NANet Professional Wrestling Association began in late 1993 on a Toronto-area BBS under the name NWC, renaming itself after a Las Vegas promotion laid claim to the same letters. It billed itself, in the same bumper on broadcast after broadcast, as “the revolutionary force in electronic sports entertainment since 1993,” and ran under a four-person writing committee after an egalitarian power-sharing arrangement replaced its earlier single-fedhead model — one of its own writers, describing a cycle of burnout and recovery common to the format, credited that committee and the friends who served on it with saving the league from folding more than once.

Its house style leaned hard into self-aware comedy. Its Oatmeal Death Match was born, by its own writers' telling, out of a cooking segment gone wrong: an arrogant newcomer named Laser shoved “Breakfast” Bobby Toast face-first into a bowl of oatmeal on-air, and the promotion built an entire stipulation around it — a tub the size of a ring, filled with oatmeal, the only way to win being to drown your opponent in it. A later card's Falls-Count-Anywhere-In-The-Mall match piledrove a World Champion down an escalator. Its weekly “Cranium Cracker of the Week” trivia segment tracked its own title history back to its first World Champion, crowned 1 September 1993, treating its own record as something worth quizzing the audience on.

Three weekly series carried the bulk of its output — Thursday Night Thrashings, Monday Night Mayhem and Saturday Night Nightmare — alongside an annual calendar of named pay-per-views. April Assault 1997 staged what its own broadcast called wrestling's first bicontinental pay-per-view, running matches live from Las Vegas and London on a split screen the same night; Hell On Earth, its own Halloween tradition, leaned into a full supernatural gimmick each year, staging a stable called the Cult against “the forces of Heaven and Hell” and dragging a masked wrestler's own reveal into its horror-movie framing.

Four championships anchored the roster — the World Heavyweight, North American, Television and World Tag Team titles — and its biggest matches were staged with genuine stakes: its Xmas Gory 1997 main event billed “Superstar” Steve Sampson against “the Wrecker” Mick McGreggor for the World title under a stipulation with no rules, no disqualifications, no countouts, decided only when one man could not continue.

No closing statement of the promotion's own survives. A newsgroup exchange in late August 1999 finds fans and a former writer still waiting on a final pay-per-view the NPWA had promised and never delivered; a December 1999 post from that same former writer recalls the league as already finished, its last card never having appeared.
