National Wrestling Council — Wrestler History

"Eric Rage's History"

24 May 1998
Content noticeA period document, served as recovered; withheld details are marked. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
http://members.tripod.com/~nwcouncil/ RUTHLESS, FEARLESS, REMORSELESS: The Story of Eric Rage Name: Eric Rage Wrestling Experience: 3 years Height: 6'2 Weight: 239 pounds Age: 24 Partners: Teamed with Magnum SB for most of his career to form The Regulators Manager: Aja (formerly) Titles Held: CWF Tag Team Championship (with Magnum SB), CCW Canadian Heavyweight Champion Ring Music: "Back in Black" by AC/DC Few sports have changed as dramatically during the past decade as the mat game. Ten years ago, wrestling heroes were as wholesome as mom and apple pie. The "bad guys" were booed as they lied, cheated and stole, while the "Real Americans" and "American Dreams" always managed to persevere in the ring and score another win for the forces of justice. All that has changed. Barbed-wire matches and chair shots to the head are now the norm. The "bad guys" are as popular as the "good guys", while the sport's heroes operate outside the law as often as its villians. Welcome to late '90s; the era of the bad ass. So maybe you can credit timing as much as anything else for Eric Rage's rocket ride to stardom. In an era when the fans demand carnage, this young superstar delivers it by the ton. Rage's cockiness, fearlessness and unabashed passion for extreme violence make him the prototype hero for the new millenium. Rage has catapaulted into the spotlight in a relatively short time, but with many stops and detours along the way. In this exclusive look into Rage's past, we'll document how this violent superstar has gone from mid-card status to one of the National Wrestling Council's most popular superstars in just a little over a year and where he may be headed next. Rage displayed incredible natural athletic ability as a teenager in Southern California's San Fernando Valley, excelling in his two passions; football and martial arts. His combination of razor-sharp instincts, superior speed and strength plus his unbreakable will and unparalleled work ethic helped him earn Superprep Magazine 1st Team Honors at Middle Linebacker, black belts in both judo and jujitsu and four consecutive United States Junior Judo Championships in his weight class. At just a little over six-feet tall and barely 200 pounds, most major football programs deemed him too small to play linebacker at the Division I level, despite his on-field accomplishments. Instead of accepting one of the number of Divison II scholarships offered him, he opted for the junior college ranks, where he earned consecutive First Team All-American honors in two years at Santa Monica City College. Adding another 15 pounds to his frame, Rage recieved interest from a few middle-tier Divison I schools, but once again chose the path less travelled. Rage took advantage of a study-abroad program in 1993 to spend a semester studying in Tokyo, Japan, where his extensive martial arts background and athleticism caught the attention of Master Hayabusa. Hayabusa, renowned for decades as the trainer of Japan's top wrestlers and shootfighters in one of the toughest and most exclusive dojos the world over, saw a diamond in the rough. Rage possessed the potential to be Hayabusa's most accomplished student ever if only he could curb Rage's bloodlust and hare-trigger temper. A one-semester stay overseas turned into two years as Rage became the first gaijin (Westerner) to train at the Hayabusa Dojo. "Master Hayabusa was a helluva lot harder on me than he was on any three of his other students," Rage said. "But lookin' back on it, I'm grateful. If he hadn't been so tough on me I wouldn't be the competitor I am today." Named "Rage Warrior" by Master Hayabusa, he made his wrestling debut with the All Japan promotion in January 1995. He became an instant sensation overseas, combining his martial arts background and natural agility into a hybrid style featuring a variety of suplexes and suicidal high-risk maneuvers. Rage failed to win a title during his year-long run with New Japan (His only two title matches ended in double disqualifications), but became one of the most country's most popular wrestlers. Longing for home, Rage sought work in the States, combining his real name with his given name to become Eric Rage. He signed with the fledgling Cyber Wrestling Federation in late 1996 where he was once again an instant success, enjoying an undefeated run in a handful of midcard matches and dazzling fans with his high-flying arsenal. His hybrid-style led to unpredictable matches, with Rage finishing his opponents with either the Ragemission, a kata-hajime submission hold, or his Terminal Velocity summersault leg drop. "Them early days in the CWF were a blast," he said. "I didn't get paid jack, we wrestled in arenas the size of my backyard and the only place they showed us was on Spanish cable at three in the mornin', but I had some damn good matches there." It was here that Rage first caught the eye of "The Canadian Crippler" James Naismith, whose Midnight Warriors stable was running roughshod over the federation. Naismith took exception to Rage's cockiness, for a wrestler who had barely wet his feet in North America. "Here I am, wrestlin' in America for the first time ever, and I come out and basically tell everyone in the CWF that I own their asses," Rage said. "Well, Naismith practically owned the company back then and he didn't like that too much. "So I did what I still do today whenever some moron gets in my business; I punked his ass," he said. This war of words culminated in Rage's interference in a title match between Kerry Smith and Naismith's top wrestler, CWF Television Champion Lothar of the Hill People. Rage's attack cost Lothar his belt, and further raised The Crippler's ire. Full-time contract talks between Rage and the CWF dissolved before Naismith could seek his revenge, but this wasn't the last time their paths would cross. During Rage's run overseas and in the CWF, his childhood friend, Magnum SB, had been making waves in small independents throughout the West. The 6'7", nearly-300 pound powerhouse usually dominated his opponents before finishing them with his vicious .357 Powerbomb. With Rage's short CWF contract expiring and the two youngsters seeking to work together, a new tag team was born. Wrestling as the Regulators, Rage and Magnum dominated the tag-scene in independents throughout the New York Tri-State area. It was during this run that the Regulators made a rare trip into the South, where Rage paid a visit to Frank Atlas' Dungeon, seeking to pick-up a few tips from the historic trainer of champions. It was here that Rage met Amber Jane Anderson, the only woman ever to train at the Dungeon. Atlas had been very reluctant to allow a female into the ring, but couldn't refuse after watching the lean and powerful 5'11", 185-pound Anderson dominate every man her size. "I show up at the Dungeon, and I see this buff chick handin' out beat-downs left and right," Rage said. "Needless to say, this was someone I wanted with me when I went to war." Anderson and Rage became fast friends and, shortly thereafter, partners. Combining her initials into a new handle, Aja, the amazon began watching the Regulators' backs from ringside, never afraid to intervene on her partners' behalf no matter who stood in her way. Word of the dynamic young trio's success spread quickly, and talks between the Regulators and the CWF, now one of the country's largest and most powerful companies, began in the summer of 1997. In front of a large national audience for the first time, success came slowly to the Regulators, particularly to Eric Rage. While continuing to improve his ring skill and becoming a fan-favorite with his fearless attitude, Rage was one of the few middleweights in a federation dominated by 300+ pound bohemiaths. But the Regulators eventually found their legs, putting together a string of victories over the CWF's top teams, including a pair of wins over The Assassins (Rockshade and Mr. Sinister). Their success caught the eye of then CWF executive [name withheld], whose renegade Schuk Foundation stable had done everything possible to take-over the promotion in the name of their power-hungry manager. Schuk issued a special challenge to the Regulators: If the team could defeat two of Schuk's three top tag teams in one night they would become the Number One Contenders to the tag titles and get Schuk in the ring for five minutes following the match. If they lost, they would become Schuk Foundation property for a month. Grabbing their first chance at stardom, the upstarts jumped at the offer in what would prove to be one of the wildest cards in the CWF's history. "This doughboy Schuk says if we can beat two of his three top tag teams in one night we'll be the number one contenders and get to beat his ass for five minutes in the ring following our matches," Rage said. "The catch was, if we lost we'd become his property for the next month. Needless to say, we jumped at the challenge." An impossible situation got even tougher when the Regulators dropped the first match of the challenge to Ray Pierce and Lothar after Rage was levelled outside the ring by "Hollywood" Tony Pride, with whom Rage had been feuding for weeks. Seeing their long-awaited chance at stardom slipping through their grasp, the Regulators rallied to beat the Stone Cold Killers, then put down the Sex Express (with CWF commentator and cult hero Frank Barr joining the Regs in their corner) to answer Schuk's challenge and become the Number One Contenders. Rage and Magnum capitalized on their opportunity, shortly thereafter winning the CWF Titles from Ray Pierce and Lothar of the Hill People with an assist from the enigmatic Black Mask. (Who was later revealed to be Pierce himself.) Later that same night, Rage and Magnum were forced to fight each other in a cage match during the CWF's Highway to Hell promotion, which pitted partner against partner in a series of matches. The hour-long bloodbath, later regarded by some wrestling publications to be the match of the year, saw both wrestlers fall at the same time, with Magnum dragging himself to his feet at the ten count to get the "W" while Rage's grip slipped from the top rope. Winning the tag team titles and fighting in the match of the year in the same night put the Regulators on the map, but lasting success would elude them once again. The Regulators dropped the belts to Cold Darkness in their first title defense and never returned to the Number One Contender position again. Frustrated by the highly-political, "boy's club" attitude he perceived to be running rampant in the CWF front-office, plus his team's inability to regain the titles in a series of matches with Cold Darkness, Rage sought a way out of his CWF contract, disappearing from the ring for nearly a month. "My grandfather taught me not to speak ill of the dead, and those clowns who ran the CWF were all dead from the neck up," Rage said. "I ain't namin' anyone, but they clearly had their favorites there and pushed them straight to the top while puttin' my partners and I in a bunch of no-win situations. "It's like this: The CWF is gone, and Eric Rage is stronger than ever. You do the math on who was right," he said. The Regulators re-emerged alongside James Naismith, the man with whom Rage feuded during his first CWF stint, as part of Naismith's new stable, the Wrecking Crew, to put a stop to Ray Pierce and his new faction, the Elite. "Man, I owe as much to the Crippler as I do to the man who trained me in the sport," Rage said. "Master Hayabusa taught me how to wrestle, but James Naismith taught me how to win." With fellow Crew members Lothar and Horace Hell taking the CWF Tag Team titles, and fellow Crew member Jon Atlas taking the CWF World Title, Rage and Magnum enjoyed highly successful runs in singles matches, before combining again to defeat the upstart tag team G-Force at the CWF's final PPV in December 1997, as the federation folded shortly thereafter. Injuries forced Naismith into early retirement around the same time as the CWF's demise. The Crippler remained in the wrestling business, heading up Canadian Championship Wrestling in late 1997, an affiliate of the newly-formed NWC. The Regulators signed onto to their old partner's company, expecting to dominate the NWC tag team scene. Fate, however, had other plans for Eric Rage. The CCW Champion would be crowned during the four-day long Onslaught in Ontario, Day Three of which featured a Royal Rumble-type Battle Royal to determine who would go on to wrestle for the belt at Day Four. Rage and "Iron Man" Tex Steel began the match as the first two men in the ring, and forty-five minutes later would prove to be the last two standing after eliminating every wrestler in the CCW. With a key save by Magnum SB, Rage would survive a vicious chair-shot to the head and two minutes in Steel's rolling knee-bar submission to eliminate The Iron Man and move on to Day Four, where he would be crowned the first-ever Canadian Championship Wrestling Heavyweight Champion with a victory over the Canadian high-flyer, Saerin Aurora. "Except for Magnum, Tex Steel is the toughest bastard I ever stood jaw-to-jaw with," Rage said. "I don't got respect for many people in this business, but The Iron Man has earned my respect forever." Both Rage and Magnum enjoyed undefeated runs in CCW before Naismith disappeared under mysterious circumstances and the NWC-affiliate folded. CCW later emerged in Virginia under new management and minus the Regulators who, like Naismith, disappeared without a trace. Fast forward to the present. Following a fall from grace, and unable to find work due to his disruptive presence in the locker room, Ray Pierce found himself in San Antonio, Texas seeking work with the highly-successful Lone Star Wrestling. He scheduled an interview with an unreceptive David Cooper, who begrudgingly agreed to sign Pierce under the condition that he help shore up the depleted tag ranks of the LSW with a partner of Cooper's choosing. Pierce accepted the deal, and brought in cagey veteran "Terrible" Jay Turner, last seen in the CCW as that region's tag champ. Throughout LSW's "Faceoff at High Noon" pay-per-view, Cooper was seen coming and going from a room backstage where he was in negotiations with a "major superstar." As Pierce and Turner's first major match began, Cooper flew down to ringside clutching a signed contract, claiming Turner was never under LSW contract and, more importantly, never meant to be Pierce's partner. Cooper introduced his choice for Pierce's partner as "Back in Black" blasted throughout the arena and Rage burst through the curtain. Pierce fled in fear as Rage dropped Turner with his dreaded RageDriver, a combination vertical-suplex/piledriver, then locked in the Ragemission to the delight of the shocked LSW crowd. Rage had changed dramatically during his ring absence, adding around 15 hard-earned pounds and almost exclusively featuring his ground game and the Ragemission. Rage now appears to be going solo, minus Magnum SB and Aja and refusing to divulge the whereabouts of either. The unlikely tag team of Rage and Pierce has proved to be, if nothing else, interesting. Rage is a fearless warrior who lives to fight. Pierce is a manipulative coward who seeks to have others do his dirty work while tying to hog all the glory. Pierce has tried on several attempts to control or eliminate Rage, even recruiting LSW tag-team Pain and Agony on a couple of occasions to attack Rage in the locker room. "This little bitch has dropped everythin' short of a house on my ass, but the fact is I ain't goin' anywhere and there ain't a damn thing his worthless little punk-ass can do about it," Rage said. With all of the LSW's top contenders locked in The Road to Gold tournament, culminating in a new LSW Heavyweight Champion at the Hangover in Houston pay-per-view, Rage has promised to give Pierce a "free ride" to the finals so he could win the LSW strap by defeating Pierce. While rolling over his own first and second-round opponents, Rage has made good on his promise by knocking Pierce's opponents unconscious in the tournament's first two rounds before dropping Pierce himself. All this while the two bickering superstars are still wrestling as a dysfunctional tag team. "I came to the LSW for one reason: to win every single strap I could get my hands on," Rage said. "But now I got a new reason, winnin' every single strap I can get my hands on while kickin' Ray Pierce's ass all across Texas." What's next for Eric Rage? He now has the size and muscle to back up the ground game he's been perfecting for years, plus over a year of North American ring experience under his belt. By moving away from the extreme style he featured in the CWF and CCW, Rage will most likely eliminate the majority of injures that often have put him on the shelf during his young career. Ask Rage and he will tell you nothing short of success lays ahead, and nobody has stepped forward to disagree. "I haven't gone through all I have the past few years to fail now," Rage said. "Love me or hate me, you know I'm a man of my word. I promised to rule the LSW before takin' the NWC Heavyweight Title, and that's exactly what Eric Rage is gonna do."
