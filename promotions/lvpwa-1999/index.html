A Las Vegas-set federation opened in the autumn of 1999 behind its own operator as an on-screen commentator, closed within a year, then reopened in January 2001 under its founding president. Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

The Las Vegas Pro-Wrestling Association's handbook, dated 29 September 1999, stages its opening as one man's story: Sebastian T. Myerson, in his early twenties, booked and wrestled for an independent California promotion before creative differences sent him to Nevada, where he got his promoter's license and “decided to open up his promotion in the City of Sin.” The handbook set out a five-title ladder (World, North American, Las Vegas, Featherweight, and World Tag Team, with a Women's title “possibly” to follow), a weekly flagship, Heavy Metal Monday, and five pay-per-views planned across the year.

Its surviving record jumps almost a year, to a pay-per-view, Road to the Gold, taped 27 August 2000 from the Pontiac Silverdome in Detroit. Alex “The Hammer” Cote won the Las Vegas title from champion Brian Taylor in a three-way that ended with Taylor's knee caught in the ropes; a battle royal crowned “Rebel” Ricky Price the first Las Vegas Death Match Champion; and a vacant tag match crowned Brothers in Arms as new champions.

“Sensuous” Samantha retained the Women's title against Katrina Leigh, then survived a post-match beatdown by a debuting Devin DeMasters, before the card turned to its own founder: Sebastian Myerson, now the Association's Vice President, stepped into the ring himself against World Champion LOCO in a career-versus-title match, having spent months, in the broadcast's own account, “secretly wreaking havoc” against LOCO through a court-ordered ban from the promotion's arenas and television. LOCO won it after referee James Pillow turned on Myerson at the finish, ending the storyline that had driven the card.

The same pay-per-view also crowned “Kool Moe” Dee Arlinghaus and The Masked Crip winners of a three-way tag opener over Maximum Risk 2000 and Hot House, and closed a Featherweight title match between the Mad Goat and Wolf that both broadcast voices called one of the greatest in its history, the Mad Goat retaining after both men had traded a tombstone and a low blow to survive to the finish.

How Heavy Metal Monday and the pay-per-view schedule its handbook announced played out in the year between the two documents isn't in the surviving record; a second tag title, the handbook's own Women's title question, are likewise not addressed in what survives. The broadcast ends, cast intact, promising a return to Heavy Metal Monday with no sign of closing.
