THIS EDITION: Part four: "Not Exactly Sushi and Noodles" ============================================ I love Japan. I'll make no secrets about it. I've seen my best times and my worst times there, and with education being in the state it is in in the USA.
I'd like my kids to grow up there too. Aki speaks better Japanese than
English,
and I'm sure little Mariko will too when she is old enough to talk.
Japan is the place that made me what I am, on different levels. On the obvious side of things, it was the place that saw me transform from being a sane - if slightly unstable - human being into the man I am today. No frills, no bullshit, no gimmicks... I got beat down, and every day when I open my eyes I feel the consequences. But beneath the obvious, the laws and morals of a great country made me the family man that I am now. The hard work I was forced to go through in order to make something of myself drove me on to be successful when
I
returned to the USA from injury. And hey, I've got a percentage share on the videos and DVDs that a lot of fans have seen of me in some insane matches out there. Not a great share, but a share nonetheless.
Now, in the eyes of the law, I'm a nut. Wacko. An utter psycho. I have several issues with my mental health. When you see me on TV, I'm smiling one moment, and screaming the next... well, the joy of writing is that you don't know if I'm smiling my sick smile or acting totally normal as I'm pouring my heart out onto paper. I have suffered severe trauma to my head, and my injuries have left me with a few long term problems: I have Attention Deficit
Disorder,
a mild form of schizophrenia that leads to my mood swings. I hear voices, sure.
I suffer from Obsessive Compulsive Disorder, although my Mother swears that I had that before anyway. I can be forced to not wrestle because of blinding headaches. My memory has suffered since my injury. I can't drive, as
USA laws
suggest... because the four inch steel plate in my skull classifies me as too dangerous to be on the road. And yes, I suffer from depression too...
a side effect of a combination of things; A horrific injury, being away from my wife and kids for long periods of time, and drinking for most of my adult life. All in all, I'm a bit f*cked up in the head.
And Japan is where it all went wrong... or right, depending on how you view things. From a career standpoint, I'm almost glad that I took that beating in the Eggdome, because face it... not only did I need it, it made me what I am today. Sure, you wouldn't want me to baby-sit, but I'll do as much crazy sh*t in a wrestling ring as my legs will allow. We'll come to the events of that fateful night on February 12th 1999 a little later on.
Japan at first was a downer for me. Not speaking the language, leaving my family back at home. I'd say that I had very few friends that I left behind back then... just my Mom and Dad, and Damien. I would phone them from time to time and listen to my Dad ramble on about how much he hated the
Baltimore Ravens for taking the franchise from the Browns, or talk about the state of wrestling back home, or whatever. My Mom would just talk about how much she missed me. I can tell my kids that I miss them, but my Dad never could... I guess that's the difference between generations. Damien would just talk to me about wrestling, and was always obsessed with my matches. I think he kept a notebook filled with the details that I gave him, even as he got older and started hanging around with girls a lot more. I was starting to worry about him... but when I saw him on a trip home with the hottest damn seventeen year old girl in
Cleveland, I was proud. Hell, I never got the girl. My first tour of Japan was nasty. I think I wrestled in front of about 1,000 people... in my three weeks on the road. Combined. We didn't go to the big cities, instead we went to Hokkaido, the northern island. Hokkaido is cold, desolate and not exactly a hotbed of activity. Many areas are almost backwards
- even when compared to Alabama. The main city - Sapporo - is very nice, but when the primary export of a city is beer, and you've got me visiting... bad news. Truthfully, I drank very little on my first tour. I didn't want to get fired, although I found that the buzz ripping through the crowd when I did something insane was worth getting disqualified for. The fans, no matter how little showed up, respected me and enjoyed watching me. Slowly, I think I lost my cynicism. In front of Indy fans back home, I was cheating for the sake of it. The only way to liven up a match was to hit a guy across the temple with a steel chair, or ring his bell with a set of brass knuckles. Here, I could wrestle - even the big guys were the same size as me, and I found my skills improving for the first time. But boy, did they want the rough stuff from me.
They needed some bloodshed when I was around, and that time I posed in
Texas
wearing the crimson mask for the Japanese paparazzi seemed very worthwhile.
To get around Visa regulations, I would work one tour of three weeks, and then go home for a week to rest. As my offence became more high impact, and the opposition I met was tougher and tougher, I needed the rest. Besides,
I was
racking up those frequent flyer miles like they were going out of fashion. Hey, gotta love free flights. But instead of taking myself away on holiday,
I would
keep getting on a plane top the Orient the very second that Bobby called me.
The fatman was enjoying his life as a faux successful businessman, and without me he had no means of support. He had long since tired of his previous stable of wrestlers, and was no longer accompanying me to the ring... in fact, he was just like an agent instead of a manager.
Without him barking orders at me from ringside, I started trying different things. I would watch the other guys pull off some great moves, and decided to steal as many as possible. In a show in Kyoto in front of about 3,000 people, I tried my first moonsault. I slipped and landed badly on my arm, breaking my wristbone. Since then, I've hit the moonsault about a thousand times or more...
and broke that same bone another two times. I debuted all of my signature moves in Japan, too... people have seen me hit my Stemkiller corkscrew kamikaze headbutt a zillion times since... off cages, balconies, whatever...
but I debuted that in a PPV show in Osaka, just off the top rope to the inside. The Living End was a Japanese invention that I'll cover later on... and my
Spikebuster was blatantly stolen by me from a colleague at the time.
To make things worse, I beat him with it... but then it was still just a Top
Rope Rocker Dropper, because I was still Jimmy Barnett. Spikyjim was my evolution. If Spikyjim walks tall, then Jimmy Barnett was the ape that evolved. It's just a nickname, but one I prefer to my real name nowadays. It's my status symbol when young guys come up to me at shows and call me "Spikyjim". Only four people call me "Jimmy": Mom, Dad, Damien and
Keiko.
The rest call me Spiky, Spikes... or Sir. One of those is a blatant lie. Here's how it came about. I'd been dressing how I wanted to the ring, got a few tattoos and generally let it all hang out. I still loved music too, spending many a drunken night listening to the Clash, the Damned, Nirvana,
Mudhoney, the
Buzzcocks, Radiohead, Joy Division... I never got into metal, but I had been into punk since I was a kid. Whether it was my sister calling me a punk when I was 6 that turned me that way, I'll never know. I cut my hair and spiked it up... and the Japanese wrestling press out there loved it. A relic of
New York
or London in 1977... for some reason, that really floated their boat.
I saw a headline one day that read "Jimmy - Spiky!" in English, probably just because the words looked cool on the front of the magazine. I loved that headline, and changed my name. At first I was Jimmy "Spikyjim" Barnett, but it only took a few months to just become Spikyjim. I started dying my hair too, and those kids went nuts. Insanity.
My stock was rising. I had started to get a reputation as one of the better Gaijin workers out there when disaster struck. Bobby wanted to be ringside with me again. Within two months, his lust to be seen with me on TV combined with my renewed drinking meant that any chance of a shot at the big time had evaporated for a while, and we had to make do with being big fish in the small pond of
Hardcore Garbage Wrestling. Called garbage by old school wrestling fans, I've got to say that I don't prefer it over being able to let my talent out, but I'm probably better at being a dirty fighter than I am a wrestler. The more insane the match, the bigger the payoff Bobby got. I was hitting the bottle really heavily by now, and I guess it was a pain reliever in advance - I'd swig down whiskey before a Electrified Barbed Wire match to dull the pain that I knew I was going to get. Back then, I wasn't fearless because I was crazy, I was fearless because I was drunk.
I was in some ludicrous matches that I can barely remember, such was the combination of pain and alcohol. I was in a match that involved an exploding scaffold... the poor sucker that I beat was probably blown into the next week.
I was in a swimming pool death match, where the goddamn pool exploded once somebody was forced into it - luckily, not me. I'm allergic to chlorine. And then there was the sickest match of all - the barbed wire, broken glass rubbing alcohol deathmatch. I kid you not. For the entertainment of around a thousand lucky people in Yomiyuri, you could watch me and my now-buddy Danny
Graham bleed
a lot, and then scream as one tossed the other into a kiddie pool of rubbing alcohol... ironic that even then, alcohol could cause me so much pain.
That night was the first night that I wrestled that man-mountain Danny
Graham, or Double N as we liked to call him (Standing for "No Nonsense"). He was a bluff, forthright Texan, but started to pull me to one side in the locker rooms and warn me about how crazy I had become. I liked him, and as I was starting to sense the greediness of Bobby, I started to listen... at least when I wasn't as drunk as a skunk.
But I stuck with Bobby, even though I noticed him getting money, and me getting an allowance. The end was near for us... and the moment that swayed it all was a match in the blistering heat of a Tokyo summer. A fire death match.
Fire surrounded the ring, and I pulled all kinds of crazy sh*t in there. Truth is, my blood alcohol level was so damn high, I was more flammable than the kerosene that surrounded the ring. I was damn drunk that night, and it was the first night that I pulled a stunt that I've become almost known for now...
the flaming chair fireball. My opponent has a flaming chair, or bat or whatever... and I spit kerosene at him, igniting him. Fun, huh? Well, I had heard that tramps and hoboes drank paraffin, so I thought I would try kerosene... I took a mouthful of it, but it tasted so damn horrible (and poisonous) that I spat it out... onto my unwilling opponent, who was engulfed in flames. I then powerbombed him onto a flaming table, held him down in the flames and got the pin.
Luckily for him, he was wearing full overalls that covered his body, and had worn protection underneath. He was doused, and walked to the back with a little help. I was wearing a T-Shirt. My arms got burned very badly, to the point that I collapsed and the next thing I knew, I was instantly sober, waking up in a white hospital room with a seriously cute nurse standing over me.
Her badge said "Keiko Watanabe". My future wife. Doesn't romance strike in the funniest of places? I had the courage to ask her out as I was discharged from the burns unit a few days later. She said yes (Obviously scarred, tattooed, blue haired freaks turn her on) and I spent all my time out of the ring with her. I was honest about my drinking, and instead of being all judgemental like previous American girlfriends, she started to talk to me about it. She was understanding. Her family had been in wrestling for a long time (sheer coincidence), and her father had been an alcoholic. Her Dad got out of things through his Buddhist faith, and the more she told me about it, the more I became interested. At first I was a unfamiliar, somewhat comical face at her local temple, but I came and went as
I pleased, and religion became an important, if small part of my life. So, for a while at least (until my return to Japan this year which was dealt with in the opening section of this book) I was sober. And life got better. I had a girlfriend who I adored, and even a couple of friends on the tours I undertook. I helped to run things a little in Danny's federation, and
I was
making good money that Danny paid directly to me, not through Bobby.
Bobby hated Danny, and Danny knew Bobby well. Danny never poisoned my mind against Bobby, he merely passed on the facts to me. But I had enough of that money grabbing bastard, and when Bobby told me that he wanted me to date more "white chicks", I took that as a swipe at Keiko, for getting me off the booze and making me see clearly. I tried to part company with Bobby by asking him nicely, but it all ended with him hitting me, and me knocking him out.
Relationship dissolved. Or so I thought. I learned even more in the ring. Keiko's eldest brother ran a wrestling school, and I studied there for a while. The style I learned there was stiff, technical
Japanese wrestling. I use it from time to time in the USA, but not as much as I would like to when guys are always bringing chairs to the ring. I just have to outdo them in the craziness stakes, see. Keiko's brothers - Eldest
Hiroshi, one
in the middle Junichi and youngest (editors note: recently deceased)
Yuji were all excellent wrestlers. Junichi was a little unstable and crazy - maybe the Japanese version of me - and we feuded back in IWC, when he wrestled under a mask with the name "The Great Mishima" owing to the fact that Japanese guys can't get over in the USA without one hell of a gimmick. We've since shook hands. Yuji was a great talent back then, but he's always been kinda small...
and yet he could suplex me out of my boots. But Hiroshi, he taught me all the cool stuff that I know. He taught me about using my centre of gravity and I found that I could lift guys that I couldn't lift before. Danny is a near seven foot 300 pounder, but I hit him with an Exploder suplex once - a move that was so shocking coming from me that he exclaimed "Good Lord! The kid learned how to wrestle!". Hiroshi was older than me, and a lot wiser. He had retired through injury, and he unlocked the glory that is The Living End. It took me about 6 months to get it right, and only Hiroshi could see what he was trying to do in his mind. Truth is, the move seemed awesome in practice, and when I put an opponent in hospital with it's first use, I was totally hooked. I didn't use it for a while in the USA because the insurers got on my back (more to its danger to me, not others, ironically)... but now I use it everywhere. Hiroshi knows I owe him for that.
With the Living End and a whole new bag of tricks in my arsenal, and no Bobby on my back or booze in my blood, I was rejuvenated. Sure, I had beat down some guys really bad before, and I had done a whole lot of sick sh* in my first four years in Japan. But my final year was my most successful, and was
"final"
through no real choice of my own. On a personal level, success was everywhere. I got married, and Keiko eventually gave birth to Aki, my fantastic little son. You know I'm glossing over a few details there and all, but if you don't know where babies come from, you ain't 18... and you know that everything I do is rated "R" at the very least. That meant that I didn't have to travel home quite as much, because through marriage I became a Japanese citizen. I could focus, train and tour constantly.
I was still in the lower echelons of the wrestling world, but I had one hell of a streak going. I was getting even more attention than I did during my initial few tours, and the phone started ringing off the hook and our little
Tokyo
apartment. A few offers from the USA... nope, I wasn't going there. I did visit Mexico and Europe twice each for a few shows, because it paid for our holidays to Acapulco, London and Venice. But the best calls came from the big guns in Japan. Tokyo based, wrestling in front of tens of thousands every week.
You got in there on a tight roster, you had it made. My combination of a hardcore style with some technical knowledge got them interested, and
Danny did
the rest. His name had weight behind it in Japan, and he let me go from his tours to go work for the big guns.
The guys I wrestled had been training since they were 14 years old, and they were tough. Honourable, but so damn difficult to put away. But the audience was changing... the old school fans who hated Garbage wrestling had faded into the background, and a combination of Puro and Garbage was now OK. I got hardcore matches. I was softening guys up with creative chairshots before hitting them with The Living End. I was on a roll, carried on by adrenaline. I was beating better guys than me, and a title reign in one of the world's biggest organisations was just around the corner.
On February the 12th 1999, I was booked for a semi-final match in the annual J-Cup Lightweight Tournament. I had won my first two matches, and was excited about the prospect of being in the final. I left my apartment, kissed my wife and son, and took the train to the Tokyo Eggdome. Signed a few autographs, and then went to the locker room area. I meditated for a while, called
Keiko, and
then prepared for my match. I drank some green tea, ate a donut, took a whizz and went out there to do my thing.
It was going well. The crowd were pumped, and my opponent (The mask - wearing Mexican, Los Perro Aguesz) was wrestling as well as me. I was caught by him, and he tried for a DDT... but I kneed him in the gut, grabbed his head and in a blur of flashbulbs, somersaulted up and off the ropes and hit The
Living End. I
turned around to pin him... and CRACK.
Somebody put me down with a shot to the head from something. I tried to get up, but someone hit me again, in the back of the head. I squinted up through the ring lights and saw darkened figures, wearing masks... they kept hitting me. At first I tried to fight back, but then I just rolled into a ball all the noise of the crowd became quieter and quieter, and the light I saw through my closed eyes faded away.
I've seen the tape since. Twenty guys hit that ring and beat the hell out of me. One particularly small guy was the one who fractured my skull with his baseball bat; You can see the guys around him wince as he hits me. By the time they leave the ring, chased away by Danny, the referee, Los Perro
Aguesz and a
few kids from the back, I was a mess. I have never bled so much in my life. I was bleeding from my eyes, ears and mouth. I didn't wake up for three days. As they started hitting me, I thought that it was payback from all those guys that
I've messed up through the years, but it wasn't. Most of the guys that
I've
messed up have been in touch since my comeback, normally to congratulate me on still being alive. The ones I put out permanently... well, part of me feels bad there, but they signed the contract. If thats a little cold hearted, please excuse this busted up son of a gun. Nope, what attacked me was a bunch of hired goons, street thugs that Bobby hired to smack me around. If a wrestler wants to deal with me... anytime, anyplace, sign the contract. No wrestler would stoop to that. But criminals? Bobby showed them the colour of his money...
because the smart bastard had a policy on me that stated he could receive compensation if I was forced to retire through injury. Thank God he didn't take out a life insurance policy on me, because those goons did a good job. If they'd have been asked to kill, they would have.
I was in hospital for a total of seven months. In the first week I had surgery four times; Twice on my brain - to drain fluid from it, and then to fix the steel plate into my skull. Once on my knee, which was shattered by one blow;
and once on my back for a shattered vertebrae that had to be fused back together. I hardly spoke for three weeks, and suffered badly from
Amnesia. I
was still the same inside, but my mind had changed... as I started to get back on my feet, I suffered terribly from nightmares and psychosis. I would panic at the drop of a hat, and my mood could change at the snap of a finger.
After the seven months were up, I returned to the USA on Keiko's advice, because she was fearful of another "incident".
The first month back in the USA seemed to consist of me doing five hundred push ups every day, five hundred sit ups and then sleeping on my Mom and
Dad's sofa
with my baby in my arms and a tear in my eye as the room began to spin. I needed to work again. Spikyjim needed wrestling. I turned on the TV and watched a little of it.
