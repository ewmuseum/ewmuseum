RAW / WAR! Magazine Presents "Stitches" - part Three

3 December 2001
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Raw / War! Magazine is once again proud to present another instalment in the life story of Jimmy "Spikyjim" Barnett. Jimmy will be starting a promotional tour as soon as he returns from Japan - where, incidentally, a Japanese version of the book will be published early next year. Pre - orders for the book are still going well, so ensure that you reserve your copy of "Stitches" at your local bookstore, or online at
www.geocities.com/spikyjimsworld as soon as y'all can. This edition: Part Three: The First of the Independents. ================================ If wrestling in the big leagues, showing your talent on the pay per view or at a jam packed house show isn't glamorous - and believe me, it isn't - then how about wrestling for the Independent promotions?
I've made a career out of it, talking about how I've enjoyed it and how I'm the "Last of the Independents". Newsflash... sadly, I don't care that much about the Indies. They pay badly, you work for assholes and the fans... well, they ain't all that. Makes me sound bitter and all, but I've hardly wrestled for an independent promotion since I came back from Japan. It's all a gimmick, decided on by my wife to prove how "For Real" I am. Heck, I've paid my dues, and they're paying me back. With me always being an underdog, the name kinda stuck - and now I get every two bit promoter in the Midwest trying to hook me up with a one off match in his bingo hall. Sadly, when you've wrestled in Japan, or in RAW, AVW, the Master of the Ring or for James Hall's late, great IWC... the prospect of entertaining twenty or so inbreds in Louisiana kinda loses its appeal.
But maybe I'm jaded now. After my debut, I had no choice but to wrestle in the Independent promotions. For a young single guy, they paid OK. I was too small to get any work in the bigger federations - hell, I was about 180 pounds at that stage - And of course, way too green. Hell, I won matches here and there, but I wasn't setting the world - or opponents - on fire just yet. On top of that, making me marketable was proving difficult. Gone were the days of good guys and bad guys. There I was, wrestling with a lame collegiate gimmick and expecting people to cheer me. They didn't do anything. You want a reaction from the crowd at least for those formative stages of your career... but I got nothing. In 1988, I'm guessing they would have cheered me... and maybe now the fans would have hated me... but back then in the mid nineties, wrestling fans were in limbo. Now of course, bad is good... and the fans cheer the hell out of me. Not that I act the way I do because of them, I act the way that I do because it works. Any T-Shirts I sell is a bonus.
If I tell the truth though, I stank back then. You may think I stink now, but hey... don't see you reading anybody else's book right now, do you? It's a sad reflection on my career in some ways when you realise that as a younger man, with no steel plate in my skull, no busted up knee, no bad back and a whole load less scars... I was a worse wrestler than I am now. I don't have any glory bygone days to look back on because even though this could be the twilight of my career, the last couple of years have been the glory days for me. I couldn't buy a win back then. I would hop in my Mom's car and travel all over the country, using either my rented place in Cleveland as a base, or my parents place in Jacksonville. I think I doubled the mileage of my Mom's car within about three months of having it on permanent loan.
And of course, I struggled financially. I gave up any jobs I had after a few weeks, because something kept calling me back to wrestling. Or rather, promoters started calling me back, and I was too nice to say no. The best job I quit was one I had for about three months, working as a legal clerk. Only when I had been in Japan for two years was I making more money than I was doing that job. I would travel around the country, earn my fifty to a hundred bucks or so for wrestling, and then crash out in my car for a while. Most guys would say that they stuck it out because they enjoyed the lifestyle, or that they knew they would make it. Not me. To this day, I don't know why
I stuck it out. I endured nearly freezing to death in the back of that car, snacking on hot dogs from Seven - Elevens, dreaming of winning the lotto. Never saw myself winning a belt. Never saw myself getting the buyrate for my employer. I was just miserable. Something kept drawing me back to wrestling, like a moth does to a light... but there I was, metaphorically bumping my head against the burning glass, flying in circles and getting nowhere.
Maybe I was trying to prove something to myself. I always pushed myself hard in my football "career". I didn't want to be known as the skinny kid who wasn't good at sports. I had some intelligence, granted... but something made me choose sports over studies, and as time progressed, wrestling over a potentially good career. My parents didn't help. They were in therapy for some of their own problems, and that made them way too understanding and caring, like little nineties models of political correctness personified. I wanted them to yell at me, or make it clear that they didn't want their son to be a wrestler.
But they didn't. Damn them, the bastards were supportive. They came to watch me. I'd lift my bloodied, beaten head off the mat and see my
Mom and Dad in the front row of whatever godforsaken hellhole we were in, clapping wildly and giving me the thumbs up. My Dad had started out by trying to warn me of the perils of wrestling... but now he was being a good guy again! Not being rebellious in the slightest back then, I guess I carried on because everybody else thought it was right for me. My parents got into it (and they're still into it), my kid brother got into it to the point that I trained him so he could turn pro himself, and even my sister got into it... mainly by dating wrestlers I didn't like but hey, at least she was involved.
Returning to my train of thought - and please, don't get used to it -
I sucked back then. I wrestled in Texas, Ohio, Florida, Pennsylvania,
Carolina and Georgia. Quite a trek. I'd work a few shows for each promoter before moving on when I realised that I couldn't cut it. The only place I ever managed to do anything was in my trainer's fed.
Mister Stannage kinda took me under his wing, and he taught me a lot. A lot of it wasn't good. He taught me how to wrestle... not good. Sure, he was a nice guy, but I think he looked at me in the ring and thought "Shit, that kid will never have it". So, I always got matches against the greenest of the green when I wrestled in Cleveland.
Seventeen year old burger flippers from McDonalds, you know the sort. Backyard wrestlers who want to do more. I'd kick their ass, and my self esteem would rise for just a second, until I realised that I'd just beaten down a guy that my sister could have taken out. Or possibly dated, I'm not sure.
I was still getting bookings, on account of my looks. Hard to believe, but I had that collegiate charm about me. Promoters would play up my football and amateur background, and it would always take them a few cards before they realised that the wool had been firmly pulled over their eyes. But one night in Corpus Christi, as I was suffering from flu and still wrestling, I met a guy that would change my life. When I say "change", I make it sound polite... so I'll change my wording. He turned my life around, sent me running for the finish post... and would keep smacking me with a ballbat just to keep me in touch.
That man was Bob Bradley. He added "Balderstein" after his two names to make him sound more businesslike, but his real name was Bob
Bradley. A fat, egotistical, loudmouthed, psychotic conman... and I'm being kind. But believe me, for a while at least, he made me a killer. Almost literally. I sat in a motel lobby drinking hot lemon after I had stank up yet another arena, when his huge mass slid onto the sofa next to me. My head nearly hit the ceiling as the weight shifted, and he had my attention.
"D'ya like what you're doin' boy?" He was menacing back then. He was bigger than me, and he was well known in the south. I was almost starstruck. He carried on: "Because I see a young man who ain't happy. I see a young man who ain't giving it his all. I see a young man who should be with me." He held out his hand and smiled a false smile. I shook his hand weakly. I was his now.
At first, Bobby's recommendations seemed like fun to me. He asked me if I liked my ring attire. I said no. So, he told me to wear whatever I liked. I lost my collegiate duds, and started to wrestle in combat shorts and whatever T-Shirt I could find. My favourite was my "I fought the law" Clash T-Shirt that I found in a charity store.
I never wore my own designs until I went to Japan. Bobby also told me to drop my amateur style. He even gave me some helpful training in his makeshift gym. Don't get any big ideas: The gym was a parking lot, and he blasted me with a steel chair three times. It hurt like hell, but then he made me focus on using it. He'd steal used mannequins from outside stores, and I'd blast them with chairshots until their heads caved in. He became the surrogate father from hell... but at the time, I saw him in a good light. I started getting a little more confident, and even won a few matches. Then a few more.
The more I wrestled, the more I cheated and the more I won. I wasn't suicidal in the ring, oh no. I was totally sane. I'd use a few moves here and there, but I'd just wait until I could use a pair of brass knuckles or a roll of quarters when the ref wasn't looking. Cold, calculated and just there to win. I eventually had a long winning streak, as I found my niche in wrestling: Being a low down dirty cheat.
But Bobby wasn't satisfied with that. After each win, he'd take me for a drink. Still wary of him, I would oblige. Now, I'd never been a big drinker in college, so I couldn't hold the pace. I started to get drunk after most matches. Then, Bobby would bring me a bottle of
Vodka or Whiskey to the locker room before my matches, to share a drink with me. Scared of getting hurt by the bigger guys that I was double crossing and bloodying with foreign objects, I'd always have my fair share before I went to the ring. He always used to raise a glass with me, but I don't think I ever saw him drink a drop. He knew what he was doing. Controlling me. The drink made me angrier too - and some would argue, pretty effective. I'd get to the stage where I wouldn't care who I hurt, illustrated in Pennsylvania one night when a fan grabbed Bobby, Bobby shouted me and I cracked the fan with a chair. That federation didn't take me back.
Drunken and surly, Bobby had me where he wanted me. I was earning good money, of which he took more than his fair share, leaving me enough for the occasional Wendy's special and a bottle of Jack. He was a pimp, and I was his bitch. If you watch tapes of me back then,
I would swing a chair at anyone, wild eyed and hungry for the pain of others through the beer goggles that I was permanently wearing. And if you look at Bobby, he's always rubbing his hands with glee, glad what his protégé had become. I could say he ruined me, but even though the first few years of my career are in a permanent haze, he did a few things to me that changed me for the better. I could claim to be a superhero, but I'm not. Everybody needs a helping hand, and I got mine. Shame I was to pay for it later on.
As I got more and more unruly in the ring - with Bobby's unhealthy encouragement - I started to do crazier things. By my standards today, not that bad... but then, you can understand why territories stopped calling me back. Pennsylvania had seen enough of me already when I tried to set a kid on fire in Georgia. Then, I pulled a fork out during a match in Charlotte and started to carve my opponent's head with it - as the mayor of the city looked on. Bye bye Carolina.
I hung a guy from the ropes until he went blue in Austin, Texas, and I was banished from another state. I wrestled one night in Florida under a mask to escape the bad press I was getting, but I pulled it off to get a better view of the guy who's ribs I was breaking with a crowbar. So long, sunshine state. And worst of all, my old employers in Ohio had seen enough of me when I brutalised Steve Stannage's son in the ring, and then saw it as a sane option to break Steve's jaw with my elbow as he ran in to tend to his son. I was becoming a monster. Steve trained me, but he won't take credit for me. He doesn't tend to talk about me much after that night, and I can't blame him.
My last match in the USA for five long years was in Portland. In front of a decent sized crowd, I won a hardcore rules matchup by torching a chair and hitting my opponent with it. I then pinned him as he lay on it, and after the bell as Bobby shouted me onwards, I
DDT'd the guy back on it. I guess he got burned pretty bad, but most guys knew what to expect by then. Through my hazy vision, I saw flashbulbs popping in the crowd as my arm was raised in victory.
Japanese photographers. Lots of them. Didn't know why, but I knew they digged blood, so I clenched my face to squeeze a few more drops out of my busted open forehead. More flashbulbs. I went back to the locker room for my customary post game drink - the buzz was wearing off from my pre-game drink - and Bobby came in with a sheet of paper, all flustered.
"Jimmy, they're saying they don't want you back here again." I shook my head. I wasn't exactly sad.
"So, where to next?" Knowing what I know now, I should have known the answer. Bobby sat next tom me, which was never a good sign.
"Jimmy, you got a girlfriend?" At first, I thought he wanted to make me a porn star. Drink does strange things to the mind. I shook my head. "See your folks much?". I shook my head again. "Got anything tying you to the USA?" Again, negative... but then I pointed to
Bobby.
"Just you, boss." He laughed a truly frightening laugh. "Then pack your bags, boy. We're going to Japan. We leave tomorrow, so say those goodbyes. This is gonna change your life!"
He slapped me on the back. He had no idea how right he was. =================================== COMING SOON - Part four: "Not exactly Sushi and Noodles" www.geocities.com/spikyjimsworld
