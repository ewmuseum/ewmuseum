A real-wrestler hardcore federation that opened New Year's Day 1997, kept a self-titled owner on camera through its own angles, and relaunched in November after a closure its own masthead blames on embezzlement. Written by AI, 2026

Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

Extreme Fantasy Wrestling opened Hardcore TV on Monday, 13 January 1997, and its own November retrospective credits that broadcast with starting “a war which would last for five months” between two factions, the Horsemen and the Crips. A community post defending the promotion's longevity that June states plainly that it “has been open since January 1, 1997 and continues to remain open to this day.” It ran real wrestlers' names under invented, roleplay-decided outcomes rather than a straight simulator feed — its own president, answering a reader who asked how a much smaller wrestler had beaten a giant for the World Title, wrote that in a booked promotion “when someone out-RP'd someone else, it just isn't fair for their size to determine the outcome.”

Its own president set the terms of what “extreme” meant to a reader asking how to judge the wave of feds now claiming the label: “The best way to find out if a fed is extreme is to read the cards,” he wrote, then pointed to the promotion's own record — a manager kidnapped by a stable and made a slave, a wrestler powerbombed on the shoulder of a highway, and a commentator beaten into a coma he did not survive, dying, by the fed's own later account, “of goat-inflicted injuries.” The same card culture staged what its own recruitment post called the first six-team, six-ring War Games in the hobby, run at the Omni in March. By June the promotion had stopped taking wrestler applications outright — “78 is quite enough” — and was hiring only for writers, its president explaining he had “pretty much written almost every EFW card myself” since the promotion opened.

Its second pay-per-view, Bloodlust, ran two thirty-man battle royals feeding into a match for a future title shot, alongside Dennis Rodman's wrestling debut against Rob Van Dam and the World Heavyweight Championship changing hands from Taz to the Giant in the main event. Its own retrospective credits that night with the Insane Clown Posse joining the promotion as its commentary team and with Diamond Dallas Page becoming “the only person in EFW history to win the US Heavyweight title twice,” beating Xanta Klaus for a belt he had lost after his own manager was kidnapped.

The promotion's own account of its ending, published at its relaunch, blames the close on an embezzlement scandal inside its own fiction, laid on its in-storyline president. On 14 June 1997 it held its last broadcast and cancelled the pay-per-view set for the next night, a card that would have unified the World and Television titles between Raven and the Undertaker. On 10 November 1997 the promotion announced it was returning, funded, by its own account, from its real owner's settlement of a lawsuit against a major wrestling promotion; the relaunch vacated the World Title Raven had won at its prior pay-per-view and grandfathered only four champions — the Undertaker, Rob Van Dam, Bastion Booger and Sabu — if their own handlers still wanted the roles.

Whether the relaunch actually ran isn't in the surviving record; its last surviving document is the announcement itself.
