Untitled

21 Oct 2005

By Ian Tense

Content noticeA period document, served as recovered; withheld details are marked. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
The thought continually ran through his head. how fragile life is . There really should be no reason for this to race through his mind. After all, Ian had given his whole life to the practice of detachment. He had learned early on that there was but one path to the top of anything and that path was cluttered with the bodies of friends and allies alike. So what was different now? Why this thought? Why now? Ian gingerly rubbed his eyes. It was a habit he picked up from his father. He always did it when he was nervous, or tired, or stessed. This was certainly one of those times. His mind had never been invaded by such an alien thought before. How Kafka-esque. he muttered underneath a sigh. The comparison was fair. Kafka made a career out of throughly confusing his readers by only giving them enough information to keep them confused, and under the circumstances, Ian was defintely confused. Ian slithered out of his chair and walked slowly across his office, dragging his fingers across the spines of his massive collection of books. He had once wondered how he made it through life with out becoming a librarian, but then remembered that he hated librarians, and more specifically, the public in general. Ian was a bit too...abrasive, for public servantry. There mere thought of it made him smirk like a madman as his fingers found the book spine it had searched so desperately for. The Trial, he said to no one in particular. This had become one of his favorite books back in college, mainly because the book really didn't seem to have a definitive beginning nor an end. It just seemed to happen , but at the same time, so much thought had went into making it seem that way. It was a paradox. Paradoxes interested Ian like nothing else. The ability to make something so organized look so chaotic. Ian was sure that Kafka was a genius. Every one of his stories were like that, and Ian always felt a sort of kindredship with that sort of organized chaos. He had, afterall, made a career out of it himself. But not in literature. His stories were told in a much different way. Ian was sure that Kafka would approve. Ian's organized chaos was much more brilliant and ingenius than Kafka's was. He was much more deliberate and peculiar with his stories. It took him much more time and effort to mold the characters and to shape their behavior to fit his will. Hell, even Pavlov would enjoy it. There were, of course, no dogs or bells. He took the book and slithered back across the room, coming to a rest in his chair. He kicked his feet up on the desk and leaned back. For some reason, he couldn't seem to get that bizarre thought out of his head. how fragile life is? The question wasn't aimed at anyone, because he was, afterall, alone. It took a second or two for Ian to regain his train of thought. Kafka. Organized chaos. Paradoxes. Ian smiled as he thought abouthis last little test of wills. He had twisted another human into willingly marching into his own professional demise. A feat, Ian thought, that would certainly make quite a story if he could find someone good enough to write it. He already had the main characters--himself, Herman Denrol, and Alexandra Korth. The stage had already been set. And for shits and giggles, the first couple of acts had already been written. But it was the end of the story that perplexed Ian so much. What would be the end of the story? [how fragile life is] The thought continued to haunt him. He shook his head as if to rid himself of the poltergiest like thought. It came in waves. He was sure it would return. Ian lit a cigar and smoked. This wasn't anything that he particularly enjoyed, but was a habit he had picked up from a Cuban friend of his. Ian searched his brain. How would the story end? He had already made Alexandra grant him the ability to write the end of the story because he got to decide how and when it would be written. She had given him full control over the manner in which the climax would be decided. He and he alone could decide the fate of Herman Denrol and his wife in one singular night. how fragile life is And there it went again. He searched his brain again trying even harder now to remember how or why this had become so engrained into his mind. And suddenly it came to him. Ian scolded himself for forgetting something so important. For now, he told himself, Kafka would have to wait. He fumbled around looking for something, and when he found it, he let out a small chuckle of victory. Ian scanned his refound pice of paper and smiled, arming himself with a pen and, determined to pick up where he ad left off, read what he had scribbled on the paper aloud; Grief serves to remind you how fragile life is and how vulnerable you are to loss. It can make your present life seem meaningless, and take away your hope for the future. My only hope is that your grief over the loss of your son Andrew won't be too difficult to bear. Regards, Ian Tense That was enough, he thought. After all, he didn't want to sound to apologetic. It wasn't his fault, he determined, that their son's deathwish had overtaken his ability for rational thought. Ian folded the sheet of paper and stuffed it into an envelope. To the parents of Andrew Scrine..."The Muppet Man" Rational thought, after all, would be for the poor bastard to disappear...at least until after Monday night.
