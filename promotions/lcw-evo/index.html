Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

LCW neo opened on 21 October 2001, taking the angle-writing half of Lethal Championship Wrestling out of that autumn's split, and its first Impulse was not a card at all: it was a press conference at which the on-screen owner, Edward Olmstead, unveiled Club neo. The club was the point. Its floorplans were published on the site, floor by floor, with the owner's own notes beside them, and every show afterwards was staged inside it.

The federation renamed itself within a few months and said why in its own information sheet: the old name “didn't accurately reflect what we were trying to do with our share of the LCW name,” so Evo was chosen, for the evolution it hoped to create. What did not change was the method — “we base the outcomes of our matches primarily on pre-planned storylines” — or the ask made of the roster, which was for segments rather than challenges, written in past tense so that a show would read in one voice.

It published its shows a segment to a page behind an index, and closed each one with a credits page naming who had written which. Two Impulses and a supercard called Dawning survive that way: a Madhouse title cage match, an Evo title defence, a submission match, a mystery opponent, and a run of backstage scenes carrying more of the story than the matches did. The Madhouse Championship was the old Hardcore title under a new name, carried across in the split.

Its roleplays lived on a board it called Legends, and they run long and strange — a wrestler journalling his way through a rehab clinic, a coach at a children's baseball game being told how two of his players came to be in a wheelchair and a coma. How it ended isn't in the surviving record: its front page of 11 June 2002 still previews Impulse 2.2, and nothing dated later than that survives.
