A federation that opened in September 1998, folded inside a month, restarted, buried and revived the wrestler who started it, ran through five presidents in a year, and was still crowning a Heavyweight Champion six years later at a second address. Ten title lineages, 17 shows and 111 wrestlers survive from its 1998–2005 run. Written by AI, 2026

Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

Cyberspace Championship Wrestling opened on 8 September 1998 and was gone within the month. Its own history page is blunt about why: the cost of running a federation had been overlooked, the receivers were called in, and it fell at the first hurdle like many small federations do. It came back on 26 February 1999 with money behind it, crowned its first champions at a pay-per-view called Irish Spring, and ran a thirty-two man tournament that summer called the Champions of the Ring, which is where The Supreme Machine came from.

Then it started losing presidents. The founder was announced dead in the summer of 2000 and Phil Burner took over; in March 2001 the founder came back, having been hiding in Europe from exploding packages, and resigned for good shortly afterwards. Four more men held the office inside a year, and the federation's own account of it reads like a ship's log — kept afloat, taking on water, sinking, and stable again by the spring of 2002. That is also the month its own rankings listed the Heavyweight Championship vacant with two men tied at the top of the list for it.

What survives of 2003 is one pay-per-view and the run-up to it. The federation had reopened again, and Revolution opens on a montage of nineteen former Heavyweight Champions flickering past with the belt before a word is spoken. Underneath, the card is the era entire: a Hardcore Championship fought over a trash can wrapped in barbed wire and a board of nails, a mud pit built into the corner of the arena, a three-team ladder match for a vacant tag championship whose winners took the belts and left through the crowd rather than wait to be announced, and a Women's Championship that changed hands on a disqualification the ring announcer declared a title change in the same breath. Blayze lost the Heavyweight Championship in the main event after pulling the referee into a charge and hitting the challenger with the belt, which is exactly the kind of finish the fed's own commentary spent the night complaining about.

The federation was a Primetime Central member from at least 2002, calls itself in its own history one of the most pivotal federations of that alliance's origins, and sent a delegation to the New Era supercard of 20 April 2003 — Michael “Spitfire” Stevens in the Global Title match and Jason Dread, Dan Logan, Ace Hawk, Scorpion and Fanatik in the Extreme Cluster — where the alliance's own announce desk introduced Stevens as a man whose company had closed and who was on his way to the unemployment line. Ten days before that, the federation had advertised a Lockdown at the Staples Center whose results were never captured.

It came back. By December 2004 it had written itself a fifteen-thousand word rulebook at a new address, and in January 2005 it aired what its own newsdesk called its second show: seven matches, a prison-riot cold open, three firings in the week after, and Legacy — seven foot one, close to four hundred pounds, billed as recruited out of Lights Out Wrestling — taking the Heavyweight Championship off Jason Dread and chokeslamming him through a table on the way out. Spider, the owner who had been gone since before Revolution, left the announce desk mid-show, took a microphone into the ring, and got his name chanted back at him.

The last thing the federation wrote is a card for 3 February 2005 and a roleplay dated the day after. The pages sat where they were until the crawler stopped coming; four years later the 2003 site was still answering every request with its own splash page and the words “Click to enter the Revolution.”

Storylines

The arcs below are recovered from a full read of all twenty-one held documents across the fed's own two addresses.

Devyl, Jack Manson, and the war on Lacey Skye Morris Written by AI, 2026

In March 2003 Commissioner Mr. Raymond turns Lacey Skye Morris's own #1 contender's match into a handicap bout against Devyl and Koriann, then beats her himself with a leather strap at ringside; Lacey wins anyway with a schoolboy pin on Devyl once the Daemonette distracts the referee (lockdown-results-2003-03). Devyl loses the rematch line entirely: Sheena Ramone takes the Women's Championship off Lacey by disqualification at Revolution, a decision the ring announcer declares a title change in the same breath (revolution-2003). By January 2005 Devyl is Commissioner Jack Manson's own lover and co-conspirator, and the two of them treat Lacey as unfinished business: Manson hands Devyl a newly signed contract for Barbzilla as "a possible solution" to the Lacey problem, books the match himself for the following week, and separately asks Devyl to "take care of" former Commissioner Lady Cat before she becomes a threat — the same night Devyl runs in on Lady Cat's own match to attack Koriann behind the referee's back (lockdown-results-2005-01).

The Heavyweight Championship's ruinous line Written by AI, 2026

Blayze pulls the referee into a charging Jason Dread and swings the belt itself at Revolution, only for Enigma to run in, lay him out, and drape Dread's arm across him for the pin (revolution-2003). The title survives the gap in the held record on Dread's own waist. In January 2005 Legacy — seven foot one, billed out of Lights Out Wrestling — beats Dread for it and chokeslams him through a table on his way out of the ring, then spends a full ring promo naming and belittling nearly the whole roster by name before Jason McAllister returns from years away to confront him cold. Manson's own security, Charles Carter and Kenji Lee, beat McAllister down three-on-one until the "comatose" Dread gets back up with a chair to even the numbers, the show closing on the two of them standing together against Manson's own hand-picked champion (lockdown-results-2005-01).

Jason Dynasty's debut and the Gabriel Nyte feud Written by AI, 2026

A feud the commentary says has run "since CCW version one" between Devyl and Gabriel Nyte draws a new challenger looking to make an entrance: billing himself a two-time former World Champion of five past federations, Jason Dynasty demands the crowd's respect, tases a volunteer fan unconscious with a stun gun to prove he isn't bluffing about the settings, then electrocutes the same man to death at what he calls the maximum charge — calling it his invitation to Nyte, who has less than an hour to prepare. Nyte wrestles him anyway, hurt from the barbed-wire triple threat earlier the same night, and wins with the Obliterator when Dynasty's own leg gives out mid-lift (lockdown-results-2003-03).

Adam Christ and Jack Manson's ruthless tag title run Written by AI, 2026

A four-corners elimination match to fill CCW's own vacant Tag Team Championship turns into the bloodiest match the held record carries: Adam Christ razors open Gobstopper's forehead with a blade pulled from his own trunks, and after eliminating Scorpion by dropping him face-first through a mirror, Christ and Jack Manson turn on Scorpion and Michael "Spitfire" Stevens with a crowbar and a garrote once the bell has already rung, running off referee Jason Dread with the crowbar too (lockdown-results-2003-03). A week later at Revolution they win the vacant belts outright in a three-team TLC match, Christ pinning Damien Nhyte, and the pair leave through the crowd without waiting to be announced (revolution-2003).

Affiliations

Primetime Central (PTC)

Member of CCW (1998–2005) (as of Apr 2002; New Era 20 Apr 2003)
Channel 8, “International Sports Network,” on PTC’s April 2002 channel map. Opened 1998; ran through at least five named presidential successions. PTC’s own profile page for CCW confirms its founding (“Open Since: March 1998”) but leaves its “Member of PTC Since” field blank — no precise join date survives. CCW also sent a delegation, including Ace Hawk, to PTC’s own “New Era” interfed supercard, 20 Apr 2003, at ccw.wrestleonline.org — a site that consistently calls itself Cyberspace Championship Wrestling, though the New Era supercard’s own program billed the delegation as “Classic Championship Wrestling.” CCW’s own writing settles the expansion its own way: “Cyberspace Championship Wrestling” appears throughout it, “Classic Championship Wrestling” nowhere, and PTC’s own member-fed profile page for CCW opens with the words “Cyberspace Championship Wrestling.” Both readings are recorded; the fed’s own usage is the one adopted.

Primetime Championship Wrestling (PCW, Primetime, 2000–2004)

Rivalry with CCW (1998–2005)
CCW’s own Revolution pay-per-view is staged “From the PCW Pavillion in Atlantic City”; a fan sign in its own Lockdown script reads “The Phantom Booker fears Sylkk”; and its own 2005 history page carries a paragraph on “PCW under The Phantom Booker” — the rivalry told three separate ways in CCW’s own voice.
