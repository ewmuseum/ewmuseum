Maternal Moulding

9 November 2003
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: Reflecting on Winter, 1997 The sun crept just above the glazed snow that topped the peaks of Córdoba's mountain range. An atmosphere of devastation and despair accompanied the bitter cold temperatures of late winter in the large city in Argentina. A few trickles of cool rain fell onto the deep layers of snow that covered the seemingly distant walkways and roads. Boot marks and excavations for snowballs were the only visible marring of the white precipitate that brought happiness to the festival of Christmas. Just a pity that the season of joy had been defaced by the sudden death of a close relative. A small procession of black suits, white shirts and black ties walked slowly, almost sluggishly behind a stretched black, old-fashioned funeral car. The slow pace of the entourage of people added to the sombre mood as tears fell freely and made their mark in the snow - Tears of sadness fell throughout the Salvador family that day. Sitting in the back of a black limousine was the closest family members, each grasping prayer beads and a handkerchief in opposite hands. A tear formed at my light blue eye and slowly rolled down my slim, smooth cheek as I struggled to contain my emotions. I rubbed the tiny droplet of liquid from the shoulder of my jacket and ran my hand through my then long hair. No one had spoken since the funeral inside the church and as I looked at my father and brother, words were not necessary to describe the feelings that we expressed. Albeit a cliché, that journey in the limousine lasted only ten minutes but it sure did feel like hours. As the door swung open at the burial ground of my dear aunt, the thick snow matched the color of the tear-soaked handkerchiefs that were quickly tucked away into trouser pockets as we approached the open grave with hands together behind our backs. "Please join your hands in prayer as we say our final goodbyes to Rosa Gonzalez" muttered the priest in charge of the distressing ceremony. Doused in colorful Catholic garments, the priest raised his arms, dragging the mock he wore high into the air with them. I bent my head forward and looked at the snow-white grass and blanked out everything in my head. The priest's words spiralled around the mist-filled air, failing to reach my hearing – selective hearing at it's prime. In my peripheral vision, I saw the majority of my closest family, together as one as they should be but under the completely wrong circumstances. My thoughts ran amok as the ceremony continued. My mother had left the world under the wrong circumstances and now my auntie, who was certainly a huge maternal influence on my life, was following the same stairway to heaven. She would reap the rewards due to her huge heart but at the time, that was only a small consolation. The year was 1997, I was 20 years old and had only my father and brother as remaining members of my immediate family. This was the way it was destined to be but it was still gut wrenching and heart breaking at the time. Suddenly, a nudge on my arm from a relative I had never seen before awoke me from my dreams. I had almost missed the lowering of the coffin into six-foot deep territory - only six-feet between the living and those that used to walk the earth. My father, who was dressed smartly and formally as normal, threw a handful of dark brown earth over the coffin as it was lowered. The priest offered me a fresh piece of earth to do just the same but I refused the task almost instantly. The crying that had once almost created the music for the priest to sing over quickly died out and was inevitably replaced by silent disbelief at my refusal to participate in an important Catholic ritual of burying. I knelled over, though, and picked up a huge wad of grass and mud, topped nicely with a layer of snow and threw the remains into the open grave, a sight that will never leave my side. The extra layer of snow made the simple act so much more than it was and the crowd appreciated this. Moments passed and the ceremony had ended. My immediate family surrounded the freshly covered grave. "That was a very nice thing you did, son," my father, Jose had said. I turned my eyes from the grave to his tanned face and replied, "It was the least I could do after all she had done to help me out in my times of need". My brother, Pablo, nodded in acknowledgement and agreement of my statement as we left the graveside to return to the hired limousine. The crisp cold air was greeted by a huge, warm breath of my own as we entered the fancy vehicle. "You know what," I said to my family members as we made the short-lived journey home, "I've taken an awful lot from this entire experience". It was true, I had. Despite the lack of conversation, the sights, feelings and emotions fuelled by the encounter had overshadowed the need for dialogue. 6 years after the death of my close aunt and almost 23 years after my mother's death, the loss of the two closest women in my life have taught me a lot. To this very day, I keep with me the photos of the two inspirations and will never forget the vital wisdom they provided and confidence they had shown in me. I was lucky – I had 2 maternal figures in my life. I can honestly say that the two motherly characters shaped my entire spirit, my disposition and helped me to become the man that you see before you. R.I.P Maria Salvador - 1980 R.I.P Rosa Gonzalez – 1997 … Long may their souls live on …
