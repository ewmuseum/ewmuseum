About

6 June 2004
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
What this is The fed’s own About page, as the Wayback Machine captured it on 6 June 2004 at its first address, rpw.sheepsden.com, and again word for word on 4 August 2004 at its later address, www.rushpro.net (our captures, lot 0178). The page’s navigation menu, images and links are not reproduced; its text is. Its sidebar placeholder and footer lines are kept as printed. The site’s own menu files the page under Rules; what it holds is the fed’s account of how it began.
Background: Rush Pro-Wrestling is an off-shoot of the former Primetime Central and/or WrestlingTattler and/or WrestlingOnline group that went up in flames due to internal affairs early this summer. Rush Mason, who had been inactive at the time, working only sparingly on PCW TV as a friend of Skylar Green's, was scheduled to return to wrestling, but then his dream was crushed when PCW (and PWC, Ron Starr's new federation) were shut down. Rush stayed out of the spotlight that many former WO employees stepped into, doing interviews about why the group had failed. After the dust had settled, Rush realized that he wanted to, if possible, bring back the most talented of that disassembled group and any other talent he could build upon. However, he had worked in PCW for only a year and a half, as per the details of his first contract (and a small extension), which left him living comfortably, but he had, through sponsorship and bonuses, only about 750k, which he could devote to a wrestling federation. He asked around amongst his circle of friends but having seen the volatile effects that the industry could have, none were willing to invest in a NEW project that could once again fall apart. The only person who WOULD help Rush out was Jeremy "Krayzie" Howard, of former RUSH and PCW fame. The two, friends from RUSH, talked at length and JKH (with a vast worth, having sold his PCW stock when he retired and now retired to Indiana with his family) thought that the spirit of the former RUSH wrestling should live on, and with it, so should the spirits of PCW. With this idea in mind, Krayzie pledged ten million dollars to the project in return for twenty percent ownership, only in fiscal terms. Rush Mason would still have one hundred percent control of the day-to-day operations, but Jeremy would receive money back until the day he was fully repaid. With that money, Rush began to scout out talent that had been left fedless after the collapse and at independent talent. He came up with an eventual starting roster (most of whom agreed to take large pay cuts on the first group of shorter-term contracts, with the idea that if RPW wasn't providing well for them by the time the contract expired, they would be free to go elsewhere) and got together with the head writers in preparation for kicking things off as soon as he could sign a TV deal of some sort. However, a TV deal was hard to come by. With the blessing of Jeremy Howard, Rush attracted some attention on his own, but the large networks and cable channels were not willing to risk their ratings (even a graveyard slot) on a fledgling wrestling fed. It was back to the drawing board on ideas, having approached many of the cables that had featured wrestling in the past (USA, TNN, Turner Networks) and having come up with zilch. RPW would find solace, however, from an unlikely source. Fox Sports South offered up a prime time slot on Sundays, hoping not only to attract football fans waiting on Sunday night’s match-up, but also to catch wrestling fans still wanting more after seeing WWE’s Heat. Knowing he’d found a terrific opportunity, Rush signed over the contract, getting another two million dollars on the spot towards his pursuit of talent, as well as rating-based incentives, for the duration of the six-month TV deal. With some extra cash towards the project, Rush then also went to pursue some big-name free agents of the wrestling industry who could help him build upon his potentially great, but inexperienced talent. He now had the roster he wanted and a TV deal, with money left over. To finalize RPW and its opening, though, he would have to find a venue at which he could continually broadcast an 8-10 P.M event. He found the underused Dale F. Halton arena, at the University of North Carolina-Charlotte, a 9-thousand plus seater that he could rent in that timeslot, in exchange for a percentage of venue profits going towards the University and for commercial advertisement during the show. Finally, Rush had all he needed, including a city that would be without a pro basketball team for another year wanting occupation of their minds elsewhere, to start Rush Pro-Wrestling’s legacy. Arena: The Halton seats 9,105 and is on the campus of UNC-Charlotte. Part of the idea is that some of the college’s students make up the fanbase, having a source for pro wrestling right next door to their dorms. The gate price is also somewhat less for students. It has lots of ground floor seating and also has an upper level that is open on all Sunday nights where RPW is holding shows. (For any additional information, see http://charlotte49ers.ocsn.com/tickets/tickets-haltonarena.html) TV Deal: The idea behind the deal is that it is set-up through three Pay Per Views, after which renegotiations may start for an extension. We will be saying that the TV deal doesn’t start until the week we get the site and Pete’s hosting, citing other athletic events having already made commitments with Fox Sports South until November (or whenever the site is fully ready). Funds: I’m not sure if twelve million dollars is a reasonable amount for the roster, so if someone is good with the specs of a real type federation that is this size and what type of funding they might have is realistic, that’d be helpful, otherwise we’ll go with a figure in this range. You have to remember that we are employing extra cameramen because of the backstage happenings and how many different angles are needed to produce a wrestling event.
