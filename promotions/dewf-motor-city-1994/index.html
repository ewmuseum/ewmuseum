A long-running Usenet fantasy federation whose own newsgroup posting gap was bridged, mid-run, by a rival operator's satirical spinoff staged as a subset of its own continuity. Written by AI, 2026

Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

The Dream Electronic Wrestling Federation opens on two dates thirteen days apart, both self-stated and neither resolved to the other: a launch script dated 15 November 1994 announces “the federation of federations is now here, the DREAM ELECTRONIC WRESTLING FEDERATION,” and its own first Motor City Mayhem Control Center bulletin follows on 28 November. It staged its entire roster on real wrestlers' own names and personas, drawn across the WWF, WCW, ECW and the Japanese circuits, under four running championships — the World, Intercontinental, World's Light Heavyweight and World Tag Team titles — and an in-fiction Interim Commissioner installed to govern between eras.

Its recurring news segment, the Ross Report, hosted by a Jim Ross persona, carried the promotion's own angle-by-angle record between shows: a stable firing its own members only to have one of the fired men run in to defend the manager who fired him, a marquee Japan match settled by run-in interference from both sides, on-air alliances forming between wrestlers with no history of trusting each other.

When the promotion's own newsgroup gateway broke and its posting fell silent for a stretch, the gap was filled by an outside hand: the same operator who had run an earlier, unrelated “real wrestlers only” league under the WCWF name until folding it in 1995 began writing a satirical WCWF strand as a subset of this promotion's own continuity in 1997, opening with his own retcon history of that earlier league's collapse — a stock deal that left its owner with Hulk Hogan as champion and audiences of fifteen people in a barn, the promotion eventually sold for fifty dollars and a pig, and a one-day invasion punished by this fed's own commissioner handing the WCWF name back to its original owner to run into the ground a second time. The writer said so himself, plainly, at the top of the first installment: “this is basically a satire, not a federation as such.”

The DEWF returned to its own regular broadcasts that May with a card built around a newly crowned World Heavyweight Champion, Vader, defending the title in a triangle match the same night his tag-team allies were still celebrating his win over the promotion's previous champion.

No closure notice of its own survives; the last dated activity in its surviving record runs to October 1997, still broadcasting, with nothing further found beyond that point.
