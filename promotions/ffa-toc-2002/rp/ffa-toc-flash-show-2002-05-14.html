The "Adagio for Strings", the theme from Platoon, plays in the background.
The setting is the bleak inside of a warehouse. There is no hint of the time, no hint of the place, not even noise from outside penetrates the old, exposed brick walls. Waterstains discolour the dull greyish-looking cement floor. Exhaustion has cut lines across her face, dulled for a moment the fire in her eyes. Salty streaks of sweat trace bright chalky tracks across her forehead and down her cheeks. She does not look glamorous at all.]
Marissa: Where I'm from ... we have to work hard at everything. Nothing comes easy. Nobody gives us anything. We get the short end of the stick and make something out of it. My mother used to feed me on tablescraps, leftovers from the neighbour. I lived on cereal and bread. Hotdogs and hamburgers when they were on sale.
Not filet mignon. Not lobster and shrimp. When I outgrew my bike I didn't get to ride around on a new one. I gave it to a girl small enough for it and I walked everywhere after that.
Sometimes there were weeks when I couldn't even get out of Coney Island because we didn't have the money for me to catch the "F" train.
Sometimes, I didn't leave my apartment because there was too much going on outside. A car blew up outside my window. My neighbours five floors down had to hide in their bathroom after a ricochet shattered their window.
But we didn't say, "Woe is me" and bemoan our fate. We worked ... hard. School wasn't the best. They tried, but they didn't have the resources. Mom had to trek into the City every day for work and didn't get home until late. She bent her back so I could have a little bit. So that I could make something of myself with a little glimmer of hope and proper guidance. I got an invaluable example from her on what hard work gets you. It got my mother a grown woman. A woman who can take care of herself and support a family. She got me through that little window of opportunity. And I made the hole big enough that she could come through too. Now we're both living better.
[Marissa wipes one heavily taped hand across her forehead, rubbing free some of the salt deposits.]
Marissa: Where I'm from ... we don't worry about platitudes like "Some men are born to greatness while others have greatness thrust upon them."
We didn't have time. And we made statements like those a lie. Everyday I watched somebody get the idea to get out and I watched them seize greatness. Some did it the right way. They went to school. They got scholarships. Some did it the fast way. They slung rocks. A lot got shot up in the streets. A lot just never left.
And then there were those of us that took greatness from a ball. I ran down there with those boys every free minute from the time the courts were cool enough to play until my mom made me come in. I still have the scrapes and scars from playing on those courts.

Do it until I dropped. And then it was pushups, crunches, situps, chinups on the rim. Everything to get my body right. Everything to be able to compete. I still don't know how my body had the energy to grow like it did. I thought I drained it. But it knew that it was taking greatness every day. It knew it had to be there for me.

Enough to be still competing against the best wrestlers in the world. But I'll never forget where I'm from. What I learned. What I was shaped into being.
