Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

World Wrestling Fantasy League was already running by the time its surviving record opens — a 22 November 1997 recruitment plug stating plainly, “We've been around a year” — and its complete rulebook, posted 1 December 1997, sets out what it had built: “The WWFL, My Fantasy Fed, with no real wrestlers,” a first-come-first-served application league capping each member at six wrestlers, splitting 55 points across ten stats, and running real-life contracts the president could void or renew. Six belts were named at the outset — World Heavyweight, Intercontinental, United States, Cage, Cruiserweight, and Tag Team — each required to defend at least once a month, cards running every Sunday.

“Stone Cold” Mcdowd held the World Heavyweight belt into 1998, losing it at the fed's own annual flagship event, 2 April 1998, to Malice — a match that opened with the special referee a no-show until a bolt of lightning delivered Mr Perfect Jr to the ring, presumed dead and back anyway, “the old Carnage trick,” before Mcdowd turned on Malice's handshake and the match itself got underway.

Malice's reign lasted less than two months. At Memorial Mayhem, 27 May 1998, WWFL President Chris Stinson worked the match as special guest referee against the champion he was billed to be protecting, fast-counted Malice's own pin attempts, and handed the title to Dogg — then took the microphone to admit it outright, that he'd built a release clause into Malice's own contract and used it rather than defend a champion he thought too stupid to hold the belt.

The Cage Belt ran through its own churn in parallel: stripped from Dogg on air in February 1998 for disrespecting it, won on a no-show forfeit by Steel Machine in March, taken by Will Shelton outside a formal match at the same April flagship card, and still on Shelton's shoulder at Memorial Mayhem after a bloodied cage defense against Steel Machine. The Intercontinental belt settled onto The Trickster for what the fed's own broadcast called the longest reign in its history, the Tag Team titles changed hands once, from the All Americans to the Freedom Fighters, and the Cruiserweight belt passed through Mcdowd and a fire-breathing Inferno on its way to The Dean. On 10 June 1998, President Stinson used the broadcast to announce a tournament for a new Television Championship, starting the following week.

That broadcast is the last one held. Whether the Television tournament ever ran, and how the promotion itself ended, isn't in the surviving record.
