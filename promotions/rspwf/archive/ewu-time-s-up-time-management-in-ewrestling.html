Time's up: Time management in ewrestling

EW University
Content noticeA period document, served as recovered; withheld details are marked. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
TIME'S UP by [name withheld] You've just come back from the most grueling day of your life, EVER, and you find that your wrestler's opponent at the upcoming Pay Per View has just done a flash. So you read it right away, knowing very well that in five minutes you have to a) go to your second job, b) cook dinner, c) do a year's supply of thermodynamics homework, or d) sleep for the first time this week. You spend the next five minutes reading the many, many pages of the insane-yet-hardcore musings of Stone Cold Captain Extremo. "Wow," you say to yourself, "I'm talking to myself. Maybe I ought to get some sleep like I wanted to." But then you think again, "40K?! 40-freakin'-K! And this guy does WHAT for a living?" And then you realize that this guy does absolutely nothing for a living, and that, for the mere trade-off of being listed on his parents' tax forms as a dependent, he doesn't HAVE to do anything. His makes his living, by, well, LIVING! So, Junior, who prides himself on having the only B+ in his English class, has a lot of free time on his hands, and finds all this time a great benefit for his life-long goal to write the first ever fiction novel whose main character is a hardcore pro wrestler. Judging by the length of Captain Extremo's last flash, you figure he's well on his way towards completing Chapter 1. I don't mean to sound sore because there are many younger kids out there in e-wrestling land, roleplaying with and against their elders while managing their own backyard wrestling career (which has become a financial hassle ever since Billy's Backyard Wrestling Federation went national on Dateline NBC). I went to high school when I first started out in e-wrestling and I'm sure there were older handlers who looked down on me. They were also jealous, but who can blame their candy asses? The point is that I'm not a bad guy -- despite my use of the term candy ass -- and that I do respect some age-impaired handlers. I can't be a hypocrite in that respect and say that the youngsters have no right to live... or roleplay. No, of course, everyone has the same right as everyone else (of same qualifications) but time, time, my friends, is perhaps the ultimate deciding factor. And if anyone has time, it's our younger fellows. It isn't just them, either, because spare time is a gift handed out to men and women of all ages.. kids just get in line twice. So how we combat our lack of time is an important question, one that ought to be answered using the art of experience and the science of logic. I recommend three points that, having followed them myself, have worked out well. Of course, in the tradition of staying true to my own preaching, I created these three pointers in record time, which means that they are neither all-encompassing nor overly profound. These are the top three things that came to me when I asked myself what I do when I don't have much time for e-wrestling. (Yes, I talk to myself -- No you don't! -- Shut up! -- No, YOU shut up! -- Stop it, we have an article to write.) Following are three fundamental hints for the engaged e-wrestler: QUALITY CONTROL Time is important. It speaks-- no, writes volumes. 40K volumes. But fear not, and repeat to yourself: "quality over quantity, quality over quantity," for this, the fed head's favorite cliche, will be your best friend when you're short on time. If you spend the time that you do have writing roleplays of better quality than your opponent's, then you'll significantly increase your chances of winning. This doesn't mean that one great roleplay is better than your opponent's five mediocre ones, but if you only have time for one roleplay then you don't have time for e-wrestling. Oh, and by the way, I'll be the first one to point out that it takes time to write a "quality" roleplay, but top handlers seem to produce quality much more often than they produce crap and so the top handler will write a top RP in as much time as a crappy writer will take to write crap. With experience and EWU, handlers will learn to cut down on crap and raise the odds of delivering quality shtuff. PREPARE TO DO NOTHING When you can predict an upcoming drought of time, be sure to do something to avoid having to drop off unexpectedly. Think up some storyline or injury that will minimize your wrestler's need to appear on TV. This would be a good time to have the wrestler "rediscover" himself and then to return with a new attitude, gimmick, look, etc. when you (the handler) return. Give your wrestler a spiffy makeover and a nice new outfit, give him a baseball bat and a ticket for a nosebleed seat, whatever, just make an excuse for a temporary cease of roleplaying. KNOW YOUR ROLE, DON'T SHUT YOUR MOUTH The easiest way to become a flash-producing machine is to quit your job and find some lunatic to pay you money for every flash you write. (On a side note, I'm personally seeking a lunatic to compensate me for time and effort spent on e-wrestling.) Oh, but the second best way to increase flash productivity is to have at your fingertips the life and lines of a character who you know very well. I don't mean invite your wrestler to a bar and get to know him. I mean know your wrestler's character traits, and in the case of a specialized wrestler (i.e., a dentist, a musician, a chimp) try to know a good amount of his field of expertise (i.e., teeth, music, bananas). Knowing a character's character (as in personality) is so important that it's as much a requisite for good roleplaying as it is for having fun writing flashes. I believe the quickest way to know the wrestler is to develop him in your head and treat him as a supposed human in a world of humans, not a wrestler who appears out of thin air only to be on his wrestling promotion's television shows. A wrestler may indeed have a social life, a family and, get ready for this one, flaws! And I mean character, not in-ring flaws. Yikes! For this, I will refer to the flaws as human eccentricities although they may be any variety of foibles. To demonstrate the effect of eccentricities, I give you a hundred years worth of literary criticism pointing to Charles Dickens' characters. The ones who have tiny flaws have received nothing but critical acclaim while the ones made completely flawless and virtuous are not representative of humans. There's as scholarly an example as I'll produce, but now you don't just have to take my word for it (you have to take my word that there are others who agree). So, to bottom line it, your wrestler should not be perfect.. your wrestler should be uncomfortable when he's around pretty women, or should have a phobia of spoons, be unable to tear open ketchup packets, or more realistically be scared of underachieving, thus setting low standards for himself. Quote me on this adaptation, people: "to err is human; to be unable to open up a ketchup packet is divine characterization." Alexander Pope, having taken from Plutarch for An Essay on Criticism, would have surely forgiven me. Final word on the topic is that of all the people you know, hopefully you know yourself best, and if it takes making your wrestler half of (or a perfect reflection of) you, then so be it. There's a little me in all of my wrestlers... in fact, I call them, Mini-Me. All of them. It's hard to keep track of who's who, but I survive. Knowing that I can always stick into my character's mouth my own opinion on, say, economic decline in post-revolution South American countries, is comforting because while I believe that economic decline is bad, I sure wouldn't know where to start creating a separate opinion for one, nay, all my individual wrestlers! The corny summary-style final thought goes like this: time will never again be such a hindrance if you have enough of it to learn the three steps to dealing with business.
