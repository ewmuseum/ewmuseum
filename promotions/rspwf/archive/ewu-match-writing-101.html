Match Writing 101

MATCH WRITING: A PRIMER By [name withheld] I`ve been meaning to write this for a long time, so I`ve decided to just sit down and write the damn thing. This is not definitive by any stretch of the imagination, and I will be adding to it, but for now, read on� Match writers. Where would E-Wrestling be without them? We`d either be in a situation of fedheads constantly burning out due to the demanding pressure nowadays that you can`t be seen as a quality fed without doing at least one full Play by play (PBP) card a week of some (at least) 100k, that`s where. Every match would probably be a couple of paragraphs, report style, which as everyone knows just does not compare for the excitement of PBP. PBP is, and will continue to be the demand, and even though there are simulators that can bring a similar effect about, it just ain`t the same as the human touch. PBP is essential to the modern fed. Players are proud to have made it in into the quality feds that do provide full PBP matches, that give all the excitement of RAW, SMACKDOWN or whatever your preference. Yet for some strange reason the players of the organisation, more often think, take it for granted, and in some cases see it as their innate human right, that other people who already spend more than enough time running a federation should also write lots of matches as well. Heaven forbid that they should be asked to put aside an hour of their time to write a match for someone elses enjoyment. No, they`re a player, not the match-writer. Listen folks, if you want to be in a fed you`re proud to be in, it`s time to pull up that chair and write that match, take the pressure off the fedhead and the skeleton match-writing staff. You wonder why that fed you liked closed? It`s probably because you and most of the roster were too self-interested to write a match. Think about it this way. If a roster has 24 players, and their were 8 cards a week…if every player wrote matches, everyone would only have to write 1 match every three weeks. 1 good match takes 1 good hour. 1 hour in three weeks. Fair trade off to be part of something you`re proud of. Fair trade off to be involved in producing something good. E-Wrestling is changing. People need to stop thinking of themselves merely as players and more as shareholders in the fed. If the fed succeeds, they succeed. And if match-writing is good for one other thing, then that is certainly the increase in respect and status that you get from your peers. But enough of soapboxing. If you haven`t written a match before or want to touch up on your skills, here are my main tips. I`m not the best match writer in the world, but I know enough to help you, and I`m one of the few people motivated enough to help you, so get your specs on and read on� These 10 tips apply to freehand, full PBP match write ups. I`ll cover other forms later. First, go dig out your favourite wrestling video, or watch a wrestling show you like. If possible, put on the most exciting match you know. If you`ve got an Edge/Christian Vs. The Hardy`s on tape, that`s not a bad place to start. Now close your eyes and listen to the commentators. Listen to what they say, listen to how they say it. Listen to the pauses, the exclamations, the banter…focus entirely on what they are saying…this is what you will be trying to reproduce. Secondly, go read some of the great matches from the great feds like EMWC or WWO. See what they write, how they write it. As with roleplaying, look at who does it best and see how they do it best - then understand how they do it so well. Now to your fed. Usually there is a FAQ describing the commentators personalities. It is most important to read this, understand this. If there is a face commentator, get him to cheer on the good guy, if there is a heel, get him to cheer on the baddest guy, and if there is a neutral, get him to tell it down the line, almost objectively. Fourthly, for the match you will write for, read through and understand the wrestlers characters sheets. Think of ways in which you can demonstrate their personalities and styles. Repeat: Read the character sheets carefully. If possible, read some of their previous matches and flashes, and try to get an understanding of what is happening with that character at that moment. Already, there should be some idea of the match forming in your mind. Try running it through your head. Fifthly, take into account the strats sent in, and do your best to fit them all in. Straightforward, yes, but important. People really appreciate it when their strats are included. By this stage you will also have been told, or will decide who wins. Sixthly, time to plan. You know how the match ends, you know how the match begins. Hopefully the strats should give you some more meat for the bit in between. Think of some highlights you`d like to include yourself. If possible, write a rough outline of the match, charting the highs and lows of each wrestlers. An exciting match will have plenty of back and forth. Anything else is a squash� Seventhly, you`ve got it all planned out, so it`s time to begin typing. Remember step one? Well, now it`s time to reproduce that. The neutral or the face commentator will usually call the bulk of the play, with the heel and/or face providing color commentary. Try and stick to that. For point eight, try and have a good handful of near falls. They always make things interesting. Try to do what you can to make the match exciting, even if it hasn`t been requested. As long as it follows the characters nature, and doesn`t contradict their strats, no one has any right to complain Ninthly, when you`ve finished the match, re-read through it, to spot for mistakes, inconsistencies. Tenthly, this is not a step by step approach to match writing. Everyone has their different methods. But here are some extra tips that will all fit under point 10� Make long lasting submission moves seem like long lasting submission moves by having some good banter between the commentators, as they do on TV wrestling to pass the time. Get them to talk about the match so far or the story the wrestler is in or the reaction of the crowd Make heels act like heels, and faces act like faces. Give appropriate acknowledgement by the commentators of the crowds positive and negative reaction to the wrestlers. Exclamation marks are useful when used, as is writing in capitals to express shouting, but be moderate with both. Use them too much and they stop representing extreme exciting moments. Don`t just describe a piledriver as a piledriver. Instead, the commentator can be more descriptive, suggesting a 'pulversing piledriver' or a 'killing knee' or 'wild punches'. Give more depth to the move. Throw in some of the stuff you see on TV - ref getting knocked out, ref being distracted, face making a big comeback…yeah, it`s all done on TV, but that`s because each match is like a little story of it`s own, and on TV, they do know how to make exciting matches. Try and keep the paragraphs short. Don`t let any commentator go over 6 to 8 lines of commentary at most. Grammar has never been a strong point for me, but make sure you know how to use past, present and future tense. 'He punched' - Past tense - Talking about what happened 'He punches' or 'He is punching' - Present tense - Talking about what is happening 'He will punch' or 'He is going to punch' - Future tense - Talking about what will happen. Which one do you think commentators use most? If you`re not sure, just listen to the TV matches. So your homework, to improve your match writing skills, is to go and read and watch some wrestling - RIGHT NOW!
