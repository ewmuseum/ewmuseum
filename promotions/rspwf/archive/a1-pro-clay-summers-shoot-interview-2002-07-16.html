[A1 PRO] Clay Summers Shoot Interview

16 July 2002
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
***** [Black. White text rolls across the screen.] INDY STYLE MEDIA INC. in association with A1 ENTERTAINMENT presents... A Lost In Suburbia production... A1 PRO: BEHIND THE CURTAIN. The Clay Summers shoot interview! [Cut to...] **** [A soundless clip reel begins to play. The reel features various footage from
A1 Promotions' run from April to July 2002. A narrator speaks over the reels.]
VO: It was one of the hottest indy promotions in the US this summer, but like most wrestling companies, it was not without its share of controversy. Much of that controversy stems from the work of one man.
[The reel changes to Clay Summers, in the ring.] VO: Clay Summers. A one time internet critic and television script writer,
Clay was the man behind the scenes who many hold accountable for the product they saw every Sunday night. Clay's style of writing was entertaining and often provocative. Yet, perhaps his greatest work was not his writing, but for the way he incorporated real life situations into storylines, as well as blurred the lines between reality and fantasy thanks to his insider dirt sheet column, Shooting From the Hip.
Tonight, we will sit down with the mind behind it all and find out the truth. Tonight, we will find out what really happened... Behind the Curtain! [Cut to...] ***** [SCENE: A simple interview set on a sound stage. The exact location is not clear. The back drop is a collage of pro wrestling magazine pictures and autographed photos. The superstars and promotions represented seem to be a fair representation of the last five years' biggest names. The set itself is pretty basic. Two comfortable cushioned chairs with arm rests are opposite of each other, at an angle, with a glass coffee table in the center. From off-screen, a man walks onto the set. He is a white male in his late thirties.
He is wearing black dress shoes, black slacks, and a simple white button down shirt tucked in, without a tie. His dark brown hair is gelled lightly. A pair of black rimmed glasses adorn his face.]
MAN: Hello, and welcome to A1 PRO: Behind the Curtain. I'm Will Hunter, your host for this special interview. For those of you who don't know, I'm the founder of Indy Styles Media, a California based company that specializes in providing the fans of pro wrestling with a wide range of material, such as video tapes, magazines, and internet web sites devoted to the world of professional wrestling, especially those smaller, independent promotions. One of the things we like to do is hold these special interviews with notable names in the business to provide the fans with insight that they can't get anywhere else.
[He pauses, walking onto the set. Will sits down in the chair to the camera's right.] WH: Tonight, I'll be taking to the man credited for the success of this summer's hot indy, A1 Promotions. Clay Summers, head writer and assistant booker, will be sitting down in that chair opposite me to talk about what really went on backstage. The drama that unfolds off camera is usually just as exciting as the drama that you do see on television... So be prepared for anything, folks. We've all heard the rumors... Executive staff and wrestlers struggling for power and creative control, production conflicts, numberous ribs between the talent, cliques, drugs, and wild Sunday night beach house parties after the show.
We're going to go straight to the man himself to seperate fact from fiction. In addition, we'll hear candid commentary from many A1 PRO personel... wrestlers included! It should be interesting, folks. I can't wait to hear what really went on.
Without further ado, let me introduce my guest... Clay Summers! [From the other side off-camera, Clay enters. He looks about the same as the last time he was on TV, except a little tanner and a lot more relaxed. Clay is dressed in black boots, dirty denim jeans, and a black button down shirt, untucked. The only change in his appearence is his hair: he now sports a goatee, and his short black hair is not gelled, but ruffled up. Clay walks onto the set. Hunter stands and the two shake hands. Both sit down.]
WH: Thanks for joining us, Clay. CS: My pleasure. WH: Frankly, there's a lot of ground to cover. I guess the best place to start is the beginning. What was your background like? How did you end up in professional wrestling?
CS: Well, I grew up in Minnesota. Wrestling was always big around there. I always had an interest in it, but I was never much of an athlete, so I ruled that out early. As I got older, I learned about the backstage elements. That interested me. I was also big into film and music... The whole entertainment field in general, really. Writing had always been my passion. It tied together all the things I liked.
I recieved my undergraduate degree from Ithaca College in communications, with a minor in scriptwriting. From there, I went to Boston University and got my master's in writing for screen and television.
WH: You're still pretty young. You're what -- twenty six? CS: Yeah. I'll be twenty seven in October. I did undergrad in three years, which helped. Grad school was another two. I was done with school by the time
I was twenty three. I got an internship right out of grad school in radio.
Around that time, I started my own internet site, where I'd write and critique pretty much everything... Books, movies, music albums... And I did some wrestling articles, too.
WH: How did you make the transition from internet columnist and radio intern to a full time member of the pro wrestling community?
CS: In my free time, I'd go to wrestling shows in all the places I'd lived.... Minnesota, New York, Massachussets... I'd help out at different shows, real tiny promotions who'd draw a hundred fans or so... Just making connections, doing whatever I could.
Then, I caught a break. Last summer, I got a call from an upstart Florida based indy. They wanted me to help write TV and audition for the heel color man spot. The guys there were familiar with my internet site and radio work, and I guess they had heard through various connections about the stuff I did for the small indies. They figured I'd work cheap and thought I had some potential.
WH: This promotion you're talking about... Do you mean HOT!? CS: Yeah, I ended up as a staff writer and heel announcer for HOT!'s programming, which didn't last very long. It was a good experience for me, though. In my short time there, I learned a lot about how TV formats for wrestling are done. When that company went under, I went back to my internet site. I focused solely on the wrestling scene, writing commentary and insider news, just basically talking about good storylines, or how I'd fix bad ones...
Just basically being the typical internet critic. I did a bunch of online radio shows, complaining about poor writing in different promotions, horrible production, mismanagement of talent...
It boiled down to this. I was watching all this wrestling and seeing all these things go wrong. I felt like I knew better... Like if I had the chance, I could do it better. All I needed was the chance.
WH: Then you got your chance. [Clay smiles.] CS: By sheer luck, Steve Nelson and Garrett A. Michaels called me, asking me to come out to California and interview for a job. They had read my articles and heard my radio shows, and they thought I was the kind of person A1 PRO needed. So, I packed up all my stuff, flew out to Redondo, and walked right into headquarters. By the end of that day, I was under contract to write TV and sit in on the booking committee.
WH: What exactly were your responsibilities? CS: The break down went like this. Steve Nelson was the CEO, but he's a business guy. He doesn't know a lot about wrestling. I mean, he's learned a lot in the past two years, after his first failed venture, but he doesn't know what works or why. He's basically just financing things and handling the business side. Garrett A. Michaels was the head booker. On screen, his title was President. He handled day to day operations. He had the final edit on TV formats, the final say on storylines, and acted as the liason between Tommy
Vaughn and Steve Nelson.
WH: The liason? CS: Yeah. Tommy was the color guy on the broadcast team, but he was also the head of talent relations. Basically, he scouted out all the wrestlers and reported to Garrett, who would then go to Nelson and tell him who should be signed, what they were worth, that sort of thing. Once we got started, Vaughn would keep Garrett updated on any specific problems with talent.
WH: There were plenty of incidents, correct? CS: Of course. I'll get into that later. My job title was head writer. I wrote the formats for all television and pay per view programming. The majority of it was done by myself, not a writing team. A few wrestlers wrote or co-wrote their segments, and a few personel would sit down and help me write some of the transitional stuff, but basically, for four months, I was a one man writing crew.
WH: That had to be exhausting. CS: It was, but the way I work, I want as much control as possible in my writing. The more people you have sitting around a table, the less of your own ideas get used. I can be pretty selfish when it comes to writing TV. I want the credit or the blame all for myself.
WH: Now, how much say in booking did you have? CS: Here's the big misconception. Garrett had the book from day one. He always had the book. Our little on screen feud was a work... A work based on some real life tension and conflict, but at the end of the day, a work none the less. For the majority of A1 PRO's run, I had a good amount of say in booking.
I'd pitch storylines to Garrett, and he'd either okay them or tweak them a little. We had a few disagreements. A lot of the early programming was pretty unbalanced, because Garrett tossed out parts of my format and tried to rewrite the show. It didn't go over very well, so he and I sat down and decided to work out our problems.
WH: The storyline between you two battling for power... you say it was a total work? CS: Of course. If it's on TV, it's a work. I'm not gonna bullshit, though. Garrett and I butted heads on a lot of things. I wanted A1 to be edgier and faster paced. I thought some of the talent was being held back. Garrett actually pitched the hospital angle to me. We'd build up to it, and then he'd let me book two weeks of programming, as long as I stuck close to the current storylines... Nothing crazy. No hotshotting the Heavyweight title or anything.
So I did, and the ratings increased significantly. Garrett returned to his on-screen role, but from that point on, he accepted me as an equal. He stopped vetoing my ideas and started to compromise when we didn't agree.
WH: Other than Garrett and yourself, did anybody else have a say in booking? CS: The two of us had the most creative control. A couple guys did sit on on the committee and toss around ideas, however. Tommy Vaughn for one, and Mitch
Decker, our other announcer, who also worked as the Head of PR. Steve Nelson would come to a meeting once every few weeks. He never pushed any booking decisions on us, though. He'd ask a lot of questions, mainly. He tried to keep up with storylines as best he could. Wrestlers weren't permitted to sit in on the production meetings - those were basically a combonation of the booking committee and the technicians and road agents -- but a few did try and get their opinions heard. Randall Crane, Shannon Roth... Those are the first names that come to mind.
WH: On that note, would you say any cliques existed backstage... any groups of wrestlers or staff bandng together, trying to gain influence?
CS: Well, a lot of people looked at Roth and Scotty Drake and myself and thought we were a clique. The three of us got along well and had similiar interests outside of the wrestling business, so we'd hang out after shows. We worked storylines together, and I helped them catch their breaks... But I don't think I ever used my position in the company to further their careers. They made it on their own merit.
WH: You're still a part of the internet wrestling community, so I'm sure you heard the rumors. A lot of so called insiders reported problems with Randall
Crane. Some claim he had more creative control than anybody else. They think he tried to use his status as the original champion to gain leverage.
CS: I can see where people on the fringe might get that impression, but Randall Crane never caused a problem. He felt coming in that he was the most established name on the roster and should be treated as such. That's justified. As far as having creative control... well, Randall was vocal at times, but he always acting in the company's best interest, not his own. I mentioned before how some people looked at Shannon and Scotty and thought they were in my ear. Those same people probably thought Crane had Garrett's ear.
WH: But in your opinion, those claims are false? CS: I'd say so. Randall Crane was a company man. He's the franchise that A1
PRO was built around. When TENZAN broke down, Crane picked up the ball. All the while, he worked with Jimmy Taylor... He gave him the rub and when it came time to close things out, Crane put the kid over. He laid down for a nineteen year old kid! You can say what you want about ego or influence, but Crane always did what was best for business.
WH: What about the rumors that the reason he won the title during A1 PRO's first run was because he was dating Steve Nelson's niece and then-head booker,
Meredith Riley?
[Clay stops, laughing. He throws up his hands.] CS: Hey, now. Whatever happened during A1 PRO's first run is ancient history. I wasn't around back then, so I don't know. I've heard the rumor, everybody in Redondo has. You can speculate on whether Randall was sleeping with her, but I don't think it's anybody's business. He was the number one draw. He sure as hell didn't win the title the second time around by sleeping with me, I can tell you that.
WH: [laughing] All right, fair enough. There's a few other issues that are still unclear. What exactly happened with Yoshikazu TENZAN?
CS: Yoshi was probably the greatest find out of all the talent. He came in with a desire that was unmatched in the locker room. Here was a guy who was dead set on being the future of this company. He went out every night, determined to have a mind blowing match. He was always coming up with new spots. He worked on his promo skills for hours at a time. English is not his native language, but he refused to let that be a barrier. TENZAN had a connection to the crowd. He gave A1 PRO a legitimacy, I guess. The fans totally bought his intensity.
WH: Which is why he was the first King of the Coast, I assume. CS: I had to coax Garrett into it, but eventually he agreed... This guy was gonna be huge. We banked on TENZAN being the sickest, most dangerous freak walking the planet... And he was right here in Redondo Beach.
WH: What went wrong? CS: It was a lot of pressure to put on this kid. He was under an incredible amount of stress. In my article, I mentioned the use of painkillers and muscle relaxants as one of Yoshi's problems... But that was mainly bullshit. I was blowing smoke to cover up for him walking out. He might've been popping soma's like I said, I don't know, but he left mainly because of the stress and some bad advice.
WH: Bad advice? CS: Yeah. Some of the guys in the back were getting into his head. It's common when a young guy rises to the top. The older guys feel threatened.
Instead of out right confronting him, they pretend to be his friend... Plant seeds in his head... try and make them self destruct. I didn't know it at the time... Neither did Tommy Vaughn, and as the Head of Talent Relations, this is the kind of thing he should've seen coming... But Corey Atari, in particular, was really fucking with TENZAN's head.
WH: Atari was fired for substance abuse problems, correct? CS: Yeah. He kept showing up late to shows, never came in to cut promos when he promised, and just basically screwed around. Well, apparently, in one of his typical drug-addled rants, he convinced TENZAN that he was a disgrace to puroresu wrestlers... That he didn't deserve his big push. Unfortunately,
Yoshi believed him. Atari got canned, and then his agent tried to smooth things over by sending us WINGER Shimizu... Well, this guy must handle a crew of flakes, because he did even less than Atari did. Thanks for nothing, man.
The crazy thing is, Yoshi was in talks with Tommy Vaughn and myself about coming back for the last show, to do a program with Keiji Zasaki. To kind of pass the torch from one A1 imported legend to the next. It would've been killer, in my opinion. TENZAN was about to sign the deal before the rug was pulled out from under us.
WH: Tell me about the first pay per view. What was that like? CS: God, it was hell. Yoshi bailed a few days prior, so I'm stuck doing last minute rewrites. We're scratching our heads on what to do. Finally, we worked out the angle to switch the title to Crane. That whole storyline was severely rushed, but I think it worked out okay.
WH: Some critics claimed the Crane heel turn and Zasaki's appearence were hotshot booking to compensate for TENZAN's walk out. Is that true?
CS: I've been on that side of the camera, so I can imagine what it must've looked like, but I can say in all honesty that both of those were planned well before TENZAN walked. Crane's betrayal of Jimmy Taylor was my big storyline.
I had it worked out weeks in advance. Zasaki was a later addition. He actually contacted Steve about coming back for a short run. We knew it'd draw huge money, so we arranged for him to come in. I think the fans deserved to finally see a Crane/Zasaki pay off.
The rest of The Golden Days... It wasn't our best work. A lot of the writing seemed rushed, thanks to the last minute rewrites. A few spots got fucked up and Dylan Johnson suffered a freak injury that hampered the California Death
Match. That really put a wrench into the pacing of the entire show.
WH: What about ONSLAUGHT? Does any particular episode stick out as a favorite? CS: Back to the Beach, of course. That was my first time writing an entire TV format by myself. A New Revolution has a place in my heart, too, for launching my story arc as Acting President. Cutting those heel promos was a blast. What ended up being the last episode, The Line In The Sand, is probably the show I'm most proud of. From top to bottom, it was episodic TV at its finest.
WH: How does it feel now that it's over? CS: I'm disappointed that we got cut off before the last pay per view. Leaving those storylines hanging without a pay off... That's shitty. The fans deserve to see the way it was supposed to end. But the production team was just breaking down. We knew we only had a few more weeks left, and as much as we wanted to tough it out and put out a finished product, it would've killed us.
We had an incredible run, but we knew from the start that this would be a summer promotion. April to August and then we're out. We always kept the option open for an extended run... FOX Sports was willing to give us a time slot, Steve Nelson was willing to put up the bank... But in the end, we decided as a team that it was best to end the project. If we stuck around, the production would've declined. I mean, it was an intense four months. We're all a little burnt out.
WH: Do you have any favorite memories or storylines? CS: The entire first show is like one big memory to me. I loved the Randall
Crane/Jimmy Taylor storyline... The Pak/Chris Walker program was sheer genius, and I can't take any credit for it... I'm biased, of course, but I liked the power struggle angle. I think the fans connected to it. We had some hot crowds. People came from all over California to watch... Los Angeles, San
Diego, Irvine, Riverside, Orange County... We packed that A1 Arena to the roof.
WH: On that note, what did you like or dislike about having one permanent location? CS: Financially, it's easier. We had a fan base that already existed, and once we got TV going, that fan base grew. Touring outside California was a big risk. You never knew how well it'd draw. The ratings were good, but people aren't paying to watch it on TV. If you went out to the Midwest, where they didn't get ONSLAUGHT, you'd be asking for an empty house. I wish we could've tried it at the end. Since we had the East Coast affiliate, I think we'd draw a good crowd in New York. It just wasn't practical. Really, Redondo Beach was the perfect atmosphere... And one of the best advantages was the beach house parties.
WH: I think we've all heard some stories. Care to elaborate? [Clay laughs.] CS: Everything you've read is probably true. In fact, the stories you've read are probably a lot tamer than what would go on. Since we were in Redondo every week, most of the talent and staff stayed in town. A few guys would just fly or drive in, but it was easier for most guys to just rent summer homes right on the beach. There were two big beach houses right next to each other where most of the guys stayed. Every Sunday, we'd wrap ONSLAUGHT around ten and be packed up by eleven. Everybody... The wrestlers, the production team, even some of the locals would head to the beach houses for a massive party. We'd be there until six or seven in the morning. It was wild.
After a hard show, everybody needs to unwind. We were all like one big family. Those Sunday night parties made us closer. It was probably as much fun, if not more than the actual shows. I'll never forget some of those nights.
WH: Anything in particular stick out? CS: Oh, if I thought about it, I could write an entire book about those parties. I remember bits and pieces of those nights. I remember Crane stuck to himself, mostly. He'd be out on the deck talking shop with Garrett or hanging out with groupies, that sort of thing. Scotty and Adrian -- you know,
Alpha -- would have drinking contests every week. I remember one time, they got Mark Fallon totally wasted. You look at him, he's a small kid, you think,
"Oh, he can't puke up that much." Well, he can. I saw it. Fights would break out all the time. Just people goofing around, nothing real serious. The floors were all hard wood, and Anthony Virtue liked to stretch out the younger guys on them. Oh, and Zoe McCloud -- that girl was a riot. She'd get so damn over protective of Jake whenever the locals would hit on him. Let me tell you, the Pak-Man drives the girls wild.
WH: So basically, these parties were just the boys cutting loose? CS: Yeah. We never went on the road. We didn't do tour busses or charter airplane flights. The only time everybody was together and not working was during these aftershow parties. So it was our chance to go crazy and enjoy ourselves. Mitch Decker used to work as a DJ before he got into wrestling.
He'd bring all his old equipment out and be working the turn tables the entire night. Let me think... What else? I remember Tommy Vaughn, Christian Madison, and
'Dre Styles would sit around in a corner together, cracking jokes about white people. I remember walking past Angel Demente and Keiji Zasaki having a covnersation, and Angel was speaking Mexican, Keiji was speaking Japanese, and
I had no clue what either was saying. I remember Jimmy Taylor practicing his finish off the roof of one of the beach houses into a pool. That kid was crazy. I don't know, a lot of stuff happened at those parties. Shannon Roth toted around his digital video camera to all of them. I'm sure he's got hours of footage. Maybe one day he'll sell the tapes or put them up on the internet.
WH: I think a lot of people would be interested to see that. CS: I'm sure. I'd like to see them again, too. A lot of those nights are a blur to me. I've got some blanks I'd like to fill in... You know, make sure I didn't do anything that's gonna bite me in the ass later.
WH: Which brings me to my final question. What's next for Clay Summers? CS: You know, I'm not sure. I'm taking a few weeks off to relax. I want to get over this burn out from writing. I'll go back to work on my old web site, do some columns and maybe some more radio shows. I'd like to work on pitching a TV show to Fox or the WB, and I'm still working on a screenplay to shop around. One way or another, I'll be around the entertainment business.
Whether that includes the sports entertainment branch, I'm not sure. I guess time will tell. I'd love to do it again someday, but right now, I need a break.
WH: Well, thanks for joining us, Clay. Congratulations on a job well done. Enjoy your much deserved break. I think I speak for everyone when I say thank you for all the work you've done for A1 PRO. Any last thoughts?
CS: I just tried to do my job. I hope the fans of A1 PRO are satisfied with the end product. We did the best we could, and despite any problems that might've occured along the way, if I had to do it all over again, I wouldn't change a thing. I'm proud of A1 Promotions. I'm grateful for having the opportunity to reach so many fans, and I hope at the end of all this, they're a satisfied audience.
WH: I think they are, Clay. Again, thanks for coming. CS: My pleasure. [The two stand and shake hands again. Clay turns to the camera and waves.] CS: Thanks for giving me the chance. Goodbye! [With that, Clay exits the stage and walks off camera.]
