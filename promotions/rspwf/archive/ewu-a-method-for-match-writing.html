A method for match writing

EW University
Content noticeA period document, served as recovered; withheld details are marked. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
MATCH WRITING: A DAMN GOOD METHOD By [name withheld] Everyone has different methods of writing matches, but for all the best ones I have ever written, they've always followed this method. They key is in the planning. With any luck, the players involved will have sent in strats, ideally with 'spots' or 'highlights' or whatever you want to call them - stand out moments in a match, which could be anything from a double clothesline to a powerbomb through a glass table. They tend to be things that the players really wants to see their character do in the match and should really be a point of high excitement or drama. If the player hasn't included any specific spots, but rather a general approach to the match, turn those into spots for them i.e if they ask to outbrawl their opponent, think of spots like ramming heads into turnbuckles, or sitting over them pummeling their face until they bleed. Arrange all these spots on your word processor in nice little square brackets, and put them in the order that you'd like to see them happen, or that they have been requested to happen. i.e. [Spot 1] [Spot 2] [Spot 3] [Spot 4] Next, read the character sheets so that you know as much as you can about the characters, and finally find out from the person who makes the decisions (usually the fedhead) how they want to see the match go, from start to end. If there are any run-ins or other non-standard events happening, make note of them as well. Perhaps someone else has stratted to be in this match. Insert that as another spot. Now, if no spots or strats have been included, make some exciting moments going from the data you have read on the character sheets. Often a list of moves is provided, which can help to create simple spots such as [Backbreaker then flying elbow drop] but it'd be more exciting to have him miss, so you put [ Backbreaker then misses flying elbow drop ] for example. Also, if thinking of moves is a bit of a struggle, there is a Big Book of Wrestling Moves to give you inspiration. This may sound like a lot of work, but it'll make writing a great match so much easier. Even now, the spots should give you an idea of the back and forth nature the match should take, and matches are always more exciting when back and forth (in my opinion, unless they aren't supposed to be back and forth) Now, get all the spots, highlights etc and type them down. Think how long the match is supposed to be. Generally, I'd say six to eight lines of description equals one minute. Now, if you are going to write in the description block format, this is perfect, because they should consist of about six to eight lines. A description box is when the action is described in square brackets, such as... [ Vik goes over to McCray, and throws him into the crowd barrier again, then returns to Eddie, punching him. Eddie takes the punches though, getting to his feet, blocking the punches.Vik kicks him in the groin, Eddie goes down again. Vik turns round, Doug is there, kick to the gut - devastating double underhook reverse DDT! Huge crowd pop. Pulls Ventressa to his feet and rolls him into the ring. In the ring, Doug locks on a twisting arm-bar, busting down on the pressurized joints with a hard-hitting elbow. He twists and twists. Outside the ring, Eddie is getting support from Rick. ] Whereas commentary would do the same, but perhaps as... So if you have a match of five minutes, you'd have five description boxes. Place the spots within those descritpion boxes, distributing them how you see fit. i.e [Intro to match, early stages] Commentary [Spot 1, action Spot 2 ] Commentary [ Action, Spot 3, Action ] Commentary [ Action, Spot 4, Action, Spot 5] Commentary [ Action] Commentary [ Spot 6, Action, Match Finish] Commentary Now, all you have to do as such is fill in the blanks, the gaps between the spots, finding a flow of action that will link them. Always use the present tense, not the past tenses, as this is more active i.e 'he suplexes' rather than 'he suplexed'. Don't let the descriptive blocks of text go over 8 lines, because that is when it becomes much more difficult to read and follow. Between the descriptive segments, use the commentators to offer opinion, speculation and analysis of the match. Essentially, you'll be at point A, see where point B is, and find the most exciting way to get there. Do not forget to include things that make matches exciting - near pinfalls, reversals, avoiding moves, fighting back etc. Very important also is to include [Crowd pops]/[Heel pops]/[Face pops]. This always makes a match seem more exciting because the crowd appear to be loving it. use it to get characters over as heel or face. Put them after big or exciting moves. A good way to make time appear to be passing then someone is in a hold is to have the commentators talk back and forth between themselves for a bit. The talk between the commentators should usually come after something exciting has happened. Don't let a match just end or just begin. Use the commentators to hype it up, then wind it down. Also, don't feel compelled to write an overly long match. Write as much as needs to be written. As I said, about 6-8 lines of descriptive text is about the same as a minute or two. And finally, moves shouldn't just be stated all the time - a piledriver can be made appear even more devastating by describing it, for example, as 'a paralyzing piledriver' or not effective all all as a 'a poor piledriver'. There are lots of adjectives to use to makes these moves more painful for the reader to experience.
