Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

Danger Zone Wrestling opened on 17 August 2001 with a handbook rather than a broadcast: a billionaire's son, cut loose when his father's own promotion lost its television deal, buying airtime on a nationally syndicated station and inviting strangers to fill out an application. Its stated ambition was modest — two hours a week, a house show here and there, one pay-per-view a month it never reached — and it opened that ambition by wrestling its own placeholders against each other on camera while it waited for real recruits to answer the call.

When the recruits arrived, the promotion crowned its first United States Champion in the most dishonest way its own broadcast could manage: a beaten opponent, paid off in full view of the cameras, lay down for a single elbowdrop. The belt disappeared from the record within two weeks, vacated without a word of explanation in the promotion's own newsletter. Its second and last champion won it back in the only way this fed ever seemed to trust — not by decree, but by outlasting nineteen other men in a rumble, backflipping to a rope three or four inches above the floor with the title still in his grip.

Its two-man announce team narrated everything with the same nervous delight, unsure from one segment to the next whether they were calling a wrestling card or a stunt show: a masked seven-foot debutant arrived to manhandle a mouthy face on his own boss's orders, a costumed novelty act broke a drum over a rookie's head and vanished, and a hardcore match ended not in a pinfall but in three men burying its winner-to-be under a table. A tag team's own broken arm, suffered mid-card, was still being avenged by ambush a week later.

The held record ends on 11 September 2001, the date its final served broadcast carries as its own. Two months later, on a newsgroup that had carried its every card, roster and recruitment plea, a wrestler and then a manager asked, ten days apart, what had become of Danger Zone Wrestling. Neither received an answer here.
