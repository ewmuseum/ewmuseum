A results-only e-fed archive, self-labeled that way by its own hand: no roster, rules, or join page survives, but its own nine named pay-per-views and 21 weeks of Heat and War broadcasts stage a full run of title lineages and angles, from a real-world dedication to the late Brian Pillman through a Tokyo tournament fought across two broadcast days. Written by AI, 2026

Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

American Internet Championship Wrestling is a keepsake, not an operating site: the whole surviving capture is show-script results and nothing else — no roster page, no rules, no join instructions anywhere in it. Its earliest dated document is its own Monday Night War of 6 October 1997, billed "the first ever" by its own on-screen Commissioner, Argie Otero, and opening not with an in-fiction cold open but with a plain, real-world dedication: "In Memoriam / Brian Pillman / 1961-1997," dated the day after the real wrestler's own death. Its own New Year's Revolution, months later, opens the same way, "In memory of Bobo Brazil."

What the archive actually holds is nine named pay-per-view and television-special result sets — Apocalypse '97, Fright Night (which crowned the fed's first Television Champion), Seasons Beatings: Violent Night, New Year's Rasslin Eve, New Year's Revolution, "No Gimmicks Needed," the Tokyo Thunder Festival (a two-day event built around an eight-man "Inoki Cup" tournament), Survival of the Fittest, and Steel City Meltdown '98, the last-listed show on the fed's own results page — alongside 21 weeks of Saturday Night Heat and Monday Night War broadcasts, of which the series premieres and one mid-run War are served. Read in full, the twelve served documents stage six championships across ninety-odd matches: a World Championship that changes hands five times — Kyle Bane to Byron Brady to Bane again to a masked debutant billed The Saint and back to Brady — a Television Championship that changes hands seven times, an International Championship, a World Tag Team Championship the Perfectly Perfect Duo hold for the whole back half of the surviving record, and an Inoki Cup born from the Tokyo tournament itself. Several of the title changes happen off the page entirely — the surviving record shows who held a belt going into one show and who holds it going into the next, with no served document in between — and one main event, "No Gimmicks Needed"'s own Bane-Brady World Heavyweight Championship rematch, breaks off mid-pinfall where the captured document itself runs out.

Seven names ranked in the newsgroup rec.sport.pro-wrestling.fantasy's own Top 200 poll under the AICW tag turn up directly inside this capture's own show scripts — Knick Man (in 86 of them, the single most frequent name in the whole corpus), "Swinging" Chandler Stone, Gino Valentini, and Alex Raptor, whose RSPWF Top 200 rows were consolidated onto these four in full, plus Vic Garibaldi, Byron Brady, and Nicky De Niro, whose own matching RSPWF rows are flagged on the same join-test rather than merged — the character-match that first resolved this wing's own frontier-ledger entry to this address.

The fed's own last surviving show script is dated 22 June 1998. Whether it closed then, relaunched elsewhere, or simply stopped being archived is not in the surviving record; the archive itself never says so, one way or the other.

Storylines

AICW's own surviving record is a run of nine pay-per-views and a handful of weekly broadcasts, with long, undocumented stretches between them; more than one of the arcs below plays out largely off the page, a title or a mask changing hands between one served show and the next with no card in between to show how. What follows is drawn from the shows themselves, read in full, cross- checked against the title ledgers those same shows fed.

The World Championship, a ladder match, and a mask Written by AI, 2026

"The Demon" Kyle Bane became AICW's own first World Champion by climbing an eight-man ladder at Apocalypse '97, then held the belt through a fatal four-way at Seasons Beatings/Violent Night and a "Stroke of Midnight" defense at New Year's Rasslin Eve so close the fed's own broadcast team argued on air, mid-replay, over whether the pinfall beat the deadline. "Hard Line" Byron Brady finally took the belt off him in a steel cage at New Year's Revolution — and when Bane is champion again by the Tokyo Thunder Festival, with no served show showing how he got the belt back, the festival's own main event supplies the missing piece from the other side: a masked Legion, the Demonweb's own enforcer through most of the surviving record, interferes to cost Bane the title against a debuting masked wrestler billed only as The Saint — and Legion is unmasked, then and there, as Byron Brady all along. Brady finally took the belt back from The Saint himself months later at Steel City Meltdown, the last title change the surviving record shows.

A career ended by a corrupt count Written by AI, 2026

"Honest" Nicky De Niro and "Franchise" Joe Banno put their whole AICW careers on the line against each other at New Year's Rasslin Eve. Referee Thripaq Nguyen counted a fast three for De Niro after a roundhouse kick nobody asked him to throw at Banno — and before ring announcer Andy Burton could finish declaring a winner, Commissioner Argie Otero took the microphone himself, fired Nguyen for corruption on the spot, and overturned the result to a double- disqualification — ruling both De Niro and Banno out of the company in the same breath. It is the only match in the surviving record to end a career outright. Nguyen himself turns up wrestling, not refereeing, for the rest of the record on file — billed by Survival of the Fittest as half of the Asian Assassination Squad alongside Blackjack Takahashi, AICW's own North American Tag Team Champions in the one document that names the belt at all.

The International Championship's quiet handoffs Written by AI, 2026

"8-Ball" Vic Garibaldi won the International Championship in the "King of the Hill" battle royal at Apocalypse '97 — two eleven- man battle royals run simultaneously in separate rings, their survivors meeting afterward in a singles final Garibaldi won over "Camel" Joe Banno — and held it, unchallenged on every served card after, clear through "No Gimmicks Needed." From there the belt changes hands three times with no served match behind any of it: Mista Smith is champion by the Tokyo Thunder Festival, where he retains against Chris Del Rio only by double count-out; Del Rio is champion twelve days later at the next Monday Night War, crediting a "Saturday night" win over Smith the archive never captured; and Del Rio's own reign ends at Steel City Meltdown, where Gino Valentini beats him for the belt after first winning a number-one-contender's match over Alex Raptor the same night.

The Perfectly Perfect Duo's long reign Written by AI, 2026

The Nubian Warriors, Nelson and Muffasa Bukola, won AICW's own first World Tag Team Championship in a tournament final at Apocalypse '97, but lost it within a month to the Perfectly Perfect Duo — Lance and Max Studd — in a triple threat at Seasons Beatings/Violent Night that also involved Money and Power. The Perfectly Perfect Duo never lose the belts again anywhere in the surviving record: still champions at New Year's Rasslin Eve, New Year's Revolution, "No Gimmicks Needed," and the Tokyo Thunder Festival, billed at Survival of the Fittest as four-month champions and at Steel City Meltdown as five-month champions, the fed's own last-recorded show.
