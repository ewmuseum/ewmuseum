Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

The Global Internet Wrestling Federation opened recruiting on rec.sport.pro-wrestling.fantasy on 17 September 1995, building toward four divisions — Heavyweight, Light-Heavyweight (235 pounds and under), Women's and Tag-Team — each to crown its own champion by tournament, with every handler allowed up to four wrestlers.

Its first pay-per-view, Turkey Day Titles, crowned three of those champions on 23 November 1995: Jacob McDonahey took the United States Championship, Chaos the Light-Heavyweight Championship, and Coach Chris the Women's Championship. Days later the promotion ran its first card outside the United States, in Juarez, Mexico, where Sal DeFazio beat Lonestar for the World Heavyweight Championship.

That title kept moving. Theseus Devlin forced DeFazio to submit for it, then defended it against United States Champion Jacob McDonahey in the main event of War of the Worlds, 24 February 1996 at Madison Square Garden, with DeFazio watching from ringside and calling for a rematch after Devlin retained; the belt later passed to Desert Storm, who won it from Blobber in a ladder match.

The record's own texture came from its recurring factions and voices: the Alliance, the Freedom Brigade, the Four Horsemen of the Apocalypse, and the Mercenary Enforcement Agency all warred across its cards, while tag teams like the Jingoists (Lt. Liberty and Major Hawk Hunter) and the Cowboys From Hell held its Tag-Team Championship in turn. A weekly telephone “GIWF Hotline,” hosted by Capt. “Crunch” O'Mally, carried the promotion's own backstage rumors between cards, and by February 1996 the GIWF was reporting itself ranked among the top five federations independent of the larger alliance leagues — five months into its own run.

Later pay-per-views carried the same title lines forward: at Gold Rush II, 8 December 1996, Mean Man-Mama retained the Women's Championship and David Stevens the Light-Heavyweight Championship, while a United States title presentation to challenger Tyler Smythe was interrupted by a surprise return from champion Steve Latini, its own outcome unresolved in the surviving broadcast. Gold Rush II's own “I Like To Hurt People” match was voted a Match of the Year nominee at the GIWF's own year-end awards show a year later.

That December 1997 ceremony crowned Justice Jackson as the promotion's first-ever Weekly War Champion, confirming the GIWF was still running at least that far. The fuller shape of its 1997 run — the Weekly War Championship's own lineage, and how or when the promotion eventually closed — is not settled in the surviving record.
