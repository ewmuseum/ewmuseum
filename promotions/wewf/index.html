Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

The World Electronic Wrestling Federation ran to a rule book. Players sent in wrestlers, the operator sent back the rules, and the results came out as a report or a SuperCard — a play-by-mail wrestling league of the kind the hobby's own directory catalogued alongside its space empires and its fantasy kingdoms. It started on 29 November 1990, the operator's own founding post to rec.games.pbm asking for players by mail; by its sixth report, at the end of January 1991, it already carried a full turn-by-turn title lineage — a World Champion, three Continental Champions, and a Tag Team Championship — across a roster invented whole, no real-world performer among the dozens of names on it.

What it did with the results was unusual. Rather than post a standings table, it owned a television show. This Week in Wrestling opened on a sponsor read — “Bud Light, Bud Dry, Bud Dark, Bud Draft, Bud Wet, Bud Heavy, Bud Large, and Buddy Can Ya Spare A Dime” — and handed off between four anchors, Magilla Mongoose, Wince McLady, Misty Radisson and Robby the Lame, with Green Jean Oktoberfest on the Rumor Mill reporting affairs, identity crises and one wrestler's inability to decide whether he wanted to work like Van Vader or like Gorgeous George. Every match came in as “[ roll tape ]”.

The show carried other federations too, which is how it survives: its summer recap of 1992 is the fullest single account of what was running on the newsgroup that year. The federation's own copyright line sat at the top of both editions, a joint production with the Lightfinger Sporting Network, “all rights reserved, (c) 1992”.

Its own business was ordinary and its own management changed twice in a month. In April 1992 a notice went out that the WeWF was changing hands again, that a World title tournament would take in every player, that lower ranked players might have to go through a preliminary round, and that everyone still active should write in and say so.

It outlived itself as a rule set. In August 1992 a player in New Zealand posted that he was running an e-mail wrestling game “under the old WeWF rules” and looking for entrants, and would send the rule book to anyone who asked. Three months later the November count of active federations still named it, ninth of nine.

Affiliations

The Summit of E-Wrestling

Member of WeWF (1990) (10 July 1991 – 30 December 1991, as the served corpus runs)
A founder member: the WeWF is one of the three federations named on the first Summit’s own opening night (“we’ve got wrestlers from the EMWA, the WeWF, and the PWGeA on hand”) and one of the four named on the second’s (“pitting the best of the WeWF, the UeWF, the UFWS and the IWF against each other”). Its own announce team called the July cards, and its own champions defended the WeWF US and Tag Team titles on them.
