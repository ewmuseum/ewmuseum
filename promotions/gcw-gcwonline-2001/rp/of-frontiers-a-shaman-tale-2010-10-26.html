Of Frontiers (A Shaman Tale)

26 October 2010
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 10/26/10 "Peace exists only if man allows it. And man is not a peaceful creature..." - Unattributed I. Pain seared through Alexander Whitecloud's leg, a burn unlike any he had felt. He sprinted as fast as a hobbled man could, through the woods, through the agony, the memories a mere blur. He wasn't certain of what had happened. He could, however, see the moments. The blazing dwellings of his village. Gunshots ringing out, echoes dancing along the fresh morning air. The screams of his people. The tortured screams that would haunt his dreams 'til his last day. He remembered watching a man he knew shot dead. A bullet through the skull, from the gun of a masked bandit, who howled in delight for his kill. Alexander knew no other thing, only of his instincts. And so he ran. They must have spotted him, for that was when they shot him. But he never looked back to face his attacker, only evade him. He thought as he stumbled deeper and deeper into the woods of how he had left everyone behind. How he was little more than a coward in the eyes of the spirits. Those that stayed and fought, they were proud warriors. They would be revered throughout the Heavens. But they were brave and dead. He was weak and alive. Alexander continued to run, until he knew not where he was. He took a seat, resting his battered body against the trunk of a mighty tree. Who were these people, he wondered, and why had they taken away everything he ever cared about? II. "Damn shame." Alistair Gideon Frost gazed down from his trusty steed on that tall hilltop at the smoldering Navajo village and shook his head. Frost's band galloped down the slope into the remnants of the town. They were all Pinkerton men, sent out west to hunt. The James brothers. The Youngers. The Renos. Longabaugh and Parker. But this time, it was a Navajo. Frost was familiar with the natives, but not in the way he had liked. He never talked about that, though. His men never asked about it either. Like the rest of his men, Frost had served the Confederate States of America in the War for Southern Independence. And though defeat was bitter, he had accepted it nonetheless. He was a man of war, but he was a gentleman through and through, and had moved on. They fought a good fight. God simply was not on their side. Though Frost truly was a believer. A believer in his true rights. He grew up in Valdosta, born and raised. Then he went and raised a family of his own with his loving wife Mary. Raised two strapping young lads, Terrence and Alistair, Jr. A ravishing daughter, Lisa Mae. A house, a plantation, a life. But when the Yanks wouldn't listen, and the call to arms was put out, he grabbed his Winchester and set off to battle. It was not his to question, but to accept. Through the war, he had garnered his reverence. There wasn't a battle he had shied from. Bull Run. Fredricksburg. Shenandoah Valley. Gettysburgh, too. On the field, he felt he could do no wrong. He was feared amongst the Yanks and his own men alike. They said at Antieiam, he was the devil come to life. They never knew how many he killed that day, but they never forgot him afterward. They cried out his name in battle. Praised him in churches. Even Stonewall Jackson was so impressed, he sang Frost's praises to A.P. Hill, before admitting in private, "I'm just glad the sorry bastard fights for us." Yes, war he could understand. Man against man, on the battlefield, a pistol in one hand and a saber in the other. That was purity. Massacring the innocent, the women, the children, the defenseless, there was no honor in that. But this... Tom Harris jumped off his steed and kicked the remains of an immolated skeleton. The cinder splashed about, hot embers rolling on red clay. He took off his hat and placed it over his chest. "What a way to go." The men nodded. Jacob Hawthorne had seen many fights, but nothing like this. A man hardened by war, and yet sickened by what lay behind him. "We should find the bastards that did this," he snarled. "Give 'em a taste of what they brought." There was agreement among his men. But Frost shook his head 'no'. Even though he might've felt the same, and could see the fury in the men's eyes, he had made his choice. "It's not ours to fight," he said. "It's not what we come for, neither." The men mumbled, but he had said his piece. Frost dug his heels into the side of his steed and sent it moving through the village. The village, one that had likely thrived, had been turned into a wasteland of desolation within mere hours. His mind raced. What would possess men to do this to each other, he thought. The horrors of mankind. But he also thought of his task at hand, and how the sheer hatred these thugs had wrought could work in his benefit. If they had killed the Navajo, the man they were tasked to hunt, then their job would be over. As he traversed amongst the smoke and the death, it brought him back to feelings he had fought to bury deep down. It was a beast that grumbled and growled from time to time, asking to be sated, asking for him to face it. But no matter how much it hurt, he would not give in. When he came across a young girl, he jumped off his horse to check. He leaned down to check her breath, but none came. Yet something piqued his interest, still. He looked, to see the girl's hand extended towards an object covered in a blanket. He was going to let it go, move past, continue to do his duty. But something came over him. Suddenly, he couldn't resist. His curiousity got the best of him. He pulled back the blanket, and he wished he'd never bothered in the first place. It was a Navajo child, his clothing half-burnt, face down in the dirt. He couldn't have been more than five. Frost kneeled down in front of the boy, and thanked God the child had passed. When he heard the boy's gruffened coughs, he thanked God no more. Frost turned the boy over, and the horror set in. Half the child's face, singed down to the skull. How he was still living was beyond a miracle. Frost scooped him in his arms. Who would do such a thing, he wondered. Thoughts filled the man's head. Things he had hoped not to think again. Things that brought him back to the battlefield. Things that he knew he must do again. Today. Frost placed his large hand 'round the boy's mouth and nose, and clasped tight. The boy struggled, but not much. It was likely he knew the same. Frost closed his eyes. This he did not want to see. When he no longer felt the breath of the child, he took his hand away. "Colonel," Billy Jones called out, hustling up from behind Frost, huffing and puffing all the way. Frost dropped the child, and would not open his eyes. He simply couldn't. "Damn war's over, boy," Frost uttered. "Ain't no Colonel no more." Billy was young and stupid, the kind of stupid that probably got a man or two in his regiment killed. Frost didn't exactly want the boy tagging along, but he was the lesser of the other vicious bastards the Pinkertons tried ramming down his throat. "Sorry...sir," Billy stammered, still trying to catch that breath. "But you need to come see this." Frost followed Billy, taking him into the heart of the small settlement, where his men had gathered in a crowd. There was hooting and hollering, but Frost pushed through to see what they had gotten up to. There was Frank Millwell, standing over a barely-alive blood-soaked Navajo girl, far too young to be involved in this mess. Frost had despised Millwell. He was little more than a mercenary, selling his bullet spray for top dollar. Frost doubted the man had ever known the word loyalty in his life, but what Millwell had in spades was a killer's mentality. Not hard to see why the Pinkertons kept him around. And here was Millwell, hardly disappointing his reputation, unfixing his belt buckle and shining his jagged grin. "I'mma fuck this bitch," Millwell claimed out loud. Some of the men cheered him on, catcalls abound of "I'm next," and "save me some, ya dirty fool." But Frost was in no mood for such behavior. "Millwell, get back to your steed. The rest of you do the same." Millwell shot Frost a glance. "Shut the hell up," he sneered, and turned his attention to the girl. "I've come to get my pay and then some." Millwell pulled up her dress and lowered his pants. She let out a labored groan, but before Millwell could finish satisfying his carnal desire, a shot rang out. A shot they would soon not forget. Millwell leaped back, his pants around his knees, blood covering his face. Millwell looked up, and spotted Frost, his smoking Colt 1860 Army pistol in his hand, pointed at the girl's head. Millwell was left speechless. Frost holstered his gun. "Get to your steeds, the lot of you. We're moving on, with or without you." And with that, Frost made the long walk back to his horse. No one spoke another word. III. Some time had passed, as he lay against the mighty oak, when Alexander realized that no one was chasing after him. It was small comfort that allowed him to forget the blinding pain of his wound. He wasn't sure of the details. Thoughts joined into place like pieces of a puzzle. But he remembered how it started. He had just come back to his village after some time spent in Tuscon. His family had allowed him to go Tuscon for some time now, to learn the ways of the whites, to be 'civilized', as they called it. He hadn't liked the idea at first, for he was young and wanted to remain true with his people's ways. Very soon, though, he became accustomed to certain things, and found himself pushing more to visit. Some days, while toiling around the city, he would often wonder if this would be the day he simply would not go back. Yet after what had just happened in Tuscon, after what he had done, he feared he may have nowhere left to go. So back to the village he did go, maybe hoping to figure out his next move from there. Try to tell them what happened, justify it. He knew if he went to the authorities, they would string him up in the town square. It didn't matter what his side of the story was. They would make an example of him. He didn't go back right away. He figured they might search for him there, decide that he wouldn't be stupid enough to go back. They would move on, forget about the village, and continue their search elsewhere. The moment he saw the black smoke flowing from his destination, he realized how wrong he truly was. He darted as fast as he could on his ebony thoroughbred, hustling down the hill at speeds he had never experienced before. The closer he got, the louder the screams became. And he found himself stopping. Wondering if he should continue, if his family would want him to risk his future for a war that they had almost surely lost by now. His innate warrior's desire kicked in. The blood that coursed through his veins, that of the great spirits of the Heavens, of men before him that were greater than he could ever fathom that had scalped the white man with impunity for centuries, bubbled up to the forefront. He knew nothing other than his duty. He had to save his people, as he might be the only one left that could. And so the pause was a memory, and the speed of the horse picked up. When he reached the outskirts of his village, he leaped off his horse and made his creep inside. With his Schofield revolver at the ready, he hid out of sight, hoping to avoid the attackers. He knew there were many, six or seven by his first count, and he would be no match for them head-on. Instead he would search for his family, for survivors, and lead them to safety. When he made it upon his family's dwelling, inside what he found was the death of his hope. His mother, his father, and his sister had all been slain, shot plain and true, point blank. There would be no reunion. He could say no goodbyes. They had already left. He fought through his tears, and his choice had switched. He would no longer save his friends, his family, his people. Now, he would round them up, and ready for a final stand. If they were to be killed, they will be killed together. He grabbed his knives, that he had kept for safe keeping in their house, and jumped outside. But there he was, a bandit, upon his horse, his gun drawn. He fired at a man Alexander knew,an the man dropped to the ground, dead. A man he had hunted with once before, knew all too well, had just been killed in front of his very eyes. Alexander froze. The bandit, in his bounding of glory, took only a moment to spot Alexander. Alexander pulled out his pistol and fired, but it jammed. The bandit's shot did not, and pierced his leg. The bandit came forward to finish the job, and Alexander did what his instincts told him to. He pulled out his knife, and whipped it at the bandit. The blade lodged into the bandit's chest. The bandit screamed, and dropped from his horse, and his steed bolted off into the village. Alexander's instincts kicked in once again, and as the yells of the pack came rumbling his way, he ran. He ran as fast as he could, until his legs burned and his breath labored. He continued up the hill into the woods, as far as he could get away. He continued, until he could continue no more. And there he was, deep in the evergreen, the bullet pressing into his flesh, his bone. He sat against the tree, a cold sweat pouring down his brow. He wanted to act like this was some sort of cruel joke perpetrated upon him for no good reason, but even he realized he was fooling himself, asking who they were and what they wanted. He knew that they came for him. This had all been all his fault. IV. The men shouted and threatened. Frost refused to give in. "I will not be accompanied by villains and scum," Frost stated. "Leave now. I will find the Navajo." "Bullshit," Millwell screamed. "You're just trying to steal our cut of the bounty!" "You're all Pinkertons," Frost said. "You'll receive your pay. Now, go home." "This ain't the damn war no more," Millwell barked. "We're a team! Not some damn regiment!" Frost took in a deep breath. "I don't fight alongside thieves and thugs," Frost said with aplomb. "And I'd sooner see you in hell than walk another step with you upon this dirt." Frost turned to go back to his horse, but Millwell was not to be bested. Harris tried to calm him down, but Millwell had made his decision. "Goddamn you," Millwell grumbled, as he marched forward, pulling his revolver out. Frost turned 'round quick, his 1860 Army drawn once again, and shot Millwell clear between the eyes. Millwell dropped to the ground, dead, never having stood a chance. The men said nothing. Only stood, jaws agape. Maybe for Millwell's sheer stupidity, or how the legend of A.G. Frost might actually have been true. No one rightly knew. And as the blazing sun set along the mountainous red rocks and the bristling trees behind him, the smoke billowing up from the barrel of his Colt, Frost repeated his declaration. This time, there would be no repeat of dissension. The men picked up Millwell's corpse and thrust him upon his horse, and left in a hurry. Frost loaded up his steed, and as the dispelled blended into the darkening horizon, there remained behind a single man. One that did not leave with the rest, one that chose to stay, for he simply would not give up. He came up from behind, and pulled on Frost's coat. Frost swung 'round, his gun raised. Billy Jones leaped backwards as fast as he could. Frost lowered the gun, and snapped at Billy. "What, boy?" "I want to come with you, Mr. Frost," Billy stuttered. "I want to help you find Whitecloud." Frost shook his head. "You're a good kid, son. But you don't want to be gettin' involved in this." Frost jumped on his horse. "Go home to your loved ones." "But I ain't got none left," Billy returned. "Then find some," Frost said. He clicked the side of the horse with his heels, grabbed the reigns, and darted off along the plain. As Frost galloped away from his troops, he felt at ease for a moment. He never did like that the Pinkertons saddled him with such unruly jackals or raw bumpkins. He wanted men like him, that understood what he knew. But anyone he knew that was worth a damn was long dead and buried, fallen on the fields of battle. While he let his mind wander, he didn't hear the gallops coming from behind. When he turned to look, it was that damn fool kid Billy Jones, the one he had sent off earlier. Frost tried his best not to pay attention, but Billy would not leave. When Billy rode up next to him, Frost didn't even glance his way. "Thought I told you to go home," Frost said. "I'm not like them," Billy replied. "I'm a man of the law. I want to find the man we came so far to find, and bring him to justice." Frost let out a chuckle. "Ain't gonna be no justice when we bring him in, son," he said through the laughter. "They'll hang that sorry sum'bitch in some town square, leave him for days while schoolchildren pelt him with stones and drunkards peel flesh off his corpse." Frost shook his head. "That's their justice, son. Not mine." Billy maintained the pace of Frost. "I still wanna help," he stated. Frost suddenly stopped, and Billy did the same. Frost took in a deep breath, and closed his eyes. As he exhaled, he made a choice he would soon come to regret. "You keep quiet, you do as I tell you..." Frost took a long pause. The stars began to poke up along the night sky, tiny white dots scattered as far as the Heaven's extended. "We should find camp," he said, and Billy nodded with the grin of a child with a new toy. Upon their horses, they headed for the woods. ****** The fire crackled under the shade of leaves, the two of them huddled around the warmth. Frost took a sip from a flask filled with spirits, while Billy stargazed through the branches, as curious as ever. It was then that Billy began to hum, hum a song of the South. Frost took none too kindly to it. "Boy," he growled. "If you don't stop that damn racket, I'll shoot you myself." Billy's lips pursed no longer. He looked down to the ground, while Frost took another sip from the flask. There was silence, something that Frost had merely longed for. And it was Billy that put a stop to it right quick. "What's your family like?" Frost stopped, mid-sip. It was not a question he felt like answering. He capped the flask, and stuffed it into his pocket. "What's it to you?" Billy tensed up, as he so often did. "I just..." He stammered. "Just was curious, is all." Frost closed his eyes, and crossed his arms. "Get some shut-eye. We've got a long day ahead." Billy complied, even though he was still antsy. He grabbed his blanket and headed to his bedding, and laid down for some shut-eye. But Frost couldn't sleep himself. He opened his eyes and gazed deep into the flames. And as they danced, he went miles away. ****** He was once a happy man. Sure, he had come from affluence, and he grew up under the umbrella of prestige, but none of that mattered to him. He had always seen himself as a man tethered to his country. He had spent his days in the army camps against his parent's wishes, learning the tricks of the trade. For in Valdosta, there was little for a man like him to do. In the meanwhile, he married Mary, a girl he grew up with and was fond of. And they had a family, two boys and a daughter. And when the war came, when he was needed, he answered the call to arms. He rose through the ranks like a shot. He embodied the leadership and fearlessness they desired. The willingness to kill, it made no difference to him. When they spoke of slavery and the savages, he avoided the conversation. To him, it made no difference to him who he killed. If they opposed him, they were considered his enemy. They made him everything they could at the time, be it corporal, sergeant, or colonel. But on the battlefield, he was something else. He was the devil incarnate. No bullet could stop him, no knife could penetrate his skin. When the Union set foot on Chancellorsville, it was said that upon hearing A.G. Frost would be present, a tenth of the men absconded from service. His name reverberated throughout the young nation. And yet, when the war ended not many years later, Frost was sent home. ****** He took the long stroll home upon foot, along the dirt road leading to his home in Valdosta. Why he took the long way home was something was something he might never be able to answer. It was a blur. The husks of the houses. Wooden frames blackened with soot, turned to tinder. He sprinted as hastily as he could, passing by the smoldering foundations that once kept people he knew safe from harm. When he made it to his house, he dropped to his knees. Tears streamed down his face. For it was too late. On the front yard was his youngest, Lisa Mae. He rumbled from his knees, on all fours, falling upon his face next to her. He picked her up, clasping her in his arms, but she had passed. Her face was bruised, her body ravaged by the cuts of a blade. He screamed to the Heavens, wondering how, why God had let this happen. He could not understand. Was this punishment for the men that he had slaughtered on the battlefield? Was he wrong to kill his fellow man? Why was God not answering his damn questions? And so Frost sat on his front lawn for hours. Clutching his little girl, covered in her blood, wondering how he had gone wrong. ****** Nothing mattered to him after that. He spent his days in saloons, drinking until he could no longer remember where he was or how he got there. Some days, back at the imprint where his home used to be, a group of proclaimed "knights" would come his way, offering a job doing what he excelled at to people he had no problem with. And he would chase them off, telling them to go to hell with the rest of 'em. Other days, he would get the unwelcomed, men trying to make a name for themselves, outgunning the infamous Alistair Frost. His challengers would often realize what they got themselves into, and tuck tail the other way. His backyard held the rest that found the requisite bravery. He had vowed to leave the bloodshed behind. Live a simple life. But when Allan Pinkerton himself paid a visit one day, they had lunch and talked. And just like that, Alistair Gideon Frost took up something he despised once again. V. Tuscon felt like home to Alexander. Over the years, he had spent so much time there, he had forgotten what his true home was really like. His family had sent him off at a young age to stay with his aunt, who had left the village years prior. They wanted him to broaden his horizons. Learn the ways of the ever-growing society. He had proven to be an apt student of life in his early years. He took well to archery, and was fast the most accurate of the men of the village, outshining even the most storied of warriors and the chieftan of the village, Ashkii Diyin, or Holy Boy to his fellow Navajo brethren. Holy Boy saw promise in Alexander, in his skills, in his abilities, and hoped to groom him as the future of the tribe. He began referring to Alexander as Nantan Lupan, the Grey Wolf. But Alexander's family pressured more and more. You must learn the new ways, they'd tell him, for their ways will soon be no more. And while Alexander would train with Holy Boy, the notion would bounce around. As they hunted deer for food, he envisioned how his life might change for the better. And when they danced and drank beneath the glow of the moon, he wondered about his future. Holy Boy had hated the white man, despised them greatly. So when Alexander asked him to bless his journey to Tuscon, to bring back what he had learned to help his people, Holy Boy refused. He told him they would just chew him up and spit him out, like so many other fools before. Alexander tried to plead his case, but his words fell on deaf ears. Holy Boy turned away, and Alexander knew that things would never be the same. Alexander still went, because he felt it was the right thing. He had taken his belongings upon his black horse he had named Taklishim, the Grey One, in honor of the name Holy Boy had bestowed upon him, and headed off for Tuscon. He was worried at first, at how the white man might react to him. Would they kill him? Chase him away? The only thing he could be sure of was that things would be different forever. ****** When he arrived into town, it was him that didn't know how to react. He had never seen such things. Wooden buildings, people flooding the streets. Everything was so busy, it was so foreign and new. He was fourteen, miles away from any of his comforts. He had no idea how he might survive. With the directions he was given, he finally found his aunt. With her modern garb, he barely recognized her, but she spotted him long before. "Hello, Amaye--" She cut him off with a smile. "I no longer go by that name, Alexander. It's Amy Brave Bird, now." Alexander nodded, and leaped off his horse, giving his aunt a big hug. She laughed as he put her down. "You've grown so much," she told him. "I can't believe this was once the young Chesmu standing in front of me." Alexander laughed. "No one's called me that in years. I'd almost forgotten it by now." "Well," she said. "You're going to be forgetting a lot more than old names. We must make room for new and exciting things." Alexander grabbed his items, and she led him into her house. It was another marvel, something he had never seen, only heard stories about; a real-life home made of wood and brick, with stairs to another, entirely different floor. There was little keeping his jaw dropping to the floor. Amy walked up to Alexander and shut his jaw for him. She led him up the tall steps to the top floor, and to his room. Inside was his bed and a small wooden desk with a chair. He was simply speechless. "You might want to get some sleep," Amy told him. "It was a long trip, and you have much to do in these upcoming days." She left the room, and shut the door behind her. In his room, by himself, he placed his belongings at the foot of the bed. He pressed down on the bedsheets, his hand forcing down the springs of the comfortable mattress he would now call his own. He flipped through the books that lay on the desk, the empty pages of notebooks. With his head swimming, he took a seat on the mattress, and laid out upon it. His eyes gazed upon the ceiling. And for the first time in a long time, he thought about his future. ****** School was an adjustment Alexander had hardly anticipated. Prowess in the and the ability to hunt meant nothing to these people. They laughed at his mannerisms, his way of dress, his behavior. He was an outcast, a mere marionette dancing about upon pulled strings for their amusement. He fought boys near daily over trivial matters. But through it all, he listened. He observed. He learned. He would visit the village when he could, but the trips would become more infrequent as the days passed. And as the years passed, he had come to like Tuscon, embrace it, just as Tuscon had come to accept him. His foreign mannerisms, the ones that made him an outcast, soon gave way to their acclimation. He spoke English clearer, without accents or confusion. He sounded more like a man, his teacher would say, and less like some ungodly savage. And over time, those words no longer meant anything to him. Savage or redskin were for the others, not him. The ones that did not wear suits like he did. The ones that could not read and write like he could. He had taken a job at the general store on account of his aunt, who pulled what little strings she had. He stocked the shelves, and before long took over the books, for he could do better math than Mr. Grover, the venerable shopkeeper. The town had come to see Alexander as one of its own, albeit from a misguided and unfortunate background. He would go to the bars, and drink whiskey at night. The women would flock to him, for in their eyes he was exotic. They'd ask him ignorant things, of spirit animals and the heart of the warriors, and he would indulge with equal parts truth and nonsense. The girls would swoon, and he would enjoy. But there was always one girl that caught his eye. He could not help it, for she was truly the beauty of them all. Sarah Penn was her name. A new schoolteacher, come from Illinois to teach two years after he had finished. Alexander could not help but notice her, as did all the bachelors of the town. But Alexander was not one to be outdone. He sent flowers, chocolates, and trinkets he carved himself of ivory. She rebuffed at first, thinking he was just another misguided suitor. But Alexander would not be outdone. She agreed to go out to dinner with him with much reluctance. Sure, the people of Tuscon had welcomed him, but for him to be out and about with a white woman...it was simply unfathomable. Yet maybe that was why she took him up on his offer. And soon, she was glad she did. He was wonderful company. He was articulate, informed, and humorous. All the other men she had been with were horrible bores, talking about their wealth or their properties or their political aspirations. Alexander talked of family, of his own he had neglected and of one he had hoped to start. Before long, they became inseparable. They were all over town, the young stalwart couple, though many still took up in arms over their union. But they didn't care. They reveled in the attention. But they enjoyed the attention they weren't getting that much more. So it continued for weeks, where they bandied about in town, a tome for the gossip hounds. But as night fell, they would be frequents of each others' domiciles. It was here that they began to talk about their futures. Tuscon was nice, sure. But they were in love, and Tuscon was not as accepting nor fulfilling as places they had heard of. Amidst their pillow talk, they talked about heading north, to San Francisco, to live along the ocean. Enjoy a new world.. Start a family, start a new life. Had it not been for that night, they surely might have. ****** It would have been a night like any other. Alexander brought Sarah into the bar, and they sat at the table for a drink. But there he was again, Jonas Pierce, drunk as a skunk again. The hardware store owner, one that had lusted handily after Sarah. That she was with that damn redskin was an affront to everything he stood for. To see them together in his watering hole, that was the night that pushed him over the edge. He rambled their way, spouting venom and malice. He called her a whore, shacking up with this heathen as she were. She had professed her desire to leave, and Alexander had obliged her request, but Pierce was not about to let such go without his say. He grabbed Sarah by the busom, and pushed her out of the way. Alexander confronted the drunken Pierce, telling him that's no way to treat a lady. Pierce grinned, the way a sinner might when he spots a believer. Pierce pushed Alexander away, told him that he didn't care about the whore. But he would not let a redskin defile his kind. The tension hung throughout the room, and before long they scuffled. Chair and tables flying, the two grappling, rolling about the saloon floor. When they both stood, Pierce grabbed a bottle and smashed it upon a table side. He lunged forward, at Alexander, but Alexander did what came natural. He defended. When the bottle hung from Pierce's neck, pouring blood upon the wood floors, Alexander realized the grave mistake he had made. Pierce lay on the ground, the red viscous pouring out of his mouth. Alexander clasped his hands behind his head, unsure of what to do. The men in the saloon, the white men, were getting restless. And there was Sarah, who grabbed Alexander and ripped him from the tavern out to the streets. They ran to the outskirts of town, to a barn that a farmer owned. To hide. To plan. To decide. And in that night, they talked. Sarah had pleaded with him to stay with her, that they would run away together. But he could not. He could not bring her into this, he told her. This was his burden to bear, and his alone. She refused, saying that she did not care. They were in love, they should be together. But Alexander would not let up. They would forgive her. She would live, but his fate had already been sealed. And he loved her too much to allow the same to happen to her. She cried in his arms, but he would not relent. Even as she wanted to tell him something, something important, she kept her mouth shut. She could see it in her eyes, he simply would not listen. Alexander kissed her goodbye, and told her they would be together someday soon. He walked out of the barn, disappeared into the night, and left the woman that he loved and the child he knew nothing of behind. ****** Alexander cringed from his wounds, trying to fight the pain, but it could not be helped. As he sat there, he wondered where he would go next, what he might do. His options had become limited. He wondered about Sarah, if she had worried about him. He thought about her a lot. But, alas, there was little he could do. What he knew he could not do is wait. He had to move. And soon. VI. Frost knelt down and placed his fingers into the dirt impression. He knew the footsteps of the Navajo by now. "Fresh tracks," he said aloud. Billy hovered over Frost's shoulder. "You sure?" Frost raised up, slapping his hands together, the soil falling back onto itself. "He's close," he said. "Real close." A snap of a twig sounded out from the wilderness. Frost ducked down, and Billy ducked with him. He turned back to Billy, and placed his index finger against his pursed lips. The index finger that he'd soon point in the direction in front of them. As they traveled forward, hunched down like lurkers, Billy's curiosity got the best of him. "You sure it's him," he whispered, and Frost shushed him once more. Huddled down, they continued through the darkening forest. Billy thought he heard something, and held back. He looked behind him, thinking something was following them. It was. "You!" Billy screamed foolishly, reaching for his gun. But he was young, and he was outmatched. The Navajo grabbed Billy from the front by his shoulder, and plunged his blade deep into his abdomen. Billy let out a gasp, and the Navajo let him go, the blade slowly slipping from his torso. Billy gasped for air. Frost spotted the Navajo, but the Navajo was quick, and disappeared into the woods. Frost ran to check on Billy, who labored to breathe. "I'm...I'm..." Billy tried to talk, but could not. Frost placed a hand on Billy's chest. "It's okay, son," he reassured. "Just calm down. It'll all be okay." Frost's hand slid off Billy's chest, and went with him into the woods. Frost had his pistol aimed, waiting for something, anything. He hadn't expected the bastard to be so fast. He was getting old, sure, but he hadn't experienced anything like this before. He wondered. Would he be able to kill this man? Or would this be the finality of his run? He continued, unknowing of what was to come. There was some noise, and the Navajo sprung to attack. Frost did not wait, and fired his Colt. He heard a grunt, but no scream, and the Navajo ran away. When he gave chase, he saw the Navajo holding his side. He had made the hit, he knew that much. And now, he had him where he wanted. The shot was clear. Frost pointed his gun. And yet, Frost hesitated. He knew not why, only that he couldn't bring himself to take second pressure. The Navajo disappeared into the forage, and Frost pursued. Soon, though, he realized his chance was lost. The Navajo was gone. Frost ran back to Billy. Billy's hand pressed down upon his stomach, as he cried out in pain. Frost forced Billy's hand away to see the damage, and did not like what he saw. But he kept his brave face upon, even though it was the most horrifying thing he could have done. Billy laughed, choking through his own blood. "Am I gonna be okay?" Frost could not bring himself to look in Billy's eyes. When he finally did, Billy smiled. "You're a good guy, you know that?" Frost turned away. Billy put his hand on Frost's shoulder. "You are." Frost's breathing slowed. He had no idea what to do. When he finally faced Billy, he was changed. "My wife and kids were slaughtered by those damn savages. Whole damn town was." Billy nodded. "I'm...sorry." Frost let out a sigh like no other. "Was my own damn fault. If I hadn't..." Billy grabbed Frost's hand. "Not your...fault." Frost looked into Billy's eyes. He didn't understand. Billy just smiled. "I thought my family dyin'...that was my fault. That me an' the war...that was why." Billy huffed, struggling to speak. Frost clutched Billy's hand tighter. "But...it's no one's fault. It's war, Alistair." Billy chuckled. "It's what we do." Frost tried to say something to defy him. "But..." "It's war." Billy smiled again, and so did Frost. They both began to laugh. Loudly. So loud it hung in the air forever. All that could be heard throughout the woods was their laughter. ****** Frost and Billy talked throughout the night. After a few hours, Billy finally stopped talking. Soon, Billy no longer expelled breath. And Frost buried him beneath the stars he so seemed to love. VII. Alexander staggered through the night-fallen woods. His body ached. He had barely slept. He had no idea where he was, nor of what was to come. Every step he took was more painful than the last. He took one last step, but his leg would no longer hold up, and he crashed to the ground. Face down in the dirt, he thought that this might be it. That the end had come. The Southerner would find him now, and exact revenge on what he had done. He had not wanted to kill the young man, but he had no choice. He could see the hate in the boy's eyes. Much like the hate in the bandit's. Like the hate in the man in Tuscon's. Footsteps came from the darkness, from all around. He could not pull himself to see, for the pain was too great. But he would will himself to. He lifted his head, and there they were. A pack of wolves had surrounded him. And yet, this brought him comfort. Better the animals finish this, he thought. Better he be brought back into the earth than shot down in cold blood. He found strength in himself he previously did not have. He found the power to stand, stand on his wounded leg. He waited, waited for them to come. Waited for them to devour. And yet, they simply stood and watched. A single wolf approached him. The wolf looked up into Alexander's eyes, and turned 'round. The other wolves followed. Were they calling for him, he wondered. What did they want? Alexander did not know why he decided to go with them, and yet there was still a part of him that did. The wolves stopped around a small shelter space, long abandoned and worn from time past. It was a place his ancestors had once lived, perhaps, or where they stopped to hunt. Alexander did not wonder, only accepted. He prepared the makeshift bed, and laid his worn body upon the ground. He took the time to re-dress his old wound, and address the new one. The wolves remained, surround him, protecting him. They stayed throughout the night. When Alexander woke in the morning, his guardians were no longer there. VIII. Frost had tracked the Navajo into the mountains of Arizona territory. The Navajo had gotten sloppy, not hiding his trace. The limp had become more noticeable, the path less errant. He had gotten him to where he wanted to. The trail led Frost into a canyon. If the Navajo had wanted to bottleneck him, this was a good a place as any. But Frost didn't care. At this point, he wanted this done with, more than anything. Frost had his rifle at his side, ready to fire at a moment's notice. Frost's trusty rifle. One that had felled many men, never betrayed. It would claim one more life, and be fed to the bottom of a riverbed. Frost remained cautious, moreso than he had ever been before. This was different for him. In battle, he was the aggressor. When he was tasked to bring in an outlaw, it was him on the point. And yet, here, by himself, after a wounded fugitive, he could not shake the feeling that he was the prey. Frost surveyed the landscape, and upon the hill, pointed not more than twenty yards in front of him, he saw the barrel of the gun. He stopped cold in his tracks. The nagging feeling of vulnerability he couldn't fight went with them. Damn Navajo went and got careless, he thought. His grip tightened on the wooden handle of the Winchester. It had come time to end this. And then, he stopped. Why now, he wondered. Had the Navajo simply given up now? He had fought to get this far. If it were as simple as giving up, why risk his life? Why not just walk out, and take the punishment? Maybe the Navajo knew that Frost was planning on killing him. He no longer cared that this particular savage had nothing to do with the past. Someone had to pay. This bastard was as good as any. Frost collected his thoughts and raised the rifle. He cautiously marched to the Navajo's careless showing. He'd kill the sonuva bitch. Take his scalp, collect the bounty, and quit on the spot. He'd had enough. He was tired. This would be his last kill, and then he would be off into the sunset for good. Just another gunslinger that time forgot. The spring in his step came just as fast, when he quelled it again. But this time, it was not the carelessness of his opponent. It was not the fight they had endured. It was not the Navajo's lost village, or his own all-too similar past. It was something else. None of this felt right. None of this made any damn sense. Suddenly, he thought of Billy. And then, he knew. IX. The blade punctured deep into the Southerner's side, and Alexander held his hand over his mouth to muffle the screams. The Southerner fought for a few moments, valiantly, jerking his body about, a desperate attempt to escape. But it was too late. The breath stopped slowly. The chest extended no more. The heart ceased to beat. The Southerner was dead. Alexander took pause, and rested along the rocks, the Southerner's body not far away. His wounds had become merely multiple choices to succumb to, be it the leg that by now was surely infected, or the bullet in his abdomen pumped the poison of his intestines into his stomach. It would not be long before he would choke to death on his own insides, a cruel way if any to die. But for what he had done many times before, and what he had done now, he knew this was the way it always was likely to be. But he would do one last thing before he went. It took four hours to bury the Southerner. The pain of metal brushing between flesh and bone was at times too much to bear, but it was the honorable thing for him to do. The man may have wanted to kill him, but the man was still a man. And without honor, man had nothing left. The burial took much out of Alexander. He felt the end draw near. "About time," Alexander wondered aloud. And so he sat next on the rocks not far from the Southerner's grave, and by then the sun had set. Alexander gazed up at the stars and the moon, and thought of the spirits in the sky. What had they planned for him? He was, in fact, a killer now. He was also once a warrior. A proud warrior. Yet he had nothing left, and was tired. He just wanted to go home now. The wolves appeared again, and again, they did not strike. They would not. He was, in fact, their kindred. They were there to protect. To watch. To guide. Alexander thought of Sarah's face and smiled. His eyes shut. His breath faded away into the night air. The wolves howled unto the sky. And that was how he waited for the end. "War is cruelty. There is no use trying to reform it. The crueler it is, the sooner it will be over." - General William Tecumseh Sherman
