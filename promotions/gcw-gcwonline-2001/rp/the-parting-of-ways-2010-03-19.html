The Parting of Ways

19 March 2010
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 03/19/10 A month ago, Giles was in a gym, training with wrestlers who were paid for their performances. It was their job, he was their preparation. Amateurs, mostly, whose passion for the sport was never under any doubt, and, occasionally, professionals who had sacrificed nearly everything else in their life for a few moments in the spotlight. T.A. Giles knew that it wasn't as simple as desiring fame, though; he had seen the effort that these men put into their calling -- the thing that they were good at: letting themselves be abused, mentally and physically, to entertain fans. There was something masochistic about the whole enterprise that made Giles uneasy, but he supported it, and every day, sun up to sun down, dedicated himself to it. He was unhappy with how wrestling had become the leper colony of sports, treated as if it was a community that was out of line with reality: looked upon as a kind of spectacle of violence where athletes are pawns to some corporate game. As far as Giles was concerned, it was the rest of the world that was fucked up and the wrestling community that was normal. At least they were honest with themselves and not leading some sort of perverted lie. The gym was his home, but after working there for almost ten years, he was growing tired of being a behind-the-scenes instrument. He had held the punching bag and played the sparring partner for a variety of wrestlers. Some were better than him, most were equal, some were worse. He didn't want to sell himself short any longer -- he knew he was capable of being a professional. The decision to make the jump was easier than he had thought. The gym he worked at -- for all its charm and solid reputation -- was beginning to suffer financially after the turn in the economy. Good friends of Giles's who had worked there were now out of work. The owner -- a man who Giles affectionately referred to as “Frankie” -- had only kept Giles and Frank's nephew around to help him look after the place. The building was in a bad way and needed repairs; customer use was in decline; and Frankie's ageing business sense was being outclassed by the management of newer gyms around town. Giles could not help but feel for the guy. He identified with the feeling that nothing was going your way: the feeling that the people around you are turning their backs on the very things that civilization relies on. On a personal level. Friendship. Charity. Giles was starting to lose faith: if hard-working guys like Frankie were unable to make ends meet, then the world was truly beginning to lose its way. Giles fondly remembered telling Frankie that he had signed a contract with GCW. Frankie had smiled warmly and embraced Giles like a son, despite all the hardships that he, Frankie, was going through. It was a rare sight, those days, to see old Frank crack a smile, and it made Giles hopeful that things might be better. And then, one fine morning -- Frankie was, of course, sad to see his most valued employee leave for greener pastures, but he understood that Giles's ambition extended beyond the walls of Frankie's Gym. Frankie wasn't very educated, but he had a wisdom about human nature that only comes with age. He knew about the thing that kept him going and kept Giles going: desire. Giles, a bit younger and maybe not as firm in his convictions, thought that love had something to do with it. The one thing that meant the most to him was his daughter, Jessica, a thirteen year old who was just beginning to test Giles's mettle as a father. Fortunately for him, Jessica's mother was still very much a part of their lives. Her name was Natalie, and though they were apart, Giles was still in love with her. It was all sorts of complicated, because she had felt the same; the problem was that it just didn't work, so their past and present complications sort of just hung in the balance while they both tried to go about their lives while raising a daughter to be not-fucked-up: not an easy task. Natalie had a busy work schedule. She was younger than Giles -- twenty-six -- and worked as an editor's assistant at Gold Publishing Inc, in downtown New York. It was Monday afternoon and one of the busiest days of the week, so, given that Giles was between jobs, he was supposed to pick Jessica up from school at four o'clock. It was two o'clock and Giles was doing sit-ups at home with “Message in a Bottle” on in the background -- for no particular reason other than it was good background music. Giles lived in a fourth-floor loft where the traffic outside didn't stop; however, it was an affordable place, one that Giles had found through a contact of Frankie's. It wasn't flashy, but it was functional, with only the necessary furniture. He didn't have a TV or a computer, only a stereo with a short stack of CDs; there were some mats that laid on the hard wood floor in the corner of the loft that Giles used to work out. In the other corner of the room there were shelves of books. In the kitchen, there was a couple of chairs around a dining table. Atop the table sat today's Globe and Mail and the New York Times, which Giles, if he had time, tried to read every day. There were some couches and a coffee table beside the kitchen; in the other corner of the room was Giles's bed. The place was tidy; Giles liked it that way. Finished his work out, Giles wiped his face with a towel and turned off the music. He had just grabbed an apple from the kitchen when the phone rang. “Hello?” “Travis.” A sigh of relief. “Thank god you're there.” It was Frankie. “Could you drop by the gym ... something came up, I'll tell you when you get here.” He sounded like he was making a concerted effort to keep his voice stable. Giles checked his watch -- it was almost 2:30, and he had to pick Jessica up from school at 4:00. “Yeah, Frankie, I'd love to, but I have to pick up Jess in about an hour. Can it wait?” “Well ... I -- I guess, I mean ... I could really use to --” Giles sighed. “Okay, Frankie. I'll be right down. But I can't be long ...” “Don't worry, this won't take long,” Frankie responded. Giles sighed -- his friend sounded different than normal. He was about to ask him what was up but decided it could wait until he got to the gym. As Giles grabbed his coat and his keys, he couldn't help but wonder -- What could be important enough that he wouldn't tell me it over the phone? Something was going on. *** Frankie's hand trembled, ever so slightly, as he reached for a glass that was half-full of amber liquid: single malt Scotch whiskey, a bottle of which was tucked in the bottom drawer of the old desk that Frank sat behind. It was usually reserved for visits by old acquaintances, but as of late Frank was frequently reaching for the drawer, hoping, silently, for an assured means of egress. He tossed back the remainder of the drink and savoured it -- the way it hit his tongue; the harsh swallow; the unpleasant aftertaste. There was something masochistic about Frank's retreat into alcoholism -- once before in his life he had frequently sought it as a medicine. Now, with that familiar feeling of worldly pressure, the drink had a renewed appeal. It was a relief -- if only a temporary one -- from the pain, the kind that could only be described as It, where It was something that hurt, all over, making it even harder to go about the seemingly-pointless but utterly necessary complex processes that everyday life required. And it, alcohol, ended up, in the long run, making it even harder to participate in those unsexy labours, day after day, so Frankie and anyone else who had ever experienced It just gets kind of stuck in this absurd catch-22 that came with a guarantee of It. As in the pain. Frankie reached for the drawer and pulled out the glass bottle. Even though it was almost empty, it felt like a heavy weight in his hand. He emptied the remainder of the liquid into his glass and set the bottle on his desk. He was having a bit more difficulty steadying his hand, now, but he moved it toward the glass, and, before it was too late, he choked back the harsh drink. In the process of taking the glass away from his lips it had slipped out of his hand and dropped to the floor, smashing into pieces. It was a pitiful sight: to see a man at his very lowest, unable to keep it together long enough to not make a fool out of himself. The saddest part was that there was no one around for him to make a fool of himself to. Today, there was no one in Frankie's Gym to see its owner with Scotch dribbled down the front of his t-shirt, an empty bottle in front of him, and a broken glass that Frankie had failed to keep control of. Of course, the whole difficulty with Frankie's isolation and pain and self-medication goes beyond his physical appearance or reputation or even his bodily health. Of course it wasn't good for him, and he knew it, but the Really Bad consequence was that it made him incapable of connecting with other people on a meaningful level. The whole thing had started because of a sort of loneliness and self-loathing, and, expectedly, the alcohol problem had just increased that loneliness and sense of self-loathing. Anybody who tried to Be Let “Into” this guy's core, his inner being, was met with resistance, a kind of hard shell or defencive wall that Frankie had built around his-self. Frankie's eyes were watering from the harsh whiskey as he thought about his nephew, Danny, who was like a son to him. Frankie always saw himself as a father figure for the twenty-year old, but he wasn't sure who needed who more: whether Danny needed him or he needed Danny. He figured it was a little bit of both, if not weighted evenly. It was Danny and guys like Giles who made Frankie keep on being the Frankie that his friends and relatives knew and loved, the kind old guy whose experience gave him a wisdom about life that he was always willing to share. Frankie was always sharing -- his current financial troubles were, in part, due to some loans he had given to friends and didn't see back. He claimed that it didn't bother him, which Giles and Danny believed, kind of, but it caused a sort of chain reaction that meant the worse for Frankie. In other words, the unpaid loans led to a tighter money situation, which led to a number of late nights that Frankie spent poring over his accounting ledgers. All the while he had to maintain his usual friendly demeanour, something that was slowly becoming a facade, one that didn't bear any relationship to the real Frankie, who, especially for an older man, had more than enough stress to strain his heart. So, with trying to keep up his usual image, and all those late nights, Frankie was quite simply exhausted. That, and Danny and Giles weren't around as much anymore to help man the gym. And so there he was, now, in his current state, feeling like he no longer had any value to anyone. He was wrong, of course, and even he knew that his nephew, Danny, would be the one to suffer if Frankie kept on being like this. And yet, he couldn't bring himself to resolve, right then and there, to quit drinking -- to pull himself together, to pick himself up, as they say, by the ol' bootstraps. Frankie was on the verge of tears and in a stupor when the door to his office opened. It was exactly who he wanted to see, but not who he wanted to be seen by in this state. As Giles entered the office, as he normally would, Frankie collected himself and smiled, the sort of forced expression that might be mistaken for a grimace. Of course, a facial grimace would be a more honest gesture, but Frankie preferred to just lock away those emotions. The kind that indicated any weakness. The fortress that Frankie had built around his-self, though, was starting to crumble, and Giles could sense it. He looked at Frank suspiciously, then at the broken glass on the floor. “Are you okay, Frank? No offence, but you sounded sort of emotional over the phone. That's not like you.” Frankie choked out a kind of painful laugh, then said, “Don't bother, it's nothing. Sit down, would you? Stay a while.” Frankie got up from his chair and grabbed a broom from the back of his office. He started sweeping up the broken glass. “Getting clumsy in my old age. Do yourself a favour and never get old, okay? I'm about as useful as a child.” Giles laughed. “Gimme a break, Frank. You still work harder and get more done than most of these asshats running around this city. No wonder the economy is in the dumpster -- everybody consuming, nobody producing. Pretty sad. Nobody knows the value of good hard work and making an honest living anymore.” Frankie put the broken glass in an empty box beside his desk. He walked to the back corner of the room where there was a cupboard -- from it, he procured a new bottle of whisky and two glasses. Already pouring the drinks, Frankie asked, “Want a nightcap?” Giles looked on and laughed in disbelief. “A nightcap? In the middle of the afternoon? C'mon, Frankie. We're not that old and jaded.” “Maybe you're not, but I am. Come on, for me. Who knows how much time we'll have left.” Giles put a hand around the half-glass of whiskey and pulled it toward himself. He raised an eyebrow and coolly moved his fingers around the rim of the glass. “You're funny, Frank. What's that supposed to mean?” “Well, with you going on to be a big wrestling star now, who knows what the future will hold?” He smiled. “But to hell with it, right?” He raised his glass. “To the future and to GCW.” Giles, looking suspicious, nevertheless clinked his glass against Frank's and took a small sip of the harsh liquid. He detected some thinly veiled sarcasm in Frank's voice as he made the toast, but Giles didn't pursue the subject. He shifted in his seat. “Well, Frank, I'm glad that you thought of me when you started this afternoon soirée, but I find it hard to believe that you invited me down here just to have a drinking buddy.” He paused and thought about it. “Though if that's the case, I'm happy to oblige.” Frank's eyes flickered and he smiled, raising his glass to his lips. The smile didn't last long -- Frank's face was overtook by a somber look. He took a mouthful of whisky and swallowed it. He leaned forward, putting his elbows on the desk. “It was ten years to this day that Sarah died.” Sarah was Frank's daughter; she was killed in a traffic accident -- a hit and run that had never been resolved. Despite an extremely difficult couple of years that Frank had following the death -- including a separation with his wife of twenty years -- Frank had resolved to move on with his life. Danny played a big role in this and, whether he knew it or not, had helped Frankie move past that tragic day. This year, however, the old wound was reopened and the pain he had felt in those fateful moments were coming back with vigour. Frank looked at Giles. Giles's face was expressionless -- he looked at the desk, unsure of how to respond. His fingers tapped gently against the half-finished glass of whisky. “I'm sorry, Frank.” Frank shook his head, “It's been ten years, Travis. You don't have to be sorry to me.” “No, I meant that I'm sorry for not remembering.” Giles took a sip of his alcohol to avert the eyes of his mentor. Frank wasn't upset with Giles. His problems went further back -- deeper into his personal history -- than this short encounter with Travis. He didn't blame Giles. Frank's problem was that he only blamed himself, despite all the evidence that suggested hardly any of it was, in fact, his fault. That was the nature of Frank, a negative consequence of a kind of unwavering self-reliance -- a trait that he had tried, successfully, to pass on to both Giles and Danny. This practical philosophy had served Frank and Giles well in life, surviving many of the emotional challenges that everyday life afforded, but even stoic Frank could not withstand the emotional holocaust that losing a daughter can wreak on a person. After a long moment of silence, Frankie looked at his watch. “It's almost four o'clock. You should pick up your daughter.” Giles, somber-faced, got up from his chair and headed toward the door. Frankie stopped him: “Hold on a second ...” Giles turned around. “Yeah?” “Do me a favour, would you?” “Anything.” “Don't let go of her.” Giles's hand instinctively gripped the door and he looked absently at the floor while Frankie stared right at him. Giles nodded as a sort of thank-you, then left the office. The weight of Frankie's statement hadn't quite hit him yet, but it would -- -- It would. *** Jessica was thirteen years old and, despite the fact that both of her parents worked in jobs that only provided modest income, attended a private school. Her grandparents on Natalie's side had made sure of that -- they were sort of the stabilizing component of whatever weird, imbalanced “family” that Giles, Natalie, and Jessica were. And so Jessica, a little spoiled but, compared to the other girls in her year, not-spoiled-at-all -- more like born with a bronze spoon rather than a silver -- was sort of an outsider among daughters and sons from well-to-do families. This well-to-doness felt championed as an ideal at this institution, but Jessica, somewhat perceptive for a thirteen year old, recognized that all was not well on the nuclear family front. This meant that Natalie and Giles didn't have to tolerate an insufferable, needy child, but it also meant that Jessica was in the kind of position where she had to make friends with girls whose parents made her uneasy. The kind of people who pitted their child against other parents' children -- who saw accomplishments and accolades as measurements of childrens' worth and success, weighing and comparing them like one would value a car or a house on how much it's worth, whether it's good enough or just acceptable, how much is the damage deposit, &c. Jessica was walking out of the school with her best friend, Kandace. As always, Kandace's mom was waiting for her, punctual and ready to treat her daughter with the kind of affection that was both enviable and suspect. This kind of behaviour made Jessica uncomfortable on two levels: first, she didn't think it was meaningful; second, she sometimes wished that one of her parents would be waiting for her like that, on time, every day. The contradictory nature of such a reaction really frustrated a perceptive girl like Jessica. She didn't want to be cliche, but yet, and yet ... “Hi Jessica,” Kandace's mother said, smile across her face. “Do you need a ride home again? You don't have to ask -- of course I'm willing, anything for a friend of Kandi's!” She laughed. There might have been sarcasm attached to her statement, but it was a laugh for the sake of laughing, and Jessica just shuffled her feet uncomfortably. “No thanks, Mrs. Butler -- my dad is coming to pick me up, he'll be here soon,” Jessica said, glancing down the street, hoping to see Giles's car. “C'mon, Jess, you can come over for supper and call him from my house!” Kandace seemed excited, and Jessica liked that it was genuine. Jessica smiled, then hesitated, but eventually said, “Why not? He won't mind.” The three girls joyfully got into Mrs. Butler's car. *** Two hours later It was the kind of lounge where working-class people -- mostly middle-aged men -- gathered after work to have a few beers. The music or the chatter wasn't loud; most of them preferred that. They wanted to soak in some relaxation after another day on the job. Giles, though jobless, was here because it was one of his favourite hangouts. Especially after a day like today -- things hadn't exactly gone as planned with picking up Jessica, and he was expecting to get an earful from Natalie later on. He figured that having a few beer in him wasn't such a bad idea. It wasn't the first time that he had let her down. He finished his beer and called for another one. The bartender, a middle-aged, quiet guy with a salt-and-pepper beard, sauntered over. He was drying a glass with a dishtowel -- he set that aside and got his customer a drink. “Thanks, Bryan.” “No problem.” Bryan grabbed another glass from the rack and began drying it, then looked at Giles suspiciously. “Everything alright?” Giles shrugged, taking a sip of his beer. “I'm not really sure anymore, man.” He took another sip and a serious look crossed his face. “It's hard to be optimistic, y'know.” Bryan smiled, setting down the dried glass. “Yeah, I hear ya.” Giles's cell phone started ringing. He looked at it: incoming call from Danny. He answered it. The conversation didn't last long, but it didn't need to. Something strange was happening at Frankie's Gym -- the doors were locked and Frankie wasn't home or answering his phone. Giles, being the only other one with a key, thanked Bryan with a tip and headed back to the gym. *** When he got there, Danny was outside having a smoke with a regular customer that Giles recognized. They made small talk over the sound of traffic. Giles got out of his car and went to the front door of the building, where they were standing. He had his keys out. “So, no idea what's going on?” Danny shrugged and took a drag. “No idea. I tried to get ahold of 'im but he ain't answering. You know Frankie, though. Never was much for telephones.” Giles frowned. “Is it just me or has Frankie been a bit off lately?” Danny looked at the sidewalk and took another drag. He hesitated, then looked at Giles. They made eye contact momentarily and then Danny looked away, shaking his head. “I dunno, man. I'm kinda in the same boat as you -- not around as much anymore, y'know? Haven't been in to work for a couple days 'cause I'm trying to set up some gigs around town. Me and the boys have a couple of songs ready, did I tell you that?” Giles shook his head. “You'll have to tell me all about it, later. Let's see what the old guy is up to.” Danny nodded. “You go ahead. I'ma finish this smoke and be right in.” Giles unlocked and opened the door. The main lights in the gym were off but natural light -- however little of it there was -- streamed in from the high windows. It was still dark enough, though, for Giles to notice that there was artificial light coming from a door that was ajar in the back corner of the building. Giles walked into Frankie's modest office, not expecting to find anything. “Frankie?” Giles knew him well -- better than anyone -- and he knew that Frankie was not a surprising person. He opened the door, preparing to see Frankie either passed out or not there at all. What he saw almost made Giles lose his footing. There was Frankie, dangling from the ceiling, rope around his neck. Below him was a chair that had been knocked over in the moments before his death. Frankie had been crushed by the almighty weight of the world, of himself, of having too much and not enough. Giles, overwhelmed, fell to his knees and put his hands in his face, unable to stop himself from sobbing. It was a half-sad, half-angry sort of cry: angry, not at Frankie, but at the conditions that had surrounded this final act of self-erasure. The eternal kind. Angry at the world, a world that Giles could not understand, and a world that Frankie had failed to understand. A world that was full of so much joy and happiness and love but also with anger and hatred and sadness. There it was, right in front of his eyes: a reason to fight but also a reason not to fight. An incentive to do something or do nothing. At this point, Giles just wasn't sure. Acknowledgments 1)The description of Frankie's condition as “It” was inspired by David Foster Wallace's description of depression in Infinite Jest. 2)The phrase “assured means of egress” is basically lifted word-for-word from Michael Chabon's essay, “Getting Out,” which can be found in his non-fiction collection Manhood for Amateurs.
