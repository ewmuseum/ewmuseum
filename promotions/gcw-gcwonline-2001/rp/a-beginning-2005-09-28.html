A beginning.

28 September 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: September 28, 2005 March 1990. Another year, another school, another reality. Rei could never get used to that. Sitting at his desk, staring out into an abyss of a world he had yet to experience, Rei knew there was more to life than arithmetic. Pronouns and adjectives. The thought of the immensity of it all made his eyes twinkle with wonder. “All together, class ? The quick, brown fox jumps over the lazy, sleeping dog. Now, let’s pick out the adjectives.” Rei stared at the teacher. Mrs. Sheila Miller. He could recognize her beauty at the ripe age of ten. Soft blonde hair tied back into an innocent ponytail. Tan face and deep blue eyes that always made focus his gaze to his shoe laces self-consciously. She was American by nationality and her California beach appearance reminded Rei of Beverly Hills 90210. He smiled at the thought. It was a show he watched when his parents weren’t regarding him with eyes of a hawk. “Ben, you go first.” Rei watched Mrs. Miller’s graceful movements, in awe of her fluidity, as he was tapped on the shoulder. He didn’t respond as he turned around. “I’m gonna tell the teacher you like her boobs if you don’t give me your lunch money.” Pete somebody. Rei couldn’t remember his last name and didn’t really care. Pete was a bully, always threatening kids smaller than him. It didn’t help that Rei was the smallest kid in his class. Petey, as he was called by Mrs. Miller, was a parasite and reminded Rei of a fat parrot. Always repeating himself and had a laugh like a bird call. “Yeah, I’m gonna tell the teacher you like her boobs. They’re squishy.” Rei imagined Pete in a cage, ruffling his feathers. He thought about feeding him crackers and listening to him talk. It was a musing he rather enjoyed. A visionary and a dreamer, Rei focused much of his time on daydreams and entertaining prepubescent thoughts of life and the world. A child’s wisdom. He was always at his desk, but rarely in the classroom. Imagination was his escape from the harsh existence. “Give me your lunch money now, or you’re dead.” Rei frowned, saying nothing. Pete was the kid nobody liked, but still played with out of fear. He was tolerated only because he was gargantuan compared to others. Fear was losing its touch with Rei. Pete smiled like a television clown. Rei smiled inwardly as Pete’s nose transformed into the huge red ball, squeaking like a toy. All around him, Rei could hear the the laughs of children. Pete reached over a meaty arm and flicked Rei’s ear, bringing him back to the world of English class. Rei’s ear throbbed, somewhat. The nose and laughter vanished abruptly. “Did you hear me, stupid?” Rei had heard him, but his care was outmatched. His discipline kept him from confrontation and his imagination, from responding. The only thing Rei really wanted was to turn around, seeing Mrs. Miller, watching her with elation as she instructed Ben on adjectives. Pete continued to pat Rei, contending for his attention. “My dad says Chinese people belong in China.” Rei was used to such. With every new school and every new city came the same set of tribulations that plagued his childhood. He couldn’t get used to the almost habitual relocation. His father always taking the next job that offered more money or more benefits. Rei didn’t understand, nor was he expected to. When they conversed about such topics, they chatted in Korean, which he had never learned. The only information Rei acquired about his Korean roots was in library books and encyclopedias. It left him feeling disconnected because of that. Feeling denied because his parents showered him with American heritage, instead. Jumbo hot dogs, baseball, and pool parties. Rei disliked it all. He knew he wasn’t American and all of the other kids went out of their way to to remind him. Pete was a constant. Rei ignored him and turned back around, catching his scowl on the way. “Very good, Ben. I see you’ve been paying attention.” Mrs. Miller winked at Ben, who blushed crimson. Rei would have fallen out of his chair to the uproar of the class. He admired Ben from afar, peering as Ben sat down, beaming with pride. Mrs. Miller had a quality about her that made even the saddest of kids smile golden. Rei had witnessed many upset kids immediately cut the tears after a warm Mrs. Miller smile. It almost made their tears fake, as if they were simply eliciting attention. In truth, most probably were. Rei often avoided packing his books just for those few extra minutes with Mrs. Miller. Those moments where she helped him pack his belongings, kneeling on the floor beside him. He envisioned her blonde hair billowing in the wind, dramatic emphasis consuming his young curiosity. “Rei, do you need help with that?” The voice of an angel. The heavenly glow surrounding her purity made Rei want to stare in amazement. A grin contorted on her lips, and she motioned for Rei to come closer. “I’ll make it all better, Rei. Come closer.” Index finger like a dancing snake enticing his body. He glided across the floor like a ghost, obliging her summon. As he drew closer, he felt the soft stroking of her fine hair against his increasingly flush face. Her arms wrapped around him and she pulled him up to her chest, gripping him tightly and gently. It took another painful ear flick from Pete to shake the fantasy. Staring at Mrs. Miller, Rei noticed her grinning. “Are you alright, Rei” You looked flushed.” Muffled snickers from the class. Rei wondered how long he had been daydreaming. Glancing beside him, he noticed Andrew sketching a crude and nude Mrs. Miller. “Rei?” Rei shook his head and smiled slightly at Mrs. Miller. “Sorry, Mrs. Miller. May I use the restroom?” He’d have to use a pass, he knew. They were allowed three extra bathroom breaks a week, besides the collective ones they all took together. Rei already used two, and Mrs. Miller kept up accordingly. A teacher foremost, she utilized nearly every minute of her class time teaching the fundamentals of literature. “Okay, Rei, but this is your last one for the week. Are you sure you want to use it?” He was the center of attention, he saw. Caught in the spotlight, he loathed it more than he did the school mashed potatoes. Rei hated when Mrs. Miller opted to stay by the blackboard, speaking to him from across the room. All eyes turned to him, seeking mindless refuge away from their school work. He caught the lingering smiles of Roberto and Tony, sitting side by side. They never even acknowledged his existence and Rei didn’t mind. That was less negativity he might have to soak up. Feeling Pete’s gaze on his back, he responded. “Yes, ma’am, I’m sure. I can’t hold it, anymore.” That was a lie. As much as Rei hated lying to Mrs. Miller, he needed to get out of his class. His focus was gone and he needed temporary distraction. Slowly rising up, he winced from pain as Pete’s knuckle ripped down his spine. Outwardly, his smile barely rippled. Wiping bits of paper graffiti Pete had sprinkled on him earlier to the linoleum, Rei brushed at his navy blue polo shirt. Mrs. Miller moved gracefully to her desk. Even her blood was worth bottling. Rei glanced down as he passed Jeff and Brian, too preoccupied with sketching on their desk to be distracted. Sean was reading a book while he waited on Mrs. Miller. Rei knew all of his classmates? names, but it was out of necessity rather than popularity. Popularity came in the form of teasing and ridicule by most of them for Rei. Mrs. Miller enforced that everyone learn each other’s names at the beginning of the year. Rei had no problems learning them, just like he had little problems learning anything else. But he hardly spoke to anyone besides Mrs. Miller. The students who didn’t tease him thought he had a contagious disease and usually regarded him with stifled glances. The same treatment in altered dosage. Their classroom was always in disarray, Rei thought. Books scattered all over the floor, orange dots on their spines staring up at Rei like prying eyes. Notebook paper and work sheets crumpled and ragged lying about. Rei spotted an apple shaped note handwritten in red ink as he stepped over everything. Notes of good conduct that Mrs. Miller loved writing. He had gotten a few himself, which he proudly showed his parents. The family refrigerator was host for his good conduct advertisement. Book bags were situated by desks depicting super heroes. Tom and Jerry, Johnny Quest and others. Rei’s was another piece of Korean heritage he had, a bag adorned with the Korean alphabet and symbols. He hoped one day to be able to read it. Just as Rei’s foot stepped into ray of sunlight shining from a pair of large windows, it landed and slipped on a small, hardback copy of Doctor Dolittle, sending him slowly sprawling to the hard floor surface. He laid there, beside the desks of Kristian and Jayce, as the room exploded in laughter. Mrs. Miller looked up from paperwork on her desk, pressing a finger to her lips as she rose to help her fallen soldier. That’s what Rei considered himself as he lay on his back. A wounded soldier on the battlefield of youth. He was helped to his feet, but wished to be buried beneath the floor. The giggles of the class slowly ceased as Rei took the pass and languidly strided to the door. Receiving one last smile and wink from Mrs. Miller, he opened the door with a click and stepped into an alternate reality. I am my own alternate reality. A world governed by distorted principles. A universe within itself. I won’t elaborate further. Memories of my not so humble childhood arrive in fractions. Fragments. Slapping my wrists, turning the skin an irritated red. The common denominator in my youth is my discipline. At a very a young age, martial arts was used as a tool to embed morals and stability. My father paid for exclusive instruction, never having the time to teach me himself. He was a business executive. An associate of sorts. Think Stephen Caldera with less influence, smaller frame and slanted eyes. Intelligent and charming, he only passed the first trait on to me. Charm was lost from Red Riding Hood’s basket as she was fleeing from the wolf. Palm smash. Punch. Thrust kick. I’m training. Swift motions slicing the air with elegance. I play out the sound effects in my head, along with a soundtrack of heavy metal orchestra. Open hand chop. Elbow strike. Palm thrust. Martial arts is more to me, now. The line of discipline and stability blurred and merged. My body and mind sculpted into a powerful weapon. I am a soldier, now. The dramatic front line is my turf, peeking out from the sandbags at the flanking enemy. Global Championship Wrestling is the hazy, gun smoke curtained field. Kidney strike. Spinning elbow smash. Straight closed fist. Careful, precise blows. Designed for target specific areas. Neutralization of their power and the forced indomitable will of mine. The set up for the climax of an Asian action flick, indeed. Call me Rei. I speak a four word sentence in English and move my mouth for a paragraph. Subtitles included. Anyway, the subject of my parents, is touchy. Unlike bar tales of lost love with broken bottles and shattered hearts, it’s a discussion I often try not to think about. But against my wishes, it’s a pie in the face. The whipping topping lingering on waiting for me to lick away. Short kick. High knee. Left cross punch. They’re not dead. Not in the physical sense. They’re breathing Korean air, while I’m inhaling United States and Canadian air. I voted to stay after graduating high school, when they decided to pack up and go back to their hometown. It’s ironic that after years of wanting Korea, I suddenly decided not to go when given the perfect opportunity. We parted ways and that’s how the story goes. An autobiography in a few pages. It’s a short monologue that can describe the life of Rei Tiu. I’m jealous. Everyone else dedicates hundreds of pages and hours of boring interview time. I’ve delivered mine in a promo minute. In the distance, I hear the soft swelling of violins. Play a song for me. Spin kick. Leg sweep. Spinning right cross. Follow-up elbow smash. I’m still reeling from the match with Zodiac. Insert random good guy compliment here. I’m just not in the mood. I never underestimate anyone as a rule. As a result, I celebrate victory more often than others. Respect breeds victory. I am victorious. Call me Rei. Learn martial arts by watching my interviews. Ducking hook. Elbow follow in. Spinning elbow smash. Palm strike. Fallout is up next. Rei Tiu against Aiden Jackson. The odds are probably stacked against me. Not that I mind. I do my best work in the face of beautiful opposition. My poetic dance of death carrying with it the crackling flames of determination. Aiden Jackson could be the next Global Championship Wrestling cruiserweight champion. But I intend to make him earn it. The hallway was colorful, adorned with the spirit of the school. Hand drawn posters pasted about, with many different shapes and sizes of elementary school crafted graffiti hanging from the ceiling. Stars cut from thick construction paper all over the walls announced star students . Some of them, Rei knew, were the very ones who made his first year at Piedmont Elementary a living hell. Star students, indeed. Despite the disconnection from most of the children, Rei maintained exceptional behavior an couldn’t count on hands and feet how many smiley faces he had next to his name in Mrs. Miller’s grade book. Mr. Happy face, he sometimes thought. Ahead of him, a janitor by the name of Charlie Hall was busy sorting through cleaning supplies. His suit was old and stained, years of wear and abuse evident to the world. A tall black man, Rei could only guess at his age. He seemed slow-minded, but a certain glint in his eyes suggested a hidden wise man waiting to speak his gospel. Charlie Hall’s face was burned, though none of the kids actually knew why. Typical fables spread amongst the masses. He lit himself on fire because he wanted to be the human torch. He was the real Freddy Kreuger. Rei learned fast to accept as truth only half the time, and even then, one had to tread throughout a river of falsified details. Charlie waved, rag in hand, as Rei passed. He smiled and waved back, feeling a connection with the man he didn’t feel with his own parents. It was strange, but it was there. He felt compassion for Charlie Hall. They were both mysterious strangers in a world that frowns on mystique. Down the hall, two pretty girls Rei knew as Carrie and Steph came out of the girl’s restroom, giggling quietly. Steph, as she was called by friends, had been nice to Rei numerous times, and Rei was relieved to see two friendly faces. Besides Andrew, Carrie was another student who went out of her way to meet Rei. He remembered her introduction, her unpredictability, and how she said hi one second, and shot off in another direction the next instant. She ahd complimented his shoes. As he passed them, they both looked up and smiled, waving. Rei winked and headed for the door of the boy’s room, listening to them giggle, again. As he pushed inside, the unpleasant aroma of bleach and stale urine hit him like a tidal wave. Apparently, Charlie had just finished cleaning, the smell of supplies still somewhat burning his nostrils. The floors were always clean. Kids could say what they wanted, but Charlie Hall was Mr. Clean, Rei thought. He was an artist with the mop. Rei scuffed his black converse shoes lightly on the bathroom tile, feeling the sleek smoothness of its surface. Spotting the pearly white urinals, he went to the sink, staring at himself in the mirror. He had to stand on his tip-toes. It had recently been cleaned, too; a small streak of Windex still visible in the lower corner. Rei studied his Korean features, foreign even to him. He was born in Korea, but never experienced anything over what a single year of age allowed. His parents, tough still maintaining some aspects of Korean ethics, seemed to want to dictate and filter out Korean roots. Rei felt they wanted him to be like a lot of blue-eyed American children, but that wasn’t what bothered him. He had never known different, so American heritage was his heritage. Rei didn’t look like the rest of the children, save Chris, who was an Asian at that school attending classes for kids with special needs. Because of his looks, Rei was judged. Different. Alien. Treated as if he had special needs, too. Some of the kids treated him like a spy, as if he were adopting their culture in order to expose a weakness. When he tried explaining that he was only born in Korea to Korean parents, he got a faceful of sand and a ripped shirt. He remembered disposing of it to avoid parent-teacher conferences. No tears. No problems. With the small exception of a handful of students and Mrs. Miller, no one else gave him a chance. Even some of the other teaches slowed their speech patterns to accommodate Rei, they thought. Rei smiled, knowing that he sometimes took advantage of that. Rei ran his small hands over his face, fingers twirling his jet black hair trimmed in a bowl-style cut. He studied his clothes, his navy blue polo shirt and khaki shorts looking like a uniform. Suddenly turning around, he lashed out at the air with lightning kicks and punches. Palm smash, punch, thrust kick. Martial arts was his discipline. It was his way of dealing with the ambiguities of constant relocation and alienation. Open hand chop, elbow strike, palm thrust. He had been taking classes under the guidance of Master Lee Kim since they’d lived in Illinois for almost three years. Before that, his instructor had been an American man whose name Rei can’t recall now. Illinois was where his family had stayed the longest, coming from Arizona. Before that, Oregon was their home. Rei felt it was only a matter of time before they moved, again. Kidney strike, spinning elbow smash, straight closed fist. In mid combo, the bathroom door busted open. A kid with a nametag that said he was Phillip ran in, stopping short and staring at Rei. No words exchanged, Phillip went to the urinal. Rei turned his gaze to the mirror again, stepping back into his own world. When Phil spoke, his voice was surprisingly deep. “A Chinese power ranger.” Rei looked at Phil as he zipped his pants and didn’t flush the toilet. Phil laughed as he left, without washing his hands. Again. Rei shook his head, turning back to his reflection. He was patient. One day, he wanted to make in impact. He wanted to witness Korea. All kinds of things. Washing his hands, he splashed the mirror with water, watching as it ran down the reflection of himself. One day, Rei Tiu would be more than the small asian kid. Okay. I’ll admit that was corny. Too bad. I can’t control the thoughts in my head. Because of that, you get to enjoy every one of them with no editing. Call me Rei. The director’s cut. I’m back to the beginning. Caldera’s universal remote rewinding my life and leaving me with nervous anticipation. D’j? vu, right? Indeed. I feel like I’ve even said that, before. Anyway, another hotel room. As nice as the last, subtracting the elegant fruit bowl. GCW treats its superstars like kings. If they have a psychologist, they’re in for a real treat. The silhouette of the butterfly is really me organizing the genocide of the American culture. I’m kidding, of course. More Reicism. I’m sitting on the bed, letting the aura of doing nothing sprinkle over me. My sleeveless gray shirt is sweaty from training. Button-up track pants clinging to my legs. I run my hands over my scalp, pushing my hair out of my face. Long day, tomorrow. The GCW Cruiserweight Title is sifting through the fine print, deciding who it wants to go home with. It’s a lady picking the most charming, swooning drunk. I can only promise her that our lives together will be miserable and demanding. I’m not sure what Jackson can offer. I’ll still hide in my attic if I win. Stage fright to a minor degree. I can hide it well, thankfully. But it’s there. When the music hits, the cheering of the fans. It’s a rush as well as nerve-wracking for me. Every suplex and spin kick. My hands are shaking. A knight facing a mighty dragon alone. A boxer who can’t escape the vastness of his domain. Zodiac knew this in our match. I’m sure Aiden will, too. I am Rei. The shy kid who blushed when someone said penis. And I need sleep. A hard day of training before the big match and I’m worn. I glance at my packed bag, wondering if there’s room for a GCW championship. I’ll make room, I decide. There’s always space for success.
