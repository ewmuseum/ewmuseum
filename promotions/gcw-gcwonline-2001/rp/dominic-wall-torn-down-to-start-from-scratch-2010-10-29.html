Dominic Wall: Torn Down to Start from Scratch

29 October 2010
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 10/29/10 You don't get dropped off like when you were a kid going to school. They send a bus to pick you up, and they make sure you are treated the same as everyone else. It starts there, on that bus, where you're told where to go, where to sit, and you have nothing to look at except your hometown that you're leaving behind. That hometown you're ready to lay down and die to protect. That hometown you're willing to surrender at least four years of your life to keep safe, the one that you're ready to give up individuality and do as you're told because that's what needs to be done. The man at the front of the bus is loud because he needs to let any miscreants know that as of right now, not when they get to the base or when they get their papers or when tomorrow comes, but right now they are in The Army. Capital 'T,' capital 'A,' it is a living entity. "Sir yes Sir!" everybody shouts and he says not to call him 'sir' because he's not an officer. The bus pulls up and the old frame squeaks and creaks because the fucking thing has been in service since the Vietnam War. Everyone on the trip has been wondering who sat in these seats before us. Was that guy's dad in that war? Was there a kid in my seat that ended up in Special Forces? Twenty years ago was there some fat slob sitting across from where I am, eyes wide with terror like he was sleeping for six months and just woke up on a bus being carted off to the military for processing? Were the brown-noses seated in the front and the jackoffs seated in the back, like high school? How many people that sat in my seat have died in combat? "Everybody out!" and we're hoofing it double time and standing in line. A couple needles in the arm and my body is immune to just about every disease the United States of America can afford to make me immune to. Everyone gets the same haircut, everyone gets the same clothes, everyone gets the same food, everyone gets the same treatment. Right out of the gates and we're taught to march and we have to learn the lingo and the slang and keep up or else it's Shitsville and we know it. We all know it because they teach us all same thing at the same time. Every order is barked at us and some people laugh once. They don't laugh again. "Sir yes Sir!" everybody shouts and the sarge tells us to fuck ourselves and never call him 'sir' because he works for a living. It's lights out when they say it's lights out. It's time to get up when they say it's time to get up. It's chow time when it's chow time, it doesn't matter if your tummy hurts or you don't like the food or you just want to go home. You want some private time to yourself? There's night watch for that. Want it during the day? You get a couple minutes to yourself in the latrine. Just don't use it to get too familiar with yourself too often; the fat guy clogged the drain with hair and semen. He found himself cleaning the latrines every day for the rest of basic training. Sarge doesn't like clogged drains, and he doesn't like his soldiers jacking off, so he barks till the fat guy's dick shrivels up inside of him and it probably doesn't work anymore. "Yes Drill Sergeant!" everybody shouts and we get ready to run a few miles. If the fat guy's got energy to touch himself, he's got energy to run. And if the fat guy's got energy to run, the rest of us definitely have energy to run. It's the same across the board in basic training because The Army needs us to know we are all the same. Some of us are going to be mechanics, some of us are going to be desk clerks, some of us are going to be artillery, some of us are going to be grunts, and some of us are going to be casualties. But we all start out the same. We are all given equal footing to start on, and what we become is entirely up to us. In The Army, that's the only constant. You are responsible for everything that happens to you. ---- "Do you think you're something special, Private Wall?" "I am what I make myself, Drill Sergeant!" Sarge asked if anybody had any fighting experience, wanted to be a participant in his hand to hand combat training. He wanted to know if any of us had the balls to step in and let him beat on us for a little while. Most of all, though, he wanted to knock some sense into the cockiest one of us. When I said I'd had some training his eyes lit up and he salivated a little bit. Nothing like kicking around some high school quarterback that thought he was tough shit because of some bullshit Tai Kwan Do school. "You've just got all the answers, don't you Wall?" "I only answer honestly, Drill Sergeant!" "Then answer me this, Wall. Do you think you can take me down?" From day one, I've never hestitated when the instructors asked me a question. Where are you from? Why are you here? Are you a queer? Are you afraid of queers? Are you afraid of insugents? Are you afraid of dying? But for the first time, there was a notable pause in my answer. In that half second of total silence, I could tell my classmates were waiting for some form of insubordination or admittance to being a pussy. Because those are the only two things I could do; either admit I'm a pussy or call Sarge a pussy. "Drill Sergeant! It is my belief that I am able to take the drill sergeant down!" Always err on the side of confidence, but always proceed on the side of caution. That's Army living. At the very least, I just made Sergeant's day with that answer. He gets to embarrass the quarterback. "Alright then. Let's see what you've got, Wall." There is no smile on his face, but there is enjoyment in his voice. He wouldn't be a drill sergeant if he didn't love his job, and right now his job is to teach us how to hurt someone, starting with me. Sarge takes a defensive stance before I move a muscle, and all the sudden it's do or die. There's nowhere to run and no way out of what I've done, and what I have managed to learn comes brimming to the surface. A couple years of amateur mixed martial arts training, a few years of boxing, a handful of fights in high school, and the competitive edge I sharpened on the football field. My muscles loosen and forget the running and the pushups and the grind of the day, and my entire body is refreshed with the task at hand. Hunch the shoulders slightly, hands protecting the face, and be light but sure on my feet. I fake one way and shoot the other, but Sarge doesn't bite my best bait and he's sprawling on top of me with a forearm across my neck and a vice grip on one of my elbows. He's expecting me to readjust and that will give him my arm, so instead I bull into it and lock my hands around his leg. Sarge doesn't like that very much. My first thought is that his leg has become lighter, like he's giving it to me. By the time I realize that's a bad thing he's already let go of my elbow and his forearm comes across my face and bends me backward like a piece of cardboard. I can feel my quad muscles screaming as my shins and shoulder blades touch the ground at the same time, and Sarge's hand goes to my throat. One of my hands grips his elbow, the other the wrist of the same arm. Push in opposite directions and Sarge rolls off my chest and I can straighten my legs, try to regain some balance and composure. There's no hesitation as I turn and keep pressure on his arm, twisting in and driving his chest into the ground. For a half second, I've got the advantage. Half a second later Sarge's foot sweeps my leg, and the impact on the ground knocks the wind out of me. Sarge is faster than my recovery, and before I know it I'm on my back with his arms around my throat and his legs trapping mine. My pulse pushes against his bones and the edges of my vision flicker dark, but I'm not about to tap. He only wins if he breaks me. I am going to pass out. "And here...is where you snap the neck. And it's over." Sarge lets go completely and I can't control the coughing. He turns away from me and addresses the group, stifling any snickering that might be building up. Everybody wants to see the quarterback get taken down a notch, but they know damn well that I was the only one that would volunteer to get choked out. I saved them all from the same fate, so they're too busy thanking my ass to laugh at me. Sarge addresses them. "Some of you may know how to fight. Some of you may have been in a couple of scraps during your life. But none of you are a trained killer. Before you leave Basic, every one of you will have the skills and the knowledge to do what I just did to somebody like Private Wall. Some kid that knows the moves but has never been forced to use them to defend for his life. God forbid some of you twats may have to use what I teach you on an actual soldier, on someone poorer than you will ever be who is fighting you tooth and nail because you are on his homeland. It is my job to make sure you mouth breathers know how to kill a grown, angry, fighting man with your bare hands! Do you understand!" "Yes Drill Sergeant!" all my classmates shout. I cough and wheeze the words out. Sarge turns back to me and yells while I'm still down. "Private Wall! What the fuck is wrong with you! I had my back turned to you, my enemy, and you did not take the opportunity to kill me! You will tell me why you did not press your one and only advantage!" My throat can't yell but I do it anyway. "Drill Sergeant! ...This private...this private understands you are teaching a lesson Drill Sergeant! ...It would undermine the drill sergeant's lesson, and it would endanger my brothers in the U-Nited States Army if I interrupted that lesson...Drill Sergeant!" Sarge tilts his head. Maybe he's not used to someone with the right answers. "Besides, Drill Sergeant...if this private were in fact your enemy, you would have snapped my neck instead of letting go! Then I would not have the advantage, Drill Sergeant!" Sarge doesn't smile. He only smiles in the morning when he's ripping us away from sleep and when someone throws up on a run. But he does the next best thing when he reaches his arm down and holds it there to help me stand up. "Private Wall...you may be the first person to ever answer that correctly. You must be some fucking genius, Private Wall! Maybe you should be sitting in the Officer's Quarters, giving orders to dumbasses like me, shouldn't you Wall?" "Drill Sergeant! I am only smart enough to do what you tell me to Drill Sergeant!" "...Acceptable answer, soldier." That takes a moment to process. The Army is Mother, The Army is Father. The Army feeds you, clothes you, gives you a roof over your head, and gives you the training to survive when nobody else in the world wants you to. The Army shows you a discipline and a lifestyle that your friends back home could never understand fully, and the only thing The Army ever asks in return is that you don't die before you are authorized. If you die before authorization is permitted, you must report to the Insubordinate Dead Grunt desk, where they do not take kindly to your presence. Hearing a compliment from a drill sergeant might be the most surreal experience of my life thus far. I had no parents, but this must be what it is like for your father to say he is proud of you. I hold back a tear and shout back. "Drill Sergeant! Thank you for this lesson, Drill Sergeant!" I'm not anyone particularly special. Yet. In school I was the quarterback. I was the kid who was too cool for the bullshit that my friends got into. I was the shit. Here, they make it a point to break away everything that you were: your hair, your clothes, your training, and your ego. Everybody gets a fresh start from the same footing, and it's up to the trainee to become what he is to become. ---- "You ever think about home, Wall?" Edwards is the best friend I've made while on tour. There are cliques within the bunkhouse and at the mess halls, and there are those that know they're going to serve with each other. It's been a year since Basic, when we were all exactly the same in the eyes of the United States Government. Now we each have a slot to fill, and it's a slot that we have earned by our work and our actions. Edwards is a sniper that was a couple of Basic classes ahead of me, but we're both from Miami so hometown boys have to stick together. He took me under his wing early on and told me the this' and that's about being in Afghanistan. Where not to step, where not to piss, where not to relax for a brief moment. They call this the New Vietnam, and while it's not quite the blood bath you read about from the 70's, it sure as Hell feels like it could be at any moment. "Nah. It's just a place, man. There's a lotta places." The cold Afghan air has managed to penetrate us as we lie in wait for our ambush target. We were told to sit, six two-person teams placed along this roadside, one sniper and one spotter. We're protecting some road from the Taliban, because for some reason or another this road was key to something or another. It is not my duty to ask. Edwards' eyes are like a hawk's, and he's able to look through his scope while he's speaking to me. The night vision binocs are up to my eyes and the world is a sterile pale green, overlooking a canyon on the opposite side of the world from anything I've ever given a shit about. "Well yeah, man, but home is home. You know? It's warm there. There's the beach. Girls." "There's girls here." "Yeah, Army girls. They're sweet and all, but they're not impressed with anything but kill counts. I'm talking girls in bars that swoon over a close haircut and a pair of shined boots. Girls that want to know what it's like in the bush. Girls that think you're something special just for being in the Army." "Easy girls." "Yeah, man. Easy poon." "Heh...if all you're missing is easy poon, you can find that somewhere besides home man." There are a couple moments of silence. You're not supposed to talk on ambush, you're supposed to watch and listen. The other five pairs of troops are probably talking just as much as the two of us, because that's all bullshit regulations and we know it. Place any two officers in our shoes, they'd be talking all damn night about philosophies or tea cups or whatever officers talk about. They'd probably talk about how much their ass hurts from sitting on the ground and how troops should be allowed to bring cushions on ambush. We're high up on a mountain overlooking a road in the dead of night. Any Taliban that knows we're here found that out a long time before he heard us whispering. "What do you think about then, Wall?" Shrug. He can hear it because the night is so silent around us. "Sometimes...I guess I wonder what I'm doing now. Like right now. Why I'm a spotter for a sniper ambush instead of something more...I don't know. Something more special." "Spotters are special." "Naw man. Snipers are special. It's easy for a spotter to be patient because we're not the one with the gun in our hands and our trigger finger getting itchy. M1 operators are special cuz they drive a fucking tank. Even the mechanics are special, cuz they're gonna have real training they can put to use back home when they get outta here. I mean, what am I gonna do?" He shrugs. I can hear it because the night is so silent around us. "Be a spotter for a S.W.A.T. team? Be a lifer in the Army?" It's a crime to laugh while on ambush, but I manage a little one at that comment. "I'm here talking about being something special, and you're telling me to re-up. Wow." "You don't think it's special just being a soldier, Dom? I mean, you're doing something right now that those people back home aren't doing. A lot of them can't do it. Those easy girls are easy for us because they know the losers they usually hang out with can't do this." "I know, man, it's just..." I want to tell him about how I'm different from everybody else. I want to tell him how I could be the best of the best, how proud the drill sergeant was of me that one time, how I could've gone to college and played football and maybe be looking at a couple million dollars in the draft next year. I want to tell him that I deserve more for my hard work than sitting in a bush with him in the frigid air in the middle of the land that God forgot. But I chicken out, because what would that make me sound like? Some day dreamer. Some go-getter. Some gung-ho adrenaline junkie that shows off, and that ain't me. Fact is I'd rather play my role and do it better than anybody else and at the end of the day, when the loud mouths don't measure up, that's when people will notice me. I'll get what I'm due in due time, and until then anything I say or do is going to sound like I'm whining about not getting enough attention. "I'm just cold is all, man. Wish somebody would just fuckin' come up that road so we can shoot them and go home." "You mean, like, Miami home?" "Naw man. Just a warm bed would be nice right now." It's about being patient and sticking to the task at hand. The Army taught me what I need to know, they've put me where I've put myself. Now it's up to me to be a man about it and do the job in front of me, do it as best I can, and go from there. Bitching about being a spotter won't get me anywhere; spotting better than expected and getting the job done will. ---- Two months later Lance Corporal Edwards triggered a rigged device. Slotted by an innocent enough looking something...maybe a trash can or a rock along a wall was hiding the explosive. I mean, that says something for how indiscreet and how unnoticed it was...we were unable to determine what it was the device was hidden under. At thirteen hundred hours and seventeen minutes, an improvised explosive device slotted Lance Corporal Edwards. We use the term 'slotted' in conversation because the word 'kill' is too real. This way, it can feel like Edwards is just out of the game for a little while. 'Oh Edwards? He's just slotted. Damn, I know...sucks.' At thirteen hundred hours and fifty-two minutes, the squad had determined that there was no hostile presence in the immediate area. No shots were fired, no enemy were killed. We use the term 'kill' when we talk about the enemy because it is more satisfying to think of it in terms of reducing their numbers permanently. Lance Corporal Edwards was slotted and the son of a bitch that did it didn't even stick around to make sure the job was done correctly. Edwards was blown up by a fucking amateur. At thirteen hundred hours and fifty-four minutes, two members of the squad were instructed to retain whatever we could find of Lance Corporal Edwards while the rest of the squad kept watch for enemy combatants. Something to be able to put in a box and send home for his parents to mourn. It's one thing to find a piece of clothing with blood on it or some indiscernible inside that you don't recognize. I found my friend's ear. The only reminder that I had been able to open up and talk to him. And that moment I felt the regret burst up out of me like a punch from inside my stomach. A bluster of air knocked a couple of tears loose out of my eyes and a cough from my throat, and luckily nobody was able to see my face to know it. It was the idea that I was holding his ear...I had had my chance to be honest with Edwards, to tell him what I was thinking about my life, just two months ago. It hit me: that was my chance to be something special. I wanted, deep within my heart, to stand out from the masses while being quiet and doing my duty, to be an exceptional soldier while not whoring myself for attention. Hard work and diligence and always being ready for my chance...and I blew it. Any words of excitement or disappointment or any fucking human connection I could have made, anything that could have given us a moment where two people turned a casual friendship into a lasting bond, would have entered through the ear that I was holding in my hand. Now it was gone. Edwards was slotted. Killed. I'd lost that chance. ---- I remember swearing I'd never let a chance to do something special slip by again. If anything, for the closest I'd come to making a friend in The Army...for Edwards. He made it. He was now something special, the way I wanted to be. It's just that he had to die a senesless, meaningless death in order to find meaning in my life. The bitter aftertaste of knowing that he was never coming back had to be followed by something good, or else his entire life was meaningless. The Army brings you down to the same level as anyone else. The Army gives you the same instructions and education to make yourself into something special. But in the end it's not up to The Army to tell you what that is or how to go about achieving it. The Army is Mother, The Army is Father, and just like a good parent The Army can't live your life for you, it can only give you the best start possible. Every chance I get will be earned, and every single one will be utilized to the fullest. No matter how hard the training, no matter how cold the frigid night air along the roadside in No Man's Land, I will not ever allow the chance to rise from the identical masses escape me again.
