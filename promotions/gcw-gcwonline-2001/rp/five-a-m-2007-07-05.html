five a.m.

5 July 2007
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 7/5/07 Five in the morning, it was, and barely had Perth begun to awaken when a man stepped out onto its streets. He was an unassuming man, no different from any of thousands of other residents of the Australian city. He took a slow walk down a main drag, eyes opened to find the first newsstand that he could see. It awaited a block down the street, and the man dug into his pocket for his wallet. From the shelves of the makeshift outdoor store he pulled a copy of the National Enquirer, along with Star, and several other British and Australian tabloids. The man paid for the magazines quietly, and left the newsstand as soon as he had arrived. This time, the man walked back in the direction he had come. He almost smiled at how easy his first delivery of the day had been. Why, all he had to do was purchase some rags for a guest of a nearby hotel. No overbearing businessmen. No insanely difficult item to track down. Not even a long distance to travel. This would be the easiest money that he had made in a long time. Why, then, did he feel like something was terribly, terribly wrong? No matter how he tried, he could not shake the feeling that this task was going to result in something bad. The request was extraordinarily strange in its mundaneness. What sort of person asked someone to go get supermarket tabloids for them? Perhaps an overly wealthy one. Or an overly lazy one. Whatever the case was’a foreboding feeling assailed the deliveryman’s gut as he pulled out a cellular telephone and made a call. “I have the items, sir,” he spoke into the receiver of the phone. “I’ll be back at the hotel in twenty minutes to drop them off. What room number are you again?” Listening carefully, the deliveryman makes a mental note of the number. “Excellent. I’ll be there as soon as possible.” Shoving his thoughts of doom and gloom to the back of his head, the deliveryman contented himself with the payment he would receive for a job well done. It was at least double the usual for such a simple task. At the very least, he’d have some money to take to the bar that night and drown his worries in a moderately decent beer. :-:-:-:-:-: Early though it was, in his hotel room, the man known to GCW fans as Tabloid was wide awake and busy, typing away at his laptop computer. A wireless card hung out of the device’s side, and it was being used quite extensively, judging from the multiplicity of open internet browsers. The titles of the sites, though, were all of the same ilk. GCWNewz.com. EstablishmentInsider.tv. Hell, there was even a shaman420news.info site. The common thread? All were dirtsheet rumor sites, claiming to have the inside scoop on all of the backstage goings on in Global Championship Wrestling. Most fans ignored such sites as meaningless, unresearched drivel. Tabloid was not most fans. Most fans did not have a very direct stake in knowing the backstage environment of GCW. Tabloid did. And so he sifted through the piles upon piles of utter manure, looking for that one nugget of information that could possibly be of use to him. Tabloid read about the burgeoning love triangle between Tessa Windsor, Chris Storm, and Markus Stone. He rolled his eyes, and continued on to the next story. He read about Steven Caldera’s cocaine addiction. Useful knowledge, that’had it any proof or basis in fact whatsoever. All entertaining, mind you, but all completely baseless. “This is becoming a waste of my time,” the newcomer growled, shaking his masked head. Even here, in his proverbial inner sanctum, the mask never came off. Ever. “At least my magazines will be here shortly. The delivery boy told me twenty minutes.” Tabloid followed the personal lives of wrestlers for work. He followed the personal lives of celebrities for enjoyment. Sighing to himself, the Global Enquirer pulled open one of the hotel desk’s drawers, coming out of it with a manila folder. Written in neat letters across the tab was the name “Brad Jackson”. Inside it was all the information Tabloid had on his next opponent. Which, was to say, not much. “Guy’s a sociopath in training, it seems,” was Tabloid’s pronouncement, perusing the folder’s contents. Classic case of angry guy with emotional issues who finally finds a situation to use the emotions in, and it all goes to Hell. I don’t think I can play this one like a fiddle. I touch him the wrong way, and he explodes. Takes me with him. I don’t want that?” Getting a DQ win over a guy like Jackson would be relatively easy. Bring up his love life. Bring up his failings as a human being. Needle him. Then there’d be the DQ’but Tabloid was not sure that Brad wouldn’t extract a pound of flesh in exchange for the DQ. “If I knew he’d be pulled off me, I’d do it in a heartbeat. Without that assurance, it’s a dicey proposition at best.” Ah well. Winning didn’t matter. Not yet. Sure, triumphing last week had been nice, but that was not the goal. Never had been. No, Tabloid was a methodical sort of man. In a sport in which men fought blindly, Tabloid’s intention was to be the machine gun that cut them down by the dozens. “I do not WANT to be the hot rookie,” he almost groused. “That brings undue attention. Undue attention makes things infinitely more difficult.” Still, Tabloid did have to consider the possibility that he would be, especially with a win over Jackson. Jackson had put in a dominating performance against Hydra last week. Most in the know considered Tabloid an underdog at best, and a stepping stone at worst. A win would catapult him out of the shadows, and into the realm of “noticed”. “I,” Tabloid pronounced, leaving the chair he sat in to flop on the room’s king-sized bed, “am going to need a backup plan.” When one’s grand plan to become World Champion and wrestling legend involved screwing over 99% of the people you come across, one had better pick a VERY good 1% of people to remain loyal to. For Tabloid, that meant only one person. “Steven Caldera.” It made perfect sense. The only person not to fuck over was the person who signed your checks and determined who you fought on a weekly basis. A knock on the door, though, snapped the newcomer from his strategic planning. “Who is it,” Tabloid called, though he knew full well who was at the door to his room. One, though, could never be too cautious. “Delivery. Got a package for ya.” Tabloid crossed the small room, undoing the locks on the door, and then finally opening it. Nodding at the deliveryman standing there, he perused the magazines, and nodded in satisfaction. “Excellent. That’s all of them.” “Of course,” the deliveryman retorted. “I get my work done?” His voice trailed off as he looked at his client. Just as he’d expected, the guy was a piece of work. Who the Hell wore a mask in every day life, anyway? “Hand over the magazines, then,” Tabloid said, clearly wanting to dispense with pleasantries, “and I will hand over your cash.” “Gladly.” The deliveryman forked over the magazines, and Tabloid in turn handed him a wad of bills. The other man counted his money, and walked away with a smile on his face. “I’m gonna drink good tonight?” With that distraction out of the way, Tabloid could close and re-lock his door. He tossed the magazines over on the desk with the computer, where they would remain forgotten for most of the rest of the day. For Tabloid now had a conundrum to solve: how to ingratiate himself to one Mr. Steven Caldera. Another file came out of the drawer which had priorly yielded information on Brad Jackson. This one contained all Tabloid knew about Steven Caldera. This file, though, was considerably larger. “Caldera’s a take-charge sort of guy. Egotistical, but who with his money isn’t?” It was fascinating, really, what made a man decide to own a wrestling company. More importantly, though, it was fascinating the techniques that he used to rule. “He seems to vascillate between hands-off periods of ownership, and attempting to take control of all aspects of his company. This often depends on what superstars are in a position of power on his roster.” Tabloid continued scanning the in depth dossier, nodding at passages as he went. “Ultimately, though, it is my belief that he is simply too self-absorbed to remain out of ring generalship for too long. He believes he is the only man who can lead the company to the promised land, both in and out of the ring.” It was more than simply that, of course. Caldera had once been a pretty good wrestler in his own right, before injuries had derailed his path. Setbacks in his managerial career, with appearances in tournaments known as “GTTs” resulting in losses by his giant sumo wrestler charge, Kimbusa. That explained a lot, as far as Tabloid was concerned. “He lives vicariously through the Establishment and any wrestlers he takes charge of. Any Establishment member will essentially have their careers made, as evidenced by the events of the Warpath tournament. With Caldera’s backing, Jordan White was able to defeat Vivica Valentine and go on to soon face Lia Ambrosi for her World Title. My solution is simple’I must get into Caldera’s good graces.” LeStatt Knight brought familiarity and experience. Jordan White brought ability. Tabloid knew that, with the right comments, and playing his hand to the hilt, he had something he could provide Steven Caldera. “Information. No empire can run without it. Caldera needs to know the pulse of his locker room at all times. And there is no man better at reading the moods of wrestlers than Tabloid?” Well. That settled it. He would have to speak with Caldera. Heaving a sigh, Tabloid flopped back down upon the room’s bed. It was still very early in the day. Soon, he would go downstairs and have a mediocre breakfast of pancakes and juice at the hotel restaurant. From there, he would come back up to the room, and read his tabloids. Only after that was done would he head to the gym for a bit of a workout. Brad Jackson may have been an above-average physical specimen with many credentials in the wrestling business. But ultimately’he mattered not to Tabloid. Victory or defeat did not yet mean anything. All that mattered against the erratic competitor’was simply survival.
