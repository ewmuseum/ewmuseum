The Story Of Everything, Chapter Six: Farmers

12 September 2008
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 9-12-08 I woke up in a field with no clue where I was or how I had gotten there, but at least those god damned fireballs appeared to have stopped coming after me. Doctor Wayne was over there in a pile of mulch some twenty feet away, and it looked like he hadn't quite come to yet. I figured it was best to let him come out of it naturally as you don't want to startle a man on magic hot sauce. I could see buildings occupying random spots off in the distance, but none of them were the garage. I needed to get my hands on this Undead Sauce. After what I had just been through, it even surprised me that I wished to repeat the experience, but I figured we could market the shit, sell it for twenty bucks a pint and live like kings. Of course, when we ran out, we ran out. Only one man knew the recipe for Undead Sauce, and that man was Mark David. I needed to get that recipe before I'd even sell a drop of Undead Sauce, but I'm sure Mark David wouldn't be too keen on me profiteering off of his sauce. There's no way that son of a bitch would let me in on his recipe anyway. He didn't trust me, and I knew it. Once I had some idea what powerful forces filled those jugs, I had no intentions whatsoever of returning them to Mark David without being properly compensated. He knew exactly how important the contents of those jugs were, but I knew, too, and I also knew that he didn't know that I knew. I saw Doctor Wayne stirring, covered in mulch. He'd know what to do, and he'd know how to do it legal, being my lawyer and all. "Doctor Wayne," I hollered, walking over to him. "Have you returned?" Wayne shot up, spooked, and didn't even bother to brush off the mulch. "We need to get the hell out of here, man. We need to get to a place without so many fireballs." "Get a hold of yourself, Doctor Wayne," I grabbed him by the collar and slapped him. "The fireballs are gone now. That was just the sauce." "Where are we?" Wayne questioned, thrashing in all directions in search. "Where the fuck is my car?" "Your car is fine," I assured him. "It's parked right outside the garage." "Then where's the garage?" "I don't know," I answered honestly. "We appear to have somehow wandered quite a distance from the garage." "You have that address written down somewhere, right?" "Yeah," I chuckled. "It's in your car." "That'll do us a whole hell of a lot of good," Wayne acerbically retorted. He sat down and rested his head in his hands, sighing. "We're fucked. You know that, right?" I sat down next to him and tried to change the subject. "So you were being attacked by fireballs, too, huh?" "Yeah, it was terrible," Wayne agreed, staring forward blankly. "We have to get back to that garage . . . get more of that Undead Sauce and try it again." "I know," I admitted. We were both fiends for the sauce. "But that's all we got. We have to get the recipe." "Are you fucking kidding me?" shot Wayne. "We barely scratched that first jug, and there were about fifty of them in there. I think we have enough to last us quite some time." "Sure, but what if we get used to it?" I reasoned. "It may have only taken us a few ladlefuls of the stuff to work this time, but what if, after repeated use, we have to down gallons of the sauce at a time to feel any affect?" "Well, who has the recipe?" "Mark David." "That burnt, old corpse? Good luck getting a recipe from a dead guy." "I know how to get in contact with him. I can show you." "What?" "I've been abusing a drug called Jenkem," I flatly admitted. "You heard of it?" "Jenkem? No." "It's a mixture of fermenting human feces and urine," there was no point in beating around the bush. "You ferment it all in some kind of container and then you shotgun the fumes." "You've lost it, man." Wayne got up and started backing away slowly. "You're fucking sick." "It lets you talk to dead people!" I protested. I stood up and walked after him, but something caught my eye in one of the dips of the hilly terrain. There was a shadow of an antique tractor coming towards us through a field. It was at a distance, but its rider was carrying a pitchfork, and his silhouette was topped by two horns, extending outwards like a bull chugging towards us as fast as his rusty engine could muster. "Wayne, get down. I think the devil's after us on a tractor." "No way, man. I've done some bad things, but I'm pretty sure he'd come after you first. If I stick with you, I'm curtains." Wayne turned around and began to run, but then he, too, caught the image of the silhouette of the devil approaching on tractor; caught it square in the eye. He dropped like a ton of bricks, and I wondered what kind of dangerous world we lived in when thousands of pounds of bricks could just topple down from the sky. I followed suit. "Now just do me a favor, and step outside of the situation for a second. Mark David has the recipe. He's dead. There's only one way to get to him, and the more numbers we have, the better our chances. You're my lawyer. I need you for negotiations." "Not a chance, Johnny. There's no way in hell I'm breathing in shit fumes." "Think about it, man. The least we can do is get the address. If I can negotiate the exchange of the barrels for the recipe, I need it in writing for it to stand up in any court. And once we have that recipe, we can make oceans of Undead Sauce and sell it." "Are you even sure that these dead people are really dead people or just figments of your imaginations?" I had yet to consider this possibility, but it seemed like such a long shot that there was no need. There it was, though, the loud hum of the motor of the devil's tractor approaching. "Shit!" I exclaimed in a whisper. "What should we do?" "Let's wait until he gets here and run in opposite directions." A purr grew into a roar, and there was the devil's tractor, climbing its way up the incline before us. Wayne, on my left turned to the right, and I on Wayne's right turned to my left, and we both charged forward and collided. The howl of the engine silenced. "Is there a problem here?" asked a loud, booming voice. Wayne and I turned up to see an old man wearing a wide, straw hat. He must have only looked like Satan's silhouette from a distance. "No," Wayne hastily replied. "There's no problem at all." "What are you two up to out here?" "We're lost," I appealed. "Our car was stolen by, um, bandits." "You get a good look at them?" "No, sir," Wayne responded, always a card at propagating bogus claims. "They were wearing masks, and it all happened so fast." "The name is Matthais Henrik," the farmer introduced. "Hop on my tractor, and we'll get this mess all sorted out." Wayne and I hopped in the tractor seat, ducking under his straw hat on either side as he piloted the vehicle. We were all dangerously close to each other. My knee was pressed against Matthais' knee; his old, wrinkled knee, but we were off on his tractor. "This here's a 1935 Lanz Bulldog Model D9531. They didn't build 'em any better than this." "It's a fine tractor, sir," I agreed. "It's the finest tractor in the world, my boy," beamed Matthais Henrik. "It's fit for road speeds and it's got an 8.858 by 10.24 inch bore with speeds of up to almost 10 miles an hour!" "Those are impressive statistics," I added. "If you don't mind my asking," chimed in Doctor Wayne. "Where are you taking us?" "We're just headed back to the farm," Matthais explained. "You two can clean yourselves off. I'll get you a change of clothes, I'll have my wife prepare you a proper meal and you can rest here for the night. There's only one condition, though . . ." "Don't sleep with your daughter?" Wayne ejaculated . . . prematurely. "No, I don't have any daughters." Matthais paused in thought. "But I only have one guest room, so don't sleep with each other. God doesn't like it, and I don't like it." *** We had finished a superb meal of freshly slaughtered meats, and Matthais had asked me to join him on the porch for a pinch of tobacco while Wayne had excused himself to make a business related phone call. We sat on crudely constructed wicker chairs and every so often, one of the shoots would poke my skin. The porch was old and creaky, and everything was painted gray so that the gray pretty much sucked the color out of everywhere. I was never one for the chew, but I was smart enough to know that when a host offers you a chew, it's rude not to oblige. Matthais, on the other hand, was a chewing machine. Muscles protruded out off of his jawbone, and his jaw was definitely his strongest feature. He could probably bite through bone, I'd wager; but why would he ever want to? God, that jaw was automatic. Pistons pumped within, propelled by some self-perpetuating motor. The man was half-tractor, but if he didn't look so much like a man and not a tractor, you'd think he was all tractor. He stared at something off in the distance; too far for me to see it anyway, so I just sat and stared at him. Those beadlike eagle eyes! He could have been staring at nothing at all, his eyes recessing into his skull so his jaw could fully work over that chew. I had to break the tense focus of silence. I couldn't stand to stare at him staring at something or staring at nothing much longer. "That was the best meal I had in quite some time," I said, politely chewing and rubbing my stomach. He nodded a little bit, chewing heavily in rhythm as he furiously rocked in his wicker chair still staring off in the same general direction at a blank spot in the foreground of a strip of land between an old shed and a pile of furniture. "This is a nice stretch of land you've got here," I graciously complimented. "I bet it's nice to have so much piece and quiet." I could tell he wasn't even listening to me. He just shifted in his chair and reached for something. It was a shotgun, leaning against the outside of his house. I hadn't noticed it from the other side of the chair, but I stood up and recoiled nervously. "Have you ever fired a gun before?" "No," I admitted. "Not a gun with bullets in it." "We need to get rid of some of these cans," Matthais said. "They're getting out of hand." I had no idea what he was talking about, but the old man gave me a pistol, and I liked how it fit in my hand. He led me out back behind a shed where a few cans were balancing on a wooden fence. "I can't sleep at night because of these lousy cans," he said. "When I was your age, cans didn't make so much racket. They knew that good cans kept their lids shut and didn't say nothing." He loaded two shotgun bullets into the barrel of the gun, locked it and took aim; but he didn't shoot. "Go on," he said. "Why don't you give it a try?" I held the pistol up and took careful aim at the first can, shutting one eye and squinting with the other. BANG! I fired, but I missed the can completely. "Again," he barked. BANG! I shot, with hurried, careless aim, and the results were no worse. Of course, the can remained even unshaken. "Again!" BANG! I shot, and I missed again. I kept shooting. BANG! No aim at all, now. BANG! A spray of bullets panned from one side to the other. I pointed again. "Last bullet." BANG! This one was louder, deeper. The can exploded with holes upon the impact of the buckshot. I looked over my shoulder, and smoke slowly rose from Matthais' gun. He turned slightly. BANG! Another can cut down in the prime of its life. He reloaded another two bullets, cocked the gun and pointed it at the third can. BANG! The fence was cleared. "You see that can on the ground?" Matthais pointed the barrel of his gun at a can a few feet to my side. "Throw it into the air as hard as you're able!" I heaved with all my might, and Matthais tracked the can's flight with his shotgun before firing another fleet of buckshot into the sky, punishing the can and dashing it to a flurry of descending bits. Matthais pointed his weapon towards the sky and blew the smoke off the barrel. "You wait out here," he said, outstretching a cautionary palm with one arm while dragging his shotgun with the other. "I'm going to go fetch some more cans and check on your friend." As he walked off, the gravity of the situation didn't immediately dawn on me. I was too busy thinking about the old man and his cans, and I wondered where exactly it had all gone haywire. It seemed simple enough, the concept of a can: an aluminum or tin or otherwise metallic container used to seal and preserve goods. There must have been a place somewhere along the lines where that somewhat noble concept was distorted -- but how to find that place? Ah! Now there was a mystery that belied most men! The cans piled on and weighed heavily on my mind. Something like guilt drove me to find some association; some way to connect the cans to my situation and the series of events that preceded me. I couldn't do it. I knew it would plague me for days. I knew I'd lie awake thinking about cans and what they all meant and how they all tied together. Maybe this is how it all started. It must have been. There were cans all around me, and there always were, but I never realized how completely surrounded I was before. As soon as it was brought to my attention, the mere promise of their maddening presence set me on edge. It was a silly thought that crept up on me. It was one that was so unlikely to be possible that it almost wasn't worth mentioning, but I had to voice it. I had to say it out loud to make it real and to cope with the implications. "That spot," I mumbled to myself, remembering the patch of field Matthais was fixated on earlier. "He's planting cans." Looking back, it seems ridiculous, but at the time, I could feel the earth trembling beneath my feet -- the sleeping crop roused, ready to burst up through the soil and flail thistly arms. I couldn't get out of there fast enough. I had to go get Wayne. "Shit!" I had forgotten all about Wayne, and Matthais, dragging his shotgun, had grown mad from the cans. I made a mad dash, ready to find Wayne and put some ground underneath our feet. *** I stopped dead in my tracks as soon as I stepped through the doorframe. Across the room, I could see Wayne standing in front of the stove. A large wooden ladle dripped with red and a few large cans and some yellow boxes were on the counter behind him. Wayne had his hands up in the air, and Matthais had a shotgun pointed at Wayne's chest. I pulled out the handgun and aimed it at Matthais. "Drop the gun!" I yelled. "Not until your friend tells me what he's doing with those cans." "I swear, I wasn't up to anything," defended Wayne. "I wasn't even talking to anyone." "That's not what the cans said," Matthais prodded. "They told me you what you said to them -- told me that you were up to no good." "Drop the gun, or I'll shoot!" I commanded. "Yes, sir!" said Matthais. "They told me that you were making a witches brew. What are you planning? You going to cast a spell on me and steel my road tractor?" "I wasn't talking to the god damned cans!" "I'm giving you to the count of three, Matthais! One!" "You wasted all those bullets missing cans." "Two!" "Go ahead and shoot, then. You have no aim. You'll miss me by a mile." "Who says I'm aiming for you?" I said, and I fired. Matthais erupted with a piercing scream. I didn't know where the bullet went. I think I heard it ricochet off of something. Regardless, when I looked up, Matthais' face was covered in a viscous red liquid. It dripped down the front of his shirt. "It's in my eyes!" He yelled. "My eyes!" He followed it with more screaming. I looked towards Wayne. He was holding the large saucepot, which now carried only a small puddle of the sauce swishing around the bottom. "Run!" I yelled as Matthais hobbled around, wiping the sauce out of his eyes. Wayne ran out of the kitchen, and I took off to lead the way through the door and through the field. Wayne started to catch up, and I could see he was struggling to do so because he was also carrying the saucepot. "Why do you still have the pot?" "I needed more sauce, man." "You still have some of that Undead Sauce?" "No," Wayne responded. "It's regular hot sauce cooked with baking soda." I turned around, and Matthais was on his way through the door, not quite recovered, but he had his shotgun and started firing it wildly. "In there!" I directed, pointing towards the large shed, but there was still distance between us. It was eerily quiet. Not even the cans chattered. I turned around again, and Matthais was on the ground. I couldn't tell if he was alive or dead, but I wasn't going back to find out. Wayne and I kept on towards the shed and ducked behind it to catch our breaths. "Is it safe?" Wayne asked. "I think so," I guessed. "I think he might be dead. Come on. Let's get out of here." "Hold on," Wayne cautioned. "Let's try some of this." I followed the tip of Wayne's pointing finger into the bottom of the saucepot as the remnants of the concoction settled. "What if that's what killed him?" "We won't have that much." "Well, you are a doctor," I reasoned. "Why the hell not?" Wayne held the saucepan up to his face and started pouring the sauce into his mouth. "Save some for me," I said, half-joking as I pried the saucepot out of his hands and guzzled down the rest. It was almost instantaneous. I threw the saucepot off into the grass and just started laughing again. Wayne was already in an uncontrollable fit, and I saw my spatial consciousness stretching again, even further and in so many more directions this time. "This is some powerful stuff," I said really slowly. Wayne laughed at the words, unable to respond. "We need to find . . . " I trailed off, trying to remember how that sentence was supposed to end, but it took so much time, I thought I'd just as well start over. "We need to find, uh, your car and take the jugs to that guy. We have to put all those jugs in your car and -- what's so funny?" I had learned once that you should only ask questions if you already know the answers. "I'm turning into a sphere!" Wayne excitedly stated. "We're never going to find those jugs," I said. "Not like this." "Hey, what if we just took a bunch of hot sauce cooked with baking soda and filled up a bunch of jugs with it? You think he'd know the difference?" Wayne was looking at the sky thinking, and pointing and laughing at clouds. "I'm not sure, but we might not have many other options." "Hey, man," Wayne interjected. "What does that cloud look like to you?" "I don't know," I said with disinterest. "A fichus?" "What do you think it means?" "I don't think it means anything," I laughed inwardly from outside of myself. "None of that shit ever means anything." I didn't want to waste time thinking about it. The answers are the same whether you know them or not, and you're better off not driving yourself insane thinking about them. The sooner you admit that you don't know what you're talking about, the better, and it'll only end completely once you stop talking. I just hoped that I'd be far enough away not to hear them when it got quiet.
