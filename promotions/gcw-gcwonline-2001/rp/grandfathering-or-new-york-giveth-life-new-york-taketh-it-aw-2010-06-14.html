Grandfathering, or: New York Giveth Life. New York Taketh it Away.

14 June 2010
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 06/14/10 His father woke up one morning and forgot his own name. It was otherwise a criminally ordinary day. His father roused himself with the first rumblings of the garbage collection trucks, spluttering up the road five blocks south like an extended cardiac arrest. By the time the grease-faced men in coveralls were piling up the refuse of the impossibly tall apartment block in whose bicycle shed he slept, the old man had washed his face in the rainwater butt, scrubbed his tangled armpit hair with the abused plastic nub of a spent Old Spice roll-on, and bundled up the collection of old sheets and pillowcases under which he slept. He reached up, stained linens in hand, and stashed them behind the iron-roofed shed, where the grasses rose up to form a wilderness mere yards from the busy urban intersection that ferried the building’s inhabitants to and from the city, where no one but stray dogs dared to walk. It begins with a B, he told himself, struggling hard to retain his composure. Or a V. One of those types of sounds. * * * His son was one month and one day into a life over which he had no control. Kate chose the name; Owen didn’t get a say. Christopher Barnard it was, and no middle name, and Owen didn’t have the guts to fight for the name of his father, a name that nobody would otherwise remember. In his dreams he held him on rooftops and in rivers; he held his baby boy with every step he took. In his dreams she was at his side. He had been selfish, obstinate -- irritating to the point of driving away all those who had ever known him. He had built a life around favours unreturned. The country was littered with his false starts, his prematurely severed ends. But now he had set down a root. Snaked through the New York sidewalk and deep into the soil. Christopher Barnard -- his flesh and blood. Owen Compliments was a father, and so he was a changed man. To him, it was that simple. * * * His father was the sort of person who didn’t like to beat around the bush. The name’s B_____, he would tell you with a rattish grin and a cursory shake of the hand (or was it V_____?). Nice to meet you, you might say in reply, and already his father would be shaking his head. I only like to make in-stru-men-tal friendships, he would emphasise, sounding out the word like it was a favourite of his, in such a hard, hacking voice that you would faithfully believe his throat could mince beef. I’ll be your best friend so long’s you got something I need, and soon’s that’s gone, I’ll drop you like yesterday’s news. Got it? Of course you’ve got it, and perhaps you’re not entirely keen on being his friend anymore after all. At least he’s honest, but -- and then you step away and you never think of him again; his patchy beard and his intense glare dissolve in your memory. This is why his father lived half his life on the New York streets. This is why his father would die in an alley. This is why it didn’t really matter to anyone whether his father was alive or dead. It mattered to Owen. * * * Once upon a time Owen Compliments had been a Bright Young Thing, or one of the literati or cognoscenti, or a recognised member of this or that Brat Pack. Once upon a time his life was great, but also terrible. He lived in LA, but without an actual home. He lurched from hotel room to gourmet restaurant to club opening to suspiciously young-looking girl’s kitchen table. The sex was great, but also terrible. He popped pills in the nights to give himself headaches and he popped pills in the mornings to relieve them. He drank a lot of water and he kissed a lot of cheeks. He didn’t launder his shirts, he bought new ones. Anyone else could have told him it would all soon come crashing down, but Owen Compliments wasn’t anyone else. He wasn’t prepared to live day-to-day: he lived hour-to-hour. Then one day the call came. They’d frozen his advance, they said. They needed to see some writing. No, not oysters and platitudes over dinners he wouldn’t pay for. Words on a page. This was a problem for Owen, because his success to date was predicated on one great novel, an ironic post-eighties rejection of the very life he had fallen into. Jackpot City was great, but also terrible. He had grown tired of goateed reporters and mouth-agape students infatuated with his leading man’s famous assertion that "The Eighties was an illusion; nobody even cared what decade it was." He had passed the time writing several decidedly mediocre short stories and serials, and watched with wry amusement as critics wrung them out, desperate to suck at the juice that must surely be hidden inside. But he was dried out, denatured. He was past his sell-by date. He was quite emphatically out of his league. He was happy enough to submit to a life little more than a conveyor belt of fresh champagne and fresh pussy, but only because he couldn’t exactly think of anything better to do. Under the surface, he reflected, his life was neither great nor terrible. But when the money ran out he clung as best he could to a ledge that was only theoretical. He wanted nothing more than what he had never really wanted. He was desperate not to lose something he wasn’t sure he’d ever had. Left with little choice, he declared himself bisexual and had magazine sub-editors overlay various pseudo-profound indictments of the human condition on a spray-tanned, airbrushed set of gay-friendly publicity shots. He felt California turn on him -- he felt it in the salt-crusted winds off the Pacific. He ran, and waited for his celebrity and his tan to concomitantly fade. * * * His father painted the pavements in Paris. Once. When they kicked him out of the country, he started to do the same in New York. He would paint with chalk-based mixtures, oranges and blues and greens and whites. He made them up himself and he kept them in glass jars. On the rare occasion that he finished a mural before the police moved him on, he would spread a lacquer over it, then hold a tarp down across the top and wait for it to dry. Some neighbourhoods, you can still see the traces of his craft. Others? -- too many feet have scratched him out of the sidewalks, and though chalk dust still sits in the cracks, it’s like he was never there at all. The day that he forgot his name, he painted himself a home. Right there on the sidewalk. Without a name, he couldn’t go back to what he’d once had. He couldn’t go back to his wife and his child, not ever -- not without a name. It rationalised things for him. It made them permanent, in a way his lacquers never quite could. And even in that alley, sheltered from the rain, no interest from the New York City cops, with only the occasional fleeting glimpse of humanity, a disoriented junkie or a stumbling tramp -- even there his chalks wouldn’t hold their colour. But though his paintings weren’t permanent, his own slippages were. He could feel himself becoming that disoriented junkie, that stumbling tramp. And he knew that there was no way back. * * * It had taken him half a lifetime to track down his father, and only a day to lose him again. So Owen Compliments felt that he should make things a bit easier for his own son. "Kate," he would say for the twelfth time in a day, "I want to be here." The problem was that she didn’t seem to want him there. She was still crying, she was still carrying the weight, she was still everywhere mentally and nowhere physically. She turned away to breast-feed. There was no question of dinner or sex or staying overnight. She let him in when the doorbell rang, but that wouldn’t guarantee anything more than a greeting and a glass of ginger ale, which was flat, the gas having long since walked out on Kate’s beautiful sad life. He knew what she wanted, but he suspected that it didn’t exist anymore. If it ever did. She wanted the Tim Shipley who had grown up idolising her, who had unwittingly led her into more danger than she should ever have had to face, but who had come to her rescue when she fell to unforeseen depths. Owen could never quite know what had passed between the two, said or unsaid, when Tim had pulled Kate’s life back on track. That did not stop him from knowing how important it was -- and that without him, she would likely still be in residential care, sleeping all of the day and screaming all of the night. That Tim was a facade, an illusion. The real Tim was out there... somewhere. Out of touch with everyone and with everything that was good and right and proper. Even Owen could see this. New York today, Australia tomorrow: both equally an escape, geographic distance merging into insignificance when Tim’s mind was so stubborn. Owen was stronger than that. Braver. You could lose yourself in a city with ease; far harder to find yourself. Owen was here and Kate just could not see it. He’d tried to make a commitment. She hadn’t let him. And with every passing day he felt his resolve slipping. * * * His father didn’t die so much as fade into the cityscape. The welts on his legs and arms, the abscesses, his glistening yellow eyes. There wasn’t necessarily something wrong -- more simply, his body wasn’t built for living any longer. He lay in his home, the colours faded, no strength to replenish the pigments. The vials lay empty, soon to be forgotten. Before he died, he heard soft footsteps. The back of a man, retreating into the alley, a cellphone grasped tight. Through dirty eyes, he saw a white shirt, careful hair. He saw the man lose his balance. He blinked, and then he saw the phone hit the concrete and burst, parts skittling away, coming to rest under bins, soaked cardboard, old pizza boxes. Through his one good ear, he heard the man curse. It was an accent he had heard many years before, in Paris. A man in a dark suit and a trilby. Over from London, he’d said, for a while, for a break, to find something that wasn’t there. The man had helped him get right. He’d bought him eggs and toast in the sort of place he would never have dared to look for. He’d talked to him for what had felt like an hour. He’d admired his spot of the pavement, the depth and richness of the colours. Then it had rained, and everything had been ruined. If he squinted, now, this one could look the same; he wasn’t, but when he was this hungry, the old man wasn’t scared to fantasise. From his painted home he watched him, and soon enough felt piercing blue eyes lock with his. They stood still. The city stood still. "The less you have," Owen’s father began slowly, "the less you lose." The Englishman approached. Though the old man couldn’t see too well, he etched curiosity across his visitor’s imagined face. "What did you say?" He was tired to repeat himself, so he waved an arm instead. "New York, son. It fucks you and, heh, you fuck it back. But it never stops fucking you, remember." He coughed three or four times. Swallowed the mucus back down. The man in the white shirt hesitated, then he squatted down, wringing his hands. "Are you... are you OK?" The concern in his visitor’s voice worried the street artist. He ran through his neatly ordered mental checklist of ailments. He didn’t believe that any had worsened. "Easy to lose yourself in this city," he swallowed noisily, before an involuntary gurgle of sorts. "Take the hard way, son. Always." He was finding it a little tough to breathe, and concentrated on that for a little while. He didn’t see the Englishman turn away. When Tim Shipley returned five minutes later with a sandwich, turkey pastrami on rye, and a can of something sugary and cold, the man was dead. It took him some minutes to affirm this, but the evidence was unambiguous, and there was no need to call an ambulance. He sat with him, for a short while, and thought about the man’s last words. He cracked the Coke open with a hiss, and drank it down, counting the sips. He left the sandwich. * * * Christopher Barnard will know his father -- though not as intimately as Owen Compliments might have wished -- but he will never know his father’s father, except by name. With the innocence of the young he will laugh as Owen recounts for him the story he knows by heart, of the long string of men who named their sons after themselves: Arthur Gaylord begat Arthur Gaylord begat Arthur Gaylord, no middle names -- just Arthur Gaylord and nothing more, no questions asked. Christopher will take some pleasure in his also having no middle name, perceiving it as a sign of allegiance to his long-lost grandfather, a second alongside his decision to pick up the brush and dance the colours of the sunset onto canvas -- though he will never confide this to his mother, Kate showing no interest in uncovering the mystery of the last of the Arthur Gaylords, whether dead or alive. The tragedy of it all will come one humid morning sixteen years from now, when the aspiring young artist will creep into a block of the city condemned for demolition, seeking some inspiration in the empty towers and dead streets. A junk-strewn alleyway will benevolently bequeath him a weathered sketch book, preserved over the years by its fortuitous placement in the nook at the side of what was once a Vietnamese restaurant. Its cover will once have read "ART COMPLIMENTS", the name by which his father went, refashioning his identity to fit the only words he saw printed in his own hand after the morning on which he forgot his own name. But now the cover will be blotchy, rain-battered; the ink will have run, the letters’ outlines merging into one another. He’ll write his own name over it. The anonymous artist whose lost sketch book will become Christopher’s life’s inspiration will be his own grandfather: Arthur Gaylord the last. But he will never know. As for Owen, it will have been three years since Art’s death that word finally winds its way back to him. After some reflection, he doesn’t much care. He has a life to live. He has a son to raise. * * * Dear Uncle Tim! he reads, the blue ink approximating a child’s hand. Mommy and I hope your having a GREAT time "Down Under!!" Today mommy is grumpy because I woke her up again and again during the night... but she doesnt mind because Im mommys little boy really!! Tomorrow mommy is shopping for new clothes for me because Im growing SO fast and she wishes you were here to see it... Love you Uncle Tim, from Christopher Barnard aged one month! And added later, at the bottom, in a darker, more fluent print: P.S. Tim, I miss you so much... xxx The man sighs, runs a hand through his hair. He looks up expectantly, and Rachel scurries over with a well-trained obedience. "Sir?" the pretty young blonde enquires. He blinks, still thinking. "He just left this," he begins, brandishing the postcard, "in his room?" Rachel twists her face apologetically. Looks away as if she’s the one with something to be sorry for. "It was in the waste paper bin, sir. I didn’t have the heart to recycle it." The hotel manager nods, a glum resignation settling on his face. He encounters all manners of people from all walks of life each day here in Sydney’s finest hotel, but finding himself enrolled in almost obscenely personal networks of love, trust, hope and betrayal... it still throws him. "The guest’s name was?" "Shipley, sir," Rachel confirms, having known the question was coming. "One of the wrestlers." Jonathan Mackleberry scowls, and the maid waits, uncertain as to whether she too should scowl. "What kind of a man throws this postcard away?" he demands, pinning the card between thumb and forefinger. "Can you tell me that, Rachel?" She swallows. "I guess it’s the kind of man who’s running away from something, sir. The kind of man who isn’t quite ready to take on his responsibilities." "But in running away," her boss interjects, "he only increases that burden. He only makes it worse." Rachel waits a moment, leaving enough space for Mackleberry to finish his thought. But it’s one that goes unfinished. "Do you want me to take that, sir?" "No," the manager says quickly, stuffing the postcard into his pocket. "I’ll deal with it. And Rachel?" She stops, halfway back to her supplies cart. "Sir?" "Tell Rhiannon at the desk to hold any more mail that arrives for this Shipley character, and stick it in my pigeon-hole." "GCW left forwarding addresses, sir." Mackleberry grits his teeth. "Do as you’re told, would you, Rachel? There’s a good girl." Between silk sheets, Jonathan Mackleberry can’t sleep that night. He feels the mother’s anguish as she inks words from her newborn’s mouth. Every sentence throbs, burns in his mind. The postcard sits on the side table, obstructing the green glow of clock time, its words digested and in time committed to memory. Now the infantile strokes of the ballpoint pen scythe through his mind. He can taste her desperation. He hopes someone, somewhere, is looking out for her.
