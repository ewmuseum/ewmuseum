Rueben

18 July 2010

Date: 07/18/10 Concrete walls muffled the crowd’s roar as Andy brushed through the curtains and disappeared into the Tokyo Dome’s guts. It had been a good night. Opponent walkout wasn’t Andy’s conclusion of choice, and Tim Shipley’s vanishing act had only strengthened his desire to knock out the Englishman’s teeth, but it would nice to wake-up the next morning and actually be able to get out of bed. Chris Bagwell patted Andy’s back as they went their separate ways. He’d warmed to his former foe recently but there was a long way to go before Andy would class him as anything more than a loose acquaintance. Bagwell had, after all, once slept with Andy’s ex-wife for ego’s sake. Chris would have to do a lot more than partner Andy in a tag match to earn his trust outright. Andy nodded politely as a handful of technicians greeted him with “well done”s and “good job”s. By now the backstage victory walk had become routine, but he felt just a little bit too comfortable that night. Perhaps it was that the match had ended in farce, or maybe it was because he was opening the show for the first time in longer than he could remember, but Andy had never felt so flat after a pay-per-view win. The atmosphere just wasn't there. Sure, the fans had cheered and sung his name, and the few people he ran into backstage offered congratulations and handshakes, but it wasn't the Rampage vibe he was accustomed to. Nothing like last year. He reached his locker-room after one of the least satisfying victories in his career glared into the mirror. Was that it?, his mind asked a flawless reflection. Barely a hair was out of place and not a bead of sweat had trickled from his brow. He'd gone out expecting a war, and returned ten minutes later with barely a scratch to blemish his pale complexion. He should've felt pleased: his night was over and all that was left on the agenda was to sit back and enjoy the rest of the show, but Andy felt quite the opposite. His locker-room was cold and empty, and barely a sound echoed around the arena's concrete hallways. There would be no visits to the medical room, no grand celebrations, and no being swarmed by jubilant crowds on the way out of the building. Those things were meant for someone else that night. A slow groan escaped his body as he began unravelling his wrist tape. Johnny had sold the match to him as a “hot opener”; an all-action, fast-paced encounter featuring four of the biggest stars in GCW today that would set a blistering tone for the rest of the evening. Andy figured that if that match was to set the tone, then the GCW faithful were in for a long, long night. Then he heard a voice drifting a long the corridor. Bright, loud, and exuberant, the unmistakable tones of Clyde Fox bounced through the building like a rubber ball. It was the biggest night of the kid's career, and Clyde sounded was more hyperactive than ever. He'd worked long and hard for almost three years and finally he was getting his chance to headline a GCW pay-per-view, albeit alongside three other wrestlers. Despite his obvious mental deficiencies, Clyde was a good kid. His will was strong, his spirit was good, and he'd never harm a hair on another man's head outside the squared circle. If there was one man in Global Championship Wrestling who deserved such an opportunity, it was Clyde. But Andy wasn't proud for Clyde when he heard him bounding past his door, no. It was jealous that crept from Andy's stomach and soured his mood. Clyde Fox was exactly where Andy was twelve months ago: as Rampage 2010 was the biggest night of Clyde's career, Rampage 2009 was the biggest night of Andy's. In 2009, Andy's stomach was full of butterflies as he paced restlessly around his locker-room, ready to tear-up his legacy and write a new one. In 2010, Andy couldn't help but wonder if that legacy was dwindling away from him. He threw a ball of bundled-up tape against the floor pushed his head back against the wall. Nothing like last year at all. * * * * * Madison Square Garden, New York City. 21st June, 2009. LeStatt Knight had dragged Andy Murray to hell, but the Scot had emerged from the flames with a bloody-toothed grin on his face and a shiny strap of gold in his hand. Now it was clapping and cheering and standing ovations and “THANK YOU AN-DEE!” and his heart pounding so hard it's about to burst through his chest and skinned knuckles and wobbly knees and gashed foreheads and not giving a fuck because the adrenaline's keeping him standing. It was Andy Murray stood on the middle turnbuckle with a fist pounding his chest and a belt raised to the air. His body was about to implode and the show had already been off the air for five minutes, but Andy didn't care. This was the moment he'd worked all his life for and he would milk it for all it was worth. Eventually he was out of the ring and had found a towel to soak-up the crimson and then he's in with the crowd, taking pictures and signing autographs and the buzz never wears off. His face is a mess and he's been through a war but the Scottish King of Cool finally has a crown. He knew it was time to leave when rows started to empty and medics carried his fallen opponent past him. Andy took his time to wander up the ramp and finally offered the remaining fans a bow before vanishing through the curtain. Pain started to grip his joints by the time he got backstage but the staff's roar was his second wind. The smile returned as every ring crew member and backstage worker under Steven Caldera's employ saluted the victorious warrior with decibels. Then it was a man's face appearing in the crowd, and it's the first man to ever give Andy Murray a chance in the wrestling business and Andy's brushing paramedics away to throw his arms over him. “You did it! You fucking did it!” Rueben Dallas called, completely oblivious to the blood and sweat left behind on his suit as he pulled away from his highest earner. “I fucking did, didn't I?” Andy said between laboured breaths, still beaming from ear-to-ear. He became away of his bleeding head for the first time when a familiar, rusty taste crept through the corner of his mouth. Andy raised a hand and when he pulled it down it was red. “I think I need a band-aid,” he quipped. “You needed a band-aid about forty minutes ago, Andy!” Rueben was as buoyant as his client as placed a hand on Andy's glistening back and ushered him away from the crowd. “Come on, we gotta get you patched-up.” “Fuck that; where's my family? We gotta celebrate.” “There'll be plenty of time for that afterwards, Andy.” By now they'd rounded a corner and moved-on to another group of clapping well-wishers. “You'll thank me when the endorphins wear-off.” Andy said nothing; just stretched his Cheshire Cat grin further as his azure blues met Rueben's deep hazels. Rueben was right. Rueben was always right. * * * * * It was just about lunchtime when John Hood found his rummaging interrupted by his door swinging opening and the click-clack of leather shoes on a marble floor. “Were you raised in a field?” John said. He didn't need to look up: there was only one man brassy enough to stomp into his office unannounced. “Knock, motherfucker.” “Sorry, I know these Victoria's Secret deliveries require your utmost attention,” Andy Murray said, closing the glass door behind him. “Very funny, Andy. Actually, I was just going through a few options for your next public appearance.” John stood-up and pulled two fuzzy, garishly-coloured costumes from the box he'd been raking through. “What do you reckon? Fluffy the Bunny, or Fido the Dog?” Andy knew that working with his former Omega Championship Wrestling colleague again wasn't going to be a pleasant experience, but John had taken to tormenting Andy like a duck to water. The past month had seen John forcing Andy into a toe-curling interview with celebrity shithawk Perez Hilton, among other things. Andy always knew his skin was thick, but soon he'd find out just how thick. A smirk appeared through the former colour commentator's fake tan as he shook the outfits on their hangers. “What's the matter, Andy? Don't you like 'em? Went to a lot of trouble to get these for you, y'know.” “I'm just giddy at the prospect of jumping into one of those fluffy monstrosities and making a tit out of myself,” Andy said, deadpan. “Can't you tell?” He raised a finger to his emotionless face. “I thought you were supposed to be a crowd-pleaser? Look at these things: the kids'll love 'em!” “I came to talk business.” Andy paced his way across the high-ceilinged, glass-walled office and parked his 275lbs on a white leather couch. His plain white tee stretched across his iron-cast chest as he extended his arms along the back. “But if you're so fond of them, why don't you throw one on?” “You brought this on yourself, you know,” John said. The costumes were dumped in their box, then the box to the floor. He was beginning to feel uncomfortable in his tight-fitting white suit. “You fucked with me, now I get to fuck with you. Deal with it.” “It was eight years ago, John. And nothing happened.” “I know people who saw her coming out of your room. Don't try to tell me 'nothing happened'.” The Scottish grappler had no response. He'd been over his side of the story a thousand times before, and repetition #1001 wasn't going to convince John Hood that there was more to his wife being seen leaving Andy's hotel room at 8am than met the eye. Instead, Andy leaned back and watched John sat down on Rueben's chair behind Rueben's desk in Rueben's office; the perch from which he ran Rueben's agency and pocketed Rueben's salary. “Business, then.” John locked his fingers and leaned forward: even Hood knew that there was little point in another verbal spar. His disdain for Andy Murray was great, but finally he found himself in a position of power over the former GCW Champion. Murray's global brand grew greater every time he wrestled and his paycheck was expanding quicker than Tim Sylvia's waistline. Owning a portion of those things was a better vengeance than Hood could ever have hoped for. “The Elite Title Tournament,” Andy said. “Get me in it.” “Ohhhhh.” One side of John's face curled into a smile. “Now what would a lowly show-opener like you want to do with a big, prestigious tournament like that?” “Because I can win it.” “I dunno, man. Desade's in that tournament. Aimz. Tchu--” “I've beaten Aimz and I've beaten Tchu. Give me Desade right now and I know I'll at least give her a fuckin' fight.” Andy paused, composing himself. “I deserve better than shitheads like Labor and Shipley.” “So it's an ego thing?” John said, slowly rising. “Big Andy Murray can't handle the fact that he's not GCW's top dog any more, so he goes hunting for another way to get his name back in the headlines.” Andy didn't reply. John's theory was a lot more accurate than he wanted to admit. Hood sat on the couch opposite Andy and looked him in the eye. For once, he found himself in a position where an upturn in Andy Murray's career would actually benefit him. “What does Johnny think?” “He's good with it.” “I'll think about it.” “Have you even seen the payouts for this thing? Here was me thinking you enjoyed taking money from me.” “More than anything else in life, Andy.” John was grinning. “Like I said: I'll think about it.” Andy stood-up and made for the door; fortunately, his contract with the agency didn't obligate him to stick around for coffee and small-talk with a man he'd rather punch than talk to. With a simple, abrupt “you do that,” Andy was out the door, through the open plan office, and stepping into the elevator. Though John's words were ambiguous, Andy knew that the financial rewards of the tournament were too much for Hood to turn down. Even if Andy bowed-out in the first round he stood to make a six-figure sum, and if he won? John's percentage would bring him enough money for a new car or five. That's what John would get from the tournament. For Andy, it was a chance to re-establish himself as a PTC competitor. He'd excelled in the first Elite Title Tournament by overcoming PRIME Champion Devin Shakur and Wade Elliot only to fall to Steve Knox in the finals, but his GTT7 run had been an epic disappointment. A minor injury had forced his withdrawal after defeating Tchu in the first round, and his replacement, Impact, would make great strides in the tournament. It made Andy sick to even think about it. The elevator shook to life but the ride didn't last long. Soon Andy was walking through the foyer and out into the parking lot. A push of a button unlocked his Benz and he was just about to slip inside when a woman called his name. Andy turned to meet the source: Cherise Dallas, tissues in hand, eye-liner streaming down her cheeks. “Cherise, Jesus,” Andy said, startled. “What's goi--”. “He's. He's.” Her voice broke before she could finish her sentence. Cherise pushed the tissues to her face and sobbed. “I can't do this.” Andy's facial muscles sagged. Lifting her spirit wasn't going to be easy. * * * * * Seattle, Washington. 25th September, 2003. His client had only been gone for half and hour, but Rueben felt like he'd been sitting outside the doctor's office all day. It was a nervous time and Dallas was flustered: his client had only just escaped the grind of the Southern independent scene, but the fates were already conspiring against him. Andy had come into NLW with the momentum of a runaway train. His reputation already considerable after winning his first World Championship less than six months prior, Andy was tearing through the competition and, by all means, looked to be on his way to another big title victory. This was the start of something big, and Rueben knew it. The kid was big, strong, and athletic: his wrestling skills were still a little rough round the edges and his style needed refinement, but his potential was limitless. But he wasn't just a convincing grappler. Andy was calm and confident with a microphone and at-ease before a crowd, and his handsome British features meant that there was never a shortage of female fans asking for his photograph when the show was finished. He had the look that the continent's biggest promoters wanted, the wrestling ability, and the natural charisma. How far he'd go in the business would depend greatly on his own ambition, but there was no reason Rueben couldn't make this kid a superstar. But this was a problem. Rumour had it that Primetime Championship Wrestling scouts were watching Andy on the night he took a fall from NLW's towering steel cage, but the botched landing would surely have put an end to their interest. Unfit to finish the match or walk out of the building without aid, Murray was hurt. The clock struck 3:00pm when Rueben scratched the prickly heat from his bald head. He hadn't been in the agent's business for long but already he'd seen a handful of young careers snatched away by hospital beds and surgical robes: he hoped dearly that he wasn't about to see another. Finally the door creaked open and Rueben shot to his feet. A twenty-something Scot shambled through, head hunched, and Dallas knew it wasn't good. “Well?” he asked, impatient. “What's the matter?” “Doc says I need an operation.” Andy looked up. “Gonna miss ten, maybe twelve months.” Fate threw an invisible brick into Rueben's gut. Bile swirled in his stomach's pit and he had to swallow to keep it from rushing up his gusset. “Jesus. But you're gonna be able to wrestler after, right? You're gonna be okay?” “Dunno. Said I might not be able to take any big hits after this.” Andy's voice was that of a man defeated, and his body language was much the same. His shoulders were hung low and his gaze had returned to the carpet, and for good reason. The rug had been swept from under his feet. Somebody had drawn a blind over his glittering future, and suddenly he saw nothing but needles and medication instead of wrestling rings and title belts. “I'm sorry, Andy,” Rueben said, gently patting the Scot's injured back. His tone changed immediately. “But don't lose your hope. This is only temporary: you'll come back next year and sure, you'll probably have to work your way back up the ladder, but you'll get there. You're too good not to.” “But what if I can't do this any more?” Andy sighed. “Fuck, man. I can't even lean over to open the freezer without taking five minutes to get back up at the moment.” “That's what the operation's for,” said Rueben, trying his best to remain calm and placid. “It's a dangerous sport; stuff like this happens all the time. Be strong, do what they say, and you'll get through it in no-time.” Andy closed his eyes and shook his head. Rueben was still there when he re-opened them and he was thankful: how many agents would make sure efforts to lift their client's spirit? He'd heard so many horror stories from colleagues who'd signed their careers away to cash-hungry vultures that he'd been reticent to in a deal with the Dallas Group at first, but now he was glad that he had. “When's the op?” “Next week sometime.” “Good. Better to get it out of the way early.” “I guess.” It was the first time in Andy's young life that anyone had every told him he couldn't do something he loved. It was a lot to handle. “Come on, chin up” Rueben said. “The game is yours for the taking. This doesn't change anything.” “But what if it does.” “It doesn't. Don't think like that. You keep thoughts like that you your head and you're gonna end-up trawling bingo halls and school gymnasiums for the rest of your life. The measure of a man is not how he acts when things are comfortable, but how he handles himself when times get rough.” Andy felt a warmth in his stomach. It was nice having someone to pick him up. * * * * * “I-... I dunno, Cherise,” Andy said, gazing through his windscreen at nothing in-particular. “That's a lot to ask.” “Please, Andy,” she bled. “Please! I can't do this myself.” “But he's your husband; I'm just a client.” “You're not 'just a client' and you know it.” Andy gripped the steering wheel until his knuckles whitened. The parking lot was empty and they were safe within the confines of his car, but Andy felt like a million eyes were upon him and not just Cherise's two. This was a big ask; bigger than anything he'd asked him before, and certainly something that needed a lot more consideration than Rueben Dallas' wife would allow. “I can't. I'm not even family.” Andy eased his grip and looked to Cherise's swollen eyes. “Don't make me do this alone. Please.” “Doesn't he have a brother? A parent?” Cherise shook her head. “No.” “Do you?” “Nope.” Andy sighed. Maybe he owed it to her. Maybe he owed it to him. Rueben had taken his fledgling career and helped sculpt him into one of the biggest stars in the business. Maybe it was time to repay him. “He's suffered long enough,” Andy said. “Somebody has to do it.” “I've known this was c-coming for months,” Cherise struggled through sniffles and tears, “b-but n-now the time has come, I can't. I can't do it, Andy. I can't.” Tear ducts opened and she was gone again. No matter what Andy said to her, there was no way he was going to be able to talk her into making this call alone. She'd been building up to this moment for months and she thought she was ready, but it's hard when it's someone you love. “Alright,” Andy finally said. “I'll do it.” * * * * * Houston, Texas. 2nd March, 2004. His working day had already lasted a couple of hours longer than Rueben had expected but at last it was time to go home. Sitting in his glass-walled office, Dallas shuffled A4 sheets into a binder and was about to pick-up his jacket when the telephone rang. Andy's name flashed across the screen. “Oh shit,” Rueben said. He'd forgotten all about the hearing. Rueben pressed the little green button and raised the cell to his ear. “How'd it go, Andy?” “She got half.” Slowly rising, Rueben's tone deepeed. “Half? Well, I guess that's what you expected.” “Yeah, it's just--...” a distorted, downtrodden Scottish voice tailed away on the other end of the line. “It's pretty ridiculous. She walks away from me and I still have to give her half of everything? If you walk away you walk away. Fucking bullshit.” “I hear you. That's not how these procedures work, though.” Rueben slowly made his way over to the room's floor-to-ceiling windows. The setting sun had cast an orange hue over Houston's skyline as the city went to sleep. “It's always the guy that gets shafted by divorce, especially when it's somebody that a few people might know the name of. Just be thankful that you've still got half, at least. What about the boy?” “I get weekends.” “Could've been worse.” “Could've been better.” “You're on the road five days a week anyway. It's not like you spend every day with him.” “Yeah, but he's my son.” Andy paused. “It's. It's just hard. I dunno, man. If I was an accountant, me and Jane would still be together and we'd be raising Marvin together.” “If you were an accountant then you'd be bored as fuck,” Rueben said. “Trust me. That's what this job is all about, man. Tough decisions. You spend most of your time away from home and it takes toll. That's just how it is.” “Just isn't easy knowing that I'm coming home to an empty house.” “No, it isn't. But there's a thousand guys in the business in exactly the same situation, Andy. Besides, you're 26: are you really willing to give it all up at this stage in your career?” There was a delay. “No.” “Precisely.” Rueben leaned against the window, gazing out across the city below. “I know you want it all – the family, the lifestyle, the career – but it takes work, you gotta find a balance. Sometimes, Andy, you need to let go of something you cherish to help you breathe.” * * * * * Andy wasn't quite ready for what awaited him. The doctor lead him into the private room and he barely recognised the man laying beneath the sheets. Completely hairless and with a face so gaunt it was almost skeletal, this was the body of a Sudanese refugee and not one of the wrestling industry's most prosperous agents. Stifling the lump in his throat with a hard swallow, Andy took the man's bony hand and held it in his own meaty paw. The man's decline had been rapid, and death's icy touch would soon carry him to another life. Doctors had subjected the patient to the finest treatments they knew from the very day he was diagnosed, but they could only fight the cancer for so long. Now they'd done all they could; the decay could no longer be stopped. The disease had won, and now there was a decision to be made. Murray dropped the frail limb and cast his eyes around the room. The equipment could keep his friend alive for another three or four weeks, they said, but he wasn't going to wake-up. His eyes had closed for the last time, and all it took was a minute alone with the man's lifeless body for Andy to see that. He left the room before it became too much for him to take. The doctors were waiting expectantly for him. “Turn it off,” Andy said softly, head bowed. “Just turn it off.” * * * * * RUEBEN OMARI DALLAS 12th FEBRUARY 1969-17th JULY 2010.
