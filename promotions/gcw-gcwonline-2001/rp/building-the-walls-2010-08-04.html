Building the Walls

4 August 2010
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 08/04/10 The nights were hot and the days were heavy. But Tim Shipley found excuses to stick around. Anaheim: city of nothings. Commodification ablaze. His unlikely fortress of solitude. In the ring things couldn’t be better. He’d ascended the ladder so quickly they were adding new rungs. Jay Terror had built a career on aura, but aura counted for little up against a ring general in the making. Another three-count, another slip for the Outlaw, another notch in Shipley’s belt. Week by week he made a mockery of Chris Storm’s billing as the Last Great Technical Wrestler. Week by week Storm’s head strained a little further to look back over his shoulder, feeling the coming wave. The veteran was on borrowed time. And with every Eternal Hope, that time became Shipley’s. But time was the last thing Tim Shipley needed. He had more time than he knew what to do with, and the time spent alone was the most punishing. Tim had never been the most social creature, but companionship kept his guard up. He didn’t want the chance to retreat into his shell, to prod at the soft insides, which shrank and shrivelled at his touch. He wanted to put reality on indefinite hiatus. Reality always winds its way back in, though, at the least opportune of moments. And so it was that lead weights lined his stomach as he pulled his body up between the horizontal bars, teeth clenched, spitting out names: one pull-up for each opponent he’d defeated. “Fujita... Action... PAZUZU... Rasa... Shaman... Terror... Messiah... Spencer... Tempest... Terror...” Rest. Repeat. “Fujita... Action... PAZUZU... Rasa--” And then he fell. He just let go of the bars and dropped, his arms locking up at the elbows, hitting the floor with a grunt. His knee crashed against the weight stack, and he clutched it, groaning. His eyes searched wildly around him, but it was three a.m.; Tim Shipley had the basement gym to himself. Rasa. It was no coincidence he’d fallen at the mention of the androgynous assassin, for more than anyone in the ring, it was Rasa that he feared. Shipley’s most valuable tool was his mind, but it was impossible to set that to work with so little to go on. New attire for every show, but never so much as a sliver of skin on show to prove the humanity of whatever was inside. Maybe his eyes played tricks, or maybe Rasa was a different height and weight each week. No one had got close enough to measure up against him, and certainly no one had dared to try and weigh him. Or her. And that made Shipley doubly uncomfortable. It wasn’t that being in the ring with a woman made a difference... except it did. You could flog liberal arts students to within an inch of their lives and you still wouldn’t knock the ingrained essentialism out of them. Men and women were fundamentally different beings... always would be. Different bodies, different brains. Different feelings, emotions, abilities. Everyone was different, everthing was different, and that mattered. Because for all the science behind Tim Shipley’s ring technique, at heart he liked to believe himself an artist. All sport was art. For if there was a sure-fire, invulnerable way to win, would humanity not have found it? Shipley was meticulous in his preparations, he gave himself every possible chance – he conditioned his body – he learned his opponents’ strategies, strengths, weaknesses – he visited the arena a day before, inspected the ring an hour before – but when the chime of metal on metal announced the start of the contest, it was just two fighters beneath those lights. Two fighters opening each other’s eyes. Two fighters sharing their souls. Merging into one whirl of motion, the brushstrokes of Picasso, the melodies of Mozart. And breaking apart again. Knowing that only one can create the masterpiece. But to create a masterpiece you must have the materials. A potter cannot work without clay. A violinist cannot play without strings. You must know them... feel them. Be at one with them. And Tim lay there holding his knee and closed his eyes tight, and saw nothing. He saw absolute blackness. How do you tailor a suit to a man who has no form? How do you draw a phantom? Behind that mask Rasa could be a god. Or the devil himself. Rasa could be Ares... Hades... Zeus. Or all three under the pure virginal skin of Artemis. Rasa could be that man who tried to talk to you in the elevator. Or that woman he held the door open for. Rasa could be none of them or both of them. There was nothing even to say that Rasa could only be considered in the singular. Rasa could be everyone... everything. Rasa could be unstoppable. * * * Kate rises with the sun, and trembles till it falls. In the twilight she walks, the streets still and grey, her footsteps the only intrusion upon the pristine silence. The trees bend under the weight of their branches, as if all of nature is bowing to her. She is the queen of her realm. The man in the black suit walks a hundred paces behind her, and he doesn’t let her out of his sight. That’s his job. The empty, flat outskirts of Saskatoon are supposed to help her get away from it all. Certainly they do, at least physically; she’s two thousand miles from Albany. She lived here, once. When she was young, just for a year. She spent a winter cuddled up to her mother for warmth, the icy wind down the chimney continually putting out the meagre log fire. She spent a summer with the birds and the animals. There were no other children. Though she was a sociable child, it was a solitude she embraced, for whatever reason. It was good for her. It prepared her. After her mother died, after her father left, she surrounded herself with people, but never felt more alone. Living with Tim was the loneliest experience of her life, sometimes. He was there, but at the same time he was not. In the bough of the tree she spots a toad, mud-coloured. She bends down and takes it into her hand. The creature hesitates before crawling into her palm. She watches it for some time, her clear blue eyes full of compassion. So small, so fragile, so helpless. She returns the toad to the forest floor, and it retreats into the leaf litter. She smiles as she watches it go, and wipes a tear from her pale cheek. She stands, and turns around, and looks steadily at the man who has been sent to watch her. He is no more than fifty yards from her, his hands deep in the pockets of a rugged knee-length coat. He makes no effort to disguise his purpose. He is young, but at the same time old. His hair is thinning, his brow wrinkled. His face lacks expression, lacks emotion. Kate does not take her eyes from his as she unbuttons her overcoat. The long woollen jacket slips from her shoulders to the ground. She unbuttons her cardigan, placing it carefully atop her discarded coat. She crouches down and removes her shoes. And then she unties her hair, pink elastic smudged into the characterless green and brown. She turns, and with her hair loose, wearing just a thin cotton vest and her long skirt that catches on the nettles, walks on into the woods. It starts to rain, but she leaves her coat, because although she’s cold and wet, it’s not too late for her to face life unprotected. * * * Time. Space. They needed both these things. They needed family, friends, but more importantly they needed strength in themselves. They needed faith, not in a higher power, but their power here. They needed to know light from dark, day from night, life from death. Comfort and punishment. Respite and focus. A future and a past. They needed these things. They may have needed each other, or they may have been better apart. If ever soulmates existed – their one true loves – bid them come forth now. For they needed them. They needed more than the sense that there was nothing. At the end of life and the beginning of death. They needed purposes, or a purpose. They needed nature, and harmony with it. They needed union and communion. They needed to go back to go forward. As if things could return to the way they had always been. The missing person – returned. Labour’s lost love – found. They needed to learn from history to write their biographies. Together or apart. They needed closures, but equally they needed openings. They needed their tabula rasa.
