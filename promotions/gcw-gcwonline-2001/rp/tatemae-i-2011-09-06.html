Tatemae: I

6 September 2011
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 09/06/11 September 1, 2011 Osaka, Japan Trans-Pacific flights were becoming a depressingly regular portion of my everyday life. I would not have flown back to Osaka after WorldWide 152 without an extraordinarily good reason. Unfortunately, life had handed me an extraordinarily good reason in the form of information that had taken several weeks to reach me due to a combination of miscommunication and the fact that it was very difficult to contact someone who was on the moon unless you happened to be a national government. It turned out that Kakihara-san, my boss at Club Dekedan, had fallen gravely ill. Doctors were blaming his condition on organ failure, and he was perceived to have very little time with. The news reached me finally in Chicago, Illinois. A flight of that length was not something I enjoyed, but the situation was a no-brainer. Kakihara was like family to me, and so I boarded the flight from Chicago to Kansai International Airport. All of that, though, seemed like nothing at all when I saw my boss lying in bed, frailer than I could ever remember. Even as he spoke to me, his voice was weak and hoarse. “Club Dekedan has no heir to inherit it,” he said, quietly. “For reasons that I will explain to you later, you are the man I think can do best with it, for both the club itself and its employees who depend on it to make a living. But before I can do that, Ryuji, I need to ask you a question. It is something that I have wondered for two years about you, if I may be completely frank.” That wasn’t ominous at all, really, was it? Still, what could I do? He deserved an honest answer. Never mind the fact that he was, you know, offering me ownership of his life’s work. “Go ahead. Ask me whatever you like.” I would really, really wish in a few moments that I hadn’t said that. He sized me up, and though he was weak, Kakihara’s eyes still burned into mine. His gaze hadn’t weakened one iota, not even in his current condition. “I need to know your past, Kamigawa-san,” he said, gaze firm. “I believe the best in you, but I have looked into your eyes sometimes and seen a look there that I cannot explain. Something that…worries me. I trust you. There is little you could tell me that would shake me from my resolve, but I want to know: what are you hiding from me?” I was utterly astonished. I’d never been asked that before. Not by anyone, and yet my kindly, elderly boss apparently saw into my soul and found blackness there. He wasn’t off. At all. “You ask me to tell you something that happened a decade ago, Kakihara-san,” I said, clearly a bit shaken. “It is something I have told no one else in my life, and it is something that, were I in your position, I would not want to burden my soul with on the eve of death.” “Your point is valid. I still want to know.” Well, then. Not much I could say to that except “yes.” It didn’t take much effort to recount the story. Eleven years plus later, and I still remembered it as if it was yesterday. January 28, 2000 Ooki Noodle Shop Tokyo, Japan I was nineteen years old. I didn’t know my ass from a hole in the ground yet in the business, but I did know that the assignment I’d been given was something that had to be done. It was a suicide mission; my superiors had made no bones about that fact. They couldn’t send a more established, more experienced hitman for that reason. The Iguchi-gumi couldn’t afford a loss like that, locked as it was in a turf war with its rivals. So that left them no choice but to rely on a nineteen year old kid with a pistol and a dream of meaning something someday in the Japanese underworld. Didn’t hurt that the fuckers had killed my oyabun – my boss, the man whom I owed everything to. He’d taught me how to survive in the family, all the customs and practical knowledge. He’d taken me from a sixteen year old kid with two dead parents and no hope to a member of the Iguchi-gumi. I’d have gladly laid down my life for him had he asked. I took this job to extend that to him even in the world beyond. I stood outside the door to Ooki’s, a popular noodle shop in a bad part of Tokyo. Behind the door was the boss of our rival family, the man who’d ordered the hit on the one person who gave more than a single shit about me. He’d be with fellow yakuza and hangers-on, no doubt, but I had the element of surprise. Once I burst in the door, I’d have a little bit to establish myself before everyone realized what the fuck was happening. Time almost stood still as I opened the door to the restaurant. Once inside, everything seemed so goddamned normal it was astonishing. I saw my target across the room, the tallest man there, enjoying a bowl of ramen noodles. To him, this was just another day. To me, this would be the last fucking day he ever saw. There was no clean shot, so I kept the pistol that was pressed against my side hidden. It felt like a burning poker digging into my side, just waiting to be unleashed upon the unsuspecting bastards. I counted seven in total – bad news considering that I only had six bullets. I shook my head. Not like I really thought I could survive this, anyway. As long as one of the six bullets buried itself in the tall guy’s brain, mission accomplished. Apparently, my quarry saw my indecision. Or he knew, somehow, who I was. It didn’t matter which, because he made things infinitely more complicated in the blink of an eye. “What the fuck are you idiots doing? Shoot him! Shoot him now, goddamnit!” I heard the tall man screaming at his subordinates to shoot me, to end my life, but I couldn’t have cared less. In that moment, all I felt was unmitigated rage. These parasites had murdered the one man in the world that I looked up to. I would take as many of them with me to Hell as I could. I had accepted this mission knowing that it was veritably suicide, and done so with a smile on my face. From my white coat, I withdrew a pistol, pointed it at the nearest dapper-clad gangster, and pulled the trigger. *BANG!* Down he went, like a sack of shit. The others in the room panicked, crying out in sheer terror. They’d never seen a gun go off before. Never known what it felt like to shoot another man, to take another life. The guy lying on the floor was the third man I’d ever killed. The first two had been hard, but I didn’t feel a damned thing for this one. One brave soul knew what was happening, though. He knew that if I didn’t go down, he was. He pulled his own gun, and fired upon me with a shaking, trembling arm. The shot sailed over my right shoulder. The shot I fired in response buried itself in the shooter’s heart. Two men down, and four more rank-and-file soldiers in utter panic. All the while I kept moving forward, the tall man exhorting his compatriots to do something, anything. “SHOOT HIM! SHOOT HIM!” From the corner of the room, another gangster pulled his firearm. I could tell just by watching that he knew what he was doing. His arm didn’t shake, and there was only grim resolve in his eyes. He took the time to line up his shot, and fired. *BANG!* The bullet hit home, catching me square in the chest. It took the wind from me, and knocked me back a step, but all it really accomplished was pissing me off more. I turned to face him, lined up the shot, and fired. *BANG!* Third man down, courtesy of a bullet in his right eyeball. The other three men parted like the Red Sea after that. Watching a man take a bullet to the chest and just keep coming was an impossibility. Only the boss seemed to be coherent enough to figure out the obvious solution to the conundrum, but his voice was barely audible over the commotion. “He’s got a vest! SHOOT HIM IN THE HEAD!” He didn’t have a firearm of his own. That much was clear; otherwise, he wouldn’t have needed to tell someone else to shoot me in the head. The clarity that I felt was amazing. There were no nerves, no tension, just a grim determination that I had never felt in my life before. As the yes men and hangers-on scattered, it was just me and the boss. I had a firearm. The boss didn’t. There was only one way this could end, and he knew it. Our eyes locked, and the corners of his mouth drew up in a wry smile. He said nothing, but the expression spoke volumes. Honda Takeshi had no idea who I was, but he knew full well why I was here. Unlike his henchmen, he would not run or hide. He would not disgrace himself as his associates had. It was their responsibility to sacrifice themselves if the time was right, and no time was more right than the present. They might live through this day, but their lives as gangsters were essentially over. From his coat, Konda drew a dagger. There were guns, of course, scattered around the room, formerly owned by his dead compatriots, but to get one would require taking his eyes off of me and mean certain death. He had to die. What he had done was unforgivable in my eyes and the eyes of my family. For Takeshi, his sin was entirely apersonal. It was strictly business, a necessary step to the cementing of his power in the bad stretch of Tokyo we fought over. His business, however, had made my issue with him gravely personal. Konda charged, the dagger raised, poised to strike if only he could buy the time needed to reach me. To the end, he would live with honor. The history books, were there truly such a thing in our line of work, would remember us both well: the man who walked into the hornet’s nest to do what had to be done, and the man who faced his death with dignity. The gun fired one more time. Konda’s body recoiled on impact, jerking hideously backwards from his lunging position to smack the hard, wooden floor, dead before he hit the ground. The other three men in the room made the immediate decision to flee, running out the back door of the establishment, undoubtedly to go inform someone that the head of the Konda family had been assassinated in cold blood. How they’d get around the fact that they had let it happen was something they could worry about for the rest of their lives. Only once they were all gone did the rush of adrenaline fade. I sunk to my knees, the pistol falling from nerveless fingers. I had done what I was asked to do – no, what my honor demanded I do. I had accomplished what had to be done. The price would be steep – I’d known that going in. It was only now, though, as I heard the sound of sirens that grew closer by the moment, that I realized that the rest of my life would be spent inside of a cell behind bars. I was nineteen fucking years old. Back in Osaka “That’s about the long and the short of it,” I told Kakihara. Understandably, the old guy looked a little bit shaken. “There’s the aftermath to tell you about, of course, but that’s all fairly obvious.” “Indulge me,” he said, wheezing a bit. I could tell by the look in his eyes that he didn’t quite believe it, that he couldn’t reconcile the man he knew and spoke to every day in his club with a man who’d been a cold-blooded killer at the age of nineteen. Perhaps the most telling part, though, was that he made no move away from me. There was little terror in his eyes, simply curiosity. It was almost as if he was struggling to reconcile the horrific story with what he knew about me. “The police found me there ten minutes later,” I told him, “sobbing like a child. It didn’t take a particularly good detective for figure out what happened. I was handcuffed and taken in. I went on trial for four counts of murder, and was convicted. If I wasn’t nineteen at the time, I’m pretty sure they’d have put me on death row. Instead, they sentenced me to life in prison.” “I….cannot imagine you taking another’s life, Kamigawa-san,” Kakihara admitted, looking at me in astonishment. “Of all of the bouncers I have had, you are the least prone to violence.” “Don’t often have a reason to be prone to violence,” I told him. “Most families do not deal in violence save as a last resort. Violence brings attention, and attention is the one thing that organized crime can abide least. That is the approach I brought to Dekedan. We do not want customers complaining that they were assaulted in our club. That brings bad publicity. Ideally, I can solve a problem with words. If not, and particularly if one of our employees is in danger, I act.” To convince him of my sincerity, an anecdote was likely needed. Fortunately, I could think of one, off the top of my head. “Do you remember, several months ago, when one of our customers attempted to assault Ayako,” I asked. “I do, very well. Moreover, I remember the expression on everyone’s face when I snapped into action and nearly broke the man’s nose by bouncing his face off the floor of Club Dekedan.” Kakihara-san nodded. “I understand. Still, this is much to process. I cannot even imagine being in prison for a single day, let alone seven years. What was it—“ “I do not talk about my time in prison,” I replied, firmly. “You are one of a very precious few to know even what I have told you. What happened to me behind bars will be something I take to my grave. It is a period of my life that I do not wish to think about, as I imagine you can understand.” He waved a hand at me. “Say no more. I understand. However, I AM astonished that you would be allowed out after committing four murders. What exactly happened?” It was a fair question. More than fair, really. Probably the most obvious one to ask. “That much I will tell you,” I replied. “After serving seven years behind bars, most days blend together, unless they are particularly bad. Suffice it to say there are few good days in prison. But October 29th, three years ago, my life was changed forever. The circumstances are shady. Even I don’t know the full details myself, to be completely honest. But I was let out of prison that day, and assigned an entirely new identity. The man I was became dead, and Ryuji Kamigawa was born.” Kakihara had taken a deep breath as I spoke, and continued to hold it, even after I stopped. “That…that is unbelievable. Why would they simply let you out of prison, and help you no less?” “Because,” I said, looking my dying mentor in the eye, “the police are corrupt. You know that to be true as much as I do.” “I had figured it was different for murderers,” he replied, a touch of disbelief in his weakened voice. “You killed four men and only served seven years in prison?” Moment of truth. Either he’d believe me now or he’d die not believing me. I wanted him to believe me, badly. I wanted him to know the man that he’d trusted for the past two plus years wasn’t just some two-bit gangster hitman. “I killed four gangsters,” I corrected. “All of whom were associated with a family that was doing business the wrong way. Very often the police are willing to look the other way at gang activity. Japanese organized crime conducts its business with minimal violence where possible and absolutely tries to avoid effecting the lives of ordinary people who do not associate with us. They violated those basic tenets. After seven years, my crime had been largely forgotten. There were those in the police who sympathized with me. Most importantly, a bribe was paid. My killings had allowed my family to largely take over our section of Tokyo.” “So you took care of them, and they then took care of you,” came the question, and I nodded. “I have enough money in the bank to never work again, if I so choose.” “I expected as much,” the old man admitted. “I have seen many people work in Dekedan, and you always seemed carefree. May I ask how you ended up here, in Osaka, working at my hostess club?” “That I can also tell you. While I did undergo some procedures to make myself less recognizable to most, I recognized that it was probably a good idea to get out of Tokyo. I love big cities, so really, Osaka seemed pretty natural. Got myself an apartment, and spent a couple of days sitting around doing nothing. You know, just watching television shows, eating good food, all the things that you can’t do behind bars. It was nice for about a week and a half, but then I got bored. I’d done nothing for seven years, after all.” “I’m six foot five, Kakihara-san. Three hundred pounds. There aren’t a whole lot of industries that I can work in. Ironically enough, I probably found both of the best – bouncing and pro wrestling. I walked by Dekedan one day, and saw you were looking for help, so I stopped in. The rest is history.” “That,” Kakihara told me, is one of the primary reasons that I want you to run Club Dekedan. You are the sort of man who will think carefully and make measured decisions out of the heat of the moment. Moreover, I want Club Dekedan in your hands because with it, you will not fear the consequences of failure. You will be able to make decisions not worrying about whether you will be able to eat at the end of the month. Having to make decisions under duress is poor business, most of the time.” I knew that Kakihara had no family to take over the club in his stead. Someone would have to continue on his legacy. Still, it was impossible to imagine Kakihara not running the club. He had done so for two years while I had worked there, and apparently many, many years before me. “You truly want me to take over Club Dekedan,” I asked, looking at the frail man laying in his bed, a far cry from the lively Kakihara Renzo that I knew and loved. He nodded. Weak though he was, he took the time to make sure that his nod was firm. “My attorneys have drafted up papers to affect the transfer of ownership to you, Ryuji. There is, however, one more thing that I want to know before I send you to go sign them.” With great, painful effort, Kakihara pulled himself up his bed, propping his weakened form up against several pillows. “Your name is Ryuji Kamigawa now. I have checked into this. You have all of the proper documentation. As your employer, I know this to be true. However…” “You wonder why I’m still alive if I murdered three men in a yakuza family,” I offered, and he nodded, sheepishly. “I simply want to know your name. Your birth name. My time is short, Ryuji. I want to know the real name of the man that I have had the pleasure to work with for two years. I want to know the name of the man who will carry on my dream and vision.” There it was. I looked Kakihara-san in the eyes, and swallowed hard. “You ask no small thing of me,” I replied, voice dry. “My name is something that, if revealed, could very easily get me killed. It is not a request that I may grant lightly. If you were not on your deathbed, Kakihara-san, I would have to decline.” “But I am on my death bed, Ryuji,” he replied, calmly. “The doctors tell me that I have a week left at best. Modern medicine keeps me alive, and I am in constant pain. That is why I am not in the hospital, trying to prolong my existence. I came home to die, here with my family and loved ones. They will be here soon.” For a moment, I was conflicted: a dying man’s wish versus a secret I’d kept ever since being released from prison. Once that moment passed, though, I felt little but shame. This man was willing to give everything he cared about to me, and I couldn’t even tell him my true name? Even if it came back to haunt me, my honor demanded that I tell him what he wished to know. “My name is Kondo,” I said, the word passing through my lips for the first time in ten years. “Kondo Kentaro, of Yokohama, Japan, a proud former member of the Iguchi-gumi. But that is not who I am today. I am Kamigawa Ryuji, proud employee of Club Dekedan, and a professional wrestler.” “Kondo,” Kakihara mused for a moment, “a good, strong name. Not that Ryuji isn’t, of course. You are everything that you have said, and in a few hours, you will be the owner of Club Dekedan. You know where the office of our attorney is. Go there, Ryuji. You are, and always have been, a loyal man until the very end for your employer, no matter who that employer is. Painstakingly slowly, he extended a hand to me. I took it, and bowed deeply. “Thank you, Kakihara-san,” I whispered. “You give me a gift for which I can never repay you.” “You have already repaid me with your loyalty and vigor,” he said. “Now go. Club Dekedan needs you and your judgment. It is difficult times for all industry, and our employees depend on you. Please, do not let them down.” “I will not let them down,” I vowed, and bowed a second time. “Farewell, Kakihara-san, and thank you.” The last words I ever heard from the owner of Club Dekedan were a simple thought, joyously expressed. “Now,” I heard, as I exited the room and closed the door behind me, “I may die in peace.” Never in my life had lying to someone hurt so damned bad as it did that moment.
