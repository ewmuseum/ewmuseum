The Past As Weapon

2 May 2011
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 05/02/11 Phillips Breach was just off Rodney Road, and while he’d been there countless times, he didn’t know if he could find his way there anymore. Or maybe it was that he couldn’t bring himself to go there. What thought came into his head to travel out here, now, after all that’d been done and been undone by the tempest that’d driven them all mad? This was nothing more than riddle at this juncture, but with wounds so fresh, he didn’t know where he began and the gaping hole left from the wake ended. Something was driving him here, to this spot, to their spot, and he was too numb to fight it anymore. The Charger was gone, as was the motorcycle. With only his feet, there would be no quick getaways. He swallowed hard as he made his way through. (1)The clouds above Jefferson County looked like campfire embers, dumping sheets of rain as sudden flashes scarred the sky. Her Chucks were tattered and waterlogged, causing them to smack against the gravel as she traveled down westbound Rodney Road. The forest scenery was static. Every step was taking her further away from the broken home and that drunken bastard she had the misfortune of calling father and the trailer park and the belt he cracked over her thighs whenever he was feeling feisty. Her porcelain skin had long since begun to chaff. The rain was doing her no favors. She’d stolen the camouflage pants from the Salvation Army, and while she wasn’t proud of that, they at least stopped the mosquito bites in the mug of June. Fifteen going on broken. A little girl far away from Kansas. She started as nothing more than a dot on the horizon, but the more ground his broken down Speedster gained, the more he focused on her. Neither of them flinched through the thunderclaps. Once he drew close, he eased up on the accelerator. A greasy mop of amber hair sagged over her eyes. She was just a tiny thing. Smoke sputtered from the exhaust as the engine hiccupped and shook. He swung his leg over the side of the bike, walking it along the lip of the road as he tried not to get ahead of her. He was staring and she noticed. She couldn’t make him out save for small details in her periphery. "Where you headed?" He smelled of cigarettes and black licorice. She turned her head to face him. His cheeks were smattered with oil, a long head of dark hair falling to the shoulders of his leather jacket. He looked like every bad boy she’d ever seen, but his blue eyes were different, comforting. Bubbles floated up her spine. She couldn’t look away. "Nowhere." He smirked at her stammer, causing her apple cheeks to flush pink. She pulled her hood over her head, trying her damnedest to hide. "Just...just...nowhere." "Don’t look like nowhere." "How you figure?" Her lips were buzzing. "Ain’t you a bit young to be riding a bike like that?" His smirk grew with every question she asked. She reminded him of his mother for all the right reasons. "Yeah. Reckon I am. Gonna report me to the law?" The storm rolled past as they walked side by side, the sun smearing the clouds in crayon before petering through. Soon, the humidity would smother them. "Need a ride?" She drew her chipped nails over her cheeks as she pretended to think about it. "New Orleans?" "Sure. Can get you there. Not straight away, but I can get you there." She wanted to believe her raw feet were the reason she was so willing to take him up on his offer, but the fluttering in her stomach made that notion foolish. She didn’t know how old he was. She wondered if she looked attractive to him. She was conscious about her lack of make-up. He pushed the kickstand down with the heel of his boot before lending her a hand. "One thing, though." His hand was rough. She didn’t mind. "What?" "Take off your shoes." She furrowed her brow. "And why would I do that?" "My bike. My rules." She tried to look menacing, but her skin was hot and her lips were curved in a constant grin. She didn’t care if he saw it anymore. If his eyes caught hers, every trick she had would come undone. She reached down with her free hand, untying the knots on her shoes before peeling them off. He motioned for her to give them to him once she did. The laces were soggy. He loosely tied them off on his handlebar and boosted her onto the seat. "Keep your feet on the side and away from the muffler and you’ll be fine." "What should I do with my hands?" "Just brace yourself on the back of the seat." He kicked the stand up and revved the engine, carefully gaining speed down the road, making sure to avoid the puddles. "What’s your name?" "Terrence. Terrence Kingsley." "I’m Autumn." "Beautiful name for a beautiful girl." He couldn’t hear her chuckle over the purr of the engine. Her hands wrapped around his waist. The side of her head found a comfortable spot along the small of his back. "Thanks." "One last thing..." He started as he yanked the laces off the handlebar, letting her ragged shoes skitter across the slick patches of broken asphalt. Her eyes grew wide as she playfully punched his thigh. "I might have to buy you a new pair of shoes." The wind bit into his reddening cheeks, his eyes at half-mast as he pushed through the holly overgrowing the dirt inlet to the barn that once was theirs. Though soundless, his chest vibrated and wavered and the pooling under his eyes burned as the trickle washed over his naked cheeks. He swallowed the flood, choking on every bitter part of it. His nose, broken and set to the side with gauze and adhesive, couldn’t take in the air, and it was just as well because she always loved the smell of lilacs and this was the place he picked them for her. New Orleans was supposed to be the destination ever since she’d heard about the nests of runaways founding their own tight knit communities, but nobody spoke about the roughness there. Not until she’d met stragglers who’d been there when she made her way through the Texas Panhandle. Even still, the bayou called out to her. Nothing could dissuade her. Until he came along. Terrence took her into his home in Rodney, and she’d found shelter along with others who’d had their reasons to run and get away from their lives. Father Fred Kingsley told her about why they’d picked this abandoned parcel of land, about how everyone deserves a chance to live free and leave behind the savagery of civilization, and while none of it made sense, the more he talked, the more she listened. Or maybe it was his son who she was busy listening to, busy trying to shy away from and not get caught with those small shared glances. The apple didn’t fall far from the tree; both spoke with pastor confidence, their rugged looks mismatched by their welcoming demeanors to their children and siblings in their Eden. She couldn’t help the selfishness she felt when she saw him with the other girls. There was the tart with strawberry blonde hair that fell past her shoulders that took up woodworking just so she could suddenly get clumsy whenever Terrence was around, asking him to show her how to carve a perfect spindle or cut along the grain. Or Mary Pike, with her button nose and flaring hips and the endowments of a girl five years older. She couldn’t compete with her tan. Sure, he’d spent time with her, his hands always hovering close. But she felt ugly. Felt distant. He’d known these girls for years. She didn’t have any skills. Didn’t have their beauty. He always kept close to her at night, the two of them tucked away in the corner of the Presbyterian Church he and his father and the militia called home. "There’s a special place I found," he whispered in her ear under the flickering light of propane lanterns, his legs resting against hers on the mattress. "I want to show it to you." For all her insecurities, she couldn’t see the obvious. Couldn’t see how he always made sure that when she sat on his mattress that he was behind her, his body acting as a comforting brace. Couldn’t tell when, whenever he noticed a chill ripple through her body, all the blankets he had were quickly bundled around her. She tried to speak, but the tickle from his big toe along her arch caused the words in her throat to catch. Her forehead rested along his jaw. Barely sixteen and his stubble scratched. She welcomed the sensation. "Yeah?" "Yeah." "Tomorrow, then?" "Tomorrow." Her naked shoulders pimpled with gooseflesh. There were always people in close proximity in Rodney. It was going to be the first time they’d been alone since the first time they’d met, and even though she thought of those bastard Weaverly Brothers and how they forced themselves on the young girls passing through their clave along the Texas and Oklahoma border, she thought Terrence wasn’t like them. Even if he was, she wouldn’t mind it. She drifted off in the grasp of his rough hands. He could still feel her around his hands. His trembling hands. And the blood was gone and washed away and the riddles were coming back but to spoil a good story would be just as criminal. The path was raised, the contours of road sharply sloping to the bayou forest floor, where the lilacs and oleanders stuck out from the murk. There was something foul in the air, and he could taste it. The soggy rot in the air made him gag, made him try to breathe through his broken nose but each time he tried, he tasted copper. Everything was tinged slate from the cloud cover. He shuffled his feet as he went down the trail. "Who’s barn is this?" In truth, he didn’t know who it belong to, but whoever had built it must of forgotten the ramshackle wooden structure given the layers of ivy creeping up its walls. It’d been stripped of paint, the boards held fast by rusting nails. Somehow, the shingles were still attached. She ran her hand along the ivy, stepping back barefoot as he bit the filter off a cigarette. He went to light it, but before he could, she quickly snatched it from his lips. "You got to stop that." The voice was sterner than any he’d heard from a woman since his mother had passed, but it didn’t stop him from perking an eyebrow. "And why’s that?" "It’s not good for you." He went to take it back, but she wound her arm back far enough for him to be unable to grab it. He tried again. A chuckle petered through her lips as she backpedaled. She turned sharply, taking off along the side of the barn. He was quick to follow, catching glimpses of her as she made her way along the edges. When she was around the front, she made a beeline to the lone magnolia tree along the clear cut. Her heart was pounding. She couldn’t hear herself think over her own breath. She didn’t even know she’d been picked up off the ground until was on her back. The landing was delicate. Her breath caught as he pinned her waist underneath his. Her t-shirt rode up her midriff, and for the first time, he noticed the color along her left side. Thick ribbons of red and pink combed up to her ribcage, little bunches of leaves and orange fruits sprouting off at different points, most prominently along the hip. "Where’d you get this?" "The same place you found this barn." "No. Seriously." "I’m being--" she squealed the second his fingers grabbed around her ribcage, her hands flailing to try and stop his touch. She tried to squirm away. "Please! Please! Pleas--" Ever since he’d seen her, he’d wanted her. Wanted to feel her. Wanted to make her smile. Wanted to hear her laugh. Wanted to taste her. Her mouth tasted of orange rolls and her tongue was quick to fight his as chest met chest. Clumsy hands reached for the other, harsh grasps to shoulders and head trying to make intense reassurances that this was what they both wanted. Their heads swam in the euphoria, idle questions so distant that they didn’t care about them anymore because there wasn’t anything more important underneath the flowers of the magnolia tree. It’d been over a year since he’d taken her here, but her spirit had to be richer here than any other after all that had happened. Every haunt they’d called home was scarred, now. Every single place he’d taken her, that held memory of her was tainted. She’d worked in a confectionary on Fayette’s Main Street, and he wasn’t the only one wounded when she left. The entire town loved her. She was the mother hers never was, and even under the rippling tide of changing time, her place was to become an institution in the community, with her weekend puppet shows and the way she remembered the names of all the kids that went through her shop and their parents she’d always done right by when she worked waiter at Edgar’s. When Jefferson County burned, the people stopped caring. Wal-Mart gobbled up decades old businesses whole. There would be no more institutions and small town slivers. Just a cindering hole. Had he known, he would’ve taken her here. Would’ve come back more often. Would’ve done something or anything and going to this spot wasn’t even amongst them, but with nothing left, all he had were the echoes to keep him company. She wore his leather jacket, not just because she liked the feel of the faded leather against her skin, but because everybody knew the jacket belonged to him. And even after his release from Parchman Farm, she still took to wearing it like she had for two years, waiting for him to get out. She’d seen the change in him when his father went crazy and started talking like he was the messiah. Saw the hope drain from him only be replaced by an unconquerable raging thirst that only the Cerberus MC could quench. It wasn’t until it was too late that he realized how much of his soul he’d freely given away. That a man who thought he had nothing to lose always had something to lose. Everyone deserved a second chance, and even if neither of them had faith in Fred Joseph Kingsley, he was truth before the madness. Terrence had been through so much, losing friends, losing his mother, losing his father, and while she never had true family for herself, she knew how much she wanted all that. That, even after he’d done things and seen things that changed who he was, he was the only one she saw herself wanting to have a family with. The letters grew more frequent. He talked about how scared he was in prison, how hard they worked him in the fields and on the license plate press. It was there that he got his GED. Where he promised her, once he got out, that life would be different. He’d be responsible. She’d worked two jobs to keep things together, but he was going to save her from all that. Save her from the shithole Jefferson County had become. It was she who drove them, in the Charger that she’d managed to keep up, to Phillips Breach after he’d gotten out. It was like a time capsule. It hadn’t changed one bit. Terrence had, though. Baby fat had been chiseled to granite under the rigors of the Mississippi sun. His dark beard spilled all the way to his chest, as did the locks from his mane. But the biggest difference were his hands. They were bony, now, the knuckles and sinews that connected to them prominent whenever he balled up his fists. It’d been so long, and all he’d had was the memory of her appearance and her words to keep him going. He took her hand into his. His palms weren’t rough anymore. The girl he’d nestled close to his chest was now a woman. Her amber hair was cut along her apple cheeks. Her figure had matured, and after years of running ragged, she’d finally began filling out her frame. He didn’t deserve her. It hadn’t stopped him from thinking about this day, and while he’d gotten lucky to be paroled so early, he was prepared all the same. The workers in the fields of Parchman Farm were paid for what they did because of state mandates, and while it wasn’t much, it could go a long way with a chunk of the modest sum the Club had left him for not ratting them out. He led her to the magnolia tree, its flowers having yet to bloom, and took both hands into his. She didn’t know what to say at the moment, so she went with the first thing that came to her mind. "I can give you the jacket back." "How bout you keep it." He hadn’t smiled so hard in months. "Looks better on you anyway." Oh how he missed her chuckle. "Don’t think so." Wordless, they stared into each other’s eyes, doing their best to maintain composure. Nothing either of them thought to say managed to escape because it felt too tame to break up their moment. This moment. Nervous energy flitted up his spine, washed through the soreness in his shoulders. She could see him swallow hard. His bottom lip quivered. "I’m sorry." "You don’t need to say--" "But it’s never gonna happen again." "Terrence..." He licked his chapped lips. "I need you to know that. I’m not the same person. That boy...that boy who was stark ravin’ mad at the world...he’s dead, now. And the man who took his place learned his lesson." She armed herself with a fake smile. "Oh has he?" He saw right through it. "Don’t." She paused for a moment. "What?" "I know it’s been tough. And I know it’s gonna take some time to earn back your trust. I need to earn it back." "I trust you." But did she? She didn’t even know if she did. Want to didn’t have much of a say, truth be told. "I just...I just want to get away from all this." "Me too." He reached into his pocket. "But I need you if I’m ever gonna, or else I’m just gonna be stuck here." Fishing around, he lolled his head to the side as he pursed his lips, dropping to a knee. He braced her standing with his other hand. She reached for his shoulders. "Is something--" "Autumn Marin." He looked up, presenting the ring he’d fantasized giving to her while he toiled in the fields. "Will you marry me?" And in that instant, she froze. Not because she had to think. Her brain was merely trying to process what she’d heard and what she felt and "Yes." That’s when the flood broke through, and her chest buckled and her face tightened and tears spurted from her eyes. Every little piece of her, rattled in the finest of ways. He slid the ring on her finger, and before he could stand up, she nearly tackled him with her embrace. "I’m gonna get you outta here..." His voice broke. "I promise. I promise." The roof of the barn had finally buckled, the structure caving into itself in a tangle of ivy and mush splinters. The grounds around the sliver surrounded by bayou were covered thick with cattails and gemma grass that reached his waist. But the magnolia tree was just as he wanted to remember it, its flowers now deep in bloom. He peeled a cigarette from his paper pack. It didn’t have a filter to bite off. He wasn’t an educated man. Far from it. But as he lit the cigarette, he walked over to the trunk of the tree, getting on his knees in spots that felt like familiar grooves, and scratched through at the grass and then the soil until he’d opened a small hole in the ground near the roots of the tree. He reached for the ring finger of his left hand, wrapping his right around the brass wedding band she’d given him, and if they couldn’t be together, it was best to bury the past. But he couldn’t. Each time he tried to put his ring in the hole, he froze. It made sense. Move on. But he couldn’t move on and nothing would ever change that. When he’d lost faith in humanity, she’d been the only one there to keep him from teetering over the edge. And he rewarded her by falling back into the life he’d promised he’d leave forever. Clutching hold of his wedding band, he slipped it back in place, rolling onto his backside and bracing himself against the tree trunk. He lowered his head, taking drags from his cigarette until he was lightheaded. His eyes scanned the plot, and even with all their travels to the breach, they’d never seen inside that damn barn. As he drew near, he could taste the heavy rot. Not all the boards were waterlogged. He ran his fingers along the rough openings from the ivy tangle, clasping them and wobbly them until, suddenly, he’d pulled it clean off its carcass. It was odd how intact it still was after all those years, all the way down to the rusty nails at the ends of the board. The jacket came off. The leather was fading fast, the black turned dull brown down it back and along its shoulder. Its bumps were now smooth to the touch. He tried to easy his fingers along the base of one of the nails, but the more pressure he applied, the more unstable the board became until he’d snapped it and the nail underneath caught him across the wrist. Still hard. He winced. But then, he looked at it, and he took it and pushed it through the peak of the jacket’s right shoulder. It barely took any effort at all to turn the past into a weapon.
