Psychogenesis :: Part 1, Telephone Tag

September 2010
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: September 2010 Thursday, August 26, 2010 Jared Sykes took the silver duffel from his trunk, taking a minute to let his eyes linger on the black and gold strap that haphazardly lay atop the spare tire. He was a world champion, the pinnacle that Sin City had to offer, but in that moment it hardly mattered. Recent months had seen his world start to crumble. He’d lost his relationship with Amy Campbell. He’d learned second-hand that his father was suffering from cancer, a similar strain to what took his brother at age twelve. He’d been kidnapped and tortured by Wyatt Connors, and the flesh on his right forearm now bore eighteen circular reminders of the incident. He’d come here to escape all that, to find sanctuary among these walls. He pulled the phone from his pocket and powered it down. He tossed it inside the trunk where it came to rest next to the belt. Anyone who needed him would simply have to wait. * * * * * Friday, September 10, 2010 Isaiah Gordon ducked into the den that had for two weeks served as his bedroom and pulled the door shut. He reached for his cell phone, hoping that the little yellow “new message” icon would give him the news he needed to get far away from home. “Motherfucker,” he whispered. He pushed the send button for the third time that day. Maybe this time there'd be an actual voice on the other end of the line. He sighed as it clicked over to voicemail. “Hi, this is Jared,” he said to the recording, “and I don't answer my phone ever 'cuz I'm a big fag an' I'm too busy glory holin' dudes named Bubba.” He shut the phone off and tossed it into a pile of borderline laundry. There was no sense in leaving another message. * * * * * Sunday, September 12, 2010 Isaiah was still out on the lawn when the car rolled into the driveway. One by one the rest of his family emerged, each fresh from church and dressed in their Sunday best. He dug a rag from his pocket and wiped away the sweat from his brow. He wondered if they saw him spit. Mark, his youngest brother, stayed behind as the rest of the Gordon clan headed into the house. He was tall and lanky like Isaiah, but with his mother's rounded features. He fumbled with the buttons on his blazer as he walked. Isaiah killed the mower's engine and leaned over the pushbar. “Been meanin' ta ask ya',” he said, grinning. “Ain't you supposed ta be in school or somethin'? Like, didn'tcha graduate last spring? Figured you'd be in college tryin' ta get laid.” “Not everyone is like you, Isaiah.” “Not quite sure I follow. Wait, you tellin' me you're gay?” “What?” Mark took a step back. “No. No, nothing like that. Homosexuality is a sin.” “That the kinda shit they're fillin' your head with? Man.” Isaiah buried his face in his hands and laughed. “Lemme tell ya, kid: there's a lot worse things you can do in this world than kiss a dude, that's for damn sure. Fuck, man, I don't care if you come home tellin' me you wanna marry an advark.” He pulled a half-empty bottle of water from back of his jeans and took a swing. “An' besides, you look like you could use a little sinnin'. College'd be pretty good for that, I figure.” “I'm taking some time to think,” Mark said. “Think? Think about what?” “I'm trying to decide whether I go to school or join the seminary.” Mark cleared his throat. “I was thinking of becoming a priest.” “Motherfucker,” Isaiah muttered. “Do you always have to swear?” “If the situation fits. Besides, thought you priestly types weren't supposed to judge. Thought that was in the rules somewhere.” “I'm being serious here.” “So am I, kid. So am I. You're startin' out on the wrong foot if you're already callin' out sins and passin' judgement when you don't even have the tiny white tie. It's what you don't get. It's what none a' you 'round here get: readin' the book is fine an' dandy, but y'all sure as shit don't live it right.” They stood in silence for a moment before Isaiah spoke again. “What'd you come over here for, anyway?” he asked. Mark sighed. “I wanted to ask you to tone it down tonight. For mom.” “The hell?” “She hasn't told you? Father David is coming to the house for dinner.” “No,” Isaiah spat. “She ain't told me.” “Oh, well, he is. He's been helping me with my decision, and she invited him over as a way to help thank him.” “Ain't that a bitch.” Isaiah shook his head. “Alright, and just what exactly am I s'posed to 'tone down'?” “I don't know. Everything. Nothing. I don't know. Just... just please don't get in a fight with her tonight. I know you guys don't really see eye-to-eye on everything, but it would really mean a lot if tonight could just go... could just go well, I guess.” Isaiah guzzled the last of his water and tossed the bottle aside. Shaking his head, he took hold of the mower's pushbar and tugged at the starter cord. Three tugs later, and the engine spun up. “Whatever,” he sighed. “Man. Fucking whatever.” * * * * * The floorboards creaked. A pair of saw blades clung to the wall only through the will of some rusted nails. Buckets and paint cans filled with god-knows-what formed a makeshift pyramid just beyond the door. As Isaiah pushed the mower back into its corner, the tines of a nearby rake pulled at his tank top and scratched his shoulder. The shed was a deathtrap. He pulled a musty blue tarp back into place and cursed it under his breath. With luck this would be the last time he'd have to wrestle with his parents junk. “I really should have your father come in here one a' these days. It's a right mess, it is.” Caroline stood at the edge of the shed, her eyes flitting about the space. “Mark said he talked to you.” Isaiah rubbed his hands on his jeans. “Yep.” “Told me it was a – what was the word he used - colorful conversation.” “Guess you could call it that.” “He's got a big decision ahead of him.” “He mentioned somethin' 'bout it. Figure I know which way he's gonna lean.” “You might be surprised,” she said. “He talks about joinin' the church, but your father and I aren't convinced it's what he wants. We figure it's what he thinks we want.” Isaiah didn't respond. “He's a good boy, you know,” his mother continued. Isaiah picked up the rusted lid of a jar, turned it once in his fingers, and casually tossed it aside. “Sure he is. Just like the rest of 'em, probably. I can see 'em now, all lined up, in pretty white suits shinin' like perfect little diamonds.” “Son,” she sighed, “What are you doin'?” “Mowin' the lawn, just like you asked.” “That's not what I mean. I mean with your life. What are you doin' with your life?” He turned, folding his arms and cocking his head to the left. In Japan, Isaiah James Gordon, the 'Evil Jar of Orange Marmalade', was someone special, someone important. But in Caroline Gordon's shed he was just a little boy who'd lost his way; he'd always be a little boy. “Is this gonna be another one a those conversations? 'Cuz I really don't think I can deal with it today.” She sighed again, louder this time. “I'm not here to tell you how to live, Isaiah. I gave up on that a long, long time ago. It's a fruitless endeavor, like tryin' to squeeze blood from a stone.” “So, what, this gonna be another request to 'tone it down'? You gonna ask me to put on a smiley face for the holy man, or somethin'?” “Tonight is important to your brother. Tonight is important to me. Seein' my family happy is important to me.” “As long as they believe the same as you, ya' mean.” “Now you know that's not true.” “Yeah? I been here for two weeks after bein' outta the country for like four years, and every day I'm gettin' nagged 'bout when I'm cuttin' my hair, or when I'm movin' on, or why I can't snap my fingers and make the sun come out.” He paused just long enough to clear his throat. “I told ya' when I got here that this was gonna be temporary, that this is a fickle business I'm in. It might be a bit before somethin' comes along, but somethin' will come along.” “And somethin' wouldn't come along in your own place?” He laughed. “I just said I been outta the country for four year. Didn't have much time ta' go apartment huntin'. Shit, maybe I really am that dumb for thinkin' it might be nice to see the fam' again.” He added under his breath, “Goddamn.” Her eyes widened as her cheeks flushed. Her son had just broken one of the cardinal rules of the house. “Don't you say...” “Yeah, yeah,” he interrupted. “I know. There's a lotta things I ain't s'posed to say, right?” He stepped past his mother into the warm fall air. “I need a shower.” * * * * * “I swear to Christ, Jared. If you don't call me back real soon then I'm gonna stab ya' in the dick with a butter knife when next I see ya'.” * * * * * It wasn't until he got to his car that Jared turned his phone back on. He'd left it with Dr. Parker upon checking in to the Vance Memorial hospital, as he'd done during his last visit three years before. As per the rules of the institution, Jared had been free to use it as he wished during his self-imposed stay, but as someone looking for a break from the world it seemed only appropriate to shut much of it out. It took a minute before the data synchronized, and as he sat in the driver's seat mulling over what his first steps should be the transfer completed. Forty-three missed calls. Seven new voice messages. He contemplated checking them later, maybe after he'd found a hotel to stay in before heading off to Sin City's next port of call, but the curiosity was too strong. Ten minutes later he returned one of the calls. * * * * * Isaiah couldn't help but stare as Father David shoveled forkful after forkful of food into his mouth. Father David wasn't in the best of shape (Isaiah tried to remember if he'd ever seen an athletic priest), but he was no slob either. Still, he devoured everything in front of him as though his days were spent starving on the streets. “Caroline, you've outdone yourself,” he said, digging his fork into a mound of squash. “These mashed potatoes are simply divine. They're among the best I've ever had.” Caroline blushed. Isaiah tried not to gag. “Thank you so much, father,” she said. “It's an old family recipe. I learned it from my mother when she first taught me to cook.” “Then you'll have to pass on my compliments to her as well.” Isaiah took hold of his fork, clutching it tight in the palm of his hand. He debated whether he should stab himself in the thigh. It might be less painful that this, he thought. “We missed you at mass this morning, Isaiah,” Father David said. “Hrm?” Isaiah lifted his head, still relatively unaware of what was going on around him. “Oh, right. Yeah. Sorry 'bout that. Had to do a few things 'round the house.” “Your mother told me as much. The lawn looks wonderful, by the way.” “Thanks.” “Isaiah is just back from Japan,” Caroline offered nervously. “He spent four years there.” “Is that right?” Father David asked. “Why that's quite impressive. Quite impressive indeed, I must say. And now I've come to understand that your brother Mark is looking to step out into the world on his own, as well. I can't tell you how wonderful it is to see a young man so devoted to the word of God.” The minute the attention had been drawn away from him, Isaiah gave in and rolled his eyes. “Yeah, well,” Isaiah started, before the subtle vibrations in his pocket drew his attention. He only hoped he remembered to turn off the ringer, else his family be subjected to 2 Live Cru's “Hoochie Mama”. He pushed away from the table and all but leapt to his feet. “Y'all'll have to excuse me for a minute. I, uhh, I need a little air.” * * * * * Isaiah slipped out of the dining room, through the kitchen, and then burst through the screen door out onto the deck. “Jesus fuckin’ Christ,” he shouted into the phone, all at once wondering whether his family had heard him. “I was startin’ ta’ think you were dead, man.” “Twenty-one missed calls,” came Jared’s reply. “And four voice messages.” “Yeah, well, I was getting’ desperate. You ain’t got a clue what it’s like here. There’s Jesus on every wall in every room. Man can’t even take a shit without those eyes starin’ back atcha. It’s like yer getting’ judged for wipin’ wrong. Right hand heaven, left hand hell.” “Sounds like… I dunno what it sounds like.” “Man, I had to watch the 700 Club with my grandma. Pat Robertson was hostin’. You have any idea how awful that is? Shit’ll change ya’, man. Shit’ll change ya’.” “So, what, you’re home? When did you get back home?” “Did you even listen to the messages I left ya’?” “Uh, no, actually. I mean I skimmed 'em, sorta. Like if you can skim a voicemail, that's what I did.” Isaiah sighed. “Been home almost two weeks now. It’s terrible. And where the hell you been? You hidin’ out from the law, or somethin’?” “I’m, uhh, in North Carolina. Just, I dunno, visiting a few old friends I guess.” “Well get your ass in a car and come visit another one. I’m goin’ outta my mind down here.” The screen door creaked open and Mark poked his head through. Isaiah pulled the phone away from his ear and nodded. “Hey, uhh, are you coming back soon? Mom’s asking questions.” “Yeah,” Isaiah whispered, holding the phone against his chest. “Be right there. Just finishing something up. Apologize for me, or some shit.” Mark disappeared back into the kitchen. No sooner was he out of sight did Isaiah resume his conversation. “Seriously though,” he continued, “what’ll it take to get’cher ass down here for a few days? There’s somethin’ I wanna talk to you about.” “Not something you can just tell me now?” Though Jared wasn’t there to see it, Isaiah shook his head. “Nah, this is one a’ them ‘in person’ deals. Much rather talk ‘bout it face-to-face. Trust me here, man, I think it’d be for the best.” “Well, I mean I don’t have anything coming up soon, so I guess…” “So you’ll do it? You’ll head on down?” “I mean I guess I could.” “Thank fuck, man. Thank fuck. I can’t wait, man. I feel like I’m stuck in a mad-house here, ya’ know? Like I'm losin' my goddamn mind.” * * * * * Eight hundred miles away, Jared glanced through the window of his car. Across from him, beyond the parking lot and past the lush green courtyard stood the Vance Memorial Hospital for Mental Health, and beside it stood the converted plantation that served as an administrative facility. “Yeah,” he said, fidgeting with the white plastic wristband, “I know just how you feel.”
