Azuma Gaiden (1): In the Beginning

15 July 2011
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 07/15/11 A man, gun clasped in hand, ponders what's next. It is, in its form, a meaningless question. Without a doubt, he must pull the trigger. He must pull the trigger because it is why the gun exists. Certainly the particulars: how and where and why, still remain to be decided. But they always have a way of resolving themselves. It's just a matter of time, of circumstances. Luck or fate, take your pick. Premeditation of any sort hardly plays a role. He is hunched over, eyes closed, knee on chair, elbow on knee, barrel of the gun resting cool against his forehead, pointed at the heavens, aimed in defiance at the dangling package of God himself. A chill aura seeps from his pores, freezing this moment in time. No question, he must pull the trigger. The trigger exists to be pulled, the bullet in the chamber exists to be fired, the man holding the gun exists to lash out at his fellow man. This is his purpose, his final cause. Everything about him and the way he carries himself suggests it. But what about the primary cause? What caused this man to be? And not just be, for being itself is nothing out of the ordinary. There are an infinitude of beings and yet only one of this type, will so hard and so sharp, honed and diamondized under the weight of the ages. No, it's not a question of being, but one of becoming, and to answer it we must go back, way back. The roots run deep in this blackened soil, the growth of not just one lifetime, but many. So follow them down, down, through the generations, and see just what ugly truths lay hidden in this tangled mass. The scars of history leave no heart yet untouched. ================== Kishiwada City, Osaka Prefecture, Japan June 19th, 1945. This is a story that begins face down in the mud, like the very first life. In the mud primordial, everlasting, man made from clay, ashes to ashes, dust to dust, never a clean day in the history of this world. “Stand up, you worthless dog!” Dae-Hyun Kim lifts his head slowly, face caked in filth, eyes blinking painfully at the blurry figure standing above him, silhouetted against the glaring sun. “In the name of our glorious Emperor, there's work to be done!” A boot to the ribs lands with an unnatural crack, no muscle left on the bones to soften the blow. He cries out involuntarily, and he is ashamed at the sound, the same yipe the stray mongrels gave when the children hit them with rocks back in his village in Joseon. So this is what he has become. Waves crash over him, first dizziness, then nausea, and he feels like laying in the mud forever, never getting up. But he is still conscious enough to acknowledge the awful truth of that sentiment, for if he doesn't stand now, the Japanese soldier will surely beat him to death where he lays. He raises first one knee, through pure force of will, and then the other, before pushing himself out of the muddy puddle, one of the last remnants of the rainy season just past. The soldier, not even a man, but a scrawny boy of 15, looks at him with a scowl. There's a moment where he lifts his rifle butt, ready to cave in the other man's skull in spite of himself and Dae-Hyun can hear his own heart-beat in the depths of his ears. Thump. Thump. Thump. A long pause. And then the boy lowers it again, as if deciding it not worth the expenditure of energy. With a grunt, he orders his men to continue marching toward the factory. While he fades in and out of consciousness on his feet over the next seven hours, somehow Dae-Hyun manages to finish his shift. ================== Though he is utterly exhausted, sick and malnourished, he still has trouble falling asleep at night. The shabby barracks were uncomfortable enough in the spring, but the muggy summer humidity has made conditions completely unlivable. Over a hundred men crowded into a space barely adequate for a quarter of that number, pressed together, sweating, sweating, poor ventilation, losing fluids, their very life force escaping in streams down their faces, backs, and thighs. It was not uncommon to wake up with a corpse in their midst. And the mosquitoes. The fucking mosquitoes. From sunset onward, swarming in at night, leaving devastation in their wake, worse than any fleet of American bombers. Not satisfied to go for the most remote appendages, but always landing on the face, buzzing in the ear. Netting much too valuable to waste on foreign workers, a million better uses for nylon in this resource starved country. It's at times like these, laying awake, unable to escape this nightmare, that Dae-Hyun dreams of his homeland. Waking dreams in a feverish haze, but they're better than no dreams at all. ================== Memories as still-frames. Green terraced rice fields extending up the hillsides. A thousand fireflies lighting up the mountain on a warm summer night. Racing Dae-Jung down the path to the general store on an errand for their mother. Su-Min laughing, flowers in her hair, tossing her head back, beckoning to him to follow. But that is one memory he must not embrace. Not here. Some memories are too sweet, too sweet to be defiled in this land. Life had been so good, back in that innocent, idyllic world. Their village was poor, barely self-sufficient, but they were happy in spite of that. Why did all of that have to come to an end? He knows the answer, of course, but it still hurts to think about. A too-fresh wound inflamed further by his inability to keep picking at it. ================== Dae-Jung hated the Japanese. Growing up, they had never known a life without Japanese occupation. The same was true of their parents, and while their grandparents may have been able to tell them stories of the dying days of the Daehan-jeguk, the great Korean empire, they had all died some decades ago, none living to see their fortieth birthday. Dae-Hyun didn't particularly care for the Japanese either. No one in the village did. But at the same time, they knew that they were fortunate compared to most others in their country. They were an isolated mountain community, of no real worth to the occupying forces. There was only a single company of soldiers posted to the region, who split their attention between half a dozen separate villages. There were some annoyances to be sure. There was pressure on the village elders to take Japanese names, to set an example for the peasant folk. The forced study of the Japanese language was another minor irritant, but for Dae-Hyun it was no more worse than studying algebra or calligraphy. Taxes were taxes, but in most people's minds nothing worth rioting over. Especially when considering what their occupiers were capable of. Every now and then a story would make its way to the village. Horrifying stories. Stories that boggled the mind. Sweeping reforms. Newspapers no longer able to print in the Korean language. Uprisings in the capital. 7000 demonstrating men and women slaughtered by the Japanese army. Dae-Hyun couldn't even imagine 7000 people in the same place together, the population of the village ten times over. Dae-Hyun and his brother would listen in from outside the door as their father had tense discussions with men from faraway places. They heard stories of Japanese soldiers raping women without consequence, beating men to death in the street over nothing more than a perceived slight, with no legal remedy available to the victims or their families. This from the people who professed to be the elder brothers of the Koreans. Cain himself would have wept. For a young boy who had never left his mountain village, these stories were hard to comprehend. Dae-Hyun knew one thing, though. The Japanese were dangerous and not to be provoked. He counted his blessings that they had heretofore shown little interest in the village. So wrapped up in these thoughts was he that he didn't notice Dae-Jung next to him, fists clenched and shaking, grinding his teeth at these tales of tragedy, nearly choking upon thoughts his own impotence. A young mind is fertile soil. And thus were the seeds planted for the fall. ================== He doesn't remember falling asleep, but the fact he's being shaken violently awake tells him that he must have. He looks up at the face of a nearly toothless old man who is telling him that he needs to get out of bed quickly in heavily accented Japanese. He nods his assent and the man shuffles away as fast as his legs can carry him. He wasn't a Korean, that much was clear from his pronunciation. Maybe from China? Dae-Hyun can guess, but really he's clueless when it comes to such things. Regardless, he is thankful to the man for looking out for him. Better to be woken up by him then under the cruel fists of the camp overseers. Joining the line of men outside the barracks, he waits for the day's meager rations to be handed out. The rice he receives fits comfortably in the palm of his hand with room to spare. A man standing next to him in line begins to break down in sobs. Dae-Hyun forces himself not to look in the man's direction, shoving the rice into his mouth in one motion and swallowing it down without savor. Dae-Hyun can see the writing on the wall. The rations had hardly been sufficient when he arrived here nearly a year ago, but over the months they'd slowly been dwindling. If they had been sent to some remote outpost, Burma or some tiny island chain, then he might have been able to understand. But to have food shortages here in the home islands was another thing entirely. It wasn't just the rations, either. Over time the demeanor of the camp guards had changed as well. While they had never been kind, lately they had been acting with a wanton cruelty, too quick to lash out, smiling savagely, as if thankful for the chance to release whatever pent-up emotions were wracking havoc behind their stoic demeanors. And they were young. Too young. Make no bones about it, the war is not going well for the Japanese. That would be very good news if he was still in Joseon, surrounded by his family and friends. But here it is quite the opposite. For if Japan loses this war, he will surely die. If he doesn't starve to death or collapse from sheer exhaustion, if he isn't run through by a guard's bayonet or stricken by some horrible disease, if he manages to survive until the day the Americans storm the beaches and begin their campaign to take the home islands, then that will still be the end of him. There is no way the Japanese would simply walk away and let him leave. There is no surrender in these people. They will fight to the death, and you can bet that the workers would be placed in the front lines to take the opening salvos of bullets. Or else forced to throw themselves on grenades in a mass suicide ritual, one final show of loyalty to the Emperor. No, it was a hopeless situation: hopeless if the Japanese won, hopeless if they lost, and hopeless if the war continued indefinitely. Hard work and inevitable death were all that lay in his future. The only option is escape. He is stirred from his revery as a commotion is raised nearby. He can hear a man shouting in Korean at the top of his lungs, hysterically, pleading for them to let him go, let him go, please, please, let him go. Please... please... Suddenly there are three seconds of silence. And then a single, unbroken animalistic scream. He didn't know it at the time, but this was to become a significant moment in his life. That man's scream, anonymous and primal, was a clarion call, an annunciation, signifying the appearance onstage of the figure who would alter his life in ways unimaginable. The Commandant. The Sadist. The man who would come to be known as the “Demon”. Saburo Azuma had arrived.
