No Past. No Future.

30 August 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 30/08/05 Things aren’t easily forgotten, that’s something I’ve come to understand over the years, it’s a truth that’s been ever present these past few months too. I guess that’s something some people might find a comfort, an immortality of sorts. She had a prim, reserved look about her. She was always so composed and she had a tidy, practiced smile. Along with my mother, she had another daughter. Seven years older than my mother. A daughter who’s heart stopped beating in a lavender scented bath. I remember my grandmother changed then. She lost weight overnight. Her face became drawn and her eyes darkened. To me she looked like someone else, someone older. The last time I saw her, she was sitting by her bay window, two pleading eyes and hands that couldn’t stay still. I slowed for a moment, and with her eyes she gave a faint smile. I hope I smiled back. Even now, that lost, tired smile comes back to me. I was ten years old, not quite done being nine, when my grandmother left me. About two weeks later my parents dropped me around at a friends house and made their excuses as to why I couldn’t go with them. After I watched the car pull away, I didn’t give it much thought. I knew I was being lied to, but I decided not to worry about it. I’d go to bed feeling the lie, but I still decided to ignore it. They believed that I was too young to understand and cope with a cremation. And I doubt that was even the real reason. While my parents put on their act by my grandmothers grave side, I did what I did most days, I played with Anita. Apart from my grandmother, Anita was the only other positive in my life. While things aren’t easily forgotten, they still can be. I’ve spent so long now trying to forget that I’ve actually succeeded. Some of those early years are blurred. I can sit and press my thumbs to my temples and nothing comes. It’s like I’ve locked memories away, as though there is some place in my brain, a distant, dark corner where I tuck things away, never to be found again. I have spaces I can’t fill, and with that, ghosts I can’t put to rest. If over the past six years I’ve thought about my grandmother and my past with her, I’ve no recollection of those thoughts now. It’s only now that I’ve allowed myself to think of her. Ten years ago, I started building the final wall. The wall that would separate me from my past. And now I’m stood behind two great big walls. My grandmother used to brighten up when I sat myself at her table, for the first ten years of my life, she was my safety net, and when she died I lost everything. Part of me believes that if she was still here now, if she hadn’t been found stone-cold in her bed, her body teeming with painkillers and vodka, and no note, my life would be completely different now. I may have survived my parents, a father who’s breath only ever reeked of alcohol and a mother who forever applied make-up to cover the latest bruises. Gracias. My father. I could have studied her a while longer, I could have sat with her at the kitchen table, my grandfathers picture hanging on the wall. I could have continued to secretly offer food to the farm cat under the table. For the past few months the only memory I’ve relayed over in my mind has been my loss to Johnny Lexicon. There has been no room for anything else. What I know as fact is that over the past two years, I’ve come no closer to knowing where I stand with him. Only that my last memory is his face when he beat me. And because of him, I broke everything down and rebuilt it again. All the months since have been spent making myself a better, tougher fighter. I've been in Santa Rosa for a while now, this is after all where I'd come to find Cal. Plus I knew it was only a matter of time until my chance came around again. And a pay-per-view was all it took. If I put my mind to it, I can remember my grandfather. I remember his distinct hatred towards my father, and rightfully so. The image of my grandfather burned into my mind is the distant figure stood across the fields watching over the cows, the dog at his heel. This beats the sight of him in his chair, a lost expression on his face, hands shaking with Parkinson’s. When I was eight, he gave me the rules. Keep away from all the machinery. Don’t go off in the dark. The dogs are working dogs, do not play fetch. And most of all, watch myself around the horses. “Never go around the back of them” “Why not?” I asked. What followed was a story about a man from the village, John Turner. “You don’t want to startle a horse, especially not from behind” was my grandfathers last warning. The story was that Mr. Turner was kicked in the head. His skull cracked. He didn’t die, my grandfather claimed that he was lucky that a few trips to surgery would sort him out. He couldn’t walk for months. “He’s not as he was” my grandfather said, shaking his head. I imagined a smashed skull looking like a dropped egg. Just to make sure, I took one of the new eggs from under a nesting hen, and as far as my grandmother was concerned, I accidentally smashed it in the bucket. I couldn’t imagine what spilt brains would look like, but I guessed it’d be ugly. It’s ironic that as adults we spend our time searching through life for something easy on the eyes and easy on the heart, yet as children, you seek out ugliness and you look for it everywhere. I’ve no idea where these memories spring from. But it’s nice to know they’re still there, burned somewhere deep inside of me. I’m still human after all. Over the years I’ve become so engrossed in my wrestling career that I haven’t given much else thought, I forgotten what I had, and pushed away what I could have had, and now it’s probably too late. A year later my grandfather was taken to hospital. While my mother visited him I had to stay with my father, measles keeping me away. And like so many others before him, my grandfather went into hospital and never came out. When my grandmother went to say her last goodbye a few weeks later, my parents and my aunt going with her, I was dropped off at some stables. What amazed me even then was the strength of my grandmother. While her children could do nothing but cry, she didn’t even shed a tear. Maybe she’s where I get my resolve from. “I know you don’t want to be here, but we all have to do things we don’t want to do every now and then” my grandmother whispered as she pulled me from the car. “The horses are nice, maybe you can even ride one”. “I don’t want to” I had to protest. “Tough. You’ll have fun if you stop moping” I never learned. She was impossible to beat. Her look said it all, I was staying. All I could do was nod in defeat. She smiled “you’re a good girl”. I dropped my head. Surely my grandfather had told her the story. Surely she knew about horses. What if I was kicked in the head, like old John Turner? Hadn’t she thought about that? Was that her plan?. I spent the whole day walking an extra four yards than was required around the horses. Losing someone you love can affect you in ways you never imagined possible. And my grandmother was one of the only people I’d ever loved. Gradually I lost what love I had for my parents. I resented the fact that I was born without a choice, I came into this world and was brought up to love my mother and my father. But I lost that love, I programmed my brain differently. One of the last things my mother said that meant anything to me was her advice to always protect yourself. “If you don’t let someone in, they can’t hurt you” she smiled. Clearly she didn’t take her own advice and I doubt she realised that I’d apply the new found advice to my relationship with her. But I’m sure that she’d be pleased to know that she taught me at least one thing that I’ve kept applied to my life, even now. Without my grandmother, there was nothing keeping me in once place. It soon made sense to move on. And for years Anita was my only tie to my past, until about a month ago. I wiped Anita out of my life and it was as easy as it was for me to wipe out everyone else. It comes naturally, it’s just something I do. You see, it’s too easy for me to blank things out, to push things away and change my concentration. I’m not sure whether or not it’s a gift or a curse. “Are you listening to me?” The bang from his fist hitting the bar brings my attention right back to the here and now. To the people around me, to the stool I’m sat on, to the drink in my hand. To the company sat beside me. I forget what was said, the trigger, whatever brought about the memories I’ve kept locked away. “Look, I’ll say one thing for you..” Cal Pistone says, folding his arms. “Nothing, and I mean nothing, is harder than facing your own fears, facing your own past.” He nods his head, picking up his glass and taking a drink. There is this tone in his voice, a tone that makes everything sound alright, like he can make anything better. At 6’4, he stands above most men half his age. At fifty-six years old, Cal has already lived my life plus another. “But that's when the greatest of courage comes, you know..” he nods again, looking me in the eye “when the thing that you're up against is yourself.' It’s six months to the day. Six whole months have passed since I first stepped into the warehouse, a broken mess, a shadow of my former self. Cal Pistone was my lucky break. “How do you think I’m doing?” I ask. He doesn’t like me to ask questions, but I have to ask. As I wait for the answer, I swirl my drink around in the glass. “Fine, you’re doing good. You just need to keep your left up” He shrugs his shoulders. He gets up and pushes his stool in towards the bar. “Keep your left up and protect yourself..” his voice is hoarse, like it sometimes gets. Too much smoking, I think. Cal stands beside me for a moment, he looks at my drink before resting his eyes on me again. “If you want to beat this Mary, you’ve got to focus. That’s the last piece of advice I can give you this week I’m afraid”. Before I can reply, he’s gone. I’m left alone in the bar. Alone to focus. How hard can it be?
