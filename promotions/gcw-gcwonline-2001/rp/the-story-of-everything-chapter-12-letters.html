The Story Of Everything, Chapter 12: Letters

undated
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: The Ides Of December I could feel all their eyes on us as we walked in the place, and having caught our reflections in the large panels of windows out front, I couldn't blame them for staring. Still, I could only do my best to describe ourselves from a stranger's perspective. One of us was taller than the other, wearing a suit so picturesquely rumpled that it looked like it had been rumpled on purpose. The shorter of the two was unkempt in all manners with a woodsman's beard and clothes like cloths that hung from him rather than being worn in a more traditional fashion. Both men were full of cuts and dirt, and their cuts were full of dirt. The shorter one was clutching a jar with tatters of towel over some of it. Those men, of course, were our reflections, and by extension, ourselves. We had originally gone in to use the washrooms to clean ourselves off a bit, wipe some of the blood away and "fit in." Of course there was something about the place, perhaps the mystique or the strictly adhered set of arbitrations. "Excuse me, sirs," said the woman from the kitchen, behind the stool-lined countertop. "The restrooms are for customers only." But then, upon seeing us, she reconsidered. The lines on her face, once rigid and defined, furrowed to apologetic admittance. She didn't say it aloud, though, as I hoped she would have. For she, too, was sworn to adhere to this maze of rules. She looked sad, like the empty stools, covered in plush in most places with tufts of cotton springing through random rips. Most of the patrons were nestled in booths along half the perimeter of the place, and the kitchen poked out into a concentricity in the middle before sinking to the back. There was something I liked about it all; something that made me want to save it from its sadness, even. I wanted to assure the woman at the counter, with her lined, drooping face and her buoyantly puffy yellow dress beneath off-white apron, that everything was going to work out after all. "It's alright," I nodded with a gruff yet unthreatening patronage. "We'll eat something when we're out." They were the first words either of us had spoken since jumping from the train aside from a peppering of assuredly profane onomatopoeias when we were rolling down the jagged, obstacle-laden decline alongside the train tracks. Wayne was too angry too complain, and I wanted time to think, but he'd walked it off by now, and thinking took me far too long to do all in a stretch of such inefficient length. The bathroom door slammed shut behind us. "What the hell did we have to do that for?" Wayne stomped around the room. The washroom was meant for one person more than it was meant for two. There was a urinal and a toilet, but both of them were just out in the open with no measure of privacy. Even the singular sink was awkward as I stared at myself in the mirror with a livid Doctor Wayne grimacing over my shoulder, curling his lip in sets as if he were trying to slowly build the strength to curl it that much more raveled. "It was for our own good," I shot back, tapping my foot nervously on the ground, most of my mind thinking about what the fuck to do next while the remaining minority concentrated on doing a lackluster job of washing the dried blood off of my face and arms. "You just fucked us out of a guaranteed quarter million dollars!" "Then go to the airport and get it yourself," I challenged, turning off the faucets and looking around for a paper towel dispenser. "God-fucking-damn-it. I hate these fucking environmentally conscious air dryers." I punched the paperless hand-dryer and wiped my face with my shirt. It hurt more than I had expected, and I opened my mouth to swear, but the roar of a plane engine shook overhead. I looked over at Wayne, but he didn't appear to be rushing off to the airport, anyhow. "Don't worry about the money," I warned, shaking the sting from my knuckles as Wayne stepped up to the sink, paying more attention to primping his appearance to rid the mirror of the scuffed and frazzled visage that was his reflection. "Emery's got our money. We got the old man's junk. He made a deal with us," Wayne rationed in chops. "As soon as we give the junk to Emery, we're in Westonbrook's cross-hairs." "Emery offered us protection from Westonbrook as part of the deal, though!" Doctor Wayne protested. "I don't understand what the problem is." "The problem is that we're in the thick of this shit, and we have no fucking idea what's going on between Emery and Judge Westonbrook. Every week it seems the carrot gets juicier but the string gets shorter, and whoever's at the other end is waving the stick more and more violently," I paused for breath and readjusted. "At any rate, we keep getting out relatively unscathed, and I think that carrying around this junk is the only reason it keeps happening. It's the only bargaining chip we have, and the longer we hold onto it and the higher the demand for it becomes, the more I think we can't give it to anyone." "So we're just going to give up over a hundred grand of our own money?" "No," I assured the irate and fraudulent doctor. "We're going to get something out of the deal." "How?" "I'm famished. Get yourself cleaned up and I'll tell you over something to eat," I said halfway through the bathroom door already, grabbing the jar with the receding veil. "I'm working on a plan. It's complicated." I hurried towards the stools and placed the jar on the table before plunking myself down on one of the stools. Wayne was quick behind me, his demeanor only about half restored, so contrasted as to have had been interrupted. He rushed onto the stool next to me. "So what's the plan?" I took my eyes down from the menu, which was painted on a large board behind the register, and thought it over. "I think I'm going to get a bacon cheeseburger platter." "I meant the plan with the money." Wayne was desperate, and as he grew more desperate his need to be informed grew in proportion. "I'm still working on it," I admitted, repeatedly. "Let me get something in my system, first. Sometimes I just forget to eat for a few days or so, and I think I might've gone and done it again. I can't think straight with low blood sugar." "Well, without the money, how are you going to pay for the cheeseburger?" Wayne challenged. "And how did you get the money to buy the supplies from the hardware store?" "I pawned some silver." "What?" "Excuse me!" I called, waving a hand in the air. I could tell the waitress could see me in her peripheral vision. She took her hand off her him and huffed, sliding toward like she was shelled. "When did you do that?" "Are you ready to order?" her heavily painted lips assembled automatonically. "I'd like the bacon cheeseburger platter, please," I started politely. "Burnt to a crisp or bloody as hell?" "Bloody," "Wait, Johnny, what the hell are you talking about?" "Soup or salad?" "It means rare. I like my burgers juicy. What kind of soup you got?" "I wasn't talking about your burger. I meant about the silver." "Vegetable beef and cream of broccoli." "I'll go vegetable beef." "And to drink?" "It's nothing. It was just some silver. Can I get a milkshake instead of a soda and just pay the difference?" "Where'd you get silver anyway?" "Sure. Is that all for you, sir?" "Yeah, that'll be all." "And for you, sir?" "It was a while ago. It's unrelated to anything, so forget about it." "Uh, can you come back to me?" "Sure, can I get you a drink while you decide?" "You got enough to cover me?" "You can't charge it?" "You owe me for so many different things I can't even count." "Fine." "I'll take a lemonade." "I'll be back with your drinks in a minute." She dragged her shelly frame away, aged more by routine than by time. "This better be good," Wayne crossed his arms. "Everyone else's food looks pretty good," I scanned, rotating on my stool. "Your plan better be pretty good." Wayne's eyebrows furrowed from arches and arched again from furrows. "Start explaining." "It's really just two letters." "Which two?" "They're basically the same ones. They say the same things, just in different ways. We need to make some deliveries that people will think are other deliveries, you got me?" "Uh, yeah . . . " Wayne was lost, his eyes on the menu, his ears on the plan and his signals all confused. "You've got to deliver some letters somewhere." "So we're going to need another jar and a pen. Do you have a pen or a jar?" "Not on me." "Have you decided yet sir?" she returned, putting down our drinks. "I think I'll go with the horseshoe burger." "Excuse me, miss. Do you have a pen?" "Would you like the platter or just the burger a la carte?" "Just the burger." "Make sure to return it," she slid me the pen, and I snagged several napkins from the dispenser. "Burnt to a crisp or bloody as hell?" "What?" "How you want it cooked?" "You don't sell anything in jars, do you? Like big jars?" "Oh, burnt to a crisp." "We sell jars of our famous home-overgrown pickles." "How big is the jar?" "Will that be all for you, then, sir?" "Yep, that'll do." "I don't know. It's pretty big." "About the size of this jar?" I pushed the jar forward, but kept the towel over it, hoping she couldn't piece together its contents through the random tears in the fabric. A plane leapfrogged the diner, and the jar wobbled in front of me like a plump and dizzy gentleman with a painstakingly grown comb-over. "Yeah, I'd say." "I'll take a jar," I bargained. "Just bring it out with my meal." "Alright," she affirmed. "It'll all be out shortly." "What are you doing?" Wayne questioned, craning over my arm as the waitress scuffled off. "I'm writing the letters." "On napkins?" Wayne jostled to try and read the words. "Yeah," I tried to ignore Wayne's constantly distractive repositioning. "You remember the name of that church we were at?" "Um, I think it was Ascension Church or something like that. Why?" "I'm writing Emery a note about how the exchange is going to go down under new terms. I'm telling him we won't exchange the goods until we have proof he's made good on protecting us from Westonbrook's retaliation. I'm telling him I want him to leave his end of the deal at the church, buried in that open grave he had on reserve for us. We come back later, and if the grave is filled, we dig it up." "What if it isn't?" "We hope that Westonbrook is still as willing to negotiate." I started to write on the other napkin, this time with more calculation, crossing off letters and repositioning them elsewhere. "What are you doing now?" "Hold on, I gotta think." Wayne waited, and I paused and scribbled, paused and scribbled. "Chance Ironsuchs? What the hell is that? Is it someone's name?" "No," I refuted. "Not anyone's name I know, anyway." "What's it mean, then?" "It's just some silly game with letters, but I'm sure someone will find some meaning out of it." The soup came, and it was decent, and then the bacon cheeseburger came, and it was decent. I wouldn't know about the Shiny Diner's Famous Home-Overgrown Pickles, though. I just poured them out in the parking lot when we were done, but I wondered how they got those damned things so overgrown. Those pickles were fucking gigantic. *** I had to dig deep within my call log and try a number of false leads and frightening agencies before I found the right number. "I take it you got my offer," he said, and his shark breath swam through the phone. "That's a hard one to refuse, I'd imagine." "You imagine right, sir." "Johnny, I can barely fucking hear you," he snapped his teeth. "Are you at an airport or something?" "No, sir." "I can hear the fucking planes." "I'm near an airport, yes." I was technically wandering towards an airport between parked cars packed in a lot. Wayne was keeping a slower pace behind me, angry that the walk to the airport was longer than I had estimated and filled with me apprehensively calling back uncharted numbers in my call log to try and reach Judge Westonbrook. "Come on, Johnny. What the hell are you at an airport for?" questioned the judge. "You have a flight to catch or something? Got somewhere better to be?" "Not while I have payment withstanding." "That's a good answer," the judge chuckled. "Let's cut the bullshit, though." "Alright," I charged, taking the offensive. "I want half the money up front and the other half at the exchange." "I know Emery makes his trade-offs at the airport, Johnny. You wouldn't happen to be trading anything of mine, would you?" "I'm just dropping off a letter." "You haven't heard of a mailbox?" "I'm supposed to be dropping off something bigger, but Emery's been outbid." "Ah, a decoy," the shark smirked. "I hear you're getting quite good at such things." "Do we have a deal or what?" I was getting impatient, sliding through thin lanes past side view mirrors and dodging terrified driers in their mechanical rowboats as they stirred through the chaos. Wayne had fallen several lengths behind, and it was on him to catch up. "You meet me at a location of my choosing, and you bring my real heirlooms. Once I get them back, I'll give you the entire sum, and not a penny until then." "Either you give me half now and the other half when the deal is sealed, or I'll give you half now and the other half when the deal is sealed." A waft of silence waited as he scaled the gravity of that ultimatum. "Fine," he said calmly, shark-like. "We'll do it your way. Where do you want me to drop off the first half?" "I'll can't tell you over the phone." You never knew who was listening in, and with all these air traffic control towers around, who knew what crazy pirate signals were at large across these airwaves? "What do you want to do? Meet in person to discuss another meeting place? Can you tell me where you want to meet to do that, or should we meet in person for that, too?" He was getting coarse with his words, and I was getting coarser at them. "You ever make any drop-offs for Emery?" I asked, answering his question with another question, a peculiar habit I had picked up somewhere along the way. "No," the judge spat. "Do I look like I ever ran his errands?" "I wouldn't know how to go about even beginning to make that assessment," I asserted, not so much for the sake of diplomacy as for the sake of brevity. "You don't know what locker to deliver it to, do you?" I didn't have anything to say. He laughed to himself, that jackass. "You don't!" He hooted like an owl bastard. "God, I can't believe this. Here I am, building you up, and you're just stumbling on by." "Have it up, Curt. Say whatever you will, cause I still have your nards in a jar, here, and if you want them back in a state of repair that you'd recognize, you're going to work with me. Are we clear?" "Yes," he sighed in defeat. "We're clear." "I know you and Emery used to run some game together. You'd keep him out of trouble. He'd keep you hopped up on power and underground powders, and you'd in turn keep those powders underground. You can't ban the unknown." "What's your point?" "Well, your into scrambling and encryption and the likes," I started. "You think you could solve one of Emery's codes?" "I'm probably the one who taught it to him." "Then I'd appreciate your cooperation. What's he mean by Jeremy Forton-Heath?" There was mumbling at the end of the line, not words with distinct punctuations and separations between them, but a random flow of edgeless sounds being dunked with a bent plug. Then there was silence. "Hello?" "You are one dumb motherfucker, Johnny, but you've got some johnson on you, all rolled up in those trousers, don't you?" Westonbrook was so angry that I think he might have even been a little impressed. "First, you come trying to cash in my junk at an airport locker, and then when you can't find the locker, you give me a call to help? That's genius, Johnny. That's a masterpiece right there, let me tell you." "You're going to just have to trust that I'm planting a decoy, then," I concluded. "Emery is expecting me to be here. If I'm not here, he'll know something's afoot!" "Give me one reason I should trust you, and it better be goddamned good," he menaced. "Because I have to trust you to get me to the locker.'" It didn't make sense. None of it made sense, but that didn't matter because there was a certain fury to it, a build that needed resolution to pound the breathless and heart-stopped back into motion like a threshed gong. It was madness, I tell you, but it worked, because we had all agreed that this was how it was supposed to work. "Let me get that name again." "Jeremy Forton-Heath." "Johnny?" "Doctor Wayne?" Then, a plane. "You still listening to me, Johnny?" "Yeah, hold on." I turned to Doctor Wayne, who was trying to catch his breath. "Keep on your feet, Doc." "Where are you in the airport right now?" "Forget it, I'm done. You said the airport was a few hundred yards from the diner. It's been a few miles." "We're in the parking lot outside terminal two." "Alright, go in the door and head towards your left, but take the hallway that branches off to your right, do you got that?" "You coming Wayne?" "I'm just going to stay here. Can you come back and get me when you're finished?" "No, what? Do I take a left or a right?" "You look to your left and there should be two hallways. Of those two, take a right, and you should go through a set of doors into a really long hallway with conveyor belts on the sides; about five of them." "Did you say you weren't going to come back and get me or were you talking on the phone?" "I was talking on the phone, but I'm not coming back to get you either, so come on." "Do you see the conveyor belts?" "No, hold on." I looked around. "No, I have no idea what you're talking about." "Look to your left, okay?" "Yeah." "Do you see two hallways?" "Yeah." "Walk down the one of those further to your right, go through the doors and tell me if you see any conveyor belts." I took a few steps towards the door, and sure enough, there they were as advertised. "You see them?" "Yeah." "Okay, good. It all gets easier from there. If you follow that down, it'll change into a hotel lobby. There should be a dead end. Take a right there." "Hold on, I don't see it. I'm still walking down the hallway with the conveyor belts." "Yeah, I know. Can't you remember the rest?" "It's a long room." "Look, just let me know when everything starts to look like you're in a hotel lobby." It all started to look familiar, but maybe that's because it all looked so much alike and at such a mechanical speed. Minutes assembled if there, there were even minutes to do so. It was like dissecting film to remove a frame and by doing so, giving that frame an entirety in and of itself. "Alright, everything looks like a hotel lobby." "Now all you do is walk until you hit a dead end, turn right, take the stairs up and you'll be in terminal two. You should be able to see a big bank of lockers." "Alright, keep on explaining, I'm almost there." "Look for locker number 665." "There's a lot of lockers here, and this place is swarming with security." I was furious. "You setting me up?" "That's just fucking airport security. They can't do anything to you, so just don't act like a dipshit, and they won't bother you." "Alright, but if I go down for this, I'm pulling everyone I can with me," I stated, and in my head, I marked off another phrase I had always wanted to say as having been said. "Now where is locker 665?" "It's in a bank directly across from this painting. It's some of that artistic shit where the face parts are all where they shouldn't be. You see that shit?" "Yeah, I see it," and indeed it was in my sights. "What's the combo?" "It's clockwise ten, counter-clockwise, once around and then to six, clockwise eight." The padlock slipped open, and there was a gym bag in the locker. I unzipped the bag enough to see inside, and it was stuffed with money. I fell silent. "What is that, Johnny?" I put in the decoy jar and realized that I had to take the bag. If I was being watched, and they were expecting me to take the bag, I'd better take the bag. It could have all been explained later. "It can all be explained later," I accidentally thwarted, stowing away with the heavy bag full of money pulling me all sorts of lopsided. "You still want to meet face to face?" He asked, and I still did want to meet face to face. "Yeah, but I changed my mind about something else. Bring the first half of the payment." "Where to?" "Do you remember that series of directions you gave me?" "Yeah." "Start from the north exit of the airport headed north, take all the same turns from the directions and meet me wherever that is." "Got it, but I've got one condition," he bargained, having kept up his end of the deal, maybe even solely to win my favor. "If I don't see the jar with the real deal when you get there, I'm going to shoot you in the ass where all your brains are at and splatter them against the walls." "If you're planning on coming at me with both guns blazing, that won't be the only thing splattered against the walls," I retaliated before calming the waves to offer compromise. "I can bring you a picture of the jar with a current newspaper. That's the best I can do." "Then that'll have to do," met the judge. "I'll be at the meeting point in half an hour." Westonbrook hung up the phone, and I clasped mine shut and pocketed it, the awkward weight of the bag forcing me to totter clumsily back and forth in order to move forward. It had given Wayne the leeway to catch up to me. "So what's the next step?" Wayne asked, obviously drawn by the scent of the money. Even through the gym bag, he could smell it like a dog, and I was slowly becoming concerned that I reeked of rotten, festering, decomposing money. "We need to find a gift shop," I tallied. "We need a disposable camera and a newspaper." *** Cars whizzed by as Wayne and I sat there on a divider off the shoulder of the freeway. The path had taken us nowhere, to a giant, concrete gnash of braided off-ramps and interstates. The sky was getting dark, and the money weighed down on my back. Wayne and I had hiked forward trading the bag back and forth in only the most reluctant of circumstances when one would fall over from exhaustion and the other would have to relieve him of that bag. After photographing the jar of focus, I had decided it best to keep the jar in a locker at the airport rather than bring it with me. Wayne agreed that this was for the best, and here we were, smoking cigarettes in the damn cold and yelling at each other over the unpleasant brush of tires. "Hey, Wayne," I poised, thinking about jars. "We have any more Undead Sauce?" "There should be tons of it at the place. Why?" "No reason," I wiped my brow to shroud it. "I just haven't done any in a while." "Yeah, me neither." Wayne had been a glutton on that stuff. I was glad he had been clean, but there was no telling if he'd stay clean once the withdrawals started kicking in. "That reminds me, though," I transitioned roughly, like an old vessel through craggy seas. "We need to stop back at the place and get some shovels. And we better arm ourselves." I could see Wayne nod by the bob of the lit end of his cigarette, and then those two tiny flares turned to dull against the sudden white, omnipotent glow of two headlights. The sedan slowed to a halt on the shoulder and shut down. A man walked out, pistol cocked with the corruption of judiciousness in his eyes. "Alright," demanded Judge Curtis Westonbrook. "Let's see it." I held out the photo, and Curtis took it.. He studied the picture intensely as if he was trying to see his reflection in a loaf of bread or something with an equal lack of luster. He kept one hand steady on his revolver, all the while, pointing it back and forth between Wayne and me to keep us both at bay. "Where's the half million?" I interrogated, bold. "It's in the car, in a couple of briefcases in the back." "Let's take a look," I suggested. "And could you take that gun out of my face already? Your possessions are secure." Westonbrook hesitantly put the gun down, scowling with disbelief and the discontent of being told what to do. The three of us headed towards the trunk, and Westonbrook popped it open, revealing two sturdy, steel briefcases that held a discouragingly weighty gym bag full of money apiece, and I swear to god, the cars were just whizzing on by, not even paying a thought to recognizing the armed, roadside calamity that was developing. "So where does the real exchange happen?" the judge wanted to know. I reached into my pocket, and his gun went up, as did my hands. In my right hand, I held the napkin and slowly lowered it to hand it over to the edgy, one might even say quick-to-judge, judge. He unfolded it and started laughing, running his fingers over the scribbled-out letters and reading aloud the some that weren't. "Chance Ironsuchs, huh?" It was with that grimace a strange patriarchy I saw, a certain and indescribably unsettling pride, "Creative." "You know what that is, right?" He nodded, and he grinned through wide, smirking lips pressed hard shut. "It should all be going down in an hour," I directed. "Don't come early." "How will I know you're there?" "Look for Doctor Wayne's car," I smiled, glad to be ironing out the final details. "It'll be parked outside. Its license plate is D-R-W-A-Y-N-E-One. If you see it there, we'll be waiting" "Anything else?" he snorted. "A little help with my luggage?" I could hear it in me now, too. There was that sneer. I was getting good -- too good for my own good -- and I had no fucking clue how I had gotten there. Curtis was furious. He turned about face and got back in the sedan, and as I heard him rev up the engine, I reached down and grabbed onto those suitcases, and then the car just disappeared out from beneath me, throwing dust everywhere as the two barbells of cash pulled me down to the gravel beside the road. The judge sped off with the open trunk of his car bouncing up and down, but never quite latching shut, as the car quickly darted chameleonic into disguise. "Does this mean that we have to go back and get Westonbrook's junk out of that locker?" Doctor Wayne was still on the divider, content to be sitting there, and with an increasing load of capital, generally more fitting to be oxen drawn than toted by two sleep deprived rogues along the highway roadside, I decided it might not be a bad idea to camp out for the night anyhow. The money was duplicitously burdensome. We'd burn it to keep warm. The cars whizzed by still, happily oblivious to the financial bonfire and the high-income drifters with their makeshift money camp. "No, we'll rest here for a while," I said. "There's a place that's not too far from here I want to visit sometime once we're feeling good enough to walk not too far." "What about Westonbrook." "What about him?" "Aren't we supposed to meet him in an hour by my car for the other half million?" As far as I could tell, Wayne was being earnest. "Wayne, we're burning money," I issued a calm reminder. "We're literally fucking burning it." "There's no way we can even get to my car from here in an hour without my car. That's a half million dollars we're going to be missing out on." I roasted some money over the money pit and made a pillow out of money. "We've got too much of this stuff as it is, and it's useless. What good is success if it only weighs you down, right? Then it's not success at all anymore. It's just failure. Tell me if that doesn't make sense." "What doesn't make sense is that you're content passing up a half million dollars." "Then I guess we'll just agree to agree that a lot of things don't make sense." "What the hell are you talking about?" "Let's just rest up by the money fire. We'll go back for your car tomorrow, and if old Chance Ironsuchs does his job, everything will be squared away, and we can go back to the way things were before we were constantly running all over the place." And from there, it was all riding on Chance Ironsuchs.
