Date: 04/19/10 1994 Despite growing up in sunny Santa Barbara she was never really a California girl, and if you asked her now she’d have no problem admitting as much. But at the time it was a real struggle for Roxanne Eleanor Sykes to form a coherent identity. Roxie lived in a town of bottle blondes where anorexia was the chief route to social victory, and felt stifled by the complexities of herself. She wasn’t welcome at school, which was more or less the same as any other school; and she wasn’t much welcome at home, either. Jack and Flo were going through one of those messiest of divorces that mutated unstoppably from week to week not only as if possessing a mind of its own, but as if that mind was clinically insane. Aged thirteen Roxie was no less sure of the state of her parents’ relationship than ten years prior, other than "It’s complicated, Ro, but your mother and I love each other very much, we just find it hard to be together sometimes, especially when she’s so insistently hypocritical." So Roxie had learned to spend little time at home and only the bare minimum at school, instead exploring summit by summit the rolling hills that dominated the skyline behind the city. If one were to stand on the beach amid the throng of volleyball players and surfboard-toting teenagers and white-skinned families down from the North for a weekend of sun and sloth, and turn away from the seafront and look past the stumpy white-fronted buildings of the town and into the distance, through the fog one would see the semi-transparent shadow of the hills, rearing up above Santa Barbara in a threateningly permanent majesty. Knowing the place, one might daydream with guilty wistfulness of those hills one day plunging down through the very foundations of the American Riviera (TM) and shaking this ethnically conflicted town left and right and left again until all the impurities were rattled out into the surf to be distributed across the ocean floor. And upon those hills, imperious in her teenage majesty, Roxie Sykes would stand with her partner in crime Kyle Raymer, and they would grin goofily out into the red morning light of apocalypse and say "Welcome to 1995, Santa Barbara. One year closer to being rid of us." Yet Santa Barbara was and remains a beautiful town, and as much as Roxie deplored how State Street ran an uncomfortable transect through more socio-economic categories than should really be allowed to exist, she appreciated the citywide ban on billboards. She liked to tell Kyle opinions learned from the newspaper as if they were her own, and so the phrases "cultural melting-pot" and "conspicuous consumption" entered her vocabulary at a young age. Kyle thought that Roxie was the cleverest person he had ever met, and with good reason, for as Roxie grew older she came to appreciate the value of her own mind, and set it to work on neo-Marxist theory and aspects of feminism more grown-up than uncritical bra-burning, and soon enough developed a taste for strolling around the town labelling buildings and people and the relations between them in pseudo-academic terms, and reading the landscape as a text, and sitting in cafés all day analysing gesture and tone and the power of branded coffee, and telling Kyle at least once a day that he was totally postmodern. Her first cigarette was with Kyle, and her first drink was with Kyle. These both came on the same day, and so did her first kiss, and that too was with Kyle. He didn’t know it yet, but Kyle would always get the best of Roxie, and when she shrank into the atrophied frame of an old and sick woman she would remember above everything else that most wonderful of times: the summer of 1994. It was evening now, too late to contemplate dinner no matter how much their stomachs groaned, and still they sat waiting for the sun to go down, watching from their perch at the crest of the tallest hill they could find. They looked out over the dusty city as it began its confused transition from day to night, the fading light unsuccessful in its bid to illuminate the scurryings home of overtime office workers offset against the outpourings from overcrowded buses of teenagers too old to like their parents and too young to drink. Roxie’s first cigarette was cushioned from the fluttering wind by her grateful hand, and Kyle lifted his head from the bone-dry grass to suck greedily at it, slurping like a thirsty infant. His cheek brushed her fingers and she felt the first sproutings of gristle from where there soon would be sideburns. He had blond hair that his mother liked to comb for him each morning, and despite his occasional protests Kyle secretly relished the daily encounter, both for the soft caress of his mother’s hand, and because he knew that she knew how to make him look good. Holly Raymer was young and beautiful, and her husband was rich and at work. This was how they lived, and so Kyle loved his mother very much, and at Christmastime and in the weeks before birthdays he would usually announce that he loved his father, too. This was how his bedroom came to be decorated with the discarded gadgets of years past, and in truth the only gift that Kyle had ever really cherished was the birthday present his best friend Roxie had bought him the day he turned thirteen, which was three months ago now. She had saved up for it for weeks (in truth it had been months but Roxie hadn’t wanted Kyle to go quiet and red at the obvious disparity between their allowances) and it was a beautiful hand-crafted wooden airplane with an old-fashioned engine that you had to pour oil into yourself and usually they would spill a little over the wing. And it would chug and chug and burst into the sky like an eagle spreading its wings and Kyle would revel in the responsibility of guiding it on its path and bringing it safely back to the hillside, his mouth wide open in unrestrained wonder, and after a time Roxie would stop watching the plane and just watch Kyle instead, and know that his pure joy was something she would have given anything – anything – to feel herself. And he had these freckles, a dense covering all over his face that would double in the summertime, and Roxie would tease him about them and he would play along with the performance, a symbolic display of self-critique that they both knew was artificial, because Kyle without his freckles just wouldn’t have been Kyle at all. So when Kyle withdrew his face from her cupped hand and dragged the cigarette between his teeth with every intention of devouring the remainder she turned to him indignantly and told him "Screw you, freckles". He just chuckled, and sniffed through his allergy-ridden nose, and once the smoke was finished he flicked it away with an affected nonchalance and hacked and coughed and spat a foul mixture into the grass. "You’re disgusting, you know that?" Roxie needled him, halfway sure that he actually was. Kyle was too happy to ever comprehend that any facet of what he did or how he acted or who he was could possibly be problematic to anyone, a product of many years’ coddling by his adoring mother. What Kyle knew was that if someone as uncompromisingly beautiful as his mother could love him so deeply and so intensely, his life would know no limits, and so he abandoned the facile clawings of the ant colony at school to retreat into the hills with his best friend, comfortable in the knowledge that when he’d aged a few years and grown a few inches he could waltz straight into college and be the node of all the buzz without the slightest hint of effort. And the one thing Roxie really, really, really despised about Kyle Raymer was that he was probably right. So even though Kyle was disgusting she was content in swigging from the same sun-warmed plastic bottle of cheap supermarket cider as him, and when she now made to pass it his way he rolled onto his side to face her as if struck by a sudden inspiration perhaps in the patterns the clouds made or the moving mosaic of colour that the city restitched each second before them, and for a moment their faces were so close that she could have frozen time and counted every single freckle and been happy in doing so. But before she could do that he kissed her, and this was her first kiss, and the ensuing ecstasy of amateur thrillseeking lasted until well after darkness had fallen. As they lay smoking Roxie’s second cigarette she felt the chill on her stomach and pulled her t-shirt back down. "Will we always be like this?" she asked with that anxiety that she would soon come to learn always follows an unconsummated hook-up, and with every second of silence the feeling doubled: that it probably had been a mistake, and if it hadn’t been, then staying together out on the hill and sharing a second cigarette certainly had. And Kyle said "You’ll always be my Ro," which first of all wasn’t really an answer at all, and second of all wasn’t the answer she was looking for. And that was the moment at which Roxie’s heart felt its first stress fracture, and those weak-limbed, light-headed, warm-hearted summer days on the hills, long though they continued, would never be the same again. 1997 Roxie Sykes at sixteen was much the same girl she’d always been, and it irritated her that nobody saw this. What they saw was her preliminary breasts and what they saw was how tightly denim clung to her muscular thighs, and she found herself the belle of a ball she had never wanted to attend. Kyle became sullen and withdrawn, the obvious by-product of Roxie’s intermittent dalliances with burger-bar dates and lesbianism. His acne too was unfortunately timed. Roxie still considered herself his best friend, but when they met at the beach they were playing from memory. Roxie had exactly what Kyle was supposed to have, and he resented this, and the steam train of their co-dependence had coughed and wheezed to a halt. "Still seeing Vinson?" Kyle spat the surname over her head, not looking at her, and Roxie loudly sucked the dregs of a strawberry milkshake through her straw, face flickering with annoyance. "No." There was clear warning in her single word, but Kyle had lost interest in heeding such a warning. "How many guys are you planning on sleeping with this year, Ro?" "I hate it when you call me Ro!" was her snapped rebuttal, and a moment later she wondered if Kyle would ever trace this now familiar refrain back to the day they had shared Roxie’s first cigarette, first drink and first kiss in the dying light on the tallest of the Santa Barbara hills. She tossed the empty cup aside and determined to torment Kyle as much as was possible. So she unbuttoned the tight pastel-striped shirt she was wearing over army-imitation shorts and slipped her arms out of its sleeves. Her bikini was plain and inexpensive, but she was only showing off what it hid. Kyle’s face stiffened but his eyes did not deviate from the eternally bland horizon. So she lay back on the warm sand and willed passers-by to stare openly and longingly at her undernourished and overworked body, the product of countless mornings sharing the gym with grim-faced executives and soccer moms. Her long legs yellowed in the glare of UV-B. There was a certain shuffling, the sound of knuckles cracking, a faint sigh of resignation. Roxie heard these and smiled with eyes closed. Puberty had reversed their power relationship in a way that was starting to suit her. "Roxie, then," Kyle muttered after a long and uncomfortable silence. "I thought you liked Ro." This was a lie, and both of them knew it, though it was unclear whether each knew that the other knew it. In any event, this was the closest Kyle Raymer was ever going to come to apologising for anything, and besides, he didn’t have anything to apologise for. Neither Kyle nor Roxie could understand exactly why they had grown apart. Neither Kyle nor Roxie could pinpoint the cause nor apportion the blame. And Roxie felt guilty for slurping her beverage so noisily. It had been the smallest of transgressions, but entirely designed to annoy her best friend, and she found that this was something she no longer wanted to do. So Roxie sat up and pushed her dark hair out of her face and grabbed Kyle by the shoulders. "Let’s smoke," she decided, and although Kyle had long since kicked the habit he acquiesced, sensing the chance to rebuild bridges that had fallen into disrepair. Roxie lit her own cigarette and decorated it with a widening smile, and she puffed and puffed and watched Kyle’s sea-specked face with intent. And when he finally caved and asked when his turn would come she parted her gloss-smeared lips and planted them on his, and he sat in limp delight as she expelled her smoky breath into his mouth, and followed it up with a flick of her muscular tongue. And he sat on the beach with his mouth open, savouring the taste of her kiss until the rising tide began to lap gently at his heels. Roxie loved Kyle, but it wasn’t as simple as it should have been. They spent the rest of the summer together, and when Kyle stopped resenting her body and her brain and her increasingly obvious superiority to him in almost every way, she opened up to him and the pair found themselves transported back in time to the heady days of their innocent youth, before growing up had ravaged her emotions and his face. One day Roxie saw ugly black hairs making territorial claims on behalf of Kyle’s respective eyebrows, and he passed an ambivalent afternoon under her stewardship, the pain of the hairs being ripped out between clenched metal jaws offset by the more base pleasure of the way in which Roxie was straddling his lap for optimum leverage. She felt something stir against her thigh and smiled to herself, saying nothing. When she shifted her weight she heard a stifled expulsion somewhere between a moan and a gasp. When she subsequently suggested they go for a walk he mumbled an excuse and sat resolutely on his side, covering his shorts with a conspicuously placed arm. She hadn’t slept with anybody, yet, and was hurt by Kyle’s suspicions. If she’d had the sorts of friends that had slumber parties and played truth or dare and asked leading questions, all with lots of giggling, she presumed that she would have said it would be Kyle, when the time was right, and, and, yeah. But she didn’t have those sorts of friends, and so she hadn’t really thought about it. All summer his hurtful words resonated in her mind, and she began to wonder how many guys she would sleep with that year, and one night with the sheets drawn up around her and her flimsy nightdress discarded in the middle of the carpet she discovered a little more about herself, and it was his harsh cheekbones and fluffy hair that swam into her mind as she came. But it wasn’t as simple as it should have been. Roxie hated the colour of her hair, which was dark dark dark dark brown, and when Kyle told her with mock disgust how he had found a strand on his pillow and had known it was definitely hers because it was long and wavy and black, she didn’t speak to him for a week. 1999 "I can’t afford to divorce your mother," Jack Sykes told his suddenly grown-up daughter one oppressively bright August morning. "Still?" That was her first thought: after all, her parents had been dancing around divorce since Roxie had escaped the womb. "What’s the problem now? You’re worried about what it would do to her? To yourself?" Roxie, recently graduated from high school, issued this half-caring over-the-shoulder reply as she poured bran flakes into a bowl. She had spent more than a decade waiting for her father to confide in her and now, as he sat at the kitchen table with his head in his hands, she couldn’t even recognise the signs. "No, I mean, I literally cannot afford it." The money had run out just as Christmastime swept Southern California into a flurry of synthetic snow. Roxie had long ago learned that it was too much to expect anything culturally qualified as normal of her parents, and on Christmas Eve morning packed a small bag and set off for the Lake Tahoe holiday village in which her parents owned a small stake. Two tickets protested at her tight grip but she checked in alone, never having mustered the courage to ask Kyle to forego the pleasures of his own family holidays. He would do exactly the same on Christmas Day as he had done on Christmas Day the year before and on every Christmas Day that his round blue eyes had seen. Roxie saw it as suffocating, Kyle saw it as whatever. But before even boarding the plane she’d got a call, and one thing led to another, and soon enough she was home and the small stake in the Lake Tahoe holiday village had mysteriously ballooned into the sort of crutch on their disposable income about which her father would periodically (inaudibly) mutter, and soon enough the company was gutted and its deposed CEO was spending Roxie’s inheritance on exotic cocktails and exotic women on a Pacific island that had yet to have its name Anglicised. And eight months later little had changed other than a standing warrant for his arrest should he ever be so stupid as to set foot on US soil. Roxie felt this was about as unlikely as her mother being satisfied with her latest tummy tuck, but Jack would talk of nothing else. Flo Sykes tired of her husband’s false hope, and her shrill pleas for the release of expunged vows slowed to the more measured and consistent assertion that she could never love him again. And so it was that Jack and Florence Sykes (née Hampshire) agreed to stage a fake divorce ceremony. On the morning of the farce it seemed that the leaves had turned brown overnight. October brought further oppression upon a household that had never been happy and Roxie swore she could see the devious glint of every minute of the day as it skipped merrily away from her. Eventually she roused herself from the pillow to the joyful shouts of her father. Not being a morning person she struggled to discern the content of his yelling but it soon became clear that gambling was not a preferred vice of her mother’s. Nonetheless it had netted Jack Sykes a not insubstantial sum of money and this precipitated the proclamation that "I can give you a proper divorce now, darling!" Aghast at the cancellation of the ceremony she had been counting on to finally give her the closure that she had spent eighteen years silently screaming for, Roxie left the house to release an unstoppable torrent of tears. She could not remember having cried before. And so it was that Jack Sykes failed to divorce the woman so desperate to be free of him on account of wanting to do her the service of having her leave him more honourably. What Flo Sykes (née Hampshire) did not know was that she had now lost this chance for good, because on her return that evening to their home of twenty years she was greeted with champagne and red roses, and before long they were making wild, passionate love of the kind she had not tasted since the gruesome mess of childbirth had wrenched her pleasures apart. Her ducts finally dried of their tears Roxie found the front door open, having heard the noises from three blocks away. "So, the pee-pee goes in the poo-poo?" she enquired with a cutting blend of amusement and disgust, and when she reached the sanctity of her bedroom she stood for some time by the door, slamming it and pulling it open and slamming it again, just for the hell of it. 2001 When he was nineteen, Kyle Raymer became an A&F model, and Roxie Sykes had never felt so betrayed. But it would get worse before it got better, and a year later he was engaged to be married to one of any of the vapid blondes that littered the beach alongside the retired and the unemployed on weekday afternoons. The lunch they met up for was excruciating. "She’s pretty," the girl said sneeringly, and never having mastered this California world of contradiction and anachronism, in which semiotics enveloped any undercurrent of material meaning that might be held in words or actions or objects, Roxie could not quite process exactly how this was an insult. But it was. "I know what you’re going to say," Kyle muttered in her ear as they waited for his fiancée to return from the bathroom. But Roxie wanted to say more than Kyle could know. She was furious with the man he was turning into. "Did you get those bleached?" she asked in disbelief as he flashed a toothy grin at Wilson, or McKenzie, or whatever stupid name the girl had. "We had them done together!" the girl said happily, inviting Roxie to peer at her own immaculate molars. There was spinach between her teeth. She wanted to strangle her. "It was so totally nice meeting you," Kyle’s future wife gushed, "Kyle has told me so much about you! Hee hee." "Excuse me?" It was the hee hee that did it; Roxie could no longer contain herself. The girl blushed. She rocked on her heels, entwining her hand in Kyle’s, which was brown and clean. The way Roxie remembered that hand, there was always dirt under the fingernails. She needed Kyle to scratch his way out of this grave he had dug for himself. But now Kyle was twisting his face into this approximation of regret, and Roxie could only frown. "What exactly have you told her about me?" Her words were slow, her tone low and threatening, and now she ignored the limpet on his arm and reserved her cold fire only for Kyle. He had brought this upon himself. "I showed her the plane," he said at last, and though this should not have been cause for conflict in itself, the shuddering in his voice told Roxie that there was more to this than the words themselves conveyed. She scented betrayal, and she grabbed it with her teeth and shook until blood spattered the walls. And she did all this with no more than a stony glare, and when Kyle’s wide eyes emptied themselves into hers she was both satisfied and immeasurably angry, and she stormed out of the restaurant and ran and ran, and she didn’t stop running until she was way out of the city and the memories it held. Before long she was standing on the top of that same hill on which she and Kyle had smoked her first cigarette and swallowed her first drink and compared the taste of their tongues. And she turned away from the coast – away from the cruel, beautiful city – and she strove to see further into America than she had ever seen before. And there was no fog that day, and as she stood on tiptoe and peered into the murky distance she realised that this was not, after all, the tallest of the hills. But it was their hill, and when she heard him approach she wasn’t surprised that he’d found her. He gasped and panted and bent over with his hands on his knees. He had been running. "Yeah?" She angled the daggers of her words between his ribs. Kyle Raymer looked at his best friend, and pushed sweat-soaked strands of carefully lowlighted hair out of his eyes, and he began to shake his head. And then he began to shiver, and cry, and soon enough they were locked together in this excruciating crouch that neither of them would break for fear of time slipping out of its standstill, and he had his head in her collar and she could feel the warmth of his tears on the side of her neck. He stank of the perfume that it was his job to spray on Abercrombie’s clothes as they hung sullenly on racks bearing unnecessarily exclusive price tags. To be fair, she thought, he did smell good. He wept and wept as she stroked his hair, and still they crouched and leant on one another for balance. His hand clutched at the empty belt loop of her jeans, over which a sliver of skin tauntingly poked. He grasped at her and kissed her lips and she pushed him gently away. He swallowed hard. It wasn’t supposed to be like this. "How’s college," he choked, and as he did so he realised that their weekly phone calls had dwindled to the nothingness of speak-to-you-soon. Kyle Raymer had eschewed college to prolong his time as a minor celebrity on Southern California’s clubbing circuit, living rent-free off his father’s wealth and blowing his own earnings on things that would never matter. He spent his days standing shirtless outside the Abercrombie store on State Street, and his nights standing shirtless in nightclubs glistening with vodka and desire. He knew nothing of Roxie’s life at UC Santa Cruz, where she had fronted an alliance with workers’ union UNITE over sweatshop conditions in New York garment factories. He knew nothing of the Foucault and Butler she absorbed like the most intelligent of sponges. Although he still knew her as he always had and always would, he didn’t know what drove her, or what she cared about, or who she cared for apart from him. And Kyle realised that he wanted to know all of these things and more. "It’s good," she replied tenderly. "Are you going to start next semester, like you said?" He didn’t know. He didn’t know anything anymore. 2020 Ten years from now Kyle Raymer will die in a horrific automobile accident. The crash will be entirely his fault, and no other vehicle will be involved. He will be found intact, broadly speaking, but the crush will have thieved his final breath. No one will ever know why he was speeding east with such demonic conviction. The morning of the funeral, Roxie will finger her wedding band and taste the silent tears decorating her ruby lips. She will think about summers past, opportunities spurned, her first cigarette and first drink and first kiss. She will think about his freckles. And with that a contentment will come over her face, and she will cross the sparsely furnished bedroom to her husband’s old record player. She will trace her finger across the neglected surface, and put its tip on her tongue, tasting the stale dust. She will reach for a 7" single, one of the dozens they bought in flat defiance of the MP3 generation, and set the needle upon its surface. "Who’s gonna sit on your fence when I’m gone?", she will hear. "Who’s gonna sit on your fence when I’m not there?" The music will compose her. This is how she will remember him, this will be her tribute, and there will be no need to go to the funeral. There will be no need to think of Kyle Raymer any longer. And Roxanne Shipley will stride out to the car with her head held high, and greet her neighbour with an affected morning cheer, and drive well below the speed limit on the left-hand side of the road. And she will not allow her hands to tremble as she takes the children to school. Quotation taken from "Suffragette Suffragette" by Everything Everything.
