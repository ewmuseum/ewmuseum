A Pull On My Fur and a Hundred Tiny Hands

25 July 2010
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 07/25/10 (1) "When I die," Clemence Shipley began, "don't celebrate my life. There would be precious little point in that." It was a difficult time for Clemence, who had hated leaving the house ever since the day he reluctantly moved into it. July was usually a time of hot sun and iced tea, but his wife had slipped on the garden path the previous weekend, leaving Clemence to negotiate the supermarket aisles alone, sharing the acrid air of what could barely qualify as a community. "Oh, don’t you start," Maggie scolded him, collecting his untouched mug of now-cold Earl Grey on her way back to the kitchen. "I’m quite serious," the old man mused, contemplating the movements of the jay perched happily in the midst of his overgrown lawn. Tim had not come by to mow it for some weeks now, and the parched grasses were yellowing in summer’s glare. "I can’t say I enjoyed yesterday... No, I didn’t enjoy that at all. You know how it makes me feel. All the singing." The Shipleys had never quite mastered the strictly regimented social circles of Albany’s elderly, but they nonetheless found themselves attending a great number of funerals as Clemence’s retirement years wore on. The most recent had been Maureen Whitlock, seventy-four years young, erstwhile secretary of the bridge club. Stroke. "What ever happened," Clemence pondered, "to a respectful service, All Things Bright, and then everybody potters on home and gets on with their lives?" "Oh, Clemence," Maggie groaned as she leant towards him, "when was it that you became so cynical?" She dabbed with a damp cloth at the ring that his morning tea had left on the end table, squeezed as it had been into the alcove between the three big hardbacks that waited patiently for the attention their paint-by-numbers content hardly merited. "Now, now, Maggie," he surveyed her with his grainy grey eyes, "I let you open the curtains, didn’t I?" This was a daily point of contention for the English expatriates, Clemence quite content to bathe himself in electric light day and night, the better to discern the words in the books he still struggled through. His wife insisted on drawing the curtains open for him in the mornings, sometimes even daring to unlatch the window and let some fresh air into the room that she constantly had to tell herself did not resemble a tomb. He watched her as she left the room, steadily defiant of the wide purple bruises blooming since her fall. Clemence was still trying to mount the courage to tell his wife about the pain erupting from deep within him. He felt it in the mornings and he felt it in the nights. He felt it as he sat there listening to the World Service. When he spoke, coughed, or farted, he felt it -- a knife tracing his insides, a meticulous craftsman taking him to pieces. He didn’t know how long it had been since he had been to the toilet. Another day, Clemence thought. Another day. * * * Before Christopher Barnard died, he saw his mother and father happy together. He heard the beautiful lullaby with which Kate soothed him to sleep. He smelled her perfume, long dormant, hastily patted between wrists. He tasted the sweet residues of her milk, a lasting treat on his tongue. And he felt the soft touch of her lips as they prodded lovingly at his forehead. This was the last time that Kate would see her son alive. Christopher was six weeks old when he died. He was merely an interlude in American life -- barely a comma in the evolutionary sentence. In a way, he was the lucky one. Before he died, all his senses were ablaze with beauty, promise, happiness, and love. He would never have to know how deeply his six weeks on earth would haunt the lives of Kate Barnard, Owen Compliments, and Tim Shipley. But first there were tests to run. There were forms to fill in. Men and women with blue uniforms, face masks, hair nets. There was waiting around -- there was a lot of waiting around. There were microwave meals, snacks from vending machines, bottled water. There were Wikipedia articles to devour with taut faces, each possible cause of sudden infant mortality hotlinked to the next in a relentless stream of doom -- so many ways to die. There were grim words, lost hopes. There were tears... agonies. Soon enough there were police to talk to. Owen’s sister, Lily, was inconsolable. She broke down time and again as the tall dark detective tried to reconcile the irreconcilable. She had only wanted to do what was best for her brother and his child. She had diligently watched the boy. She had held him. She had put him to sleep. On his front or on his back? They asked her the question, not meeting her stricken eyes, but she couldn’t say. The psychiatrist said that the stress had made her forget. That she might never remember. But Tim had been the last person to see the child alive -- or so he thought. He couldn’t be sure, he explained, because he’d only looked in, and assumed Christopher was sleeping. The boy wasn’t his, he emphasised. He hadn’t wanted to pick him up, rock him in his arms, feed him, sing to him. Without Kate there, it wouldn’t have felt right. The psychiatrist nodded, and said nothing. Owen took the blame almost by default; he was used to blame. Blame had followed him around since his childhood -- harder to shake off than his white skin. Owen didn’t want to hear a shrink tell him it wasn’t his fault, so he didn’t go to see the shrink. He went somewhere else instead. Kate was placed on suicide watch. * * * Tim Shipley’s hands closed around the steel bars. He drew the free weights to his chin and fired insults at himself. Too slow-witted. Too weak. Too afraid. His triceps cried out, but past midnight there was no mercy. He quietly bent and placed the weights on the floor, careful not to wake his parents. Pushing the curtain aside, he cooled his burning cheeks against the glass. In the garden stood a fox, its snout nuzzling cautiously in the rosebed. Its fur was dull, downtrodden. He hadn’t been able to go home since the boy had died. His parents had taken him in with open arms; how could they not? But he was restless here, out of place. This wasn’t the house he had grown up in, and so it wouldn’t let him regress -- devolve into the quiet, studious boy he had been, devoid of friendship, devoted to learning. Happy alone with himself. Now all he wanted to learn was how to forget. Tim let his hand fall to his side, the curtain slipping shut across his face. The room was dark. He had never valued the 60-watt bulb; the gloom helped him think. Science had done a lot for the human mind, and vice versa, but Tim couldn’t help but think that both were rotting from the peripheries. He took a long, slow breath through his nose, and squatted to take hold of the weights once more. He would raise them up until he could not feel his fingers. It was the only escape from the guilt he felt. If he had been in the mood for reflection, he might have noted the irony that his resolution to stop running had only conjured up a situation that made him want to do just that. But for Tim, the choice was not between running and staying. With just days until Japan -- the night that would send him stratospheric, creating Tim-Shipley-the-superstar, after these long and tortured years -- a spinal fracture, most vividly; and every other bump in the road that had accumulated along the way, building a seemingly insurmountable wall -- the choice was, for Tim Shipley, no choice at all. And no matter how much guilt he felt, he’d already made that choice. He was atop that wall. All he had to do was drop down the other side. It was harder not to. So he crunched his teeth together and forced his leaden arms upward, because on this unseasonably cold Albany night, it was all that he could do. Perhaps he was doing it for the boy. More likely he was doing it for himself. But derailing was at this stage not an option. He would fly to Japan. He would win the Rampage Tournament. And then -- only then -- would he stand tall to face whatever happened next. He threw the weights down, gasping. When he looked out once more at the still life of night-time Albany, the fox was gone. Tim smiled and willed it on its way. The rosebed would still be there. Waiting for it. * * * Nobody sees Clemence vomit behind the crematorium. On his knees, trousers flapping around his ankles in the spluttering wind. Bent up with his head in his hands. Coughing up yellow, green, a touch of red. As he picks his untidy way back through the nettles and long grasses, he listens to the sick pulsing of blood in his ears. He sees it in his vision, spots forming and disappearing just as quickly, little taunts from the heavens above. He gets to the door just in time. He might have passed out, otherwise. When the tiny little coffin starts to disappear behind the stage -- after the hymn has been sung, the tearful speeches made, the collection box passed round -- no flowers please, donations only, for the furtherance of SIDS research -- Clemence loses his resistance to it all. Suddenly. Like a body from the window of a burning building. It doesn’t fade, it just goes. The tears cascade down his face. Unstoppable. He has to hold onto his wife’s shoulder. It’s not even his grandchild. The police said go ahead, have the funeral. The police said they’d done all they could with the body. "It’s so sad, Maggie," he chokes later that night, pulling his wife close, sobbing into her shoulder. "Nothing to be done... No way to know..." "That poor girl," Margery Shipley murmurs, her eyes like marbles, stoic in their sockets. They hear a creak on the stairs, and Clemence stops crying. "Tim?" Maggie shrieks. Silence, and then a reluctant sigh. "What are you doing still awake?" As soon as he edges the door open he wishes he hadn’t. His parents are locked together in a terrible embrace, his father’s face grey and lifeless, his cheeks worn. He meets his mother’s eyes, expecting to see compassion, but getting only fire. "You weren’t there." Tim looks at the floor. "I couldn’t bear it." "Everybody else bore it. Clemence and I bore it. Look at your father now, Tim. Your father bore it. Look at what it’s done to him." The old man shakes his head, opening his mouth to protest, but the words will not come, and he accepts his indignity, rendered forever helpless, a shadow of a man. He closes his eyes and lays still on his wife’s breast, shutting his ears as best he can. He wonders when his own time will come. He feels his intestines tense up, and reasons that it might be soon. "I’m sorry. I just... couldn’t." The words are like cancerous lumps, and he feels sicker with each one he coughs out. "Well, get to bed, now," Maggie orders, forever retentive of her maternal authority. "And you can go to Kate first thing in the morning. I’m worried about her. She shouldn’t be alone in that place." "She’s not al--" "He didn’t come, either," Maggie interrupts, getting Tim’s attention. He stares at his mother’s expressionless face. "Owen wasn’t at the funeral?" "The boy blames himself," a low rumble from Clemence, impelled to intervene. He raises up his exhausted head and surveys his broken son. "He shouldn’t. Neither should you." "I... I don’t suppose anyone’s to blame." "For the baby, no," Maggie begins shrewdly, "but there’s more to it than that." "How is there?" Tim retorts hotly, his cheeks flushing. "Christopher died, mother. The baby died. Forgive me for not exactly rushing to move on." She shakes her head gravely. "Your father and I are older than you, Timothy, much older," Maggie says, delivering the one verdict that her son can never contest. "It’s a terrible tragedy. Of course it is. But you must be strong, because Kate can’t handle this on her own. Nobody could -- but especially given her... background. Kate can’t do it by herself. Go to her tomorrow. She needs you." Margery Shipley gazes at her son, never more sure than now of the man he could be. Which is what traumatises her all the more when she sees his hand shaking. His skin turning pale. "Mum, dad," he reverts to the roles of his childhood, names long dormant, "I’m going to Japan." Nobody breathes, the silence is so morbidly pregnant. "I’m going, um, now, actually. That’s why I, ah, I didn’t want to wake you. My bag’s packed and the taxi’s waiting outside. So, um, thanks for letting me stay, these last few nights. And -- I'll call you from Tokyo -- and Kate too, and -- I mean, I really -- I'm sorry. I’m so sorry. But I have to go. I’m sorry." Tim’s mother taught him that everything is excusable so long as you say sorry. Tim’s father taught him to never say sorry without meaning it. * * * Japan is everything that he needs it to be. After he lands the Eternal Hope, he looks up at the Tokyo Dome’s bare roof and thinks for a moment of Christopher. Then Roxie bustles him away, before Tempest can snap. Before either of them can do anything they’ll come to regret. He finds himself being sat down and surrounded by grim faces. They move his head this way. They move his head that way. When he turns it to look for Roxie, they panic and clamour for him to sit still. Then to lay still. He feels something forced over his head. A brace. A needle slipping into him... He sits bolt upright. "Stop." He doesn’t expect them to hear him, or he doesn’t expect them to listen. But they do. Awed faces, bright eyes. He pulls off the brace. Stands tall. He closes his eyes and gives the wreckage back home a moment’s thought. His parents... Kate... Owen... the child. He sniffs, swallows down some blood. He devotes that moment to them, knowing full well that it can’t excuse the selfishness of what he’s about to do. Then he finds Roxie, forces her into a cab’s back seat. Calls to Chris, Johnny, even Andy. They give him curious looks. Tessa and Clyde are fighting for the belt. AXEL’s fighting for his vocal chords. They say maybe they’ll catch him later. He shrugs, and a half hour later they’re taking bites out of sushi and hurling away the pieces they don’t like. They’re drinking champagne from the bottle, and after that they’re gargling sake. Tokyo isn’t ready for them, and things get a little blurry but it’s dark in the hotel room when Roxie gets down on her knees for him. I need this. I deserve this. I want this. That’s what he thinks. Morning comes, and he doesn’t want to know himself anymore. (2) * * * "He -- he called me -- a whore," Lily whispered tearfully, unable to meet the shrink’s eye. The room smelled of mahogany and secrets. "Did that matter to you? What he said?" Lily closed her eyes, thinking about her brother. "I think... I don’t know. What did you ask me?" The psychiatrist folded his hands on his lap. "I asked you if it mattered to you, at the time. That he called you a whore." She pulled at the dark, wavy hairs above her ear. "I don’t think anyone likes to be called a whore." Her voice sounded far away as she said it. Like it wasn’t hers. The man leaned towards her, so close that she could count the blackheads on his nose. "Perhaps we should move on." She started counting. After a silence that could have lasted a minute or longer, the man stood up from his chair and turned to the window, catching Lily’s attention. "What are you doing?" she asked, conscious that every hour she spent in his room was a burden on already overstretched public finances. "I’m thinking," he replied. Lily noticed that his jacket was too long, and the padding in the shoulders too heavy. "We’ve met three times now, Lily, and I’m struggling to believe that we’re accomplishing anything." Lily ignored him. "Ah -- perhaps a thought best kept to myself," he acknowledged. Lily ignored him. She was getting used to it. "Have you talked to my brother yet?" she asked tentatively. "Oh -- I suppose you can’t say --" "He’s disappeared," the psychiatrist announced, turning to look at Lily. She gaped at him. "What did you say?" "Nobody knows where he’s gone," he rubbed a weary hand over his eyes, turning back to the window. "We thought he would have gone to Ms. Barnard’s residence, but it appears that’s not--" The door slammed shut. Lily Compliments was gone. * * * He meets her at the door, thankful for the complimentary dressing gown. "Sorry about last night." "Hey, if only you won your matches that quickly." Tim doesn’t know what to say, so when she tosses him a satsuma, he starts peeling it. "You’ve got a message," he notices the flashing red light on the handset built into the wall. Roxie shrugs. "It’ll just be Tempest again. Guess she’s a little bitter." Tim crushes a segment of the citrus fruit in his mouth, still waiting for his brain to catch up with his body, or maybe the other way around. He’s not all that used to hangovers. "Listen," he begins, but she doesn’t seem to hear him, bounding around in figure-hugging sportswear packing the last of her bags. "Hey," he says, grabbing her arm this time. She stops and gazes up at him, her lips inches from his. "Did we..." Roxie waits patiently, but it doesn’t seem like Tim’s ever going to finish the sentence, so she elbows him in the stomach and goes about her business. Tim gulps, dropping the fruit. "Hey. I’m fragile this morning!" "That’s life, Tim. Life is fragile." Yes, he realises. Yes it is. And right there in the middle of Roxie’s hotel room, he starts to cry. His gown falls apart in the middle, but he doesn’t notice. He just cries. When he buries his head in Roxie’s shoulder, it’s not enough to assuage the dread creeping like ice through his veins. "Why did you come back, Roxie," he sniffs after he’s calmed down some. Roxie’s on the floor, laying on her back, legs behind her head. He tries not to think about this too much. "Question should be ‘what took you so long’, ass. You’ve gotten complacent without me here to remind you what a dick you are." She says all this from her position with her legs behind her head, which is fortunate for her, because it allows her to blush without Tim realising why -- and so Roxie ducks, for one more day at least, the issue of why she really came back. "Hey," she protests as she rolls into a sitting position, "you were totally looking at my ass." He smiles, grateful of the opportunity to ignore his red eyes and his blocked nose for a moment. "It’s infuriating me. I literally cannot remember if we..." Roxie sticks out her tongue, her eyes dancing as she relishes putting Tim through the ringer. "Keep guessing, superstar. Maybe this’ll teach you to drink a little less next time." He rolls his eyes. "You’re so mean." "And yet so thoroughly awesome. Hey, I had one message this morning that wasn’t from Tempest. You’ve got Terror in Anaheim." "Jay Terror?" "How many other Terrors you know?" "Oh, Jesus Christ. My neck really fucking hurts, by the way." "You refused a brace. You remember that?" Tim shuts his eyes and lays back on the bed. "Yeah," he admits bitterly, "I remember that." * * * Owen trembled as he held the bag, almost losing the red chalk dust in the Atlantic wind. That wouldn’t do at all. His lip bled, but still he chewed at it as he measured out the quantities of pigment, water, and glue. In time the mixture became a thick, sticky paste. A milky red colour, like a mascarpone-based pasta sauce; it made Owen hungry. He didn’t want to remember when he’d last eaten. He shrank inside his flimsy jacket, no protection against the rainstorm that had caught him outside the crafts store. His car was parked fifty yards back, the doors open, the key maybe even still in the ignition. Somehow, Owen knew that today he would be left alone. God understood. He collected the colourful paste into his cupped hand, not having thought to buy a brush or a roller. As he drew the first stripe down across the gritty concrete of this long-abandoned pierside basketball court, he felt broken glass stick in his palm. He ignored it, pushing harder as he forced his message into the ground. He had chosen the location quite deliberately. The space that the city forgot. His tribute would rest undisturbed, the sole beacon of hope in this beaten-down wasteland. Even after the rain washed it away, the city would remember it. He wept as he worked, a cathartic cry that repaired his determination even as it fell away. He was trying not to think. All he knew was the absolute necessity of his gesture. The link he was etching with his hands, physical and familial. For a moment he wondered whether his father might see it, whether from the streets or from the skies. The work was long and exhausting. When his right arm began to cramp up, he used his left. By the time the job was half-finished, both his palms had been repeatedly sliced by the jagged edges and the broken glass. Owen didn’t lose a moment cleaning himself up. The blood and the paste mixed until they were indistinguishable, Owen and the city marking themselves as blood brothers. When he had finished, he rolled over onto his back and looked into the grey sky, as if his act could have conjured up a heaven heretofore unseen. The next morning they found him, clothes soiled with a terracotta promise. His face was grey, his feet numb, but his message clear. "FOR CHRISTOPHER," it read.
