Nature

11 June 2011
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 06/11/11 He didn't see the knife until it was too late to react. His father was waist deep in the bog, the fingers of his left hand digging into the hollowed cypress stump to keep balanced. His right arm was locked in follow through. The water moccasin tried to scurry away, its tail wildly beating back and forth until it coiled up and stopped fighting. The blade had cut straight through, just below the head, staking its body in into the embankment. Blood oozed over its scales. "Why'd you scream," asked his father, sloshing through the murk and his arms pumping forward to keep him from sinking. He wasn't paying attention, and that's why he was on his backside, digging the heels of his hands into the mud and pushing away. The forests behind long forgotten Rodney were filled with snakes, not all of them hiding away in the underbrush. At night, he could hear them writhing below his mattress, underneath the floorboards of the Presbyterian Church his family called home. His father pulled himself out and over, dirty hands reaching for his son's forearms to pull him to his feet. His eyes were fierce. The thick black mustache hid his sneer. "Asked you a question." But there was no response. He just shirked away, hands patting down his overalls and rubbing mud deeper into jean. His father had raised him better. He grabbed him by the scruff of the neck, wrenching him around and to his knees, forcing his head mere inches away from the snake's carcass. His eyes went wide, his body trembling and trying its damnedest to writhe free. His old man wasn't going to let him. "I saw it when it was there. Why didn't you?" "I--" Another hard push at his neck. His lips touched leather, the body underneath meekly squirming under his kiss. He couldn't answer through his stammers. His mouth tasted of rust. His father spoke through clinched teeth. "Cause you weren't paying attention? Look at what happens when you don't pay attention." He had to force him to look at it. Had no choice. It wasn't anger that drove him. It was fear. But then, it was shame. He let go of son's neck, stepping back and drawing his hands together, running them through close cropped silver and black. Lip met forearm, rubbing blood away with the peach fuzz that had just begun to grow. Pushing up, he beelined to the basin of the bog, cupping his hands and filling them with cloudy water to wash it all away as best as he could. "Terrence..." He dried his hands off on his pant legs, his eyes tracking the ground as he started to walk towards his father. Sometimes, the best response was no response at all. His old man had a temper, and he knew it all too well. But his father always explained his reasons, even to a fault. "I didn't mean to do that to you. I'm sorry." Fidgeting hands reached for his pockets, digging deep for a cigarette that wasn't there. "Just don't know what's all out here, is all. We ain't been out here long." He nodded his head and bit his lip as he drew closer and closer, looking at the moccasin. It struggled to survive. The body flopped about, its head trying to reach for the blade. "It was just under the leaves and then I saw it." "That snake's path, it wasn't toward you." He wasn't sure if his son had calmed down, and he wouldn't blame him if he hadn't. He didn't shirk away from his comforting grasp. "Most times, they're more scared of you than you are of them. Even if you step on em, they only bite if they been feel they can't get away. It won't bother you if you don't bother him." "Snakes--" "Are scary. I know." Stooping to his knee, he reached for the handle of his hunting knife, yanking it out and rubbing the blade down the stomach of his wool shirt. Crimson matched crimson in a perfect, pulpy blend. His son faced him as he turned the knife to his side and patting shoulder. Terrence tried to look away, but his father made sure to draw his chin back so he couldn't. His smile was warm. Reassuring. Just like the muted chuckle he couldn't keep locked in his throat. "Thing is, I was worried you'd gone got yourself bit. Or something else might've gotten you. You're my son. I got to protect you." "I'm sorry. I just...I dunno." "I know." He nodded his head, tousling the mop of dirty brown hair on his son's head. "I know." To be honest, Terrence didn't know why his father had been so upset. He'd seen him draw his blade plenty in the circus, and he'd made sport of hitting the bulls-eye on dartboards just like he coaxed life out of dying fair rides when nobody thought he could. It was why everyone in Montgomery Queen loved him. Why they were sad when they had to leave. "Nature's got a way of being cruel though. I understand why you're shook up. I really do. But the snake...he meant you no harm." His father looked at the moccasin, grabbing it by the tail and dragging it through the peat. "And I know it ain't your fault, but it is. Partly." He redirected his son, stepping behind him, putting his palms into his and slopping onto the bog floor. The son didn't understand. His back rested against his father's chest. The knife was put into his right hand. "Going to learn you a lesson. An important one. Pay attention." His father made him pick it up, it's skin still warm and its muscles spasming inside. The body unwound itself without a fight. Forcing the grip up further and further, he moved the blade of the knife in his son's hand just underneath the jaw, turning it up and tensing as he forced him to try and rip upward. "Put it out of its misery." But he couldn't. The skin was too tough and he didn't apply enough pressure. His father forced the yank upward until he'd met the spine. His hands filled with life. He stopped him, pulling his son's hand back and dropped the moccasin to the ground. "Pushing up doesn't get enough pressure, you see. You have to push down. Use your weight. Using gravity." It took four good hacks to finally sever through skin and flesh and bone, the head lopped off and brushed aside into swamp water. "Now you see that white part? Get your fingers and put them through. You're making a pocket to keep the meat intact while also cutting off the skin." He wanted to throw up the second his father wormed his fingers along the mauled hole they'd made together. His index and middle dug in, frantically trying to peel the scaled skin away from the insides. And when he did, his father had him lead the knife through the space and trimming downward until they were half way down the body. "Now put the knife down and just pull. Don't worry about being gentle." He focused on his father's hands, his fingers long and boney, the veins and arteries protruding outward, as he took control. All sorts of pieces and parts from inside the moccasin botched his chipped nails, mixing in with the dirt both underneath and licking their contours. Once he was finished skinning, he took the blade and began trimming down the exposed length of the body, cutting away what was good and flicking away the bad. The white had already begun tinging gray. He wiped the marrow on his hands away on the back of his son's overalls. "Not too bad. Not too bad for a first time." He wasn't expecting his father to hand him all those moist, slimy pieces, but he dared not drop them. "We'll make a man out of you yet." He chuckled at the thought, rising from the ground and staring back from where they came. "Mom should be able to make a fine meal out of them filets." The sun hadn't been up less than an hour, but the garage of Comstock Autobody was nothing more than a tinderbox. He hadn't slept. How could he? Autumn worked the graveyard shift at Edgar's, and the bed never felt right when it was him on his own. All he could focus on was the drone of cicadas and crickets outside his bedroom window. It was better than thinking of the road and the argument they'd had and the crank he was about to drive across the New Mexico border to the Mescalero Apache Reservation. All to earn his stripes. He'd never been there, and if it was anything like he'd heard, he'd of loved to have kept it that way. The oscillating fan wasn't cooling him down quick enough for his taste. His skin was greasy. Sticky. Sheets of sweat poured from all over his body,staining through his flannel shirt and worn bluejeans. The shower he craved wouldn't have helped him any; the mug of Mississippi summer had a habit of oppression as thick as the day was long. The cool rush of menthol was his only saving grace Nobody was in the shop on Sundays, save Opal. If he could help it, he'd rather they not cross paths. Them being alone only led to trouble. He flicked the butt against the cement slab, stomping it out with the heel of his boot he'd had for so long the leather at the toe had rotted away, showing the blue painted steel underneath. Everything he needed for the road was laid out on the workbench, fitted around soldering irons and crescent wrenches. The canteen with a circle of felt on both sides of the drum, because God help him if he got stranded in the Panhandle without a drop to drink. A flash light. State maps of Mississippi, Louisiana, Texas, and New Mexico. Two pairs of jeans, boot cut. One white t-shirt. A black button short sleeve from the shop. A stack filled with vittles Autumn had smoked in teriyaki from the rack of beef he'd bought for their anniversary. And then, there was the glass methamphetamine. Five pounds of it, cut and separated evenly into five ziplock bags. It's just chemistry Royce would always say, but the man was a Goddamn artist when it came to cooking. Terrence grabbed the manila clasp envelop, lined on the inside with plastic wrap, dropping the bags in and thumbing the metal prongs to a strong seal. He stuffed it inside the weatherproofed military surplus bag last, wedging it between his clothes as a stopgap in case the weather took a nasty turn. He dug into his pocket with one hand as he lifted at the bottom of the aluminum door with his other, pulling it up just enough so he could snake underneath the rubber and force it all the way up its metal rollers. Old habits died hard. Lucky Strikes were the only brand his father let him smoke. Maybe it was a ritual why he still bought them. It wasn't their clean finish. He lit up, licking tobacco up the tip of his tongue as he reached for his leather vest, pulling through it with his arms. His clothes sagged off his wiry frame. He had just been able to grow a full beard. Retreating back from the harsh bite of the sun, he grabbed the surplus bag by the scruff and slung it over his shoulder. The Club didn't believe in sissy bars. He'd have to lug it with him all the way. Once he stepped out of the garage, he pulled the door down and he reached for his sunglasses. The Fatboy was all fueled up and ready to go. "Hey." Opal spoke in a rasp that was hard to hear over his oxygen tank, air pumping and whining through the nasal cannula stuck his nostrils. He could've acted like he hadn't heard him, but turned to face him, cracking a plastic smile and faking interest. "What's up?" Pushing into his sixties, Opal Crews was ancient beyond his years, his body falling apart at the leathery seams. His jowls flapped as his hoarse voice tried to croak out of his throat. "You're doin' a good thing. Don't let that bitch mess with your head. The Club's your family." How dare he talk about her? Why the hell was he even going to do this? No matter what he did, Opal busted his chops about how, in his day, a scrawny kid like him would've been chewed up and spit out by the real cowboys. He hadn't done anything to him. Hell, Autumn hadn't either. But there he was, even as she was getting off her shift, greasy as could be and stark raving mad because he was too stupid to say no. "Yeah? Whatever." He couldn't see the bastard's grin through all his tufts of beard and missing banana yellow teeth. "Havin' second thoughts? You really wanna piss away your jacket? Could keep you a pledge for a lot longer." Bourbon soured the air as they ground noses. He was sick of backing down. No matter what came of this, at least he'd have his pride. "What the fuck's your problem?" Opal was intolerable whenever he hit the Maker's Mark. Even so, he'd lived this long by knowing when to pull back. "Nothing." Terrence's glower was too forceful for him, stepping back a pace and staring at his shoes. "Look, I know it might not seem smart, but you're driving a long stretch without a lot of places you can stop off. And Vulpe ain't a patient man." "So I'm talking with you?" "If you need a pick me up," he started, rapping his palm against the bag, "what you got is better than coffee. Keeps you sharp too." "So use product." Opal couldn't see the roll of his eyes underneath his sunglasses. "That's what you're telling me to do." "Everyone does from time to time." He would know. Crank was the only thing that got him out of bed in the morning. "I'm serious. He won't wait. So take a hit if you need to. Alright?" "I'm sure Royce would tell me the same thing." "He'd tell you too. Trust me." His handshake was worth as much as his word. "Yeah." He tongued his bottom lip, taking a long drag, finally backing down. "I'm sure." They didn't say anything more. No reason to. But from a safe distance, Opal kept staring, those beady little eyes and the strangest of smiles almost making him laugh if only they weren't so foreboding. Let the old bastard shut the door he thought. Maybe he'd die of heart attack in the process. Wouldn't a big loss for anyone. Cerberus were supposed to be the hounds of Hell, but he was too scared to let loose on the chains. Mortality had a bad habit of changing a man. But Terrence Kingsley was invincible. At least he felt that way as he kicked the stand back with his heel and revved up the engine of the Fatboy, burning rubber and barreling down the longest stretch of highway he'd ever known. The wind tossed his long locks wildly as he cut through the wind, gaining more and more speed as gravel slowly turned to pavement. Fred Joseph Kingsley was a man of courtesy in a den of snakes. Handshakes, amongst their kind, meant nothing. They talked over cigarettes, like men of their ilk should. He, Opal, and Royce Valley. Better to stab from the front than from behind. Valley was the only one he could trust, and it wasn't because he was a broad mountain of a man. He had hard times etched into his soul and his flesh. His cheeks were pockmarked, ravage by cystic acne. Weatherworn scars lined his face in indiscriminate patterns. He led as a follower, earned respect by being the stern, deep voice of reason. Unlike Opal, who, old as he was, wasn't cut from their cloth. Terrence liked Royce. Taught him how to look someone in the eye and talk to them like a man. Always made sure to tell him he had a good head on his shoulders. It's why, when they'd backed up the truck and pushed the tonneau cover out of way, he didn't have a problem taking the bricks stacked four deep in the bed of the truck into the basement of the Old Presbyterian Church. Jethro thought they were for more additions and projects, though he'd never seen them wrapped in shrink-wrap and brown paper. Terrence knew better. He'd heard the whispers. He said nothing, though. It wasn't as though his friend was bright enough to understand, as good as he was. The two of them climbed up the tailgate and worked on offloading the load, taking it down to the basement they'd hollowed out and cemented themselves. Rodney was no longer a ghost town. It had been a camp of transients for years. A husband and a wife who'd spent their lives running, who saw the generation following theirs without a compass. Most of who had stayed were now old enough and had left to be on their own. They had sunk their roots in Jefferson County, working jobs at the paper mill and the moving company and all the shops up and down Main Street. Just a few of them were left; he and Jethro and Autumn and a few others who came from the hobo camp in Dallas Fort Worth who hadn't taken to Kingsley transcendentalism. Or maybe the lessons had changed. It used to be about finding your own way. Now, it was about natural order. Skill and survival instinct to overcome hardship. A return to an era before government encroached on everything and warped it to their own designs. They kept unloading the truck, the men just out of earshot, talking business. His mother's eye bore dagger stares toward them, toward the filth they'd been unloading from the back of the truck. She wasn't strong enough to stand as the anchor of reason for her husband anymore. Her once auburn curls that framed her candy apple cheeks lioness were ramrod straight, thin and brittle to the touch. Ridges etched her forehead in constant stress lines. She'd been of drained color, her skin now a dull gray. She braced herself against the brick and mortar for balance, cursing the cancer rotting her away. Terrence wasn't blind. He knew the end was coming. He just didn't know how soon it would come. He'd made it through to the Caprock Escarpment, trying to beat the storm clouds and setting sun to squeeze out a few more miles. The rain had won. It belted his face like razor wire. Lightning lashing the towering faults on the distant horizon. The rumble of thunder jittered up his forearms. The deeper he got, the more blackened his periphery was. It was just as well; the white forests of scorched caliche on either side of the road was blinding The interstate was warped, water pooling in cracks and sudden depressions. The front and back tires of his bike were hydroplaning. He dared not touch the breaks. The storm reminded him of Isabel, the frothing spit and harsh gusts of wind threatening to turn his bike inside out. Luckily, there was a break in the distance; an old gas station with a Sinclair signpost, long abandoned from what he could tell. As he drew closer, he eased off the accelerator, making it easier for him to turn onto the chipped gravel and roll underneath the withered tarp canopy belting around in the wind. Kicking aside wooden crates, he dug inside the surplus bag for the flashlight. The particle board covering the windows and door were spray painted with red spanish words he couldn't suss out, dollar signs at their ends. The rain had soaked through the flannel. Soaked through everything. It just helped cool the sizzle of his sunburned cheeks and forehead. There had to be another way in. Swinging the pack over his shoulder, he brought his forearm up to cover his eyes from pelting rain, charging around the left flank of the building. Once at the back, he saw the backdoor, padlocked with a chain wrapped through the hole where the knob cylinder should've been. Readying his shoulder, he stepped back. He roared and it echoed through the distance until thunder cut it down. Shoulder met door. It didn't budge. Another two steps back. He gritted his teeth. Bit into his lip. He threw himself into the door. Nothing. His shoulder screeched in pain. Lightning flashed and blinded his eyes. Another few steps back and he dug in and charged and the pain shot through from his arm to his toes. Panting, he braced himself against the door, quaking and throwing his fists against until he drew his pistol and grabbed the chain and fired. Everything unraveled and the door eased open on its rusty hinges. He patted his shoulder as he slithered inside. The flashlight wasn't quick enough. His boots caught on something, forcing him to put his hands out to brace for the fall. Tthey skittered and sloshed through a mess of pulp. Newspaper so old and water saturated that nothing but the imprint of ink remained, the rest washed away over the years. He grunted and picked himself up, lighting the way as best he could. The guts of the gas station were like a time capsule cracked and exposed mere months after being buried in the ground. The boards were worn down, small patches of chipped green and white paint the only thing dulling their gray. Castro and Cornflake displays were pushed over. The metal rims of window pane doors for the cooler and freezer were all that remained, the motors inside ripped out. The stripped wires dangling down like frayed vines. The thunderclaps sounded just as violent and close as they'd had on the road. He found a dry corner, resting back and putting his hands on his thighs as the constant drip drip drip puttered from somewhere he wasn't sure of. Unlacing the surplus bag, he reached inside, grabbing a few lengths of jerky and sinking his teeth into them as he eased onto his backside. He tried to keep his mind occupied. You couldn't grow much in this Hell, which was why the rye caught him off guard. There was a crack in the middle of the tile floor, the soil underneath exposed. He picked himself up, collecting the flashlight and walking close toward the long ears, picking a few out and watching them fall apart as he rubbed them inside his palms. A clatter. Aluminum or metal or maybe just a broom handle, but he drew his gun and trained it toward the sound. He'd lived amongst rats. They'd been better than the snakes he'd had the misfortune of depending upon for most of his life. Maybe it was something bigger. No. It couldn't be. The door was locked shut, and it wasn't like the damn chain gave when he'd tried to knock the door off its brackets. Even though the storm didn't sound like it was ready to quit, he'd made it this far through it. He needed energy. His back was sore, his shoulder the same way after lugging the bag across two states. Just a taste. It wasn't wrong, was it? Opal had done it. Hell, he'd seen him snort his fair share from batches just to test if they were street ready. And he had parts of his hip sheared off from crashes and needed pills just to manage the day. Crank always gave him that boost. Maybe that's what he needed. Something to sharpen his instincts and barrel on through to New Mexico. Making it back to his corner, he holstered his gun inside the waist of his jeans, digging into the bag to pull out the canteen and the clasp envelop that was dry as it'd been when he buried it inside. He pushed the metal binds in, dumping one of the pound bags into his lap. He just focused in on the shards inside the plastic. Screwing the top off the canteen, he took a deep chug, brushing the runoff with his forearm as he undid the seal and reached inside for a piece. Yeah, Opal used. Most in Cerberus did. And they were missing teeth. Their skin was leathery and splotched with sores that bleed at a moment's notice. They beat women and shorted the coffers and it made everyone's take from gunrunning and protection smaller than it had any right to be. But it could get him back on the road, which was what mattered. Vulpe wasn't a man to keep waiting from the horror stories he'd heard. He put the shard back into the bag, zipping it up tight and clasping the envelop closed before forcing it to the bottom of the weatherproofing. All he had was the jerky and the water and the constant drip and the rats milling around for dusty scraps. He'd slept in worse places. The body of the Old Presbyterian Church was filled with propane light. The humidity was bad enough. The greasy soot burning through the lanterns stuck to his skin, and every movement just massaged it in deeper. He couldn't sleep. It was just one of those nights. No it wasn't. His father sat in the pews, his hands wrapped around the forend of his Winchester. His temple cut into the barrel's ridge. Terrence feigned sleep, peaking through narrow eyes toward his father propping himself against his shotgun. The weight of the world rested on those slouched shoulders. He pushed himself up by the stock, trembling hands looking for sanctuary, looking for shelter. There was none to be had. He didn't know why, but his father had never seemed so weak. So pathetic. So defeated. He'd talked about losing himself thousands of miles away years ago. That no matter what you could do, there was always savage in you. His wife was sick. They'd known it, but they didn't know how bad or how or how much it was going to take to stop the inevitable. It'd brought the Club deeper into their lives,those Hell Hounds frothing at the mouth for a chance to do business with a man so close to the sheriff. The basement was a testament to his love for his wife, but nobody, save he, was accepting of it. Money solved all problems. All weaknesses. He'd become a paradox. Nature had its way with all. When the time comes, the time comes, and everyone is washed away, making way for the new. His father never was one to accept his fate. He'd forgotten which leg was the one he'd injured in Vietnam. As he limped out the front door, he didn't know it'd be the last time he'd see his father alive. Just before Ruidoso, the land transformed from the rigors and boil of harsh Panhandle to lush ponderosa pines and effervescent green. It looked of postcards, of place he could only dream of seeing. The Fatboy traveled the faint hills licking up and down the expanse, two lane highway cutting through with only ski lodge signs and driveways to log cabins breaking nature's hold. The Sierra Blanca hovered out in the distance, diagonal waves of Lincoln National Forest resting below its prominence. Fog from the foot of the mountains hovered and salted the air. Otero and Lincoln Counties were fueled by gambling and skiing, all done by businesses that were tax exempt because they were run by the Apache. They targeted affluence and lived the shelled American dream, stacks of money cast with a chastising wave to what had happened to people three or four or five generations before any of them were born. The Christian God was fair. Opal hadn't been forthcoming about what he was looking for, but Royce was. There weren't street names to go by. Just the feel of the land. There was an old trading post that dealt essentials and permits to the mountain's summit. Driveways of dirt and chipped gravel swirled and disappeared behind the trees, their property lines fenced with concertina wire. The further he got, the thicker the snake heads lacing the top length of wire became. Piked on the barbs, their mouths hung open. Their eyes had been pecked out by the crows. Forest turned to clearing, the grass giving way to peat and cracked dull clay. He'd expected some kind of compound. There was just some small house, the white of the wood panel siding chipped in patches and a large garage with swaths of SUVs and cars parked around its swinging doors. Either Vulpe was a collector, or he kept a lot of company. He ambled the bike up the path to the house, killing the engine and kicking out the stand. His back and shoulder were as sore as they'd ever been. His inner thighs had chaffed from his saddle. At least the trip was over, for now. He walked the small flight of stairs before the porch and knocked on the door. No answer. He waited, reaching into his pocket for a pack of cigarettes that had finally dried out. The door shot open and a young face greeted him with a callous stare. He wasn't sure of the language, so he pointed to the patch of the three headed hell hound on the breast of his leather vest. Without a word, the young man shut the door. Terrence flicked his zippo and lit up, taking a drag as he peeled off the fingerless gloves on his hands. His fingers were bright red. Every time he tensed his digits, it felt like the skin would peel off. When the door opened again, there were three men standing behind one in a Hawaiian shirt, his spine curved and forcing him to a hunch. His hair was a long and gray, his rough skin shining. The ink from the tattoo on his left forearm was had bled together until the patch was indecipherable. He spoke with quiet confidence. "Opal'd said you were coming. Hadn't expected you so early. You make good time." "Thanks," Kingsley said, smoke oozing through his nostrils as he extended his hand. "I'm Terrence." He didn't extend his in kind. "I'm Vulpe." The stares and stone cold faces only tightened his tension. Struggling to swallow, he looked for a way out, for a way to end this with a clean getaway because the men behind him were at least twice his size and had shotguns. "I got what you need." A smile cracked the old man's tanned face. He wasn't sure if it was just for show. "I'd hope so." "We gonna do this in--" "The garage," he was quick to cut him off. "Don't ever do business where you sleep." As Vulpe brushed past him, he stopped, shoving a can of Comet against his chest. "There's a hose and a bucket out back. Just mix them up." He withdrew his hands, staring through his eyes. "The garage will be open when you're ready." Better to let them lead. He didn't want to show them his back. Butterflies soured stomach as he watched them make their way into the garage, unlocking the doors and disappearing inside. There wasn't a choice. He had follow. His father hadn't left them anyway to defend themselves, not that it would've made a stitch of difference. His mother knew. Knew it all too well. The law was coming. She didn't tell him that his father had run away, or that he'd tried to get her to leave them all behind. She never would. It only built up his hatreds. Made him think his father was even more of a coward for abandoning everybody when he finally realized what had happened. They hadn't asked for what was in their basement. His mother never wanted to be so desperately sick that it was a viable option. Or maybe Freddie would've chosen to do it anyway. A snake travels the path it wants to go. "You can leave." His mother rocked back and forth in her chair, her eyes staring toward the rising sun. "Everyone else has." He didn't respond, just clutched his shotgun tighter to his chest. They'd managed to get who they could out of Rodney. Promised to meet them when it was all over. Autumn wanted to stay, but he couldn't let her because of what they feared might happen in this storm. His father and the sheriff knew each other well. Whatever was coming couldn't be stopped. Were they going to fight for their survival? Would they have a choice? Rodney had seen conflict before. The Presbyterian Church they'd taken as their home still wore its scars more than a century later, the cannonball from a Union gunship stilled embedded in its edifice. The SUVs ambled down from the distance, their doors stickered with US Marshall tags. Five in all. A squad of police cars tailed them. This was going to be big. There were hundreds of thousands of dollars in the basement. They'd never seen such a production. "Put the gun away," she whispered, her head swimming in awe of their fury. But he wanted to fight. He knew why his father brought this to their door, and it had everything to do with her. To make up for the time they'd pushed off on her symptoms. She was going to die soon, but she didn't have to. Money made sure science had gone a long way in their life times. The officers parked their vehicles around the ironwork fencing, blocking any path in or out. They opened up their doors, stepping out in perfect harmony. They all wore heavy vests. One of the officers carried a breach. They were armed to the teeth. "Put it away!" One final fire crackled through the embers of what she'd become. A distant glimpse to woman she used to be. The one that had raised him. This wasn't their last stand. They'd be slaughtered. Her husband had chosen his path. They at least owed their son the chance to choose his. Struggling with shaking hands, she pushed herself out of her rocking chair, limping her way to the door and unlocking it. She opened up, raising her hands in surrender, getting out of their way as they stormed up the steps and flooded inside. He'd never forgot how naked he felt at that moment as they ripped apart their home, looking for the score that would bring newspaper headlines and the commendations and the fatal blow that would break the back of those Hell Hounds. But he felt something else as well. Disgust. Resentment. Helplessness. He watched his mother as slinked to pews, slouching inside and making sure to stay out of their way. It was the last time she ever walked. Six weeks later, she was gone. The bucket of water he carried sloshed about as he lugged it to the garage, the bleach foam spilling over its top as he walked. The ground inside wasn't paved. Workbenches combed the walls, tools and ash trays spread out thin. He took another drag as he stood in the doorway, watching Vulpe sit at his table under the glow of fluorescence. He stepped forward, setting the bucket down and walking to table, pulling out a chair to sit. The surplus bag rested against the table leg. They stared through each other, eye to eye. The old Lipan eased into the back of his chair, swinging his arm over and resting his elbow against its crook. "You look like your father." The comment came out of nowhere. "Opal told me who you are. Gave me your name." He locked his fingers together. Terrence wasn't going to do anything. He'd heard the spook stories about what that monster was capable of. A dying breed of bastard who was too weatherworn to care and too good to die. "You can speak up anytime, boy." What was there to say? Preservation overcame pride. "Ain't you supposed to ask me how I know your old man?" "How do you?" He went to speak, but he stopped, gathering himself. "Vietnam." One of the men grabbed the bag by the drawstring, opening it up and spilling everything on the ground. Vulpe waited for a reaction, those callous, soulless eyes boring through him. "I won't tell you what I did. But not a day goes by I'm not proud of what I did piloting those planes. They were savages. And savage people don't have rules. They don't stop. They just keep coming." After milling through his things, the man grabbed the manila envelop, taking it out of his periphery. "Your father," he chuckled, "those kids. He liked kids. But he wasn't smart about it. They used to line up. Like vultures. We'd give them candy." Terrence looked over his shoulder, to the men who pulled out the bags and started lining them up. He saw a pendulum scale through the part between them. "And he gave one of them a Hershey's bar, and, Christ above, the sounds that rat made trying to get to that chocolate?" His fist slammed into the table, forcing Kingsley's eyes back towards his. He scooted up the table with his elbows. "Not five minutes after, the kid reached underneath his rags and he pulled a pin and blew himself to fucking Hell, taking about five good honest men with him. And for what?" "Because he was told to." He pursed his lips, looking away. "Your old man was a Goddamn mechanic. You know that? He tell you he fought out there? He was just some kid. Drafted out. Probably too dumb to do much else." Every little jab was landing, and he knew it. He brought his eyes back in place. "Everything he was was lie. All the way down to that name he took as his own." He could've shied away. Shrunk in the moment. Instead, he dug in and stood up for himself. "I ain't my father and I don't answer for his sins." "Fair enough." He laughed. "But I'll be the judge of that." Time crept by slowly. All those trained eyes, all on him, waiting for just one little flinch to cut him down forever. Every part of him screamed with raw, nervous energy he barely could keep bottled up. If they were going to kill him, he rathered they got it over with instead of drawing it out. The men at the scale had to weigh the haul a second time. And then a third. And then a forth. One of them checked the plate. The other looked over the axis. Their eyes went back and forth, from scale to Vulpe to Terrence and on the fifth time, there just was no denying it. They put their guns on the workbench. Walking over slowly, the one who'd spilled the bag hunched over Vulpe, cupping his leader's ear. That fierce look drained of confidence, if for only a second. His eyes started having trouble finding the young man in front of him. After a few vacant blinks, he nodded his head. A razor thin smile tickled his lips. "Did you listen to Opal?" And now he knew why he was out here. Why he'd been sent hundreds of miles away to some old remorseless fox in the peerless forests of New Mexico. "I only listen when it makes sense to." "Good to know integrity ain't extinct." He pushed away from the table. His hand was the one extended now. "Your father was a good man. Maybe we ain't such a dying breed anymore." He took the hand, shaking it firm as men of the old guard were supposed to. "Maybe." Vulpe pointed toward the peat, toward the clothes and everything else that had been spilled out of the bag without care, motioning with his head. All three of them followed like the domesticated dogs they were, grabbing and folding and stacking his things neatly inside the sack. Terrence would live to regret it, but it was the start of a fruitful friendship.
