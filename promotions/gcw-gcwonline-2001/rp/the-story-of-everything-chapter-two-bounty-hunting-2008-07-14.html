The Story Of Everything, Chapter Two: Bounty Hunting

14 July 2008
Content noticeA period document, served as recovered; withheld details are marked. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2008.07.14 The confinement had started to break me, and I could tell that their intentions of my capture were far more devious than I had expected. They were going to interrogate me, and if they didn’t get the answers that they wanted, they’d torture me. It wasn’t at all like they played it in the movies. Empty cement room, exposed bulb dangling over a cheap folding table, good cop trying to level with you, bad cop pacing irately while inhaling a cigarette; no, it didn’t go down like that at all. In fact, you couldn’t smoke anywhere nowadays. About the only public place you can smoke anymore is prison. I’ve committed a minor felony or two just to have that luxury for a couple months. No, your run of the mill interrogation room is too much of a dumb, dramatic event to suffice in the real world. The criminal can expect the interrogation and be better prepared to defend himself. I knew that I had to be on guard at all times. If I let the wrong words slip out to answer the right questions, I’d be a dead man walking. Lorenzo Price, the slim, middle-aged, black guard mixed cream and sugar into his Styrofoam cup of coffee while his partner Jorge Zavala, a wider, younger, more Hispanic man, shuffled a deck of cards. Jorge had tattooed arms, and I could’ve sworn I had seen him on the other side of the bars during one of my smoking vacations. The penal system must have reformed him. “Hey boss,” Jorge called in a thick accent. “Should I deal in the detainee?” Lorenzo looked at me with the droopy eyes of wisdom worn. “Sure, go ahead,” he conceded. “A game of Fish is always better with three people.” “You in?” Jorge asked, directed right at me. I looked away and pretended I wasn’t paying attention. “It’s not like you got anything better to do.” The son of a bitch wouldn’t take no for an answer. I knew this was some tactic they were using to lull me into a false sense of security. I was going to up the ante. If I played my cards right, I could beat them at their own game. “So, what’s your name?” Lorenzo asked, tapping on the bars before sitting down in a folding chair within arms reach from the cell. Jorge dealt the cards. “Seven cards each, right boss?” Jorge asked. “Right,” Lorenzo confirmed, nodding for emphasis. “The name’s Johnny,” I dealt. Jorge looked up when he heard my voice. “You do any time at state” Maybe a few years ago?” “Here and there,” I responded, looking at my hand. It didn’t look good; just a bunch of random cards that didn’t seem to connect in any way whatsoever. “I thought I recognized you,” Jorge gloated with a tiny flickering of renewed penchant for the very mischief that had gotten him in trouble in the first place dancing across his eyes. “Lorenzo, check this out. They called this fool Garbage Bag Johnny cause he could make wine out of garbage bags. It used to be you give him anything you can find in your cell, and he can find a way to get you high from it. That’s why this fool made it out in one piece.” “Garbage Bag Johnny, huh?” Lorenzo buzzed lazily. “Even nicknames are getting too complicated nowadays.” I was busted. Jorge had rediscovered my craftiness as an adversary, and he had warned Lorenzo not to take me lightly. When the time came, they’d revert to more extreme methods of exerting pain on my body. “What were you locked up for anyway?” Jorge asked while ordering his cards. “Murder in the first degree,” I stated matter-of-factly. “Bullshit.” “I was only an accessory, and the cops knew that, but I wouldn’t talk, so they locked me away for a while.” “Can we just get on with the game while we have a chance to sit?” Lorenzo protested. “Fine, you have any threes?” Jorge asked to Lorenzo. “Go fish.” Lorenzo turned to me. “You got an eight?” I examined my cards, and I was never much good at bluffing. “Yeah.” “Hand it over,” Lorenzo said with an outstretched palm before confiscating my cards. I didn’t like the way this was going. The eight was perhaps the one card that unlocked the key to the meaning of the other cards. I was doomed for sure. I was had. “Jorge, you got a jack?” “Go fish, boss.” Now Jorge turned to me. “You got a three?” Wizened from the first round, I knew too much to let information flow so freely. “I refuse to answer that question.” “You have to, Johnny. It’s the rules.” “Fuck the rules,” I lashed. “I refuse to answer that question until I speak to my lawyer.” “Are you kidding me?” Lorenzo sighed. “I got my rights.” I had the figurative ace up my sleeve; or in my pocket, rather. “Furthermore, I have a note from my psychiatrist, Doctor Wayne Gunn, stating that I have an extreme anxiety of confined spaces, and by keeping me in here any longer you are putting me at serious medical risk.” I presented the folded up note from my pocket and handed it over to Lorenzo, who read through it, shaking his head. “This says that you have a fear of drowning,” he slapped the note against his palm. “And it’s written on the back of a flyer about a lost dog.” I could see it in their eyes. They were piecing things together. It was only a matter of time before they connected the lost dog flyer with the dizzily scribbled handwriting on the note with the deflated floatation balloons around my arms. “You can torture me all you like, but I’m not saying another god damned word until I can call my lawyer. Just keep it away from the balls, please. That’s all I ask.” Lorenzo and Jorge just looked at each other, exchanging some highly sophisticated expression I couldn’t decode. “Fine. Give this nut job his phone call,” Lorenzo broke, tossing his hand to the ground. Jorge tossed a portable phone through the bars, and I caught it after a frantic bobble. I tried to remember the number and dial. [detail withheld]. It almost had a certain symmetry to it; surely some kind of meaning. The line was ringing. “Wayne Gunn, attorney at law.” “Wayne, it’s Johnny, and I’m in trouble. I need you to come get me.” “Slow down, Johnny. You’re talking a million miles a minute.” “I’m in an airport holding cell. They’re fixing to torture me, and I need a quick and lethal dose of legal representation.” “I’m a bit busy, Johnny. Can’t this wait?” I could hear a woman’s voice telling Wayne to turn his cell phone off, and I could tell Wayne was being easily persuaded. “Is that another client?” I asked. “Uh, yeah, sure,” Wayne replied, distracted. “Can you give me fifteen minutes?” “Look,” I reasoned. “That’s fine. Just hurry it up.” I knew that Dr. Wayne Gunn, attorney at law, wasn’t much for keeping a tight schedule. Fifteen minutes meant at least three times as many. Time was of the essence, so it weighed heavily in my mind that I should do nothing to delay his arrival. “If it’s not too much trouble, can you refill my prescription for me before you come?” “Yeah, sure,” Wayne paused. “Which one?” “My pain medication.” “Ah, I believe I had you on White Nurse,” Wayne correctly ascertained. “I’ll be there in a little bit.” Wayne hung up the phone there, and I knew that I’d have to buy him quite a bit of time before he arrived. I could tell that Jorge and Lorenzo were getting closer to figuring things out. They had gotten enough out of me to figure out the rest. It’s easy to put together a puzzle a few pieces at a time. I needed to add more pieces; give them too much information. If they had to sift through all the pieces, it’d take them ages to find one it fits with. I’d overload them with information, given in a devilishly unordered fashion, and I’d start with the flyer about the lost dog. I whistled over to the guards. “You guys want to know about the lost dogs?” I began, ready to distract them with a long confession that would surely throw them off course in the grand scheme of things. “I’ll tell you all about them lost dogs!” *** I was wandering around some neighborhood, and I can’t recall why I was doing it, but I can’t say I didn’t do it frequently. On this particular occasion, I saw a sign posted on a telephone pole. It’s good practice to read signs posted on telephone poles, and I hear that ignoring any notification posted to a telephone pole would give you five years of bad luck. Some poor lady was offering a reward for the capture of her lost dog, and it couldn’t have been better timing. I was down to scraping out the dirt underneath my fingernails and smoking it. It was some kind of dachshund that responded to the name Charlie. He was what we in the business call a fish in a barrel. With those stubby legs and that long body between them, the dachshund was not much of a runner ? prone to swerving to deal with that awful, disproportioned torso. My knowledge of the alleyway layout helped me capture Charlie in no time. I negotiated the terms of the exchange with his owner, and through the wisest of tactics, walked away a man who was two hundred and fifty-four dollars richer. Naturally, I’d planned on investing half of it; split it even between hedge funds and bonds. I had more immediate plans for the other half. The problem was that I had overzealously spent it all in one night. If it was for sale, I bought it, and I had walked home with an unregistered firearm and enough junk to knock myself senseless into next week. I felt I deserved to kick back and relax a little bit, but I knew tomorrow would be another day. If I wanted to get ahead in this dog-eat-dog world, I was going to have to keep on the lookout for more runaway pets and collect the rewards before the dogcatchers and their fancy licenses got a hold of them. I could’ve been a dogcatcher. I had the skills, but I could never deal with all the regulations. There are just tactics that they won’t let you use that were deeply rooted tenets of my dog catching philosophy. They also won’t let you drive the little trucks without a valid driver’s license. But nuts to them. I didn’t want to deal with all of their red tape anyway. I had a surprising amount of success working freelance, and I only had two casualties before I had really grasped the most effective way to operate a crossbow. Now, one thing you’ve got to understand is that the media was out to get me all along. When word got out that a lone freelance dogcatcher was catching thrice as many dogs as the tax-funded department, and taxpayers were getting angry. The local government had to wipe the egg off of its face, and admitting defeat to an unidentified vigilante would weaken those suits to the point of crumbling. Careers would be ruined, lives destroyed. So they put their thumbs down on the local press. All publications are secretly sponsored by backers with certain agendas. Everybody knows that publications can’t fund themselves; not with all the flash and sizzle of instant pornography and sensational violence to compete with. I remember the day I met Cary Spivak. I had just gotten paid 2,500 bones for returning a French Poodle to an old woman, and even before I could turn around to leave her stoop, he startled me. “I didn’t mean to startle you, sir, but you’re that hero that rescues dogs, right?” He emphasized the word hero, and opened his mouth wide when he said it like he was trying to fit an entire hamburger in his mouth at once. “It’s my job. I don’t think doing my job qualifies me for a hero.” “My name’s Cary Spivak, and I am an editor at the Semi-Weekly Cornet.” “I’ve never heard of you. What are you selling?” I tried to brush past him, but he kept up briskly by my side like some sort of briskly walking side shadow. “No, no!” he said. “I’m not selling anything. I just wanted to do a piece on you for the next edition of the Cornet. You’re the talk of the town. People want to know who you are.” I thought it over. “I don’t think so.” “What?” he asked, exasperated. “Why the hell not?” “What if the dogs start to recognize me?” “That’s ridiculous.” “Not really. There are papers in free kiosks all over town. A dog gets his hands on one, reads the article and my career is over.” “You do know that dogs can’t read, right?” “Remind me which one of us is the dog bounty hunting expert again?” I asked rhetorically. “Dogs are a lot smarter than you think.” “You’re turning down a chance at fame. You’d be a celebrity. No more running around catching dogs. You’d be living the high life, doing commercials and autograph signings.” Something told me not to trust Cary Spivak, but I was starry-eyed, and the dangling carrot of fame swung too closely past my face too many times for me not to try and bite at it. “You’ll need a catchy name,” Cary thought aloud. “What’s your name, kid?” “Johnny.” “Not heroic enough,” he cut in quickly. “I’ve got it! We’ll call you Johnny The Dog Bounty Hunter.” “That’s the most awful thing I’ve ever heard,” I rebutted, but it was no use. The presses were already running, turning out freshly inked paper at incalculably rapid rates. From there on out, Cary spent the next three days by my side, going on hunts with me and experiencing the peaks and valleys of dog bounty hunting. I led him by one rule, and that rule is that if he were to come out on hunts with me, he would do so on my terms. He willingly agreed. I could tell that the lust for the hunt welled strong in his heart. He was wild eyed and frothing at the mouth, but perhaps that was just his reaction to my terms. Term number one was that we were to be junked up at all times while hunting. The last thing I needed was for some rabid beast to jump out from behind a trashcan and chew off some of my skin. If I’ve got to run home to my fridge for an emergency dose of rabies vaccine, I can’t have the searing tear of a hanging flap of skin slowing me down. Every second is crucial, and Cary, who I assumed hasn’t been infected with rabies quite nearly as many times as I have, wouldn’t make it a block if he was shredded up unless he was madder than a caged dragon on the junk. I have to admit, the first night was rather uneventful. Maybe I had caught all the dogs already. I could tell that Cary was getting itchy as we were walking down that empty alley, dragging dog bounty hunting sticks against the pavement. “I’m sorry, Cary. Dog bounty hunting is a very streaky thing. Sometimes I’ll catch three dogs in one night, and sometimes I’ll go three days without seeing a single fugitive canine.” “So you’re saying that we might not see a single slice of action until after the next edition of the Semi-Weekly Cornet” “I’m saying it’s likely. It’s no trade for the impatient.” He turned, suddenly, and grabbed me by the collar, a sense of earnestness in his eyes. “I need this story. I’m banking on it for the next edition!” “What do you want me to do” Start kidnapping dogs and holding them for ransom?” His eyes sparked up. He was reacting badly to the junk. I had seen this before, and I knew how to deal with it. As long as you went along with anything a junkie who has spun out of control wanted to do, the situation would not turn ugly. “That’s exactly what I want you to do.” We spent the next day looking for any dogs in unsupervised backyards or mutts leashed to signposts outside of shops. Through the course of 24 hours, we were able to corral 50 dogs into my apartment. They were all starting to get hungry, and some of them were howling. They were also freely shitting and pissing all over my floor, and the whole place was starting to reek of the palpable heat of the collective droppings. “We need to do something about this,” I said. “We need to spread some newspaper.” “I’ve got a case of the last edition of the Semi-Weekly Cornet in the backseat of my car. I can go get it,” Cary offered. “I’ll stay here and watch the dogs.” With that, Cary was off. I sat down and tried to get a handle on the situation, but it was to no avail. The dogs were growing restless, and it was only a matter of time before they banded together and took control of the apartment. Cary was taking his time. Minutes passed, and before long, I was considering going out after him. “Desert the dogs?” I reminded myself. “You’re crazy. They’ll have the locks changed and the doors and windows boarded up before -- ? my thoughts were interrupted by a knock at the door. “Cary?” I got up from the wall. “Is that you?” I opened the door, and I immediately realized I had been sold out. Animal Control rushed in like a SWAT team, weapons drawn as they filed past me on either side. When the line of specialists had completely infiltrated my apartment, I saw Cary Spivek standing there with his hands on his hips, ever so smugly. “You son of a bitch, Cary. You’re not going to get away with this,” I snarled. “I decided I’m going to run a different piece on this dramatic rescue on behalf of our local experts at Animal Control. There’s nothing that the people like to read more than a shining example of their tax dollars at work. It’s a double jackpot when a scumbag gets taken down in the process.” “I’ll go public, you fucking snake. I’ll tell everyone that you put me up to this!” “Who are they going to trust, a dog bounty hunter with something to hide or the most reputable source of news in the Tri-County area?” He had me beat, and he laughed to himself as he turned and walked away. The next day, the Semi-Weekly Cornet had published a piece praising the efforts of Animal Control. The story continued on page six and focused on me as a despicable perpetrator. The article also claimed that I had made an off-color remark to a Black Labrador that could be construed as fairly racist. “Slander!” I cried, and there I vowed to get revenge on Cary Spivek. *** “And then what happened?” asked Jorge, mesmerized by my story. Lorenzo didn’t seem to have cared much for it, and he had left the room. “What do you mean, “what happened?”” I inquired. “With you and Cary; did you get your revenge?” “No,” I admitted. “It must have slipped my mind. I guess I’m a terrible procrastinator.” I could tell that Jorge didn’t understand the lesson that story tried to teach. Lorenzo reemerged a few moments later with my lawyer, Doctor Wayne Gunn, following in tow. “There he is,” said Lorenzo, pointing an envelope at me. “You sure you can get him out of here, alright?” “I’ll manage,” nodded Wayne, who was attired in a sky blue jacket with matching pants. He was wearing these ridiculous sunglasses, and my face looked very distorted in them as my reflection grew in the lenses. “What the hell are you doing, Johnny?” Wayne spat. “You look like absolute shit.” “Thanks, Doc,” I said sarcastically. “You’re looking dapper as usual.” “I have no time for small talk, Johnny. You interrupted me in the middle of something important.” “More important to my constitutional rights of Habeus Corpus?” I argued. “I can leave right now, and I won’t feel the slightest bit bad about leaving you here.” “No, don’t do that!” I grabbed at him through the bars, but his slick, silky jacket slipped right through my fingers. “Alright, then you’ve got to calm down.” “Did you file all of the paperwork?” “What paperwork” I slipped the guard some cash, and he’s going to let you walk out of here and forget this whole thing ever happened. You can pay me back out of your first check.” “Shit!” I exclaimed as Lorenzo opened the bars and let me free. “I forgot all about that.” “What is it you do again, exactly?” “I don’t have time to explain. I’m going to be late.” I briskly walked out the door, and Wayne kept right behind me. I tried to lose him, but I had no clue where I was going, so I stopped and I let him catch up to me. “Wayne, do you know the way out of here?” “Christ, Johnny. Your breath stinks like shit.” “It’s a long story,” I said as Wayne led a safe distance ahead of me. “Speaking of which, I’m in some deep shit. An old girlfriend of mine approached me, and apparently, she just gave birth to my kid.” “When did you have a girlfriend?” “Do you remember that Eryn girl from a party about a year ago?” “The one who overdosed on heroin?” “Yeah,” I confirmed. “Her.” “Didn’t she die?” Wayne reasoned. “You know, from the heroin overdose?” “Yeah,” I said, not giving it much thought. “Anyway, you got the morphine?” Wayne jammed a paper bag into my gut, and I pocketed it. “Should I just add it to your tab?” Wayne asked, a hint of frustration present n his tone. “Sure, that’ll do just fine.” “You know, Johnny,” Wayne looked at me enigmatically as we had reached the elusive exit doors of the airport. “You’re my least favorite client.”
