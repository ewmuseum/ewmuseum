A young boy puts his piece on Cheap Labor.

20 November 2009
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: Nov. 20, 2009 Not long after signing with GCW, an acronym he still could not define, Cheap Labor got a telephone call from an unknown number. His cat-like curiosity urged him to answer it, and so he did. On the other line was a young sounding lad. The caller spoke with a heavy lisp, which automatically gave the impression that he also had terrible acne, liked to play Dungeons and Dragons, and had never had a girlfriend. (Only one of these unfortunate stereotypes was incorrect: the young lad didn’t like to play Dungeons and Dragons, he loved to.) As the answerer of the call and an ever-present optimist, Cheap Labor did not give even the slightest of second thoughts as to why he had gotten the phone call in the first place. Then again, the young lad -- he identified himself as Dana, whom momentarily made Labor question whether or not the young lisping adult on the other line was a man or a woman -- had a rather convincing story. “Hi, Mr. Labor…“ Referring to Cheap Labor as “Mithter Labor” was chuckle worthy. “…my name is Dana and I’m a writer on staff with Wrestle-zine. Did you have a minute?” If it meant exposure, and exposure meant endorsements, and endorsements meant money, Labor had all the time in the world. “Great! Thank you, Mr. Labor. Wrestle-zine is an independent wrestling magazine, admittedly a small-time operation, but we're trying to bust through the glass ceiling. I was wondering if I may ask you a few questions. We’d like you to be the subject of our “New Faces in New Places” monthly bio.” A smug impression crossed on Cheap Labor’s face. Why yes, Lisp-o-sauras, ask away. Labor sat himself down in a comfy chair and that’s where he sat for the next hour. The questions covered a rather extensive spectrum, ranging from Cheap Labor’s pre-wrestling life, tours with Just Wrestling, and his defection to GCW. Dana, like any good journalist, took extensive notes. He admitted to recording the conversation and hammered away at a keyboard the entire time. Before the interview kicked off, Labor asked a few questions of his own. “When will this be published, and where can I get a copy?” “It will be published in two weeks. Let me take down your address now, and we’ll send ten free copies of the finished magazine for your own personal use and enjoyment.” This statement was filled with half-truths and flat-out lies. The article would be submitted in two weeks for sure, because that was Dana’s deadline as proposed by his college professor. Dana’s assignment was to interview a minor local celebrity and to craft an article in the stylization of a biography. There only be one copy of the article created and that would be the one submitted for Dana’s grade. Cheap Labor, disillusioned by visions of fan mail and autograph signings, and with dollar signs dancing brightly in his eyes, never doubted the college-kid-posing-as-a-journalist’s authenticity for a nanosecond. Whether you call it killer instinct or just plain old common sense, Cheap Labor lacks it. For those concerned, young Dana would achieve a perfect score on his paper and would pass his journalism class with flying colors. He would later wrap his car around a telephone pole, which is bound to happen when we’re stuffing your face with a drive-through double cheeseburger while desperately trying to text a girl who has no interest in you whatsoever. Dana would be pried out of his car by the Jaws of Life. There would no visible way to differentiate between the smeared ketchup and blood loss from head trauma. The young lisper bit the “dutht” that day. Dana’s parents would eventually locate the address of Cheap Labor buried beneath a pile of video game cheat codes and roleplaying scenarios. Smartly connecting the dots, the article would randomly show up in Cheap Labor’s mailbox with the newspaper clipping of Dana’s obituary and a note explaining the entire situation. This is where we find Cheap Labor: in his front room evaluating all of the information. He reads through the enclosed documents with a creased brow and his breath choked in his throat. A few blank-minded minutes follow. And then came a fog of melancholy. He wasn’t saddened for the loss. His mind focused on the sponsorships that were never there and the royalties that he now knew were never coming. Being hoodwinked by a dead guy somehow hurt more; Labor felt similar to the Maitland’s, the folks terrorized by the rogue bio-exorcist Beetlejuice. But, hey, it wasn’t all bad… right? The article was well written and kind of flattering. When you’re as happy-go-lucky and clueless as Cheap Labor, you never get hung up on anything for too awful long. This was Dana’s article: ++++++++ TALK IS CHEAP AND SO IS HE. By Dana Svorek His birth name means “God Will Increase,” but it will never characterize the man as well as his alias does. He is as pure and naive of a human being as you’ll ever meet; it’s like his cognitive development ended long before he was able to cross the street on his own. It’s not cutesy in a Clyde Fox sort of way. Instead, it’s dismaying for others to watch unfold. He’s an educated person, but he’s not smart. When you speak to him, you will spend a majority of time during the conversation wondering if he is an idiot savant or simply the village idiot. He goes by Cheap Labor. He is blessed with the gift of gab and little else. Cheap Labor was born in raised in the lakeside town of St. Joseph, Michigan. He is an only child. His childhood was cluttered with one mother, two men he identified as “dad,” an abuse of alcohol by all the aforementioned folks, and unsettling lack of marriage or general continuity in his young life. Of all the embarrassing and unfortunate details of his life, Cheap Labor is least likely to speak on these subjects. He scored above average marks in school. He participated in no extracurricular activities and fit into no particular clique. He was always the awkward “funny guy” who was interesting for the brief moments you spoke with him, and then he was immediately forgettable. Throughout high school, Labor worked in myriad customer service jobs and rarely made above minimum wage. Despite this, Labor says he has always enjoyed working in customer service -- “people will genuinely respect and appreciate you if you provide them a good service.” You will find this to be a common theme: an exceedingly desperate search for respect, appreciation, and love. When you reduce these sentences down, there is one bottom line. Cheap Labor is an attention whore. When you’ve got a verbose personality and are desperate for attention, you are more or less pre-destined for a career in radio. Naturally, this is where Cheap Labor ended up. He became a minor celebrity when he began working with a local rock radio station, which happened to be the most popular in his hometown. He went onboard as an intern and was given an on-air name. The rest is history. As a radio intern, he was publically humiliated for swag. He would stand outside of restaurants and guess the individual weights of women who were clearly obese pounds and over-sensitive about it to begin with. He had to kiss carnival workers. One would argue that Labor’s first experience with wrestling actually was a result of working in radio. On the radio show (The Happy Hangover Morning Show), when interviewing mixed-martial arts fighters, Labor was put in a submission hold. Callers were asked to estimate how long before Labor tapped or passed out. The caller who guessed four seconds had won that day. Labor recalls the experience, “Being choked out wasn’t all together unpleasant.” Unsurprisingly, attention-seekers will almost always exhibit masochistic tendencies. Thanks to his radio station aura of publicity, Cheap Labor quickly met a girl who, he admits, was way out of his league. They quickly began scampering all over their hometown together. Within one week of dating, they were expecting their first child. Labor tells this story with laughter-veiled sadness. Trying to be a responsible parent-to-be, Labor approached the radio station management and asked if he may start receiving more rewards in the form of money and less rewards in the form of free movie tickets. He was immediately rejected and promptly removed from his position. He lost his limelight. Any attempt to receive further information about this period in Cheap Labor’s life was unsuccessful. “It was a murky time.” Odd, considering he was experiencing the miracle of child birth. Fast forward a handful of months. Cheap Labor enters Just Wrestling, much the chagrin of his wife and infant daughter, but to much fanfare from others. He starts strong and fizzles out quickly. He jokingly draws an analogy between this statement and his sex life. He guffaws briefly, gives a resigned sigh, and then quickly resumes on topic. One can only assume Labor’s charisma and masochism led him to wrestling. The question of “why wrestling?” was never appropriately clarified. In Just Wrestling, Cheap Labor was twice teased with the championship gold -- falling victim to the mostly unstoppable Violence Jack and then later to Aaron Nothings (whom he had beaten in a non-title match not long before). Labor spat venom while speaking on this account. He would also go into long, muttered tangents about random happenings during his short tenure with Just Wrestling. He met a man named Dave Goodenough and enlisted his services as an agent. They worked well together albeit briefly. As Labor’s performance began to slip, Goodenough bailed and left Labor with a bill. While he would never outright admit it, this is probably the sole reason behind Cheap Labor’s constant quest for cash. By himself, he is not a greedy individual. In Just Wrestling, because of associations with wrong people and a start of bad habits and myriad questionable decisions, Labor’s estranged wife and infant daughter left him and, even to this date, the family has not reunited. Disgusted and disheartened, Cheap Labor headed to GCW for a new scene and a new start. The general air of the interview was light despite the heavy matters discussed. The wrestler’s talkative nature is unquestionable. The conversation was peppered with awkward humor and copious amounts of sarcasm. One cannot help but love this person and worry about them at the same time. His boyish charm makes him magnetic to wicked and manipulative personalities. He is blinded by his own hopefulness and never recognizes a problem until it is too late. He has a short, irrational temper that is usually escalated into misdirected anger. It’s his lack of understanding on how the “real world” works that causes this to happen. Cheap Labor is a man who is desperate, but not destined, for success. What makes him so loveable is his ability to relate to blue-collar professionals who struggle just to survive. Granted, most people in Labor’s position don’t live with such affability. Perhaps Labor learned his life motto when he was 14 years old and working his first job at McDonald’s: keep a smile on. ++++++++++++++ The wrestler scanned the article and Dana’s obituary several times over the course of the next few hours. Despite the blunt edge of the entire piece, Labor was flattered to see his name in print and was happy just to be thought of. Consciously, Labor had begun to feel that there was now an even greater motivation to compete against Cade Carson. It wasn’t solely because of the Television Title being at stake; it was now also because of Dana. Cheap Labor would fight in his memory. … The memory of that dead, fat fucker lying to him. If Labor could, he would’ve punched the lisp right out of the college kid’s face. He would kill him again if he had the chance. But, alas, Cade Carson would have to do.
