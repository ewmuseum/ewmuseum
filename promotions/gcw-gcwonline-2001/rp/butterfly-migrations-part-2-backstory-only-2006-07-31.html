Butterfly Migrations Part 2 (Backstory only)

31 July 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 7/31/2006 (OOC: Posted only for Backstory and to set up the WW 51 RP.) Jessica Donnolley and her fianc?, Timothy Thompson, signed the papers that Percival Rottonbottom’s half-brother, Stanley Schwartz-Rottonbottom, had prepared. Stanley’s paperwork and research had been meticulous and completely accurate. The papers arranged for Jessica and Timothy to manage and run the Rotton Apple in Percival and Mary’s stead. They would earn a fair salary and a portion of any extra commissions. “Under new Management” signs went up at the Rotton Apple explaining the reasons for the change in management, and that Percival and Mary Rottonbottom were still the legal owners and operators of the inn. Jessica and Mary wept on each other’s shoulders for a good ten minutes after the paperwork was signed and finalized. Percival and Timothy went to the kitchen to prepare turkey sandwiches and tea for all of them. The two cousins were also the closest of confidantes, outside of the men in their lives. Mary’s family was extremely close-knit, and had been for many generations. The rest of the family was en route from Dublin to give her a proper farewell at Harry’s Pub in County Kerry. Scores of friends of the Mulvane clan, as Mary’s whole family was affectionately called in County Kerry, would all be there at the pub for a party that would last for hours... just like had been done in the end of 2001 when Mary first came over to the United States to wrestle in a global American league, under the stage name Katie O`Clare. The turkey sandwiches were halfway finished when the school bus dropped Denise Rottonbottom off from school. Mary’s niece by birth, Denise had been formally adopted by Mary and Percival shortly after their wedding; a few months earlier, Denise’s parents, John and Susan Mulvane, had been tragically killed in an automobile accident that left Denise hospitalized. Fortunately, she recovered, and was the flower girl at Mary and Percival’s wedding. Denise took the news and developments pretty hard, though not as hard as either Mary or Jessica. The prospect of living and going to school in America excited the eleven-year-old girl. The hard part, of course, was having to say goodbye to all her friends in Ireland, and of course, the rest of her family. “Sure and we shall see that ye receive the finest education the States have to offer,” Mary said to Denise as she stroked the child’s hair. “Even a Catholic school, should ye like to attend one... as I did.” Denise nodded and tried to be chipper. “I think I would like that, Mary,” she said, giving her adoptive mother a hug. Denise still called both of her adoptive parents by their names. Mary and Percival were insistent on it, as a sign of respect to Mary’s late brother and sister-in-law. Mary had always been very close to her brother, and in fact her son was named after him. John, Susan and Denise had been there when Mary got on one knee in front of Percival and proposed to him on Christmas night in 2002. Mary could never imagine herself replacing her dear brother as a parent for Denise, and so she would never let the child call her “Ma.” “You’ll need to contact your landlord in Vermont, Mary,” Percival said, returning from the kitchen with a few more turkey sandwiches and tea, which he and Timothy carried on trays. “Your apartment is only one-bedroom. With Denise and John, we’ll need two bedrooms at least; more likely, three.” “Sure and I think Mr. Yates can accommodate us,” Mary said, leafing through her contact booklet for his phone number. “More bedrooms means a higher rent payment, of course. But he was always very good to me, and I think he shall be with the different rent. We can count on him for a good deal.” Mary’s prediction was pretty accurate, as when she called Mr. Yates, he said one of the three-bedroom apartments he rented out had recently become available. It was in a different building than the one where Mary had lived previously; about four blocks further up Hickory Street in Burlington, and it was on the 14th floor of a 15-story building. He told her to come by her old apartment when she arrived in Burlington and he’d show her the three-bedroom one. Eager to meet Mary’s family, and to see her around Burlington again, Mr. Yates gave her a deal on the new rent that Mary doubted could be beat anywhere in the city. The turkey sandwiches and tea helped relax everyone and lighten the somber mood. Truthfully, the idyllic life of Mary and Percival, and indeed much of the Mulvane clan, had been shattered by these latest rule revisions by the U.S. Probationary Board. But they were all fighters, and they were all adaptable. They had to make the most of these changes in their lives. The next few hours were spent in meticulous planning. Jessica talked to the rest of the family, all of whom agreed to help pitch in with keeping the inn going. Percival spoke with Stanley again and with his half-brother’s help, lined up several contacts about potential work as a financial analyst’including several work-from-home opportunities. Mary called some of her old friends from her previous wrestling league. She’d need to visit some of them, as well as invite them over for coffee or even dinner, and catch up on current events. She wasn’t quite sure what she’d do to pass the time while required to live in America full-time, other than raising her family. Of course, she wasn’t expecting the next big “shocking news report” she received ten days later, on her next meeting with her probationary officer. “Come in, Mary,” Officer Burke said, waving her into his office. Mary walked in and shook hands with Officer Burke. She sat down in the comfortable chair that rested in front of his desk, as he pulled her case file from his cabinet and opened it in front of him. “All settled in your new apartment?” he asked conversationally. “Aye, and we are just about done moving me furniture over,” Mary said. “We still have to enroll Denise in a new school, and bring me motorcar over... sure and we will need to pay for sea transportation on a ship, just as I did to bring it over to Ireland two years ago.” Officer Burke nodded. “Please be sure to bring me a copy of the new registration and inspection stickers once you have them. Your car will need to be re-registered here in Vermont, and then it will need to be inspected again.” Mary gave a silent nod of acquiescence, and Officer Burke read her face quickly. “I’m not fond of the new regulations either, Mary,” he said sympathetically. “You’re one of several exemplary probate clients who could have easily been given special exemption from having to come back to America full-time. These new, tougher, no-exception policies are hurting a lot of good people, in more ways than one.” Mary tried her best to smile. “Aye, that they do,” she said thoughtfully. “But it could be worse.” “It’s worse,” Officer Burke replied quickly. Mary’s budding smile melted away an instant. “Sure and what do ye mean, Officer Burke?” she asked. “I also have to speak with your new employer soon as you have a new job here in America,” Officer Burke said, showing her some forms he’d been supplied with. “I have to request a monthly report from your immediate supervisor or manager, to compile data on your conduct at work.” Mary frowned. “Sure and I think I shall be busy enough just raising me children and seeing to Denise’s education,” she said. “Percival has some good opportunities to choose from... what would be bad about me simply being a stay-at-home Ma?” Officer Burke looked down at his desk and made a slight hissing exhale of his breath in a way that told Mary he was looking to phrase his answer delicately. “Choosing not to work is not a violation of probation, Mary,” he said honestly. “We can’t require you to show good employment conduct as long as you’re showing good overall conduct. We can’t make you hold a respectable job’or even any job’as long as you’re able to pay your bills on time and come to your meetings here with me regularly. The catch is, not working doesn’t look good on your record. You’ve got four years to go before you’re free to return home to Ireland, Mary... that’s a long time to stay at home helping your daughter with homework assignments, especially when the Probationary Board has special services available to probate clients with families. Staying at home while on probation can send the wrong message to Board administrators... even when that message is entirely unfounded and unjustified’it’s the pencil-pushing bureaucrats who balance work and family, and wave that balance under your nose, who make the hard decisions on your case file. If they’re not very happy with your continued good behavior, in the ways they think it should be... they can always refuse any motions to commute the rest of the sentence if you become eligible for said motions. If your sentence is commuted, you’d be free to return home again immediately... but they can refuse every request and force you to stay here for the entire reminder of the sentence; even the two years left on the suspended sentence AFTER your official probation is over. If they’re not very happy with your case file when it’s up for final review and closure, they can put a bad mark on it... and that can lead to revocation of your entry visa into the United States.” Mary gasped and put up a hand to cover her gaping mouth. “Surely ye don’t mean to say that they...” But Officer Burke’s expression wasn’t encouraging. “If you were an American citizen, or had permanent resident alien status, you’d probably have much more stability and freedom of choice regarding your future,” he said. “But as someone who only came here to work, and was then happy to return home to another country, they can see unemployment as a lifestyle change to that of a lazy slacker. They can see you as trying to make the government provide you with a living while you just count the days until you can get out of here again. Is that fair, for them to label you that way” Damn right it’s not. But can they do it?” Officer Burke drew in a slow breath and pushed it through his teeth in a whistling hiss. “Yes, I’m afraid they can. And if they do apply that label to you, they can turn around and say that your conduct was “satisfactory” to the point that you wouldn’t have to serve any further time, either on probation or in prison, for your legal infractions... but not acceptable enough that your case can be closed until the two years remaining after the end of probation, have passed... and inadequate toward proving that you’re fit to return to the country freely and pose no compromise to the general public. They can treat you the way they treat any illegal alien’by deporting you. All your friends here in America, with whom you have close relations’you’d never be able to come visit them here again. You’d never be able to take a vacation in any American city. If for any reason, your business in Ireland failed, you’d never be able to work in America again. You could even lose banking privileges in America, meaning any purchases you make over the internet from an American company would be subject to stiff customs tariffs and international currency-exchange fees.” It was another stiff kick in the pit of Mary’s stomach, before she’d even recovered from the pain of the first. She cried throughout the trip back to her apartment, and up the elevator, until she collapsed onto her sofa in bitter tears. The cushion she laid her head on, was soaked on one side by the time she finally cried herself to sleep... an uneasy, restless sleep that provided no comfort or relief from an onset of mental fatigue. Percival did his best to comfort and reassure his wife on his return home. He held her in his arms and rocked her slowly, much as a mother might rock their infant children to sleep. “I’ll bring you every newspaper I can get my hands on, so you can go through their classifieds,” he promised her. “I’ll give a copy of your r’sum” to all my contacts and my new employer.” Mary smiled sardonically despite her misery. “Sure and running an inn doesn’t require that ye be able to type as fast or as accurately as a professional secretary or receptionist,” she said. “It doesn’t require even a third of the general office skills that an office worker would need. I am but fair in me speed and accuracy in word processing, keeping ledgers, preparing faxes and order forms, and everything else that we had professional bookkeepers do for the Rotton Apple. What kind of job could I apply for after spending two years in Ireland as an innkeeper?” Almost as if in answer, Denise came running into the living room with a new friend she’d made in the building; a boy about a year older than she, with straight, short brown hair and a face full of freckles that made him look like a friendly puppy. They were playing with action figures of professional wrestlers; the slim figure in Denise’s hands was her favorite one of all... an action figure of Katie O`Clare; Mary’s stage identity during her days as a professional wrestler here in America, in the Super Athletes Wrestling Federation. Mary swallowed a stiff lump in her throat as she watched Denise and her new friend play with the action figures, and listened to Denise gleefully cry out, “Moonsault!” as she spun the figure of Katie O`Clare in a backward loop onto the action figure in her friend’s hands. “Jesus, Mary, Joseph,” Mary whispered nervously, reaching up with her fingers and crossing herself.
