The Key to Saving My Life, Chapter I

13 December 2011
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 12/13/11 Kati's blood was no longer fresh on my hands and, after 9/11, people seemed to stop taking notice. I stopped trying to scrub it away. With all due respect to Lady Macbeth, there isn't enough soap or hot water to get the stains out and scouring brushes are impotent at guilt. Guilt is a tattoo and, if you're lucky, it'll fade. It was the morning of 9/11 that faded my tattoo. Besides, in a world awash in blood, what's one life - give or take? Even my daughter Sandra started talking to me again, if but once a week and with a robotic detachment that seemed to hurt more than her year of silence ever did. In the wake of 9/11, all of us New Yorkers were just that for a little while; robots. With the pain of my divorce from Carmen, the dissolution of our relationship had barely registered. It took twenty years of secrets to destroy my first marriage and about twenty minutes to destroy my second. I began to wonder if there were odds at the High Spirits Casino on how quickly I could fuck up a third. I missed Ian, Carmen’s son by another man and, for a very brief time, mine in every other way that mattered. He was less than a year old when Carmen moved up to Montreal and so I doubt he misses me. At my age, on the other hand, you start to spend a lot of your time missing people: some of them living, some of them dead, and some of them just gone. Despite Carmen and I no longer being together, I decided to keep our firm, Sorkin & Mendez Investigations, open - at least until the end of our lease. I couldn't see putting Joe Rena and Brian Sully out on the streets because my marriage fell apart. It turned out to be one of those rare things done with the best intentions that paid unexpected dividends. Business boomed in the months following 9/11, as you might expect. But it was an odd kind of business. Other than our contract insurance work, we continued looking for people. That much hadn't changed. It was the nature of the people we were hired to find that had changed. Suddenly our clients, especially those forty and up, had an urgent need to reconnect with people who'd fallen through the cracks of their lives. We had a lot of jobs finding old flames and first loves, childhood friends, teammates and coaches, and black sheep relatives from the "other" side of the family. National tragedy has a way with fucking with people's minds. And in the days when Chicken Little screamed that the sky was falling, nobody seemed included to argue. But the sky didn't fall and, in terms of our once burgeoning caseload, the affection for reconnection inevitably did. But that was half-a-decade ago and our lease had long since expired. I closed the office and watched as Joe Rena and Brian Sully went on to open their own shop in downtown Manhattan. Nobody needs to pay to find someone anymore. Well, at least not old flames or high school heroes. People find them where they find everything else these days: Google. And if you can't find them, close your eyes and count to ten; they'll find you. My generation has always had mixed views on the power of the 'net. While some of them ate up the fact that they could see their grandchildren in real-time halfway across the country, others despised their sudden lack of anonymity. I always found myself straddling a fine line between the two. While the internet had inadvertently crippled my business, it also gave me promise that someone might one day seek me out and fill one of the many voids I seemed to have encompassing my life. "Mr. Sorkin, your 9:30 has just arrived," a voice called to me, breaking me out of my stupor. The voice was that of my assistant, Cassandra. After exiting the private investigations business, I needed a way to make ends meet. My best friend at the time had been a case of Coors and so I determined that a new partnership was in order. I opened up a small specialty store in Brooklyn and stocked micro-brews and imported beers from around the world. My attachment to Coors Light had made me realize that I really needed to branch out. In the year or so I had the store open; I had developed a bit of a cult following by a number of self-proclaimed connoisseurs. Cassandra was a young, naturally beautiful blonde who was quite blessed in a number of physical traits. I suppose she was less of an assistant to me as she was a shop-keeper and a bit of eye candy for my customers. While she wasn't much of a beer drinker herself, her days of doing promotional modeling had helped her develop a good understanding of the product. I refused to refer to her as just a cashier. She was a lot more than that. And, to be honest, I didn't know very many cashiers who banked $40k a year. It was a sickening amount of money to pay for a person who just rang up your customers, which is why Cassandra spent a great deal of her time setting up meetings with micro-breweries for me. She doubled as my sales rep. Or at least that's how I tried to justify her salary. "You can send him in, Cassandra." She set me up with one or two meetings a week to keep me on my feet. She knew that too much time alone would often result in me aimlessly staring out the window, indulging myself in a case of Sam Adams Utopias - a 50 proof beer that retailed for a $100 a bottle. It just wasn't good for business...or my health. But this meeting wasn't Cassandra's doing. It was Sandra's. In one of our routine weekly conversations, Sandra mentioned that she had run into a gentleman whom she thought I should meet. I don't remember what reason she gave or even if she gave one at all. I just had Cassandra take down the details from Sandra and tell me the date. Sandra wasn't particularly keen about Cassandra. I wasn't able to get Sandra to stop into my office to say hello to me, much less squeeze out of her what she disliked about my over-priced secretary. But I ultimately concluded it was a combination of Cassandra's age, her salary, or the idea that I might have been romantically interested in her. While I had no trouble admitting Cassandra's beauty, I wasn't naive enough to believe there was a shadow of a chance we'd sleep together. But then again, it could've also been the fact their names were so similar. Sandra. Ca-Sandra. Maybe it was just my way of subconsciously trying to fill my daughter's void with a girl that reminded me of her; a girl that still looked up to me. The door to my office swung open as Cassandra escorted my 9:30 inside. And suddenly, I felt as though I'd stuck my finger into an electrical socket. I was jolted; speechless. Numb. "Mr. Sorkin, this is Mister Bagwell," she said with the utmost innocence. "Please," he corrected, "just call me Chris." I forced a smile and extended my hand, "Adam Sorkin. I'm the guy who runs this joint." Chris shook my hand with conviction and throughout the entire duration of the handshake, I continued to worry whether or not he'd try and break it. Cassandra nodded, indicating that she was leaving the room. She closed the door behind her, leaving Chris and I alone completely. "Please, take a seat," I encouraged. Chris obliged and, based on his demeanor, led me to believe that he didn't quite know why he was sitting in my office. "So what brings you to Brooklyn, Chris?" He shrugged. "To be honest, I had ran into your daughter Sandra several weeks ago through some mutual friends and she insisted that I meet you." And then it hit me. "She said you'd have quite a bit to tell me," Chris went on. "But gave me no indication as to what that would be." I had heard many stories about Chris Bagwell over the past ten years. I had heard about how he manipulated a fellow co-worker into raping a woman that also worked with them. I had heard how he bullied a child with down syndrome. I had heard how he verbally assaulted representatives of the NAACP. I heard a lot of things that pinned Chris as a jerkoff - an arrogant, self-centered prick. But the man sitting before me was none of those things. The man sitting before me was the same guy I remember Kati telling me all about. He was the man Kati wanted her daughter to eventually marry. The man who I failed when I let Kati and her entire family get slaughtered. I knew why Sandra had sent Chris to meet me. She wanted me to tell him the truth; to end the years of heartache he must've suffered after Kati and her family all perished. "Chris," I struggled to spit out. "A little over ten years ago, my ex-wife and two daughters were brutally murdered." As I would've expected, he remained incredibly silent. He didn't really even need to speak - his eyes told enough of a story. "The reason you're here is because you need to know the truth." Chris leaned forward in his chair, eyes sparking interest. "What are you saying?" "Kati Paris...she was my wife," I said as I watched his eyes widen. I didn't need to finish my sentence as he knew where it was going. But I did, "Rebecca Paris...she was my daughter." Chris Bagwell slumped back in his chair, shaking his head. I could only imagine the questions he had for me. Or shit...how much he wanted to break my fucking neck at that very moment. But he didn't. He remained surprisingly still. "So then you know," he said in a near whisper. I sat puzzled. "Rebecca," he added. "is alive." I had spent more than a decade living in a pool of guilt. And in that very instant, I felt like I had been reborn. Rebecca was alive. My only connection to Kati was alive. And here was Chris Bagwell, sitting in front of me, holding the key to saving my life.
