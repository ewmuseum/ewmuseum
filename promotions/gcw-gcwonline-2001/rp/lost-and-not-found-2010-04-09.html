9 April 2010

Date: 04/09/10 It was hard to be happy about the arrival of spring when rain was coming down without restraint. Danny Boyd looked unhappy: twenty-one-years old, in a black dress shirt, standing outside of a church. The only protection from the rain was the roof overhang above him, and even then, the wind made it difficult to light his cigarette. Having burned through the rest of them in the late hours of last night, Danny was on his last one. The people he looked up to and respected told him that smoking was bad for his health: his doctor and one of his best friends, Travis Giles. He thought about that for a second and then felt something in his chest – a sort of unrelenting sadness that worked its way up into the back of his throat. All of a sudden, his eyes were burning and it took effort to hold back a sob; the kind of effort that came naturally, as if Frankie himself was standing there making sure he wouldn't cry. Don't cry over me, Frankie would have said. It's not worth it. Danny had once looked up to Frankie, an uncle who he had taken on as a surrogate father and had taken him on as a surrogate son. Frankie had assured Danny that he, Frankie, needed Danny as much as Danny needed him. Danny had just stepped out of the church that was hosting Frank's funeral: a funeral for a man who saw it fit to take his own life. Danny, perhaps too perceptive for his own good, had made the connection easily: he had betrayed Frank by not being there for him when he most desperately needed someone. If Frankie were older and on his death bed, not being there the whole way might be excusable. But, it didn't play out like that. It was avoidable. To Danny, that it could have been prevented was what made it tragic. And he wasn't alone in thinking that, but he was showing it more than any other. Everyone Frankie cared about told Danny that asking speculative questions wasn't going to do any good, especially to himself. But he couldn't help it. He couldn't help thinking about death – about how he had never lost someone so close to him, and now that he had, it felt like Death sort of just loomed over his shoulder, everywhere he went. He entertained the idea of catching a flight to LA with his friends, but he knew that there was no escape. He didn't have to be trained in psychology to know that he couldn't escape something that was in his own head. He took another drag from his smoke and looked at his watch. The funeral was starting in ten minutes and Giles still wasn't there. * Two hours earlier It was a long and tired day for Travis Giles. The kind of day that made him feel like he had accomplished a lot, but, the feeling was transient. It was also the kind of day that made him wonder why he got out of bed to do it all in the first place. And then, he wondered, what was the point in getting out of bed the day after that just so he could go through the whole process all over again? So he could feel like this, again? It was a grim prospect. He shook his head, bewildered. It was as if, by giving his head a toss, he was ridding himself of those sorts of questions. The existential kind. He had spent a long time convincing himself that the only life worth living was one where he wasn't plagued with that kind of doubt. The kind of doubt that nags, day after day, to the point where, every morning, you're asking yourself: what's point of getting out of bed? For Giles, the world awaited. But he was having a hard time believing that the world really cared about him. Or anyone else, for that matter. And he was probably right, but, it was no excuse to be sitting in a bar, drinking heavily, on the day of his former best friend's funeral. Or, maybe it was a good excuse, only not aligned with contemporary ideas about friendship ethics. As far as Giles was concerned, he thought he was sufficiently justified by the fact that he had done everything he could to help Frankie when he was still alive, thank you very much. He knocked back another shot of bourbon as a validation of his own excuse. Or, as a way for him to show everyone that even he didn't believe it. The bartender had already set another drink in front of Giles and it didn't take long for him to grab it. He raised it, as if for a toast, and to no one in particular, he slurred, "To Frankie!" He started emptying the vodka and soda into his throat as the other guys at the bar, including the bartender, looked at him in disbelief. Giles finished the drink and slammed it on the table with conviction. He looked satisfied with himself but the bartender wasn't. He walked over to Giles and put both of his hands on the table. He leaned over the bar, casually, and said, "Listen, Travis, don't you think you've had enough for one day?" Travis Giles' eyes wandered to the clock on the far side of the bar. It was just past one o'clock in the afternoon, but, to him, it may as well have been in the AM. "The night's jus' gettin' started," he said. "Gemme another one." The bartender shook his head and chuckled. "Listen, man, I think I'd better call you a cab. Let me have your keys." Giles looked at him, holding back a laugh. His expression was a little more sober, though, and it was tense. The other men in the bar looked on. Giles got up from his stool, trying to stand still. He reached into his pocket and produced a couple of bills. "I'm serious, Travis. Give me your keys." He had his hand out and opened; he meant business. Giles stood there in slight disbelief. Everyone stared at him; he stared at the bartender. He glanced around at the other faces in the bar and they looked down at their drinks, avoiding his hard eyes. "You guys too, huh?" His tone, like his eyes, was accusatory. They hadn't done anything wrong, but, Giles, for one of the few times in his life, was making wrongful judgements at a wrong time. They were trying to help, but for his money, he thought that anybody trying to instruct him was out of line. He managed to walk out of the bar without losing his footing. Keys in hand. * One hour until the funeral "Jessica, has your Dad called yet?" The girl being hollered at, Jessica, teenage daughter of Travis Giles, wasn't doing very much in her room. Already she had her black dress on, ready for her first funeral, and she was pacing around - a little restless but old enough not to make a fuss about it. She looked into the mirror over her dresser to fix her hair. The mirror wasn't a whole lot different than most teenage girls' mirrors, with pictures lining the border. Her friends, though, didn't have a whole corner dedicated to pictures that they had taken with their Mom and Dad. Jessica's did. "No, Mom." She sounded annoyed. "I told you that I'll tell you when he does, okay?" Jessica closed the door to her bedroom, shutting out whatever her Mom, who was getting ready in her room, might be saying to her. Jessica couldn't remember the last time that they had spent an entire morning in the house together, despite their closeness. Her Mom, an editor at a publishing company, was increasingly busy with work; Jessica, too, was turning into somewhat of a social butterfly, but only because her best friend, Kandace, was seemingly born into the role of the school's future cheerleading captain. In any case, the social demands of the early teens required a sacrifice of mother/daughter time. There was a chair in the corner of Jessica's room that was covered in stuffed animals. It was strange that a girl her age still had them, but she had never thought to move them. She wandered over to the chair and picked out one in particular: it was an old bear, the fuzzy brown kind, which was missing its eye. The button had probably been lost to an old washing machine, and it looked sort of ragged, but Jessica looked at it like she would an old friend. Her Dad had given it to her and she wasn't sure why she still kept it. Maybe it reminded her of him. The phone rang and Jessica set the bear down carefully, as if it might fall into pieces. She grabbed the phone. "Dad!" She sounded slightly unpleased. "We're waiting for you ... Mom's having a fit. You better be on your way." On the other end, Giles was having trouble with his words. "Listen, kiddo," he began, then cleared his throat, "Could you put her on the phone? Your mother." Jessica raised her eyebrow as if she recognized his tone. "What's wrong now, Dad." It sounded more like a statement than a question. "It's nothing, it's nothing. Just a little misunderstanding. Just get your mother. Please." "Alright ..." she moved toward the door and hesitated, then said, "You better be on your way." No response. Jessica got to her Mom's room and handed her the phone. Her Mom – Natalie – asked, "It's him?" Her daughter nodded in silence, then left the room without a word. "Strange child," Natalie muttered, then put the phone to her ear. "Travis, you better be calling me to tell me that you're on your way." She sounded stern – and it didn't sound pleasant coming from such a beautiful woman, the one that Giles once had and had been trying, for a long time, to make her want to be with him again. He was fortunate enough that they were still a part of each others' lives, though – linked by the daughter that they both loved. He wished he didn't have to tell her this. * Half-hour until the funeral The holding cell at a New York prison still had drunks in it from the night before; Giles was the first one of today. Fortunately for him, he was unable to get into his car and drive away from the bar; instead, he had a verbal altercation with some men who identified themselves as officers of the law. The rest is history. Giles was the first one they had hauled in that day, but last night's leftovers were still mingling. The stink in the holding cell was retched. Giles felt, and he was quite right, that this was the lowest he could possibly get. It went against everything he prided himself in – he looked down on people like this. And here he was: one of them. There was one sentient person in there besides Giles. He wasn't well-groomed, but he was sort of clean, and talked intelligently. "So, what'd you do to get thrown in here?" Giles was reluctant to respond; he preferred to just stare into space. "Nothing? I ain't surprised. These guys will throw you in here for something as little as looking like you're homeless." Giles looked at him – this social commentator who, through the vodka-induced haze, was sounding sort of intelligible. He looked sort of like Giles's boss – the commissioner of GCW – but Giles was sure that that, too, was a vodka-induced vision. Giles said, "I sorta got a little carried away this afternoon ..." The man leaned his head against the prison bars and laughed. "Nothing wrong with a little afternoon soiree. Let me guess – your wife left you?" Giles chuckled. "Nah, that was years ago. A friend of mine ..." he hesitated, then said, "Passed away." The man looked at him. "Sorry to hear that." They looked at each other momentarily. A security guard interrupted them. "Hey, you there! Travis Gill, is that the name?" Giles looked at him. "Well, it's Giles ..." "The fuck do I care? Today's your lucky day." The guard took out his key and unlocked the cell. "Instead of sitting here with this deadbeat, you get to leave with some nice lady who decided you were worth her time to come and get you. I wouldn't've guessed! An afternoon drunk like you having a girl like that!" He shook his head as he swung open the door. Giles didn't ask questions. Another guard let him out into the lobby, staring at him as if he was an escaped convict. Natalie was waiting for him – arms crossed. He felt a pang of guilt as her eyes cut into him. "Thank god you're here. How can I thank you?" Natalie could smell the alcohol – and whatever else was in the holding cell – emanating off of him. She didn't say anything as she walked outside. Giles followed. He guessed that he hadn't hit bottom, after all. * After a few times in and out of the church -- and after talking to many of the people attending -- Danny was outside for another cigarette. One of Frankie's old acquaintances had given it to him. He looked at his watch again. The funeral was going to start in a few minutes; he was glad for the turnout. Frankie had been liked, of course, but his popularity wasn't going to bring him back. The rain was more gentle, now, but there was no indication that it was about to stop. At last, Danny spotted a few more people coming toward the doors of the church. A mother and a daughter, who he recognized as Natalie and Jessica, rushed toward him with an umbrella. Not far behind was Giles, with jeans, a t-shirt, and unkempt hair. Danny opened the door for Jessica and Natalie. "Thanks for coming, girls," he said with as much of a smile as he could muster. "Frankie would have appreciated it." "I'm sorry, Danny. Is everything alright?" Danny nodded, then motioned them inside. He followed in after them, closing the door. Giles, trying to catch up, had to open it for himself.
