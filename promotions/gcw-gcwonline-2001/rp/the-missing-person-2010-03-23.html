The Missing Person

23 March 2010
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 03/23/10 They invented the internet, you know, but no one thought to tell Clemence Shipley. And if he still had the energy to be annoyed about this, he’d be annoyed about it. Because for a man who played out his days glued to the depressed cushion of his battered blue armchair, unfettered access to news reports and old-boy networks and historical documentaries and wartime propaganda and girl-on-girl pornography would have been a godsend. Day by day he sat in the small, fusty room at the back of the house his son had bought him. He lived by electric light, curtains pulled tightly shut over the big windows he insisted were draughty. He rarely changed his clothes, and if he had his way he would never have left his chair. Clemence had all he needed here; there was a battered black wireless within arm’s reach, whose dial he had jammed at the particular long-wave frequency that allowed him to pick up the BBC World Service, and there was a small stack of yellowing paperback books that he insisted he was working his way through, even though his eyes were no longer good enough for him to distinguish the letters from one another. He refused the indignity of large-print editions. But Clemence did have to leave his chair every once in a while, for his wife Maggie was healthier than he was, and still insisted on the propriety of meals at the dinner table and the companionable snoring of her husband by her side at night. In truth Maggie wasn’t too sure she liked the luxurious king-size bed her son had insisted on buying for their new house; she rather missed being pinned uncomfortably close to her husband’s hairy back, and the seething cold that would seep up through her feet when Clemence commandeered the quilt. Now in her dreams she invariably ended up alone, and, awakening before the first peek of dawn, would revel in secretly nuzzling her face against his red wrinkled neck, savouring each pulse as if it were the last. And the horrible knowledge that one day soon it would be kept Margery Shipley invested in the rejuvenating power of routine. She arose at seven o’clock, prepared a breakfast that her husband would only pick at, and washed and dressed herself before making the bed. At ten she ventured into the dense and overgrown garden, rain or shine, to refill the myriad feeding cages that lured the birds into putting on a wonderful show at the living room window, just for her. Before the morning was up she would complete any household chores, because only through freshly laundered clothes and a respectably clean house could she and her husband ensure the preservation of their impeccably crafted dignity. The afternoon was equally split between the kitchen and her own hobbies, the latter consisting primarily of needlework, though she always managed to find an hour here or there to watch the birds or maintain her collection of postage stamps that spanned four decades. Five o’clock was the arbitrary hour at which she topped her husband up with his daily banquet of vitamins, supplements and the fearsome yellow and red pills which the doctors prescribed -- she kept the ominous clear plastic bottle hidden behind her spice rack lest a nosy visitor cast a probing eye. And usually having failed to rouse Clemence from his chair for cold sandwiches or unseasoned salads at lunchtime, Maggie cleverly offset these unappetising prospects against the mouthwatering hot dinners that she took such pride in. Seven o’clock was the appointed hour at which Clemence should seat himself at the head of the needlessly long dinner table, and she was never a minute late in serving food from ornate silver platters, tender meats glistening in the candlelight, wholesome potatoes piled high and threatening to tumble. Clemence wasn’t hungry very often these days, which he suspected was a consequence of the heavy medication that for years had staved off the debilitating disease that was turning his red heart to grey. But even a man so wasted as he could not be oblivious to the joy in his wife’s eyes when the two of them enjoyed a long and hearty meal together. Margery Shipley cared little for the attitudes of feminists, and took defiant pleasure in the sight of her husband tearing through a succulent sirloin steak and not stopping until the plate was clean. Sometimes this took a toll on Clemence, but he was careful never to let his wife infer this from his demeanour, which invariably took on a jollity that defied his atrophying body, sometimes forced but at other times genuine. Once upon a time these dinners would pass with an expensive, dusty and unopened bottle of red wine sitting between them, doctor’s orders preventing Clemence from drinking it and guilt stalling Maggie’s impulse to do the same. But two Christmases ago Clemence had had a revelation when Maggie had opened a bottle for the first time in years and implored him with tears in her eyes to drink it with her, and now they discarded any instructions for self-preservation that would inhibit their enjoyment of the time they had left, and polished off a bottle together every night without fail. And Clemence was all the healthier for it, and no one was more surprised by this than Clemence himself. He celebrated by sitting in his chair, and listening to the wireless, and pretending to read his books; because it was his life, and he would live it how he wanted to, and nothing -- not even the intermittently mortal manifestations of heart disease -- was going to change that. It was the first morning of spring, Maggie would later recall, when their comfortable little life fell apart. She was tending to the flowerbed, gently pushing at the soil to allow the most ambitious of the daffodils their best chance at getting enough light to reveal their colours before the week was out, when she heard something between a yelp and a wail. The sound was pitiful, and it was coming from inside the house. Her heart momentarily forgot to pulse, and Maggie felt a little light-headed. A moment later her gloves were discarded as she urged her brittle frame to rush back to the house as best it could, fearing that some sort of disaster had befallen her husband of forty years. “Clemence?” she called anxiously into the gloomy corridor, striding through the kitchen as quickly as she could, paying no attention to the mud her faded old wellington boots distributed liberally about the tiles. The door to his study was open. “In here, dear!” she heard his voice croak, and Maggie ventured into the room, lit persistently if weakly by the dusty old 40W bulb fixed in the ceiling, to be confronted by an intruder. “I’m so sorry,” came a small voice, “the door was open, and...” Maggie stared, and stared. She knew this girl, she knew her very well. “Kate?” she questioned, as if asking herself if her mind had at last, after seventy determined years, started to play tricks on itself. “It’s me,” the girl smiled, and nodded her timid head, and after a moment stepped forward and gave Maggie the gentlest of hugs. Maggie knew it was rude to stare, but she just could not take her eyes off the huge bulge at the girl’s midriff. There were no two ways about it. Kate Barnard was pregnant. * * * She’d deny it, of course, with blazing eyes and an impeccably pressed suit. But the touring lifestyle was taking its toll on Jody Monroe. As acting booker, acting manager, acting promoter, acting accountant and -- when showtime came around -- acting actor for Just Wrestling, Jody had stepped into the breach by all accounts nothing short of admirably when Tim Shipley had deserted the young and vulnerable promotion. With neither a television deal nor a permanent roster, Just was in the risky position of spending big money to be a part of the Primetime Central network and all the trappings that came with it, all with guaranteed income barely stretching past minimal. Jody worked long, hard hours, but she didn’t mind that. That was her style. But there were some things about the job that she just couldn’t understand. “Uh, totally, like, what.” Roxie Sykes was somewhat sleepy when she answered the phone. The reason for this was that up until the first ring, she had been asleep. In the mornings, Roxie was invariably grumpy. It wasn’t the morning. She’d stumbled back into her Denver hotel at the same hour that the businessmen were leaving it, and Roxie figured they’d probably all been wondering which of them had tried to wriggle out of paying his prostitute the night before, because that (she wasn’t bothered about admitting) was how she might have looked. Right now was totally a different matter; she’d three times shooed the petite Filipina who had asked ever so politely if she could clean the room before at last falling into the deepest if not the most peaceful of sleeps. She vaguely remembered an erotic nightmare involving Garbage Bag Johnny and an industrial-size container of mustard. She shook her head to clear it, but it was very strong mustard. “Roxie? Did I wake you?” Jody heard the stifling of a yawn in progress. She didn’t have time for this. “I don’t have time for this.” “Oh, relax already, we went late last night. Jody, is that you?” “It’s three in the afternoon.” Roxie inspected the watch on her wrist. It was one of those expensive affairs that favour style over substance, so much so that you can only really read the time on the hour, but fortunately it was close enough for Roxie to affirm that it was indeed three in the afternoon. “Motherfuck. Lemme unpaint my face.” Jody waited with all the calm authority she could muster. Roxie couldn’t see it, being the other end of a telephone line, but Jody was chewing hard on the inside of her lip, and her nails had dug deep divots in her palms. She heard the running of a tap, and a few half-hearted splashes. “Yeah. Done it.” “Roxie, where’s Tim?” “Shipley? Uh. Hey, I’m not his keeper.” If she had to tell you the truth, Roxie really didn’t know where Tim Shipley had got to the previous night, but in all honesty that was because he’d been totally lame and practically knocked himself out at the show. On the half-bottle of Southern Comfort she’d insisted on Tim allowing her, Roxie had been buzzing as WorldWide went off the air, and she along with half the roster had headed into downtown Denver to sample the night. If she really sat down and thought about it, Roxie probably, definitely, maybe hadn’t seen Tim Shipley since he’d turned out the lights in the Pepsi Center locker room and lain down to rest his eyes. “I called his room. He’s not answering his cell either.” “Lemme check the room,” Roxie blurted. She was reluctant to do what she’d just suggested, but she was equally keen to get off the phone because there was every chance she might imminently need to puke. Besides, Tim might have aspirin. Over in the Albany office, Jody ran her hand under her shirt and picked absent-mindedly at the thick stubbly hairs that were sprouting beneath her belly button. It had been easily a month since she’d taken the time to pamper herself. She half-suspected she’d forgotten to shower that morning, but there was no way to tell. “Christ,” she said to herself and to no one at all. The office was a mess. Jody had concluded many months ago that the paperless office was a myth, but perhaps would have been wise to take more seriously the less fashionable story of the well-maintained filing cabinet, for she was almost literally swimming in paper. She’d been plunged into a nervous sweat only the day before when searching for the misplaced personal file of one Bruce “Violence Jack” Shanahan, who hadn’t been returning her calls; and selling out the final show of the tour depended on his assenting to a rematch with The Back Alley Brawler. And now that Impulse was pulling out of the tour citing a chronic back complaint, she needed to prove that Just Wrestling accepted no liability for recurring injuries, and yet the paperwork inexplicably eluded her once more. This was not vintage Jody Monroe. “Yeah, he isn’t in there,” came Roxie’s staccato reply, punctuated by occasional sounds that might just have qualified as miniature dry-heaves. “He didn’t answer?” “I checked the room, he isn’t there, he didn’t sleep there.” “You have his key?” “I have his key,” Roxie confirmed, shaking her head at how inconsiderate Jody was to so needlessly prolong a conversation that was clearly prolonging Roxie’s duress. “There anything else you want? Jody?” She lit up a cigarette, and when her hands shook, she nearly dropped it. “Anything else, hey, how about Chris Bagwell on my goddamn roster.” Jody closed her eyes, remembering the bewilderment she had felt the previous morning when an email had leapt out of the ether, Shipley inexplicably overriding the quiet coup she’d pulled off in signing the former GCW star. “What?” “Roxie, I think we have to talk about Tim,” Jody said resignedly. She really didn’t see any other way. Not anymore. “Huh? I’m totally not following you.” “You’re his manager, Roxie. Or, er, something. You’re with him, at least. You must know what’s going on with him.” “There’s nothing going on with him, Jody. There really isn’t. Now, look. I feel kinda green, well, to be quite honest I think I’m going to puke, Jody, so.” “I’ll call you back, alright? But I really need to know.” “Know what?” Jody took a deep breath, but it felt good to finally say out loud what she’d become increasingly certain of over the last several months. “I need to know why he’s become such an asshole.” * * * If Margery Shipley’s parents -- Mr and Mrs Eugene Stanton of Poole -- Dorset’s finest coastal town, thank you very much -- both dead now -- had taught her anything, it was that tea solved everything. Born as they were of the British imperial impulse, they had been firm believers in Progress, and so it had been with great cheer that the Stanton household had celebrated V-E Day on May 8, 1945, with the scrimped and saved tea leaves that rationing had denied them the regular pleasure of, all boiled together in a pot which for that brief and scalding moment held histories it would never know. Aged five, Maggie tasted for the first time that great bastion of Englishness, and when some forty years later her soon to be precociously intelligent son was born, all she could think of as she squeezed him out through her overstretched self was how much she was looking forward to sitting down with her husband and toasting the birth of their first and only child with a nice cup of tea. And so it was that her immediate reaction to the apparent materialisation of a proto-grandchild in the womb of her son’s longtime girlfriend was to brew a pot. She left the leaves in for longer than usual, leading them a merry dance in the hot water with the elongated spoon designed specifically for the purpose. She didn’t know whether this was because she thought they could do with a particularly strong brew, or because she was a little scared to return and confront the situation that had so rudely been thrust upon her. In truth this was an exposition of her cowardice, for despite Clemence being frail both physically and mentally it was he who had to make small talk with the puffy-eyed, red-cheeked girl who had nervously ventured into his study. He bade her take a seat, and she did so, not caring to notice the thick layer of dust that adorned the desk chair unnoticed in the unlit recesses of the room. “It’s lovely to see you today, Katherine,” Clemence began in his slow, measured half-voice, which for most (and especially his wife) was a continual effort not to tune out from. He chose it deliberately, partly to assist his brain in having the time to form complete sentences that would not descend into mere agglomerations of scattered words, and partly because he no longer believed that what he had to say truly deserved anyone’s full attention. “It is so lovely to see you.” His words were accompanied by a crinkled, dewy smile, and it was genuine. Clemence was besotted with the girl his son had fallen in love with, and had never doubted for a second that if he had been forty years younger he too would have fallen in love with her. She was polite, delicate, softly spoken. She was pretty, certainly. She was very pretty. Kate wondered if the old man could see how much her hands were trembling. She had accepted Maggie’s offer of tea (though in truth it had been hard to refuse) but didn’t believe she could hold the cup. It had taken all her nerve to come here today, to confront the parents of the man she had once loved. “Here, dear!” Maggie announced with an affected joviality that curdled Kate’s blood. She accepted the drink without a word and looked at the floor, lower lip caught pitifully between her teeth. “I’m so sorry to impose on the both of you like this,” Kate began in her pleasant East Coast lilt. Maggie smiled indulgently. “But of course, dear, but of course! Clemence and I were saying just the other day how we hadn’t seen much of you lately! Weren’t we, Clemence? Since Christmas, it must have been. Yes, it was. We haven’t seen you since Christmas, dear!” The last part was louder. Kate grinned emptily, knowing precisely what it was that was making Tim’s mother babble so effusively. Making her eyes flit briefly yet noticeably downward, scarcely daring to believe what they saw. Kate fixed her gaze instead on the vacant stare of his father. “We need to talk about Tim.” Clemence saw the haunted look that affixed itself to this most painful of declarations. He sensed the months of lonely reflection, of meals cooked and never eaten, of days and nights that merged into one another without any effort at structure or meaning. He saw the fear in her, not of his son, but of what had become of his son. And he saw that things were far more complicated than he could ever begin to understand. Maggie, however, saw none of this, and on hearing her son’s name, mentally validated the imagery that had thrust itself into her consciousness and danced before her, unwilling to compromise its limpet-like hold on her attention. And when she said it, it was with a squeal. “You’re having a child at last!” As she turned to face the woman she once thought would someday be her mother-in-law, Kate was more pained than she could remember. “It’s not his,” she muttered hoarsely. And a tear crept from the corner of her eye and cut its selfish track through the gently yielding silence.
