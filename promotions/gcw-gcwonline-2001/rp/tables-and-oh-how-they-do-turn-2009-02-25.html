Tables, And, Oh, How They Do Turn!

25 February 2009
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2-25-09 Tables, And, Oh, How They Do Turn! A Garbage Bag Production You can sit at a table, you can sit on a table, and you can even sit under a table. All are acceptable ways to sit in relation to a table. There was a man I had heard of once who supposedly sat upside-down on the bottom surface of a table with nothing to support him. The sheets said that he was able to defy gravity, and this was something I had to see with my own eyes to believe. I had my theories, many of them which concerned lengths of wire too thin to see from a distance, but I had tried reconstructing such an illusion with no great success. I tried several adhesives, none leaving me with any more answers, just glued and tattered pants, but never glued as to repair the tatters. It could have also been a series of strategically placed mirrors to make a man sitting upright on an upside-down table appear like he was seated upside-down on an upright table. There were a lot of things you could do with mirrors. That's why I didn't trust them; didn't even believe in the damned things. The whole idea of reflections filled me with ire. Reflections ain't nothing but vampires if you ask me. I hadn't seen anyone in days, then, including myself. It had to have been some bender. I woke up slumped over the table, an array of multishaped bottles filled with what I perceived to be uncorrelated amounts of liquid in them. It was some mess I was in, and there I was, staring eye to eye with a skull over crossbones so that in each eye there was another skull over crossbones and again and again with each eye within, all markered onto the white label of the foremost bottle. This was perhaps the deathblow. Just a few days prior I had arranged carriage by rail to observe the spectacle, or so I thought, that a man sitting upside-down, unpropped, on the bottom surface of a table would be. I had negotiated a discounted rate at the expense of boarding at an unmarked location between stations while the train was plodding nocturnally along its somnambulent course. The accommodations were hospitable enough, I suppose. I was in a convertible bulk carrier's hold filled with track ballast. I certainly could've done worse, but I had had quite enough of ballast already. It filled through the holes in my shoes running alongside the track, and now I was bathing in it like one bathes in shallow quicksand. "Ballast!" I cursed the inconvenience by its name. Then there was a shifting in the pallor of crushed stone, a figure emerged from the ballast and spat out dust. His oversized coat folded jagged at the sides, giving his torso a prickly appearance as it hung like dead fish over his hooked shoulders. He nearly startled me right out of the bulk carrier. "A fellow fiend of the ballast, eh?" he squinted an eye at me. "Come to answer the call of the Pink Lady, did you?" "Call of the Pink Lady?" I quizzically parroted. "I'm not sure I follow you." "You know," he demonstrated, shoveling a palm of the ballast into his mouth. He talked while he chewed, and I couldn't tell his sparse cracks of teeth from granite. "Go on. Give it a chew." "I should really just stick to the plan; see the guy and his table, you know?" I assessed. "I'm on a strict schedule with no margin for distractions." "Give it a chew!" he demanded, hostilely hoisting a hand of granite at me. I felt I had no choice at this point, and I pinched some of the coarse shards and unenthusiastically placed them in my mouth. It wasn't so much the taste as it was the texture that I deemed the source of my displeasure, and he talked at me as I worked at the ballast with my molars. "Put some more jaw into it!" he encouraged, wheezing with laughter that sent craggy crumbs spitting through air. "That's the Grade A stuff right there." I nodded as I chewed to humor him as he pointed down at the bulk. He was chewing, too, and smiling really wide with his shut mouth inflated. His cheeks had grown stretchy from chewing, and it looked like he could fit a discus in there; a regulation sized discus. "So what, am I supposed to swallow this junk?" I asked dentally. "Just keep chewin'," he nodded, tapping on his bony knee with his even bonier fingers. He was all mouth and bone like some sort of yelling skeleton over the rush of the train. So I kept chewing and tried not to look at the disfigure that was his mouth as if puffed on some invisible trumpet. I could hear it playing the devil's music; some wild jazz that leaves the suggestive mind more prone to its own deviance. I could feel it. It was kicking in. "What does this have to do with tables?" I stood up. "This doesn't have anything to do with tables!" I squeezed angry words through masticated stones, and blasted around through the ballast, hulking and sulking and grasping at tablular illusions like they were straws. The thirst overwhelmed me. I wondered if I could juice the ballast, but I had no glass, and furthermore, no surface to set it upon; the ultimate clause heavily factoring into my decisions. All the while jazz danced with the roar of the train, stabbing at me with brassy flourishes where fingers once bony now flapped boneless up and down disorderly valves. There was no construction to any of it! Then it would swell, and the noise grew so loud that at some point, there is no jazz and there is no trumpet. It's all just a singular element of sound with no characteristics guiding its existence. It's always that singular element, and we always hear it as loud as it always is. It's that we only perceive the changes, and it only changes when we manipulate it, and depending on how strongly we manipulate it, and in what ways, the more of it draws our attention. The more attention we pay to it, the less attention we can pay to everything else. I was paying attention to it all, and perhaps that was the mystique of Pink Lady. I could no longer taste the stones in my mouth or feel the near insurmountable mound of Lady underneath me. It went on for hours, it seemed, but what meaning did time have when I was underneath the spell of the Lady? And what of the tables? "The fuck?" I rolled from my stomach to my back. It was day, and I was confused. Had I missed my stop? I wiped dust from my eyes, but I couldn't tell if it was from the track ballast, the sleep or the combination of both. "Hey, Carl! Get over here! We got ourselves a stowaway!" I looked up, and readjusting to light, the figure of a uniformed man settled into focus. There was nothing to be said of the other passenger. "That sneaky son of a bitch!" I snarled. "He must've sunk back into the ballast!" The uniformed man backed off a bit at my arousal. He appeared unarmed, save for a radio that cracked and fizzled with use. "What are you fixing to do to me?" I asked. "Please stay calm, sir. We're going to have to kick you off this train, but it'll go a whole lot easier on both of us if you just comply." I had a feeling he was only telling half the story. You could see his nerves, and nerves don't lie like words do. I had to cut a deal, and I had to do it before Carl arrived, or I'd be outnumbered. I wasn't a big fan of numbers to begin with, and those were nothing compared with outnumbers. "I'm not trying to stir up any trouble. I'm just looking for a guy who sits on tables upside-down." "Well, we don't have anything like that on this train, sir," he regretted to inform me. "If you just leave peacefully, we won't have to get the police involved." "How do I know you won't just follow me?" "We're on a track," he pointed. "You'll know which way we're going." "I want to see it in writing." "Come on. I don't have time for this." He was getting impatient. "Are you going to exit the train, or am I going to have to get the cops out here? This is the last time I'm going to warn you." "Everything alright up there, Max?" It must have been Carl! He climbed the ladder up the side of the bulk carrier, and soon I could see him. There was something about him, but I couldn't put my finger on it. "This guy's not cooperating," Max took off his hat and scratched his head. "I think we're going to have to call in." "Nonsense, Max. We don't want to do that," Carl stretched his arrogance. "Let me have a crack at him." They had tried reason, and that having failed, must have been left with torture as the only viable option. Carl had the look of a professional torturer in that he looked like he would act professionally while torturing. I knew myself too well to know that I would talk, but I had no idea what I would say. "Wait!" I called out as Carl approached. "Wait. There's another stowaway on the train. He's dangerous. I'll tell you where he is, but you've got to let me leave here unimpeded at once." "I don't care," grunted approaching Carl. "I just want to get you off this train so we can get moving. We're on a schedule." "He's in the ballast!" I yelled, but it was too late. Carl hoisted me under the arms, perhaps even by my own petard, and he carried me to the edge of the carrier. "He's burrowed deep in the ballast! Check it! Check it!" It wasn't long to the ground, but it was hard, and the fall knocked the wind out of me. I heard Max ask Carl if he was allowed to do that, but I didn't hear Carl say anything. A few moments passed as I regained my bearings, and the train was building off down the track again. Then it went from building to bolting until it had passed, but the other side of the empty tracks wasn't empty at all. There he was again, that actor of inconveniencing disappearances! "Why I ought to stab at you with this bottle opener," I fumed, displaying said opener as proof. "You left me out to dry back there." "I know," he grinned. "But I had to test you." "Test me?" "Yep, you'll understand." He shifted his weight. "Now why don't we go on and see about that table." We were off without transition. We had no need for one. What did it matter anymore, anyway? He looked like a spectre of a bespectacled spectator as we neared the crowd, and he had such a holoform about him that I refused to inquire his name out of fear that he had never been given one. His eyes were perked, not on the stage afar, but rather the gathered crowd before it. "Now pay attention to what you're watching," he directed. They milled about, wearing drab hats and jackets, and occassionally, they would cross paths and amble by with awkward chatter. "I think he uses magnets," one voice rose above the crowd. Then other voices joined, and soon the voices wove around each other like a quilt of sea. The waves would crash, and something coherent would slip through once and again. "Yeah, he had his tailbone surgically altered. It's like he's got a little screw on the end." I realized there that I may not have been watching a man sit on the underside of a table. Perhaps I was there to watch a crowd watch a man not sit on the underside of a table as they watched instead for loose seams in a taut illusion. "You got it the other way around. His father was a man and his mother was a table. How's a woman going to birth a half-man, half-table, you schlamiel?" Maybe there was no man, and maybe there was no table. Maybe the whole spectacle of the thing was watching a bunch of men in drab hats and jackets try to come up with the most cockamamie story to explain the mechanics of such a stunt. Then, on the stage, a man walked out with neither pomp nor circumstance, carrying a table. He slammed the table down, and the audience hushed and turned. Finally, he sat upside-down on the underside of the table. No other words could explain it. That's what he did, just as you could have imagined it so as it wasn't worth description. He dismounted, picked up the table and left to silence. "They're disappointed," I noticed. "I think they wanted to see him fail." "They're only disappointed in themselves," he offered. "How so?" "They didn't think it could be done, and now that they see it was done, they won't find it amazing if it is done again. Thus, it must not be amazing that it was done." "It was alright," I admitted. "I don't think I'd go through all the trouble to see it again." "Don't think I blame you." We parted ways, and I haven't so much as thought about seeing another man sit on the underside of another table. I could barely stand the table I had. I waited for the next train back, so I could poison away this failed endeavor from my memory and start anew.
