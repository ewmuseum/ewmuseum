Hero

12 August 2011

Date: 08/12/11 No matter how much he drank, Terrence thought he couldn't get drunk anymore. Sheets of sweat bled together the checker pattern of his button down cotton shirt. Biting the filter off his cigarette, he spit it out as best as he could with his mouth as dry as it was. He lit it up as he stooped to his knees, brushing burning salt from his forehead and his eyes with his forearm. He took a drag. He readied his bolt cutters. Swells of flatsedge wrapped along and underneath the chain link fence, their whorls crumbling and their stalks bowed from weeks of drought. They scratched at his calves and ankles so badly he rolled the legs of his jeans down. The bed of bermuda underneath had turned brown, the soil pluming to dust underneath his footfalls. He remembered when the fence used to be only about seven feet tall. Those were the good old days. Morton's Bottling Company got sick of the break ins, so they strung concertina wire at the top. A piece of old carpet could get past that, though, as long as its padding hadn't worn to the thread. Eventually, they sloped the wire outward. And when that wasn't enough, they layered it inward as well. Nothing they did worked. They finally caved in, laying down a brand new fence more than twice the height of the original. He snipped the steel twirls from the wire base of the bottom, following from post to post. When he was finished, he grabbed the length of chain link and pulled up, running the cutters through to the other side. Next came the fire extinguisher. He wound a length of rubber hose around his knuckles and shimmied underneath flat on his stomach with his elbows. Dirt clung to his sweat. Masked his cheeks. Became one with him. Gathering his things on the other side, he made his way through the property with confidence. The overhead lights screamed fluorescence, moth thickets batting each other for a taste in the August night. Unwinding the hose, he brushed along the Coca-Cola trailer on the lot, spiking his cigarette off the concrete as he hugged along the left flank of the building. The tanks were just around the corner, big and bright white, jutting out lengthwise from the building, resting on a bed of sod. He laid the extinguisher on its side, sliding the coupling over the valve of the source tank and loosening the valve wheel. Slouching over, he rested his back against the concrete base which kept the tank off the ground. He dug into his shirt pocket, grabbing his flask and unscrewing the cap, taking a deep swig of whiskey. It burned down his throat as pressurized anhydrous ammonia droned from tank to tank. Another gulp. He couldn't let the process go for too long or else it would overflow and oxidize. Or maybe he could. Maybe he could pull on the hose until the rubber broke from the copper coupling so he could take a full frost to the face. At least then, he'd be able to sleep at night. He'd done this dozens of times before, to the point where he could do it on autopilot. As he screwed the valve wheel shut on both the tank and then the extinguisher, he got up and grabbed the handle. His head bent and his vision on the ground, he pulled another cigarette from his pocket and bit off the filter. When his head rose as he swung the tank over his shoulder, he caught blurry sight of a pair of troopers just a few paces in front of him. Their flashlights blinding his eyes. He drew his forearm over his eyes. Their belts carried pistols. They weren't much to look at, and he could fight them. A wild smirk crossed his lips. Slumping the fire extinguisher to the ground, his hand pawed at the grip to stabilize his wobbly legs. And then he laughed. Wide eyed and unrepentant. His hands clasped on his head, the fingers intertwining and the heels of his hands pressing against the contours of his scalp. He laughed until his lungs were sore. Their faces soured the moment drew near. It'd take near an hour before they could smell anything else but the stench of cheap booze sweats and fermenting urine. Another hot night in Edgar's. It was a small edifice, one that stood out against the brick and mortar of Main Street with its own yard and white plywood siding. Those in town took to calling it The White House. You couldn't find a better omelet in all of western Mississippi. It never closed. It was well past three, and no matter what Autumn tried, she just couldn't keep herself cool. The place never had good ventilation, even when the windows were cracked open. The last hour and a half, she'd only had one customer, and all he did was nurse his cups of coffee because they were free. She tried not to stare at him, his thin body and sun beaten skin comically contrasted by a bleached beard that sank below his chest. Nicotine stains slashed through the tufts around his lips. He said drove a rig, that he needed a place to rest his bones, that the heat wasn't too bad. Then again, he was the one dozing off in his stool, situated as close as possible to the oscillating fan. The red, white, and blue tassels tied from the grill licked at the back of his ragged linen shirt. A summer spent hunched over the griddle had melted away any baby fat she'd had left. Sweat and mascara poured down her burning candy apple cheeks, her brow furrowing as she squirted water across the griddle's surface from her mustard bottle. She locked her wrists as she raked her metal scrapper up and down and across the surface until it was clean. Her jeans and black button down were covered in vegetable oil, batter, and grease. Her bandanna needed to be wrung of sweat, as did the ace bandage wound around her right forearm. Her other arm had small blotches of pink. And those were just from oil splashes. Money was money. She had to work through it. It became harder to force smiles, to seem genuine when you knew the man you were waiting on didn't tip. She wasn't going to make much tonight. At least she could scrap together some food on their dime. Another look at the clock. There was still a good three hours left on her shift. There was nothing left to do. She'd cleaned the bathroom in the backlot. Brewed fresh coffee. Mopped the floors. Washed the windows, then the tables. She hoped the bar crowd had called it a night. She was too exhausted to deal with any of their catcalls. Bunching up her wash cloth, she threw it over her shoulder and walked over to the far end of the hard wood bar, rapping her knuckles against the varnish. The man drew his chin from his chest, wiping pooled sweat away from his spectral eyes with his bony fingers. “Going outside for a minute.” He nodded his head, folded his arms across his chest as he drifted back to sleep. She made her way toward the front door, briefly stopping near the register to pop the tray open so she could grab her cigarettes and then lock it down with her key. Stagnant air washed over her in chilled gushes. Outside, it must have dropped a good ten to fifteen degrees. Reaching into the pocket of her jeans, she pulled out her cardboard pack of menthols, thumbing the lid up. She pulled one out, touching it to her lips before striking it live with her matches. The first drag made her cough. It always made her cough. So did the second. And the third. In truth, she didn't know why she smoked. Whenever she did, she felt a sick, sloshing lethargy in her veins, followed by headaches and sweats that felt filtered through charcoal. It was a stupid childish habit, one that Terrence had given her. She didn't like to think about how wild he'd become since he'd come home. How he'd come back to the trailer every night, liquor on his breath belting her in the face. And that was only if he came home. There was a pension from the Army. A little cushion. Things were supposed to be different now. But there she was, cobbling together whatever money she could scratch between part-time jobs just to keep the bills and the mortgage paid. Her and her alone. Not even half way through her cigarette, she pitched it to sidewalk, pressing it out under her heel. She watched Fayette's Main Street absently, waiting for traffic that never came. When she heard the ring from the rotary phone inside, she turned around. Most folks didn't have the number to Edgar's. Had to be Isabella. She had a mean habit of calling in on short notice because she couldn't be bothered to pull herself out of bed. That meant another eight hour shift on top of the one she was working. Weary and barely hanging on a thread, she couldn't handle that. Pushing through the door, she quickly slapped up the wood separator to behind the bar, maneuvering behind and picking up the phone. “Edgar's. This is Autumn.” The second she heard the breathing on the other end, she knew who it was. “I'm in some trouble. Need you to pick me up.” Terrence's voice was rough. Ragged. Distant. She couldn't keep disgust from permeating her voice. “Are you kidding me?” His laugh was a sick, pathetic thing. “I...” There was a long pause, filled with groans from deep in his stomach. “County and I'm...you're still in town. Just need some bail, like ten thou--” With how hard she slammed the phone, it was shocking she hadn't broken the receiver clear in two. He's falling. All the time, falling. But he can't move around in the tunnel he's trapped in. That he can't touch. Can't feel. It's a practiced path. It's what's scariest of all. Peeling potatoes. Scrubbing them with hardwire brushes until their skins are peeled away. His fingers pruning, the muscles in his hands taut for too long. They swell. Burning sensations undulate through his wrists. His forearms. His biceps. His shoulders. It sear toward tip of his spine, then through it completely. The water in his bucket is a dirt brine. And just when he thought he was through, another box of potatoes, overflowing at the brim, was slapped onto his table. Standing in the same place, his soles throbbing inside his leather boots. They were a full size too small. He'd been given them on purpose. There was no scream, no shout from he who plays panopticon. His felt hat shadows his face, save the whistle between his lips. Smoking a pack of cigarettes as Arab spring turns violent. Sand storms. Not a sight to be seen. Waiting. They were always waiting, keeping vigil over ramshackle caches and the people who wanted them there so badly they refused to help them when they were called to stand against the Taliban. Always an excuse. They hunch down from talc perches, and he and we volley fire at them. All it does is scar the night sky. Rigid structure, built on a foundation of yawning rust. This wasn't what he promised. He doesn't remember how, because the flash uproots the entire truck from the road, He wakes up to excruciating pain from rebar puncturing through the underside of his thigh. Shaky hands pull and yank, and he wonders if it he should leave it in because it hurts less if he does. He doesn't want to lose his leg. Fifteen men gone, and he prays to God not to lose his leg. The Firebird crept down Rodney Road, its driver doing her best to avoid heat fissures pockmarking its surface. The air conditioner clouded the windows, frosted her perspiration to oily stickiness that made her want a shower in the worst of ways. Streaks of molted mascara and make-up pasted her forearm from unconscious brushes. She'd been mainlining cigarettes and coffee and her nerves were fried. So was her patience. Eight miles outside of town, and a few passes left from Grady Street and Toadvine brought her to the Stonewall Motel. She shook her head as she neared. The Stonewall became a dive when the owner's wife passed on. The metal pole the flickering road sign rested on had begun to corrode. She pulled in. The motel's parking lot was a mess of jagged concrete rises and depressions. The rustic brick structure was coated with grime. A set of eavestroughs on the far end of the stretch had broken away from the overlapping roof, teetering just above sidewalk. Jethro sat outside room 103, rocking back and forth in his weatherworn wicker chair. Shirtless. Like usual. He looked up from his chair at the approaching vehicle, his eyes leaving the tree branch he'd been whittling into a walking stick with his kitchen knife. Pulling up in front and easing on the break, she shifted into park and cut the ignition. When she stepped out of the car, she tried her best to avoid eye contact. The two of them had known each other for years. Longer than she ever cared for. He spit a thick rope of tobacco off to the side as he pushed himself up from his seat. She was a full head taller than him, but that had more to say about him than it did her. For what he lacked in height, he more than made up for in muscle. He was shredded, a fake tanned Adonis with a blond flat top that couldn't hide his receding hairline. Dropping the knife on the seat of his chair, he rested the heels of his hands against the tip of his walking stick, beaming and showing off his rotting, yellow maw. “How ya doin?” She pushed past him, almost blown back from the collision with his shoulder but still managing to keep on her feet. She pounded on the door, folded her arms across her chest as she tapped her foot, waiting. He went to grab her by the arm, but she quickly wrenched out of his grasp. Her skin was hot to the touch. Sneering, she finally made eye contact, growling as she did her best to size him up. “Don't touch me.” If he wasn't scared of what Terrence would do to him, she'd of been picking teeth out of the divots of concrete. “You best calm your ass down--” “Shut up!” “What. The fuck!” “He was with you, wasn't he?” She knew the score. She hated him. Hated everybody from the Cerberus Motor Club. It wasn't always like that. But then again, she wasn't naive anymore. It was easier to blame them than to blame him. To blame herself. “What're you talkin' about?” “Where's Royce?” He pointed at the door, trying his best to keep his composure. “He's busy.” “Of course he is.” Her words dripped with sarcasm. She turned away, smacking her palm against the door until the door eased on its rusty hinges. Slumped at his shoulders, Royce Valley stepped out into the early morning mug, his thick and gnarled fingers fighting at pockets of sleep in the corners of his eyes. A yawn shook the black bristles of his horseshoe mustache. He didn't say anything, just looked at Jethro then at her, then back again. His barrel chest was naked, save for the wings of a fallen angel. The once crisp and prominent tattooed feathers blurred into his sun-beaten complexion. There was no beating around the bush. “I need money.” A vacant stare. A blink, then two. She'd gotten him out of bed for this? He shook his head, grabbing at the door handle and pulling back until she propped her foot in the way. She did her best to hide her wince. “I'd never ask for me. But Terrence is in county, and I figure you have something to do with it.” “You'd be figurin' wrong then,” he groaned, his hands bracing against the door pane. “Probably just got a little bit crazy at Griswold's or some shit. Ain't my problem.” She tongued the inside of her cheek, her hands tightening to fists. “Don't treat me like I'm stupid, Royce.” “He ain't treatin--” “Shut up, Jethro.” He cut him off, looking down his nose at her after he'd done so. He tightened his lips. Swallowed hard. Exasperated, he loosen the strained muscles in his neck. “How much?” He never gave up money without a fight, no matter who was involved. Her stomach churned with nervousness. Her knuckles had gone white. “Ten thousand.” It was a bitter sting, causing him to run his hands through his mane. For a moment he breathed through his teeth, lazily pacing out of the doorway and grabbing the metal support beam to the roof for support. “Look,” she started, trying to find the words to continue before she stopped. It was true that she'd never liked him, but she'd be lying if Royce hadn't been the one person who always tried to do right by Terrence, no matter how twisted his concept of right was. “I don't have that kind of money lying around.” “He's out of control.” Royce looked to Jethro, then to her. A subtle nod of the head was all he gave. “Look, I'll ask Opal.” She knew the truth, now. And it made her sick. “He's gonna say no.” “Autumn,” he stopped her from leaving, trying to brace her shoulder with his hand, but she shied away from it. What else could he have expected? “It's all I can do.” It was too typical. She didn't need to stand there and take it. Spitting at his feet, she sharply stomped away, opening the door of the Firebird. “Thanks for the help.” She slammed the door, backing out and getting away from them as quickly as she could. He hadn't expected her to peel rubber to the road, and he didn't blame her for being disgusted. But he had no other options. It wasn't like Opal liked Terrence to begin with, and that went doubly so for Autumn. It wasn't Jethro's place, but he couldn't help himself from asking. “He was supposed to bring back the tanks last night, wasn't he?” “Yeah.” He shook his head, an involuntary smirk hiding realization. “Shit.” Cages. They keep them in cages nestled between agril cliffs. The ground doesn't give. The sun bakes them to delirium. Drinking his own sweat. Chewing through the skin around and underneath his fingernails. He was going to die here. They were all going to die here. An American promise. Sir, yes, Sir. Infection oozes from the wound on the underside of his thigh, bleeding through makeshift gauze made from strips of camouflage pant legs. The mixed hasn't devolved. They simply waited, like they've been trained to do. The heat gets worse. Their air reeks of foul that defies description. Tight spaces. He has to steady his breathing. He pounds at the earth when the rest sleep. Rattling the cage to see if it would give way. Tight spaces and escape and it makes him feel like he's falling. Everything else was vague, but this he remembers in complete clarity. There were things worse than death. For Terrence Kingsley, it's the cage. Sheriff Wyatt Conyers drank his coffee, his free hand wrapped around the bars of the cell door. He peeled his widebrim hat from his crown, flapping the brim against the steel. He was bald as a stone, the sweat beading above his brow. The sun hadn't been up for more than fifteen minutes. It was going to be another hot one today. He stood there, waiting, chewing on cud as he had a tendency to do. He was looking forward to this. Felix Salmons came in early, just like his father used to do. Wyatt respected that in a man. It was hard to find that in someone so young. His skin color might have had something to do with his philosophy. Habits die hard for those who remember the old ways. The two congregated outside the cell. He was looking forward to this. He pulled the key ring from his belt, thumbing through the jingle before putting in the key and unbolting the lock. They gathered about the cot, the stench flooding their nostrils. Terrence had nestled himself into the fetal position. He looked serene. Wyatt took a sip of coffee. A smile ran through the wrinkles of his face. He splashed the rest of his cup against the small of Terrence's back. He bolted from the bed, grabbed at his burn until the molasses running through his sobering body brought him back to his seat, his face souring and nostrils flaring. Wyatt hadn't armed himself. Felix wasn't as foolish. His fingers trembled against the grooves of his billy club's handle. Terrence opened his eyes, walleyed anger cooling after a few slaps to his own face. As mindlessly violent as he'd become, he wasn't stupid. It'd been over seventy years since Jefferson County had a sheriff without the last name Conyers. And with what he knew? He tried to stay straight lipped, emotionless. “What're you doing here?” No response. Conyers rested his back against the binge concrete blocks lining the wall. “Breaking an' entering. I could go through a whole mess of your behaviors. All unbecoming of a war hero.” Still no response, the heels of his hands vigorously rubbing against the stubble grain of his cheeks. “Your father was a good man--” “Yeah?” Terrence cut him off, beaming wide eyed and malicious. “You found him yet?” He tipped the brim of his hat, covering his face as best as he could. He folded his arms across his chest. Better for secrets to be buried, even if he didn't know where they were exactly. “I owe that man a great deal, and I figure I'd pay it forward.” Words filled with false meanings. He could see them from a mile away. It had never been hard to get a read on Conyers if you'd seen what kind of man he truly was. “Yeah? How's that?” “I'm gettin' too old for this. But Felix and some of the boys? I know you don't much care for old mister Crews.” A wild snort. “I don't much care for the crank I thought I'd taken care of when Spisak was shackled up and sent to Parchman Farm.” He stepped away from the wall, his hand finding his officer's shoulder for a nudge. “It's good out there, this time of year, don't you figure, Felix?” “Oh yeah. Be mighty fine weather to be out in the fields.” Terrence didn't hold the cards, no matter how willing his spirit was. He tongued the roof of his mouth, tried to lick his dried and cracked lips. He wasn't sure if he was trying to bluff the man or if he was serious. “So...what? Just...what?” “Don't think I'm bargaining with you, boy, 'cause I'm not. I don't give a damn if you got a medal. I've let shit like you slide for long enough. But you can make it easier on yourself.” He stepped toward the cot, stooping on arthritic knees just to make his point clear. Eye to eye. Like how he was brought up. “That ammonia you were stealing? If I rounded up what I could find at that trailer of yours? I could hit you up with five years.” His hand found Terrence's thigh, his fingers rubbing up and down. “Ten if we start throwing in firearms.” Terrence wanted to swing, even if it tacked on another decade. It was better than losing his dignity. But, like a good little boy, he just stared. Scowled and stared. Acted like dagger glares meant something. He spoke with gravel from deep in his belly. He grabbed the Sheriff's hand by the knuckles and squeezed. “Yeah?” Frantic trembles shot through Wyatt's arm as Kingsley tightened his gripped. He couldn't keep from falling to his knees. All allusion of control was lost in jittered breath. He could feel his knuckles popping. Felix tensed, his hand ripping at Terrence's forearm, to try and pull Conyers free of the vice. Kingsley let go before Salmons could waylay him with his club. He wasn't even trying. If he was, the old bastard's hand would've long been shattered. Stress the bonds, no matter the cost. As long as he could hold his head up high, even if for a fleeting moment. Coyners tried his best to pick himself up immediately, to show he wasn't hurt. His hands splayed out and smacked against concrete that hadn't been mopped in months. He grunted and groaned, reaching at Felix's belt to pull himself up. And once he did, he backhanded Terrence across the face with his knuckles. He shot from his cot, but the club caught him in the sternum, caused him to double over and flop to the floor. Rough, sandpaper guffaws spat from Conyer's mouth. He steadied his breathing, pinning long locks of Terrence's hair under his toe. “Maybe you should think of that girl of yours. I wonder who she'll fuck when you got stripes.” There was no hesitation. He stomped on his fingers and kicked him the face and let him roll over from the force. He'd do more, but times were changing, and so were his enemies in Jackson. Better to leave him lying, to let him ponder who he was and where he was set to go on the welcoming cushion of slab. All Terrence could do was moan as the gate to his cell was clamped shut. His mouth was numb, his lips frothing with blood. The toilet was as good as anything to grab hold of. To pull up with. He spat a pair of teeth into the bowl. Clinching his eyes shut, he readied himself, pinching his nostril with his grip. One jerk left, then right. All to reset the break. A straight drag of scorched clay, flint lacerating from the edges of the path. His eyes became their eyes. Their panics. Their fears. They are pushed forward in a line as men, shawled and beady eyed, steady their assault rifles from their hips. They sing hymns. Maybe they don't. Bible verses have no place in their match underneath papered dusk. It is their injuries that brought them here, because insurgents are cowards who pick at the weak because the strong bowl them over. It is why they were so willing to cackle at the few and the proud like they'd brought New York to its knees again. Those men stand stoic in the end. They have to. He can't let them die without the dignity they deserve. He fights forward, gangly and stooping, every step a long drag. A long wince. Hiccuped whines spurt from his mouth, his eyes lacquered and his cheeks chapped. He collapses. As he's done before. Eight men lie flat dead in haphazard patterns. Gnats and flies trail the tendriled exit wounds from their brain pans. He doesn't want to imagine what it was like, but he knows what it was like on the other end now, after what he'd done to his father. Savages have no need for common courtesy. They gave their lives for his, but they didn't know it. The insurgents didn't care. They'd left the cage clear open and thought him dead as they marched the mishmash platoon into the distance. Maybe they'd come back for him. He picks himself up, his palms blistered and his skin aching dust. His fingers work in spastic twitches. He isn't long for the world, but he is a good medic. All he can do is laugh. He reaches for their tags. Someone needs to know what had happened to them all, and he is going to make sure of it. She batted her pen to a staccato beat against the clipboard, her eyes gazing through the print of the bond agreement she'd done everything but signature. Ten thousand dollars. They hadn't even told her what he'd done, and they were asking for ten thousand dollars. She'd have to work most of the year to scrape that much together, and that was only if she was lucky. But even as her head was screaming at her to stop, her heart complicated things. It always had. He'd been there for her when she'd needed someone the most. Gotten her away. Been her sanctuary when nobody else could've been. She couldn't forget all of that, no matter how much he changed. She owed him that much. Anger. Everything they knew was supposed to change. All she'd had was anger. A deep, bone hurt from disappointment and shame. She reached into her purse, sifting through bottles of eye shadow, lipstick, pamphlets for colleges she'd never attend. Anger worked well because it was explosive and unyielding and it was a fine mask for the guilt that was crushing her. After all, enlisting had been her idea. She pulled out the deed to her property, to her trailer, and clipped it to the board. Her signature came next. The principle was more than what they asked, but she had nothing else for leverage. Getting up from her chair and swinging her purse over her shoulder, she walked up to the desk, sliding the clipboard inside the metal tray of the glass partition. The man behind the desk stamped his dates, signed his little asides. A loud buzz sounded. The door to the inside opened. Terrence trudged to freedom, his head downcast because he couldn't look at her. How could he? He only found clairvoyance in the small crack between splitting headaches and drunken stupors. She'd had it in her head to give him a piece her mind, to break him down and make him sorry he ever dared fall back in with the kinds of people who'd dried him of his humanity. But just seeing him mottled her vision. His teflon smile couldn't hide the obvious. The base of his nose was bulbed, his nostrils faintly uneven. Dried blood foamed his lips. Her lips parted, taut breathes trapping her words before she could muster up the strength to utter them. She sighed. They walked out side by side, their shoulders dangerously close to rubbing together. When they got to the car, he waited for her to give him the keys. She pushed through him, unlocking the door and getting in the driver's side. He stood there for a few minutes, ducking down to the window. She refused to look at him, her glare forward. He took the hint and got in on the passenger's side. He watched the rows of flags lining Main Street as the air chilled the cabin, his hand reaching for the air conditioner dial to turn it to two. When his hand reached his lap, she reached over and put it right back to four. He shifted in his seat, resting his shoulder on the window as it began to frost. A stiff groan followed sharp pain, and then he righted himself. “What you think of Montana?” She pursed her lips, swallowed and closed her eyes. And just as quickly as he'd said it, she'd let the notion fall to silence. “I don't know. Maybe it's time to get out. Just out and run. Nothing leaving us here. Nothing to leave behind.” He watched her as she rolled her eyes, her grip on the steering wheel tightening. “You can say something any time.” Her eyes remained forward, but he caught their ever-so-slight shift. “And what good will it do?” “Plenty of good.” “You ain't running. I won't let you.” He chuckled as best as his ribs would allow him to. “Yeah? How you figure to stop me?” She'd had enough. Veering off to the lip of the road, she slammed on the brakes and ripped the gear into park. She shot her head to face him, her hands clapping against her thighs. “You listen to yourself talk anymore?” “What the hell--” “Shut up!” She wanted to choke him. Slap him. Knock some sense into him. But by all appearance, that had already happened, and all it got him was spinning his broken record rhetoric she'd heard far too many times. “Some of us have responsibilities. Some of us have to carry the load while others mope around.” “You need to listen to--” “Fuck you.” A vacant blink. He hadn't expected that. “What'd you say?” “You heard me.” Her voice rattled. “That house you don't pay for, that I do? I used it to bail you out of jail. What were you even,” but she stopped, mid-sentence, pushing her hands out to stop him from answering. “I don't want to know.” He chewed on his thoughts for uncomfortable moments that stretched on and on until he could only sigh, defeated. He'd been staring her down the entire time. But when he needed to muster up reasoning, when he needed to step up and be what she needed him to be, he folded. “It doesn't matter. We just got to run.” “To Maine?” Rapid blinks caught tears, her contempt scowling her cheeks. Numbing them. “This where you tell me about Maine? About how it'll change in the winter time? About how I can start my own bakery in some small town like you see on postcards? Where you promise everything will be different.” A wild pull through his hair. His fingers clinched at his scalp and his face purpled and he lost control. “And I sacrificed for that! More than you'll ever know!” Pathetic. She watched his tensing, his tightening, his clinches and thought him pathetic. Realization dawned on her. What sat across from her was a shell. She'd been mourning at the wrong grave. She didn't love him anymore. “And I can't keep waiting for you to come home. ” Her words poured over him with surgical efficiency. He knew she was right. Part of him was still out somewhere, somewhere worse, and all that was left behind was a malaise. A despondent, limping thing tgat did nothing but taint a better man's name. He watched Autumn. Thought of Parchman Farm. He hyperventilated. Pushed against the cabin's ceiling and the window. He was falling again.
