A Decade in the Making: How GCW's King Made 2009 His Own

16 January 2010
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 01/16/10 A Decade in the Making: How GCW’s King Made 2009 His Own John Reinsing, 16th January 2010. The first thing you notice about this man is his size. Put bluntly, Andy Murray is huge. You neglect it when you seem him on television - brushing shoulders with the likes of Jorge Samuelsson and Shaman makes his 6’7” seem entirely unremarkable - but in-person his stature is truly immense. When you shake his hand it feels as though he could crush yours with minimal effort, when you walk with him you feel like a child at your father’s side again. Even now, seated in a bustling lunchspot in Austin’s Hyde Park district, he’s much like an elephant in a china store. “It can be troublesome every now and then, sure,” GCW’s reigning World Heavyweight Champion says. “I’m not going to complain about it, though. So I might get a couple of gawking looks from passers-by every now and then, but it brings its benefits and there are people born with far worse afflictions than I. Besides, it helps make my job a heck of lot easier.” It most certainly does. You don’t have to built like a Redwood to succeed as a professional wrestler at the highest level, but it certainly won’t do you any harm. Would the likes of Bryan Mayhem and LeStatt Knight prove to be so successful with their intense psychological warfare without that added element of physical intimidation? Would Murray himself have found success in GCW so quick to come by if his size hadn't gifted him the strength to throw his opponents around like ragdolls? Probably not. Although he may joke that if he was a foot shorter he'd “probably be fighting in a Wyoming basement for an audience of six,” Murray's ring game is built around more than just raw power. Over the past decade and a half he's grown tremendously. Those that frequented the Floridian indy scene during the late-'90's may recall a lumbering, green rookie with all the technique and grace of a dying swan - a young man who struggling to convincingly apply a sleeper hold, let alone a Chickenwing Crossface. To watch Andy Murray in 2009, however, was to watch a man who's chosen his craft and honed it to a tee. His strength is almost unmatched (this is a competitor who managed to single-handedly dump Kimbusa over the top rope at GCW's 2008 Dangerous Games event, after all), yet his lack of extreme muscle mass makes him nimble on his feet and just as agile as some of his smaller compatriots. His experience means you'll never see him make rookie mistakes, and his knowledge of different holds and moves is such that he's got an answer for anything that his opponents can throw at him. “I've been lucky enough to work with some of the industry's greatest over the years, so if I've improved then the credit's gotta go to them,” Murray says modestly between mouthfuls of smoked salmon. If you work with people who are better than you then some of it's gonna rub off eventually. I'm never gonna be able to chain wrestle, and if you ever see me heading to the top rope it's because someone's knocked me on the head and I ain't feeling right, but I like to think I've built up a varied enough skill-set to cope with just about anything.” There's a multitude of evidence to support this, too. From scrappy brawls with Jay Terror, to technical stand-offs during his early battles with Chris Bagwell, to the violent furnace that was his final showdown with LeStatt Knight, Murray has eventually overcome every opponent he has encountered in GCW. Every single one of them. When Chris Bagwell turned him over at High Stakes 2008, Murray avenged his lose at Warpath in a bloody steel cell match. When LeStatt Knight toppled him with minimal effort at last year's Shockwave event, Murray produced his greatest ever performance inside a GCW ring to capture the GCW World Heavyweight Title. The only other competitor to ever better him in a singles match, Jay Terror, tasted defeated by Murray's hand at least year's NC-17. Put bluntly, if you mess with Andy Murray, chances are you're going to pay for it. It hasn't always been plain sailing for the big Scot, though. As Murray explains, “it's been a struggle at times. Everyone's heard the horror stories from the indies - there were nights you'd work a thirty-minute match in front of a minuscule audience for a $10 paycheck. Then you'd spend the rest of your night trying to find a parking lot the cops wouldn't chase you away form just so you can get a few hours sleep.” Those long years spent grinding the independent promotions of the southern states weren't all negative, though. “Everyone starts the same way, I guess,” Andy says in a soft, Scottish tenor that sounds remarkably different to the hyperactive flow that GCW viewers are familiar with. “Times were hard every now and then, but the crowds, however small, were just so much more receptive than any you'll find inside a big, soulless arena. They really appreciate that you've just travelled two-hundred miles in a beat-up Ford just to put on a show for them. The backstage camaraderie's like nothing I've came across since, too. When you're grinding like that, you go through a lot of rough stuff together. The bond's inevitable.” It was for these reasons that Murray took the decision last year to surface in Tim Shipley's Just Wrestling promotion. “I just wanted to recapture that thrill. Don't get me wrong, GCW crowds are excellent. I've never had more fun performing than I have in front of them, without a doubt, but there's just something different about an indy show. Something special.” His sojourns with his former AWC roster-mate's upstart company were mixed at best, however. The consistency that had become his trademark in GCW evaporated as soon as he headed back to the smaller stage, and he left the promotion in December on a 6-5 record. Despite capturing the Just Wrestling Championship along the way, Murray's Just tenure will not serve as a career milestone. “I won't be going back,” he explains. “Am I glad I did it? Definitely. Some of those shows were great fun, and I got to work with some incredibly talented guys like Violence Jack and Erik Magnum, but it just wasn't the same. Maybe that scene's not for me any more.” For the towering Scot, it was a throwback to the early 2000's when his career could have best been described as a stop-start affair, and at worst a complete shambles. Murray burst from the indy scene in 2001 and first came to prominence with cult regional promotion Online Championship Wrestling. He captured gold within his first three months in the fed, and went on to win the promotion's top title belt twice in just under a year. Things were looking promising for the twenty-three year old Brit, until the first of a series of career-threatening injuries struck. “I tore my knee ligaments. It was the night after I'd won my second World Championship; I was working a match with a relative newcomer to the fed, and about halfway through the match I felt my leg collapse,” he recalls vividly, going on to describe the immeasurable pain of missing over a year of in-ring competition as a result. “Sitting at home with your leg raised, watching these guys you work with doing their thing on television while all you can do's curse your luck? It's hard.” It was not the knee area that would prove the greatest injury blight on Murray's career, though. “I first hurt my back during a cage match in 2003. This guy, James Rande, tossed me from the top. We'd rehearsed the spot a hundred times, but we must've got our angles wrong on the night, because the landing couldn't really have went any worse.” The initial affliction would only keep The Scottish King of Cool on the shelf for six months, but it was a burden that would soon come back to haunt him. Both of his stints in Dave Harber's Atlantic Wrestling Club were cut short by recurrences of that same injury, as well as his 2007 tag team run with younger brother Cayle in FUSE. “The only thing in my life that I regret not doing is getting the back sorted after the first injury. They gave me the option of surgery and I turned it down. I was young, mid-twenties, y'know? I thought I was invincible, and getting surgery would've meant at least another year sitting around doing nothing. I couldn't handle that.” Opting instead for intense courses of physiotherapy, Murray would pay for his decision. AWC stuck by him when the injury first recurred in 2005, but when it struck again in 2006 they had no choice but to terminate in contract. Similarly, his stint in FUSE lasted only a few short weeks before an old enemy came back to haunt him. “It was getting to the point where if I stepped into a ring, there was a 50% chance I wasn't going to be able to walk out on my own accord.” He speaks slowly and with the somberness you'd expect from a man recounting his darkest days. When I got hurt in FUSE, the doctors told me that if I didn't get the operation and continued wrestling, I might've ended up in a wheelchair by 30. I couldn't live with that.” Finally, the penny dropped. Andy had the back surgery immediately, and spent much of 2007 recuperating in his native Aberdeen. Fortunately a raw of light shone on Andy Murray. Things began to balance themselves out when his back began healing a lot quicker than any of his doctors had anticipated. He was off his crutches and walking almost freely by the middle of the year, and then, in November, a miracle. “I remember the phone ringing on a November morning. It was cold outside and I couldn't really be arsed getting out of bed. Figured it was probably some telemarketer trying to sell me a conservatory, anyway,” he laughs. That telemarketer turned out to be Steven Caldera. “There have only been a couple of occasions in my life where I have felt genuine amazement. That was one of them.” After almost 4 years of woe, Murray thought that his wrestling career at the highest level was over. Yet world had filtered through to the American wrestling scene that one of it's once brightest prospects had had his back fixed and would soon be on the market again. It was an opportunity that Mr. Caldera jumped at. “You can say what you want about the guy, but I'll be eternally grateful for the chance he gave me. I'd been thrown out of every promotion I'd ever signed a contract with because I'd they'd ended up paying me wages to lie on a physio's bed, so for the figurehead of one of the world's biggest companies to call me up like that was unreal. His GCW career is true fairytale stuff, from start to finish. Two weeks after the phonecall and a rusty Murray was in front of 20,000 people again, wrestling a WorldWide dark match. Two days later and he'd inked a one year deal with Global Championship Wrestling. The coin an old cliché - the rest, as they say, is history. Despite the operation, doubts remained over Murray's long-term health, but they all subsided from the very moment The Scottish King of Cool hit GCW Television at NC-17 2007. “I've hardly had a niggle since, touch wood,” he says, rapping his knuckles gently against the mahogany table-top, before raising his wine glass. “To good health.” For anyone who's not had their head buried in the sand for the past two years, Murray's GCW impact needs no explanation. From usurping Chris Bagwell as the company's top rising star in 2007, to completing a clean sweep at the 2009 year-end awards, there is quite simply nothing else that Andy Murray could have been expected to accomplish during these 24 months. “How do I think my GCW career's gone? Awful, man. Devastatingly awful,” he says, cracking a pearly white smile and chuckling heartily. “Nah. I'm sitting here at the of the world. I'm holding the most prestigious title in one of the world's biggest promotions, and people like me enough to vote me the Wrestler of the Year. How can I complain?” The road has not been without his bumps, though. His struggles with Bagwell, Knight, and Terror are well-documented, but he has always rolled with the punches and emerged victorious. In Bagwell he overcame a narcissistic ego hell-bent on being the most talked-about wrestler in the world, in Knight he defeated one of the greatest talents the wrestling business has ever seen after a struggle spanning six months, and after toppling Jay Terror he proved he could stand up to any authority, no matter how powerful, and still emerge with his head held high. As 2010 begins, though, new challenges await The Scottish King of Cool. I've skirted around the elephant in the room all afternoon, but it was inevitable that Vivica J. Valentine's name would be brought up sooner or later. “I haven't heard from her since Christmas Chaos,” Murray sheepishly admits, his face turning grey at the mention of his absent partner's name. When Vivica J. Valentine cracked at Christmas Chaos, nobody was more surprised than Andy Murray. The idealist in him may write the incident off as “something that happened in the heat of the moment; that wasn't her, it was a mistake,” but the fact of the matter is that at the end of the night, he'd fallen by her hand. Such is his infatuation with Valentine that it's impossible not to notice the fact that he's checking his phone every five minutes for confirmation that Christmas Chaos' conclusion was nothing more sinister than a notoriously volatile competitor losing her cool. “You know what she's like,” he says, tapping his third text message of the day in Valentine's direction. “She loses her head sometimes. We'll work it out.” It's the first time all afternoon that words have come out of Andy Murray's mouth and I've not 100% believed them. For a man blessed with more charisma than an American president could ever wish for, even The Scottish King of Cool doesn't sound sure of his words. Still, it's clear that the topic is not something Murray wishes to dwell on. It is here when we witness the unflappable GCW World Champion at his most vulnerable. Beads of sweat begin to form on his brow, and his movements become twitchy and agitated. It's clear that this is not the Admiral of Awesome that GCW audiences are more accustomed with. “Well I definitely don't roll around town blasting my theme music and chasing people with haggis,” Murray says, perking up. “I think a lot of people are probably quite surprised when they meet me. They expect this larger-than-life, in-your-face caricature, when really I'm just your Average Joe.” It's an overused statement, but one that's certainly relevant for the GCW World Champion. On television he's a firecracker of charisma and one-liners, but behind the cameras he's a much more subdued personality. Aged 32, the Andy Murray of 2010 would rather spend time helping his 12 year old son with his homework or playing golf with his agent than take part in some of the hedonistic acts that some of his peers are known for. “I think it's important to have a balance - you can't got at 100mph all the time,” he says, “you're only going to burn yourself out. I know guys in their 30's who still party like they're 18, and that's cool, but it's just not for me. I'd rather not waste money that could be going towards my son's future by hitting the bar every night.” It's refreshing to find a man so grounded in an industry populated with big egos and even bigger mouths, especially when you consider all that Murray has accomplished in the past couple of years. Still, when you think about the man's background, it is perhaps not so surprising that he's kept his feet on the ground. Brought up as one of four children in a three-bedroom house, the son of a North Sea fisherman and a shopkeeper, Andrew Hugh Murray's upbringing was not one of silver spoons and gold coins. “We didn't exactly have it all on a plate, but we never lived outwith our means. There was always bread on the table and milk in the fridge. Mom and Dad worked hard all their lives to give us kids a good standard of living. I mean, they didn't have much; both of them left school at 15 with almost no qualifications, but they couldn't have done a better job of raising their children.” Sadly, the Murray family lost their matriarch to natural causes just under three years ago. “She put her head down to sleep one night and just didn't wake up,” Murray explains. mournfully “Of course it was hard for everyone, but she had a good innings. I guess it was just her time to go.” Eighteen months later he lost his father to cancer. “Dad going was a little harder to take. I loved my mother with all my heart, but most fathers have particularly tight bonds with their sons, and we were especially close. I still have great memories of fishing on the pier at the end of our street and kicking an old football against the garden wall. Dad might be gone, but those images will be with me 'til the day I die.” So stricken was Murray by his father's death in 2008 and he immediately pledged 15% of his monthly income to British charity Cancer Research UK - a donation that he has kept up ever since. “It's the least I could do, really. I have enough money to set me and my son up for the rest our lives, what would be the point in letting the rest of it sit in my bank account doing nothing?” Although he is quick to play down his own generosity, Murray's charity doesn't end with these donations. Since 2008 he's sent made monthly payments of roughly $300 to his younger sister to fund her journey through the Scottish university system (“I'd rather she doesn't leave university with £16,000 of debt like everyone we grew up with did,” he says), and he's let his younger brother lodge in his Tallahassee home since his release from prison almost 2 years ago. “I'm not the eldest in the family, but I still see it as my responsibility to look out for the rest of them. I'm fortunate enough to be in a position to do so, so why not? I'd be lost without my brothers, my sister, and my boy.” It's quite surprising given Murray's dedication to his family, then, that his luck with the fairer sex has been so rotten since he first moved to America as a 17 year old. Shortly after his Transatlantic relocation, Murray was married to Jane Thomas, a woman of English descent with whom he had his only child, Marvin. One divorce, another marriage, a second divorce, and a messy child custody battle later, Murray's dealings with Jane ended when Chris Bagwell roped her in to try and destroy Murray's squeaky-clean reputation ahead of their Warpath 2008 showdown. Kayla Jarosz, a Nebraskan farmer's daughter four years Murray's junior, came into his life soon after, only to depart eighteen months later when it became apparent that she could no longer cope with being in a relationship with a man who spent four days out of seven on the road. “I guess it's not easy, being involved with someone who spends more time travelling than he does at home,” Murray muses. He remains upbeat and quite cheery when discussing Kayla and Jane, but there's a weariness in his eyes that suggests he may finally be growing tired of the string of failed love affairs that have hung over him like a graven spirit. Similarly, you wonder what will become of the man's confidence should his relationship with Vivica J. Valentine implode as a result of Christmas Chaos. Still, this is a man who has taken every barrier that life has dropped in his path and smashed it to pieces. He's overcome injury woes to become on of the most captivating performers on the North American wrestling scene and built a life for himself through a combination of hard graft and his own God-given talent. Not bad for a man who is referred to by his greatest critics as a “naive man who lives in a dream world.” “No matter how many babies you kiss or hands you shake, you're always going to have detractors,” Andy shrugs nonchalantly. “I try not to let it get to me. You can't please everyone, and if someone wants to criticise me that's fine, because I'm doing fine for myself. They have their opinions and I have mine.” Criticism can be quite a touchy subject for a man who has enjoyed almost universal adoration and acclaim since making his wrestling comeback in GCW. During his feud with LeStatt Knight in 2008, Murray infamously lost his trademark cool when GCW Colour Commentator David Yale launched a scathing attack on his character prior to that year's Dangerous Game event. “The incident with Mr. Yale was unfortunate,” Murray says. “It's his job to give an opinion, and if he doesn't like what I do then that's totally cool. What he shouldn't be doing, though, is passing judgement on a man's character in the public domain. If he had something to say then he should've said to to my face, not to the media.” At the end of the day, though, Murray proved Yale wrong, obliterating the commentator's claims that Murray “had his head in the clouds” and was too much of a “deluded fool with no grasp on the reality of how the wrestling business really works” to overcome LeStatt Knight. Andy claims he has not spoken to Yale since. “He's given me some credit since, but he's got a forked tongue - the type of man who can flip even the smallest compliment on its head and turn them into an insult. I have no time whatsoever for that kind of person.” You can tell immediately from the scorn that laces his words and the way he frowns when discussing the topic that the verbal spat left a sour taste in his mouth that still exists to this day. Clearly, David Yale's words struck a raw nerve with The Scottish King of Cool. While Murray will gladly take any professional criticism on the chin and his quick to reflect the jibes and insults of his fellow competitors, it seems as though such comments from men in Yale's position are not things that he deals well with. To Murray, David Yale is a powerful observer. He has called more wrestling matches in his career than most wrestlers will work throughout the entirety of their career, and so his knowledge of the sport is nothing short of immense. He has seen every type of character, good and bad, pass through GCW during his tenure with the company and is in a better place to call judgement on a GCW employee than most people are. The question remains as to whether Yale was right to air such criticisms of Murray's personal integrity through the media, but perhaps this was a sign of things to come for The Scottish King of Cool. Recently, Yale has gone on to question how Murray will react should the fans' partisan loyalty to him ever fade away, and it's certainly a valid query. Could Murray's reaction to Yale's words in 2008 have been a sign of things to come should his own wrestling empire implode on him, or were his words merely those of a man who found it unbelievable that, despite all the hardship he'd overcome on the road to GCW, critics would still find a way to question his ability to operate under adversity? Time will tell. Still, when looking to the future, Andy Murray is very much a “glass half-full” kind of guy. He refuses to even consider what might happen if the notoriously-fickle wrestling industry tires of him, and insists that he focuses on his career on a match-by-match basis. “I never look any further ahead than the next show. It sounds corny, but I'm not going to be able to do this, so I try to enjoy it while I can without worrying about what might happen in the future.” Right now, for example, he is far more concerned with defending his GCW World Championship against AXEL Action at WorldWide 124 than anything that may happen at NC-17. “I'm really looking forward to it,” Murray says almost gleefully. “Me and AXEL came into GCW within a couple of months of each other. I'm big fan of him as a wrestler, and backstage he's probably one of the nicest guys you could ever hope to meet. Beating up the bad guys is all very well, but you really can't beat the thrill of putting on a spectacle with someone you have a lot of time for.” Does Murray view AXEL as a threat to his title? “Of course, I'd be stupid not to. The guy won two awards this year for good reason - he's improving at a frightening rate, and that United States fiasco last month, he deserves a shot at the top of the card. He's a great wrestler and I know he's beyond pumped to have this opportunity, so it's up to me to match him.” Despite his respect and admiration for his challenger, Murray remains confident of his own chances. “I'm not just going to shake hands and let him take my belt, though. This is a results-driven industry, and I'm not quite finished with this title just yet. My first defence was against Jordan White, a man I have more respect for than just about anyone else in this business, and I knew that if I beat him he was gone from the company, but did I let-up? Not for a second. I knew that I'd be sending my friend packing, but that's just how the cookie crumbles.” It is with those words that my two hours with The Scottish King of Cool draw to close. The restaurant is beginning to clear out, and Murray has a tight schedule to adhere to on the eve of another WorldWide broadcast. Still, I have thoroughly enjoyed my time spent with GCW's King. In-person, Murray remains a captivating personality, although one that is quite different from the energetic Scot we see on our TV screens every week. Here is a devoted family man who spurns his own wealth to ensure that those close to him can enjoy the same standard of living that he does. He's a man with big ambitions; a dreamer, yes, but a man grounded enough to avoid delusion. While his struggles to find a perfect balance between his career and family life may persist until he retires, he will work his fingers to the bone to find a way to make sure everything works, even if his quest is a futile one. He has recovered from a series of misfortunes that would have left lesser men dead and buried. By the end of 2008 he was Global Championship Wrestling's heir-in-waiting, and by the end of 2009 he was it's undisputed king. Where he goes from here is uncertain, but in two years Andy Murray has accomplished more than 99% of GCW's alumnus will throughout their entire career. A gentleman at heart, Murray refuses to disclose to how much of a tip he's left as he picks up the cheque, but the wad of notes missing from his wallet doesn't exactly make it hard to guess. We part with a shake of hands and a pat on the back. I leave knowing exactly what makes this man tick, and the opinion that Andy Murray is the kind of guy that absolutely anyone can get behind. After all, all he really wants is a secure future for his family. Isn't that what everyone wants? As my car pulls away from the restaurant's small parking lot, I catch Murray's looming figure out of the corner of my eye. He's stood still by his vehicle, tapping a number into his cell phone. Probably Valentine's.
