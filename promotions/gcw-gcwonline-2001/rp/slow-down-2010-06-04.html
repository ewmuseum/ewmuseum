Slow Down

4 June 2010
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 06/04/10 Acting as his own agent was a combination of two of the things Andy hated most in life: paperwork and cleaning-up other people's mess. An abominable amalgam of tedium and hassle that had ruined his plan to get an early night and made more thankful for his agent's services than ever before. He'd sat there for almost four hours, going through a fortnight's worth of letters with a fine-toothed comb. Though he estimated that 99% of them were junk, Andy followed the same process for each individual letter: open, read, file accordingly. It wasn't the most efficient of systems, but if he didn't examine every single letter than he might have ended-up discarding the one out of every hundred that was actually of use to him. Some of the junk was easily identifiable – anything wrapped in cellophane or with an envelope doubling as advertising space was tossed immediately – but the differentiation between what was trash and what was important was more often than not complicated by nondescript packaging. Andy removed his reading glasses and gently massaged his greasy temples. It had been a long night, and one that he wouldn't have had to endure had his brother fulfilled the meagre administrative duties assigned to him in Andy's absence. With his agent's health deteriorating and his own road schedule intensifying, Andy didn't have time to keep up with everything. Normally such mail would be handled in-house by Rueben Dallas' agency, but with Dallas on his deathbed and the future of his agency in doubt, somebody else had to take charge of the mind-numbing day-to-day procedures that comprised much of a pro-wrestling agent's workday. Andy's younger brother, Cayle, seemed like the perfect candidate. His criminal convictions made it hard for him to gain regular employment and, having lived in Andy's house rent-free for the best part of two years, he had a debt to repay to his younger sibling. It was a chance to reduce his brother's dependency, put some coin in his pocket, and save himself some seriously soul-destroying work. Perfect. That's what he thought, at least. Cayle's commitment to the task lasted less than four days before he decided to stop checking the post and replying to email, leaving Andy to work through them on his days off. Andy wasn't entirely surprised that his brother's lethargy had got the better of him again, but his disappointment could not have been understated. His brother had been in and out of work for the past twenty-four months but hadn't been able to hold onto any. A few small scale wrestling gigs hadn't provided the springboard into the sport that Cayle had hoped for, and once again he'd become a serf to his brother's wealth and stardom. Andy thought that he'd have welcomed the chance to regain some of his independence with open arms, but alas. The clock read 2:13am and the stack was almost gone. Andy set aside an invitation to a charity dinner and slid a handful of advertisements for dietary supplements into an ever-expanding black refuse sack. A manilla envelope adorned with the logo of one of the Dallas Group's rival agencies was last to make its way into the trash bag and Andy was done. Relieved, he leaned back in his creaky and breathed deeply, briefly closing his eyes before waggling the mouse before him and disengaging his computer's screensaver. A quick email check then off to bed, he thought. He logged into his business account and felt his heart drop when the inbox appeared. 89 new emails in the space of forty-two hours. "Fuck this." Andy had had enough. He was already up far later than a man with a 6am start should be, and the thought of working through 89 separate emails seemed about as appealing as a vacation on the Gaza strip. Slamming the laptop shut and turning the desklight out, Andy rose from his chair and shuffled away from the dinner table. It was bedtime: everything else would have to wait. So he walked through his shadowy house and went upstairs. Discarding his plain white tee at the bedroom door, he walked into the en suite and splashed his face with warm water before resting his head on his pillow and closing his eyes. A loud clatter filled the air and shook the building's foundations as soon as Andy had curled into a sleep-ready cocoon. Tiny shockwaves reverberated through the walls as a giggly female voice and a familiar Scottish baritone drifted upstairs. Cayle was back, and he wasn't alone. It took what seemed like an eternity for Andy's younger brother and his companion to stumble their way upstairs, and their brash ballyhoo was making Andy's blood pressure rise. Resisting the strong urge to leave his bed and smack his brother's nose, Andy pulled a pillow over his head and seethed. A very loud and very audible conversation eventually faded as Cayle slammed his bedroom door with enough force to dent an anvil, but the sound of their voices would persist long into the morning. Andy groaned. Sleep was a long way away. * * * * * "This is getting ridiculous," John Hood said, swirling pale brandy around his glass. "Almost two months to sign a goddamn contract?! Why do I feel like I'm being taken for a ride, Floyd?" "Slow down, cowboy. Guy's got cancer, remember?" The lawyer's liver-spotted skin stretched as he raised his brow. "They tell me he can barely recall his own name, let alone sign it on a contract. The agreement's in-place; this'll get done, we just gotta wait it out." "'Til he's ready? How fucking long is that going to take? The guy'll be a vegetable if we wait any longer, and then what are we going to do?!" "He has good days and bad days. More bad than good, but things improve when they change his medication." "Then why can't we just catch him on one of these 'good days' and get this shit done?" Floyd shrugged. "You think it's easy for me to get hold of Ruben Dallas' doping schedule?" He softened his Southern tones to cool Hood's volatility. "Look, I can see you're stressed about this, but we can't rush it. You know that I want this wrapped-up as quickly as you do, but the man's dying. Cut him some slack and allow him this time with his family before he goes. We'll get it sorted eventually. There are more important things on his mind than the sale." "That's if he even still has a mind," Hood snarled. His lawyer's fleeting glance of disapproval told him to calm down. John necked his alcohol and sighed. "I'm sorry, Floyd. It's just so frustrating. I've been waiting to get my hands on this capital for such a long time; I just want to get it done." "Pretty desperate to get your hands on this man's business, aren't ya?" Floyd asked, smiling. He was a portly man of sixty who hadn't done a good job of looking after his body over the years. All that remained of his hair was a few wispy yellow strands, and his weathered face was a countered mess of wrinkles and sagging skin. "Mind if I ask why?" "The Dallas Group has something I want." Hood said nothing more; just looked his lawyer in the eye. "Ohhhh, a secret." Floyd slowly nodded his bulbous head. "Well. Your prerogative, I guess. You might wanna think about dropping this 'angry pitbull' shtick before that vein on your forehead bursts, though." Prickly heat crept across Hood's scalp and he felt moisture growing beneath his arms. With a deep breath, John ran a hand though glossy stands of black hair and bit his bottom lip. Floyd was right – anger would only do him harm. The situation wouldn't change no matter how worked up he got, but something he'd waited the best part of a decade for was just a heartbeat away. He was closer to Andy Murray than he'd ever been and the only thing keeping him from revenge was a vegetating sports agent. His frustration was not without a reason. "So what's the plan?" John asked finally. "Same as it's ever been. We wait 'til the hospital call, then we get the contract signed. Simple." A deflated Hood groaned. He'd done more than enough waiting, and the prospect of doing more didn't exactly thrill him. "Can't his wife just sign it for him?" Hood asked, hastily. Confusion twisted across Floyd's face. "Come on, John. Don't be stupid." "I just need this done ASAP. What happens if he croaks before we seal the deal?" "Should Mr. Dallas, God forbid, pass away, then the agency goes to whoever's in his will." Air from Floyd's chair hissed as he pushed his rotund form up and waddled towards the alcohol cabinet. "Everything we've done will count for nothing." "But we have an agreement," John said, watching his lawyer slide a displaced bottle inside the cabinet before closing it's door gently. "We have an agreement to sell, yes, but nothing's been signed." Floyd shrugged. "Unfortunately, that's all we got. The man's dying and if he goes the deal's null and void; the best thing we can hope is that Mrs. Dallas would honour our little agreement, assuming that she's in the will." Floyd's words were doing little to pacify John's mood. He balled a fist and tapped it against his leg as Floyd strolled back around the desk and, with great effort, plopped himself down again. Floyd's standpoint was understandable; he was a good man despite his profession's nature, and he would not willing coerce a dying man into further stress. Unfortunately, that was a problem that John was just going to have to work around. "Meet me tonight, 8pm." "Can't. My daughter's got a recit--" "How much am I paying you?" Floyd had to double-take. "Excuse me?" "How much am I paying you?" "Uhhh, seven." "Make it nine if you show-up tonight." Floyd glared curiously at his client, then slowly nodded. He didn't know Hood's motives, but the thought of that extra $2,000 was too good to shake. "I'll see what I can do." "Good." John quickly rose from his chair. Grabbing his suit jacket and throwing it over his shoulder, he strode quickly towards the door and quickly twisted the handle. "See you later." "John!" shouted Floyd, stalling Hood as he was about to disappear. "What's the plan?" "We're going to finish this." * * * * * "Well, do you want the good news or the bad news?" asked Dr. Stearman as he stepped away from the x-rays and turned off their backlight. "Good first, I guess," Andy said. "Well, it's not getting worse, despite the blow you took last week." The white-shirted, clean-shaven doctor slid into his chair, swung round, and scribbled something on his notepad. "The bad news is that it's not getting any better, either." "You almost had my hopes up there." Andy's posture was as awkward as it had been since Tim Shipley and Cheap Labor broke his rib. When sitting, Andy could do nothing more than straighten his spine and sit perfect upright as even the slightest slouch would push his expanding lungs against his tender rib, sending shots of pain coursing through his chest. This wouldn't cause much inconvenience for a normally proportioned man, but things become a bit more difficult when you're 6'7" tall. "So getting smacked hasn't set me back?" Andy asked. His doctor – spectacle-wearing and blonde-haired; a man he'd known for most of his professional career – wheeled his chair closer to Andy's and leaned forward. "A little. Put it this way, if it hadn't happened, you'd be a week closer to being fully healed right now, so it could've been worse. You must've really pissed someone off, though: not everyone's willing to punch a wounded man in his broken rib." Not everyone's a vindictive little swine, Andy wanted to say. Instead, he kept his disdain for Vivica J. Valentine internal. "Heh, yeah." "How are those painkillers doing?" "Not bad. Well, they're not having as much of an effect as they were a few weeks ago." "Your body's getting too used to them; I'll write you a strong prescription." Richard Stearman turned back to his computer and pulled his keyboard close. Andy sat and watched as he'd done countless times before. He was a good man, Stearman; a doctor Andy knew he could rely on to give him an honest diagnosis. No-nonsense and straight down the line, but not without compassion. It took a matter of seconds for his fingers to glide across the keyboard, and soon he was tearing a printed prescription from it's perforation. "Here you go," he said, handing it to Andy. "Cheers, chief." The GCW grappler folded the prescription bumbled it into his pocket, wincing as he readjusted his position. "Long night, huh?" Stearman asked, noticing the grey bags beneath Andy's eyes. "Long fucking year so far, let me tell ya." "I'm sure the seven-figure salary goes some way towards mending it." "True, but barely being able to walk without wanting to scream does begin to wear thin on a man's patience." Andy's tone was quiet and subdued; that of a tired man. He closed his eyes and it was a good few seconds before he was able to reopen them. "I don't like complaining about my job, but sometimes a man just needs to take a break." "Then why not take one?" asked Richard, ignorant to the demands of Andy's employer. "It'd do you body a lot of good, and you definitely look like you could do with a couple of stress-free weeks at least." "You think there's such thing as a 'stress-free week' in this job?" Andy cracked a smile as he spoke. "I can't take a break; there's just too much going on there. It's easy to fall down the pecking order if you don't show your face for a couple of weeks. Besides, I gotta get my hands on this Shipley creep, man. Him and his goon are driving me mad." Richard Stearman, despite having been Andy's doctor for over seven years, knew about as much about the wrestling business as Garbage Bag Johnny knew about personal hygiene and grooming. The name "Shipley" was completely alien to him, but he did his best "understanding smile and nod" routine. "Can't be easy dealing with that and what's going on with your agent at the same time, either." A lump formed in Andy's throat. He swallowed it back. It had been more than a month since Rueben's diagnosis, and he still hadn't been to his bedside despite Cherise's requests. It wasn't that he didn't care. Far from it, in fact; Andy cared so much that he didn't think he could bear watching his agent slip off his mortal coil. Andy's business relationship with Rueben Dallas extended longer than he'd known Stearman, and he didn't quite know what he was going to do when Rueben's bell finally tolled. "Hard times, Rich," Andy said, rising to his full height. "The storm'll pass, though." He didn't want to dwell. "Well, good to see you as always, Andy," Richard said, shaking his patient's hand firmly. "You go home and put your feet up now, you hear?" "Heh. If I have time when I'm finished running around cleaning-up my brother's bullshit, maybe I'll do that." "I mean it, Andy," the doctor said, releasing Andy's hand and looking him sternly in the eye. "You're almost thirty-three; maybe it's time to start slowing down?" Andy gave Richard's suggestion just a second's consideration. "Thanks, Doc. See you in a fortnight." * * * * * John Hood's day has been a long one, but at six-thirty it was finally over. Retrieving his briefcase and locking his silver BMW, Hood climbed groggily onto the porch and went inside. Immediately the scent of his wife's cooking tickled his nostrils (Mexican, he estimated) and the air conditioning soothed his clammy skin. Glad to be away from the office, Hood hooked his jacket over the oak bannister, dropped his briefcase by the stairs, and walked through to the kitchen. "Hi, hon," John said, heading instinctively for the drying rack to get a clean glass. He slid it under the fridge's ice dispenser as his ovenglove-clad wife pulled a steaming hot dish of enchiladas from the oven the set them on the worktop. "Dinner smells good," John said, taking a bottle of mineral water from the fridge. "Hey," Alison said, taking off the gloves and turning to her husband. Though propped-up by her shoes' medium heels, John's petite wife still stood at no more than 5'4". A slender woman who'd done well to maintain her shape after the birth of the couple's second child, Alison sported shoulder-length auburn hair and a brilliant white smile that was worth a million dollars. "Hard day at the office?" "Aren't they all?" John said, taking a sip of water before kissing his wife on the cheek. He sat down at the table and kicked his shoes off. "I'm gonna have to pop out after dinner for a little while. Hope you don't mind." "Oh?" Alison said, "for what?" John couldn't help but smile. "You'll see." "Are you talking about what I think you are?" "Maybe." "So Dallas is finally ready to sign?" "Uh-huh," said John, nodding. "You'll be waking up to the Dallas Group's brand new owner tomorrow morning." Alison's reaction was a little less excited than John had expected. She forced a smile and turned back to the cooking, before scooping enchiladas onto plates that had already been prepared. "What's wrong? I thought you'd be happy." "I am happy, John. It's just..." words escaped her for a brief second, and Alison had to turn back around. "You are doing this for the right reasons, aren't you?" John felt his facial muscles tighten. " I'm doing this for you and the kids. Do you have any idea how much money this is going to make us?" "So it has nothing to do with Andy Murray?" John's wife's words hit him like a smack in the mouth. His tired mind began to race as she glared accusations at him, and he felt himself begin to squirm in his chair before he could stop himself. Alison had found out about Murray; it didn't matter how she'd found out, just that she had. "What do you mean?" John asked. "Don't play dumb with me, John," Alison commanded, folding her arms across her chest. "I've done my research; I know that the Dallas Group provide Andy Murray's representation. What are you trying to pull here?" "I swear to God I didn't know Murray was Dallas' until we'd begun due diligence." John raised his hands innocently. "Besides, is Andy Murray being involved really such a bad thing? That guy's the most marketable wrestler in the world, Alison. He'll make us thousands." "So you're telling me that this deal has nothing to do with what happened in Cleveland? Nothing at all?" This was going to take more effort than John thought. The wooden chair ground noisily against the tiled floor as he rose and took both of his wife's hands. "I swear to God, Alison; this has absolutely nothing to do with Cleveland. That was a long time ago; I'd almost forgotten about it until you mentioned it. This is a business decision, nothing else. Okay?" Alison's head ducked as John looked her in the eye. He placed a hand beneath her chin and pushed gently so that their eyes met again. "Okay?" Two young voices filled the air as Alison began the slightest of nods. John turned as his young son and daughter slid into the kitchen and hugged their father's legs with calls of "daddy!" and "yay!". John breathed a sight of relief. Lying to his wife was never easy. * * * * * Andy was presently surprised by the amount of letters waiting for him when he got him; only twelve at first count, and at least a third of them were clearly junk. He slid the offending items into the trashcan as he walked through to the dining area and sat down without a moment's rest. The laptop's standby light was blinking, reminding him that he still had 89 of yesterday's emails still to work through. He pulled the laptop open and brought up his browser. The 89 changed to 145 as Andy hit refresh and Andy groaned, but there was no point in prolonging the inevitable. If he didn't cover them today then he'd only have to cover more tomorrow. After five emails he found his mind start to wander, and by fifteen he was looking for excuses to procrastinate. A trip to the kitchen, perhaps? The dog hasn't been walked today; what better time than now? Is that a wrapper under the sofa? Better pick that up. Andy had given-up at thirty without even realising. He glanced at his watch; seven o' clock and he hadn't even eaten yet. His belly rumbled, his rib ached, and his head was all over the place. Time to do something else. He walked through the open plan great room towards the kitchen area, and set about making a sandwich when he got there. Part of the reason why he chose not to go to university was his inability to concentrate in front of a computer screen, and it was a problem that he still hadn't been able to shake. He was an athlete, not a grey collar bracket basher; built for wrestling rings, not office cubicles. There was only so much more paperwork he could do before driving himself mad, but he'd sit down after dinner and work his way through the remaining emails, and in the morning he'd wake up and do it all over again. It'd take him a lot longer than it should, but Andy would get it done if it killed him. Andy was slapping ham onto buttered bread when he made the decision to seek help. There was no way he'd be able to manage paperwork, training, travelling, and spending time with his son, and tomorrow he'd be jetting off to Australia. The thought of all the mail he might accumulate during that period sent a shiver down his spine. Tomorrow morning, before the flight, Andy would place an advert. If his brother couldn't be trusted with the work, then Andy would have to hire somebody else to do it. A long yawn forced his jaw open and brought water to his eyes. Andy was running on just a couple of hours sleep and felt ready to conk out, but there was still work to be done. He still had to pack his bags and try as much light cardio work as his broken body would allow, and none of it before the inbox was clear. He sighed. Maybe Richard was right; maybe it was time to slow down. Footsteps rumbled down the stairs as Andy munched on his meagre dinner. His youngest brother, Cayle, staggered round the corner and slumped his lifeless body down on the leather couch. Curious, Andy wandered over. A funk of aftershave, alcohol, and nicotine stung his nostrils immediately as Cayle, wearing loose sweatpants and a white vest, vegetated. Andy frowned. He flicked a switch and the lights came on around the television, drawing a displeased wince from Cayle, who covered his eyes with a tattooed forearm. "Hey, quit it," he croaked. "What's the matter: feeling rough?" "Rougher than a badger's arse. Heavy night." "Wanna do some work?" "My stomach's in knots, bro. No way." Andy frowned and set his sandwich down on the TV unit; Cayle's answer was predictable, but that didn't ease his brother's disappointment. "What the hell are you doing with yourself, kid? It's dinner time and you're just crawling out of bed. Have you even showered today?" Cayle's head moved in what Andy thought was an attempted shake. "You've left me in the shit with this paperwork, you know. I thought that if there was one thing I could trust you with, it would be this." "What can I say? Ain't my thing." "This isn't a fucking joke, you know." Andy's tone soured as his brow furrowed. "You're taking the piss." "Quit sounding like Dad, man." "'Quit sounding like Dad'? You're living in my house, RENT-FREE, and you can't even cope with filing a few letters every day. I'll say what I want, thank you very much." Sensing his brother's anger rising, Cayle sat up on the sofa, and looked at his brother through squinting eyes. "Chill, Andy. If it's just 'a few letters' then what's all the fuss about?" "I have a schedule, Cayle. You know, like a normal human being? I can't be doing this shit when I've got Shipley, Labor, and Bagwell to worry about, let alone Rueben." "Alright, I'll try again if it means tha--" "No, you can forget it." Andy wagged a finger at Cayle. "I'm going to get someone else to do it; I can't trust you any more, Cayle." Guilt sunk its claws into Cayle. Though strangled by his hangover, he could tell by the exasperation in Andy's voice that he wouldn't tolerate another disappointment. He knew he'd done wrong, but something was preventing him from holding his hands up and making right his wrongs. He swallowed, knowing that there would be no more half-baked apologies and excuses. Andy shook his head. "You need to get your shit together." "I'm trying, man," Cayle groaned. "It's just... hard." "You've been out of prison for two goddamn years, Cayle! Don't feed me that bullshit." "I'll try to--" "You'll 'try' nothing, Cayle. You will, on the otherhand, have found yourself a job by the time I get back from Australia." Andy picked his sandwich up. "Otherwise, you're going to have to find yourself a new place to live." He stormed away with such haste that his brother had little chance to respond. The emails would have to wait another day. * * * * * Floyd and John drew a few curious glances from doctors and nurses as they strode through the barren hospital corridors. 8:30pm was a highly irregular visiting hour, but the fact that they'd gotten past reception gave the medics little reason to be suspicious of their motives. "Where is he?" John asked as they made their way through the building's most isolated ward. "Third door on the right," Floyd wheezed. His stumpy legs were struggling to keep up with Hood's brisk pace, and perspiration was beginning to build on his forehead. John saw the door ahead and stopped at its threshold. His pulse quickened. Rueben Dallas lay just a few feet away. Soon he'd have what he wanted. Hood turned to Floyd as the lawyer pulled out a handkerchief and dabbed the sweat away. "You got the contract?" he asked. Floyd nodded his head and pulled a large brown envelope from inside his jacket. He handed it to John who pulled out several sheets of paper bound together at the corner by a single staple. Thumbing his way to the end of the contract and folding the pages over, John looked for the signature box. "Right here?" Floyd nodded. John returned the gesture and went to enter the room, but found a plump hand clamping to his wrist before he could do so. "What are you doing, John?" "I'm going to convince him to sign." Hood shrugged his lawyer's grasp off and entered Rueben's room. A white-walled chamber illuminated only by the dim light of a lamp on the bedside table, Dallas' room wasn't the most extravagant space in the world, but what more did a dying man need than a bed, a heart monitor, and a drip. John wasn't there to admire the scenery, though. His eyes latched onto Rueben Dallas' dying form almost immediately, and what he saw jarred even him. The man was a shell. Drooping skin hung loosely from a heavy bone structure, and spots and blemishes had appeared all over Ruben's dark complexion. His breaths were long, slow, and laboured, and it looked like every heave of his lungs was the hardest of missions. Seeing Rueben in such a state almost made John feel guilty about what he was going to do. He carefully navigated around Rueben's bed and sat down. Looking at Dallas' closed eyes, John articulated: "Hello, Rueben." Rueben Dallas woke-up. His eyes parted to the slightest of gaps, but that was his body's only movement. A smile crossed John Hood's lips. He rose he the contract so that Rueben could see it and tapped the edge of his pen against it. "You've been ducking me, haven't you?" John dreaded to think what Rueben might have said had his body allowed him speech. "Come on; the quicker we get this over and done with, the better." Placing the contract on Rueben's bed, John tried to slide the pen beneath Dallas' skinny fingers. The pen predictably slid between the digits and tumbled down to the floor, drawing a quick sigh from Hood. "Come on, you can do it," he said as he picked the pen up. "It's easy." Rueben's eyes closed. He'd been reluctant to sell the agency to a man of Hood's reputation in the first place, but the money was too good to turn down. Either way, he'd just woken up. It would be at least an hour before he had enough strength to perform even the simple task of writing his signature. He thought about hitting his panic button for a moment, then remembered how far away it was. John put the pen in Rueben's hand again. This time he took a more vigorous approach by grabbing his wrist and bringing it to the paper. Again, the pen slid down to the floor. As Hood lost his patience, Rueben felt fear begin to permeate through his withered body. He let his head slump to the side, and that's when he saw the panic button. A simple nudge of that button and nurses would be in the room in seconds. It was only a few inches away. He had to go for it. It took all of Rueben's strength to slide his arm towards the button. Moving glacially, his limb made it to within a centimetre of the button before Hood noticed what was going on and pushed away the cabinet to which the button was attached. "Uh-uh, I don't think so," said John. But Rueben would not give up. It wasn't supposed to happen like this. He would fight John Hood with what little strength he had left. "Now sign the fucking contract." The pen found its way into Rueben's hand again. Hood gripped tighter this time. "Sign, damnit!" Hood was losing his patience. He wrapped his own hand around Rueben's and pushed it against the paper. "Sign the fucking contract!" Still nothing. But Rueben could not fight as John pulled his hand across the contract, etched a messy, inky scrawl across the bottom of the contract. Hood pulled the sheet away immediately and smiled. "Now that wasn't so hard, was it?" He stood up, sneering at Rueben Dallas and wagging the contract in his face. "It's mine now. Mine. It's a shame you won't be around to see what I do to your precious little agency." Floyd was already gone by the time Hood left the room. He wanted nothing to do with that John had done; not even for an extra $2,000. * * * * * Alison Hood was so nervous she was almost shaking. For ten minutes she'd had a number she'd never deleted up on her phone's screen, yet she was still struggling to find the bottle to hit the call button. Would John find out about what she was about to do? Probably. Would she live to regret it? Definitely. But somebody had to do something, and she was the only one that knew. Though her husband insisted that acquiring the Dallas Group was strictly business, she knew that he had never fully forgiven Andy Murray for what had happened in Cleveland. Despite his good points, John Hood was never one to forgive and forget. She new he still harboured ill will for Murray by the way he juddered every time his name came up on a television screen. She knew that he would love nothing more than to see him burn. She couldn't just sit by and watch it happen. Andy Murray was a good man; a man who'd suffered enough of her husband's wrath already, and a man who didn't deserve to lose his career over a decade old misunderstanding. So she hit "call". * * * * * Several states away, Andy Murray saw the unknown number appear on his cell. He hit reject. Companies could send him as many letters and emails as they wanted, but he would not take agency calls at this time of night.
