One Little Indian

February 2007
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: February 2007 One little, two little, three little Indians! Four little, five little, six little Indians, Seven little, eight little, nine little Indians! Ten little Indian boys! Lesser chief Walks-in-the-rain gave a small smile of amusement. “Hiawatha?” he asked Katie, noting the book that was in her duffel bag as she unpacked her things in the guest cabin. She smiled a little sheepishly. “Aye, Hiawatha,” she said. “Study fiction as well as fact, was always me wont.” Native American peoples on the continent were divided into many tribes, and studying with all of them was not an option. What drew Katie to ask to learn of the Maliseet tribe was a simple matter of location. She was pretty clueless on what differentiated the many tribes from each other, except possibly what they hunted and gathered for food, what clothing they normally wore before ‘modern’ times, and what tribal languages they spoke. Names were a little different, too, although most Native Americans now also adopted a regular English name for use with the general populace of the United States. Lesser chief Walks-in-the-rain used the name Paul Eastman. The ‘Eastman,’ he explained, was a reference to the direction in which the sun rose. His family always loved the sun, and the heralding of a new day. Walks-in-the-rain was a loose English translation of the name given him by his tribe. Of course, the study session Katie had been granted, meant that she was a guest of the Maliseets... and had to follow their customs. Despite standing within the boundaries of northeastern Maine, the diminutive Irishwoman was not, as she was told, standing on American soil... these lands where she was studying, were the legal property of the Maliseet tribe. The federal government granted the Maliseets full authority over this area, to govern themselves—and their guests—as they saw fit. Fortunately, the worst punishment that a guest could incur for unacceptable behavior was to be cast out of Maliseet property, not to be allowed to return. Crimes against the Maliseet people generally resulted in the elders turning the offender over to the federal American government for prosecution. “You say you came to our people because of location,” Walks-in-the-rain mused. “Tell me more of this.” “Ye people are in lands not far from where I live,” Katie explained. “I rent an apartment in Burlington, Vermont. Sure and Maine is a relatively short distance. Further, I have always loved the New England region... ‘tis why I chose Vermont as a place to make me home.” Walks-in-the-rain tilted his head and seemed to ponder the answer. Katie lowered her head slightly in a sign of respect. She did not know if her answer would be considered weak, or strong, poor or good. But it was from her heart, and it was the truth. Finally Walks-in-the-rain nodded in approval. “Enjoy your stay,” he said. “There will be a dinner in the communal cabin at 6:30 pm. Please arrive a little early. Even as we take American names for use with the American populace, you will be given a Maliseet name to use with us.” Katie gave what she understood was the Maliseet gesture of gratitude. “Sure and I would be honored,” she said. 6:15 pm Katie sat with the Maliseet community in the cabin, introducing herself to the people and telling them a little about her desire to learn more about their culture. She always liked to know a little about upcoming opponents, and she was facing a Native American tag-team at the upcoming World Wide. “The Suquamish and Dumwamish tribes live on the opposite coastline, and have never had direct contact with the Maliseet,” said one of the elders. “And yet you come to us for study?” Katie nodded, this time keeping her head erect, facing the elders directly, and speaking firmly, though still earnestly. “The clich’d comparison of apples and oranges aside, I feel I can learn a great deal from ye people, even if ye be a different tribe,” she said. “Sure and I would be honored to be ye guest over the next few days, and learn what ye have to teach me about ye culture.” The elders conferred among each other and with Walks-in-the-rain. Finally the oldest among them turned back to Katie. “Sit with us, and receive your Maliseet name,” he said. Katie bent her head in respectful assent and sat in the circle among them. She tolerated a puff of the ceremonial pipe with every ounce of fortitude she could muster. The Maliseets understood the difficulties of non-smokers partaking of Native American pipes and took no offense at the slight but noticeable reddening of her face. They gave her a parchment on which were written the lyrics to a traditional Maliseet song, printed out in English phonetics. The community sang the song slowly so that Katie was able to keep up with pronouncing the foreign words. Finally the oldest of the elders placed a necklace of beads around Katie’s neck, and clipped a pair of bone-and-feather earrings to her earlobes. He spoke a short phrase in Maliseet, which Katie realized was the name they were giving her. The elder then gave the loose English meaning of the name. “We dub you Vixen-running-through-the-grass, young visitor... and welcome you as a guest of the Maliseet.” The Monarch Butterfly blushed a deep rosy color, blinking slowly at the Elders. There was no mistaking the name they had given her, as it was spoken in English... and it was not given in jest. She bit her tongue to avoid disrespecting her hosts... but why were they not returning the favor? The youngest elder’s daughter, probably the most Americanized member of the community, raised a hand, smiling gently. The elders nodded to her, recognizing her to speak. “Modern American slang uses the word vixen in a much more negative connotation,” she explained. “They use it to describe a girl or woman that is considered... promiscuous.” The community all nodded in sober understanding, and the elders gave Katie a gesture of apology. The late-teen girl turned back to Katie. “A vixen is merely a female fox. The name has not been given to you in disrespect or insult. Please remember what early American settlers called a male donkey... and a female dog.” Katie let out the breath she’d been holding and nodded, and she smiled at the young lady and the elders. “Thank ye for the honor... and the explanation.” The Maliseets laughed softly and the meal was begun. Rain-cloud-on-the-sun, the Malisset guide assigned to Katie, explained Maliseet cooking and the traditional diet they ate before modern times, as the meal progressed. Katie listened intently to the stories, asking a question or two here and there and graciously sampling whatever was offered her. The community children hustled about collecting plates and utensils as the meal was concluded, and musicians prepared their instruments for a traditional Maliseet celebratory dance. “Close your eyes and focus on the music,” Katie’s guide said to her. “Hear it with your heart as well as your ears.” Katie closed her eyes and listened. Not just listened. LISTENED. Before the dinner, Rain-cloud-on-the-sun explained some ways in which Maliseets composers got inspired for the music they played. Katie focused on the same inspirations so she could let the music into her heart and her soul. Her arms lifted slightly from her sides as her lips slowly spread into a small smile of admiration for the music’s simple beauty. Some of the Maliseets sang along to the music as they danced. Katie opened her eyes again and was shown the various steps, arm and body movements, and postures that made up the dance. It was all a very different experience from the various dance styles Katie had ever studied or done. It took a short while before she was able to perform it correctly with the rest of the Maliseets. Once she had the dance right, her guide explained the song and its meaning. Once she knew what the words meant, the dance took on a whole new meaning, on a whole new level. Her face was lit with a sense of wonder at the end of the dance. The Maliseets smiled at her with a knowing look in their eyes. They knew better than anyone, the spiritual experience of the dance—the very purpose it was meant to provide. “A much better story than Hiawatha?” Walks-in the-rain asked Katie, and all the Maliseets laughed. Most of them had read the story. The mattress and pillow for Katie’s bed were filled with goose down, though they used modern wool blankets and comforters. The diminutive Irishwoman slept soundly and peacefully; dreaming of the experience of the whole evening; the feasting, the dancing, and the storytelling. 10:30 am After breakfast, Rain-cloud-on-the-sun and his wife and daughter took Katie out onto the lake in a traditional Maliseet canoe for some fishing. They used modern rods and reels, in acceptance of changing times, and because they believed that the modern rods worked better than older Maliseet fishing tools. One point that the Maliseets took care to explain to Katie several times was that modern tribal life was a careful balance of traditional, older ways and modern American ways. They generally lived in modern houses and apartment buildings, with many modern conveniences like microwave ovens, televisions, automobiles, and even computers and internet web sites. They wore mostly modern clothing, though they preferred cotton and denim over other fabrics, and they were all well schooled, often attending American schools and universities, and were all fluent in English, both written and spoken—though many Maliseet communities living in Canada wrote and spoke French instead. While some younger Maliseets embraced modern American and Canadian cultures completely, some understood the need to preserve the old cultures, the old traditions, and the traditional Maliseet language... and the tribal communities all worked together to keep them alive and going. “Women generally grew crops and foraged for edible plant-based foods... nuts, roots, fruit, tubers, and vegetables,” explained Rain-cloud-on-the-sun’s wife, whose Maliseet name meant ‘Dandelion petals’ in English. “Despite our people accepting the modern advent of women’s rights and gender equality, few Maliseet women have taken up hunting... though quite a few Maliseet women have become accomplished anglers.” Katie teased her line along the water the way Dandelion-petals had shown her. “Sure and do ye still fish primarily for food, when ye can go to a grocery store?” Dandelion-petals and Rain-cloud-on-the-sun both laughed softly. “Modern grocery stores ensure we always have enough to eat, while in the older days, there were leaner times,” Dandelion-petals explained. “But while we still hunt and fish primarily to keep our traditional ways alive, we always consume what we catch. We still make tools and simple jewelry from bone pieces and feathers, and coats and boots from hides. To discard carcasses back into the wild would offend the spirits we still honor.” Katie blushed a bit and lowered her head, saying nothing. She’d learned not to preach her devout Roman Catholic faith to others who did not follow it, although it was hard for her to understand how others still believed in these older religions. When she looked up again, she noticed Rain-cloud-on-the-sun and Dandelion-petals, and their daughter, Eldest-cub, all grinning at her. Eldest-cub opened the top button of her shirt to show a small silver crucifix she wore underneath it, very similar in design to Katie’s. “The spirits we have always revered were never believed to be gods,” Dandelion-petals said. “And even with the acceptance of Anglican and European religions by many Native American tribes, including many Maliseet, we have never seen them to contradict our older beliefs... in fact, many of us feel your religions actually encompass them. Revere and honor do not mean worship. In fact, one of the Ten Commandments of the Bible is to ‘honor your mother and father.’ The animal spirits are revered and honored... but many of us worship the same God you do.” “There is a ceremony some tribes undertake,” Rain-cloud-on-the-sun added. “Perhaps if you took part in it, you would understand the distinction better.” Katie looked at all three of them and then nodded. “Aye... sure and I think I would like that.” Dusk Rain-cloud-on-the-sun explained that holding the ceremony at dusk allowed them to call on both daylight and nocturnal animals. He had learned modern hypnotic techniques to aid in the trance work used in the ceremony. He used a soft kaleidoscopic strobe light to help induce the trance. His voice was carefully controlled as he instructed Katie through the mental exercises. “The first animal you see, will be your guide,” he said. “I do not see ye here with me,” Katie said. “I am not,” Rain-cloud-on-the-sun said quickly. “For others to tread the same ground you are treading during the ceremony, would offend the animal spirit.” Katie slowed her breathing. It wasn’t hard. She had been studying yoga for a few years now and breath control was as natural to her now as blinking her eyes. “Think about the various fauna you know to inhabit these regions,” Rain-cloud-on-the-sun said to her. “Be patient. Think about all the fauna, and let the one that answers, answer.” Katie imagined herself crossing a meadow toward the border of a forest. The soft lapping of a gentle stream flowed beside her. A rustle in a nearby bush caught her ears. “Vixen-running-through-the-grass...” Katie turned her head instinctively, although she was still getting accustomed to being called this. “Approach me and fear me not, Vixen-running-through-the-grass...” Katie walked over to the bush and looked down. The hint of red fur glided lithely through the foliage. “Know me, and you will know cunning. Know me, and you will know the instinct of nurturing family. Know me, and you will know both predator and prey, for I am both in the cycles of life. These are my gifts given by the Creator.” Katie blinked. “By God?” she inquired. “Is that the name you call Him by?” the voice was curious. “Aye...” Katie’s voice dropped as she spoke; a sense of wonder filling her. A twitching of salt-and-pepper whiskers emerged from the foliage. A long snout tipped by a twitching black nose trailed behind. The gentle dark eyes regarded Katie with curiosity and calm. The ears stood erect; poised to detect danger. “Know me, and know the gifts given me by the Creator,” said the red fox that had emerged from the bushes. Katie’s eyes snapped open to see Rain-cloud-on-the-sun watching her calmly. She smiled at him. “No,” Rain-cloud-on-the-sun said as Katie opened her mouth to speak. “Your bond with the animal spirit is sacred. Tell no one else of it... not I, not Walks-in-the-rain, nobody. Just as the Bible tells you to go into your room and close the door to pray in private... keep the animal spirit in your heart... between you and the spirit. The Creator made them into living souls just as we were made into the same.” Katie nodded. “I understand. Thank ye.” Katie left for home the following morning. Most of the community gathered to see her off. The new lore tingled in her breast and in her head, and she was happy for the experience. Now, though, she was happy to return to her family... her husband and her children. She would have a day to spend with them before she had to leave for Worldwide. She passed a Maliseet working as a state trooper at the border of the Maliseet lands. He tipped his hat pleasantly, and paused, doing a double-take. “Oh,” he said. “Have a good day, Ms. O’Clare. My son likes to watch you on television. The Monarch Butterfly.” Katie smiled at the trooper and gave him an autographed photo for his son. She waved as she drove off. The trooper looked at the photo and paused at the signature: “Katie O’Clare... Vixen-running-through-the-grass” He looked back at Katie’s car as it cruised down the road, blinking curiously.
