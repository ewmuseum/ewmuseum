The Ghost Of Joseph Glidden

Date: Fall, 1873 The Ghost Of Joseph Glidden A Garbage Bag Production Special Feature I. Without Nail Or Spike When I woke up, I had no idea where I was or how I had made it there. I thought about old Rip Van Winkle and how he'd been asleep for twenty years, and how if I'd have slept that long, I'd have been more concerned with being ripped forever from whatever dream I had been living for the past two decades than with a world that had passed along without me. The thing is about dreams, you forget them if you don't write them down, and even if you do take the time and scrawl them out, you can only capture so much before you start making logical assumptions that merely attempt to reconcile an imaginary continuity in a place where the normal functions of reason do not apply. The act of recording does little to preserve the memory of a dream, just the memory of trying to remember. All the unrecorded details flutter away. Perhaps death was the eternal act of waking; the moment before the reassurance of recognition kicked in stretched like a dense taffy. I was in a dark barn that rank of dust and shit. It seemed fitting. It distracted away first the most recent details of my arrival, and I felt as if I were being introduced to something I was to pretend foreign though I suspected just the vaguest recollection of the scenery. I was certain that though I had never been here before, I had been somewhere before in bones like these with things arranged around me in somewhat similar fashions. I contemplated rising to approach and examine things, as if there was a certain feasible purpose to it, but I was just as well. I couldn't remember how to go about the task and remained there where I was that nothing bad could come of it if I was to continue lying there. It was all I knew for sure, despite many hypotheses that presented themselves to me untested. "What in the hell are you doing in my barn?" breathed through a shotgun. I eyed the nozzle, or what I called the nozzle for the lack of remembering a better term. "So this is it, huh?" I thought, perhaps aloud. "I asked you a question, son!" "My last moments spent knowing that it's not called a nozzle, but for the life of me . . . god damn." "I know what you are." He poked the gun at my ribs. "You're a spy." "A spy?" I parroted uncertainly, still trying to reverse define the piece of object in front of me before my untimely but inevitable demise. "You've come to steal the secrets of my wire, haven't you?" He barked. "Haven't you!" "Haven't?" Perhaps that was it. It sounded familiar; the haven't of his gun pistoning off my back as the unrelenting flow of matter slowly exploding through space slowly rolled me over to my other end. "You're coming with me," he informed, dragging me by the leg for emphasis. "And you're going to tell me everything you know." It was more the impression of a barn than a barn itself. It was far too dark to have walls and other such physical features that you would imagine barns to have. He found a rusted iron ring, the work of some somesmith, and pulled it from the darkness to uncover a trapdoor along the dragging surface. He struck a spark to torch a bundle of sticks and weeds ablaze and continued the dragging down a slope with several nearly equidistant corners. How it had felt to feel, I thought, as my body awakened from a long numbness. I had almost forgotten. It was something to think inside of yourself I thought, rather than having thought I thought, whence from dreams all thought is external on a concentric imagination of self, the external pressure combated by an opposite determination only realized by its own destruction before the eye once again opens and all is blank, momentarily. So this is what it was to be alive, huh? It was quite a dream for a figment of a dream. The other figures in dreams could have dreamt, too. It was unimportant, but seemed importantly so, as if to ward off babble. He sat me in a chair, as if I were some rigid form, but I didn't sludge through the handiworked slats. To prop me up, my captor bound me in a wound, prickly wire, the hot ironed melt of hardened, twisted pins poking me. It felt strange. I think it was nice. It was something. "Who sent you?" He demanded, still waving the shotgun like it was spangled. "Who's trying to steal my secrets?" I didn't know how to respond, or whether, even or not, I should. I realized the body wasn't my own. It belonged now to the malleted pins. There wasn't much I could do about it, they were digesting me. "No doubt about it," I, laughing, coughed and shook back and forth, only to learn the nature of thorns and the stillness of meditation. "I'll give you to the count of three." He cocked the shotgun. "One." He raised the gun and aimed. "Two." His arms shook, not enough to notice, but I felt the vibrations quiver off him. He lowered the gun. "I'm going to find out who sent you," he rasped. "I'm going to get it out of you by hook or by crook. You think about that." And then he left. He left me there. It was the old fencing craze of '73, and people were going mad over fences. I, as a figure myself, figured things like this happened often enough anyway. II. It Must Be Laid Crooked Or Else Every night, the thought of sheep and other livestock jumping over fences kept Joseph miserably awake. These were the thoughts that haunted him. It was only when he could envision livestock, securely contained in pens, that he could drift to dream, and when he did, I made sure to be there. I watched him from a distance. I always watched from a distance. Whether it was some distance or some more distance, I could not say; just that I always watched. It was peculiar, to say the least, being in someone's dream to have seen what he's dreamt. In busier circumstances, it can oftentimes be a task just to solve whose dream it is. He stood alone, this time, in a field, and it was either his dream or mine. I wasn't certain, and at the time, I wasn't aware it even was a dream. The indicators were all there, though: the forfeit of control, the absence of thought, the backdrop blurred with dead details. It didn't feel like one of my dreams. I may not have even been in it. I was too riddled with horns to recognize myself. Horns and legs, hooved ones; hooved horns, even. There he was, pacing around in those boots, stepping amok a much, checking the cows, taking stock of stock, stuck amok a muck. I had been spotted, and also, by the patterned thoracic pertubances gathering about me. He turned, shifting the earth beneath him that I felt the world skitter beneath me at his determined soles, like a pair of hatchets to hack the world in two, chop by chop by chop. He skated towards me, carving sluices behind him; fixing divots like a ballpeen hammer fixes. Heft the weight over the shoulder and let gravity do the rest. "What kind of animal are you?" he demanded. "And why aren't you in your pen?" "I reckon I'm some kind of mythical something or other," I spouted gushing infinite wisdom. "Did the devil send you?" "No," someone said, but it wasn't him, and it wasn't me. It was the remarkable work of some great ventriloquist, and I puppeted about with all my hooves and whanot. "I sent the devil." And then, space swooshing like a falcon, I had flattened out and felt all crusty like old paint on an old man who was alive before and alive once again through the vesicular art of portraitry. There, ricketing aloft in their sturdiwork of conversations and wooden furniture things, he exchanged words with Lucinda. "Is everything alright, Joseph? Elva told me she heard you talking to someone in the barn." "A rather curious one, that Elva. Rather imaginative, though." "I just wanted to make sure everything was alright." "It's fine." The transaction complete, the lady retired to her quarters and the gentleman his, in stocking capped, somber night dress. I had tried to escape. That's what dreams were, just a bunch of obstacles; chains to unlock, pens to hurdle, ropes to catch before the floor inevitably spirals to oblivion beneath you. III. The Corners We Played I had meshed with the wire so infused that it was impossible to tell where wire ended and flesh began. To escape a wire, you must yourself become a wire. I must myself became a wire. He visited me again, much to the same accord as our introduction, for two days, and on the third day, he had gone unreasonably mad. Where I once was a man wrapped up in wire, sat a reel of wire wrapped up into a man. "What have I created?" his shotgun trembled. He was going to do me away right there, at that distance. "The world must never lay eyes on you. I hope you understand." How could I have understood, though? I was a man made out of wires, constantly readying myself for death. Safe bets make steady rewards. "Father!" Joseph turned, and there was Elva. I looked at her through eyes at the end of tubes, like a lobster's eyes, and she glanced over for a moment before returning her attention to her father, who stepped between us to shield her from my visage. "Go back to the house, Elva. I don't want you to see this, and I don't want you to tell anyone what you did see . . . not even your mother." "I'm not leaving, Father." "You obey me, or so help me," he incompleted. "You're not well, Father." "He's a god damned spy, Elva! He's after the wire!" "That's just it," she huffed. "Do you know how long it's been since you were last in the house?" "I was there this morning." "No you weren't. You've been in here for almost a week working on your fence. Please, just come back home and give it a rest. You're not well." "What day is it?" He looked at me as he asked. "Sunday." His mustached lip furled in response. "I can't rightly kill a man on Sunday." Her eyes searched, confused as he pouched his shotgun. "What are you looking at, Father?" "That man over there. Don't you see him?" He motioned towards me. "The man made of wire." She didn't respond. I don't know how she could have. To be fair, his descriptions were generous. I was nothing if not a crude doll, just a wretch of tangled wire, not wound tightly enough to span a fence so that it took a vague shape that might be mistaken for a man under the right conditions. "He's a fine man, I reckon." Joseph examined me with the newborn flash of madness stirring up something wicked. "He would make a fine suitor." "You're scaring me, Father." "My empire will need an heir: one that's half Glidden and half wire." She left, then, and he stayed back, still looking at me. "And we'll need one before tomorrow, because I'm still going to have to kill you." He removed the chair from me, which was starting too threatingly to become me as did the wire, and led me hidden out through the light as I slithered behind him like a wiry snake of man, caught in the grass by my own thatches in soft permeations of dirt. IV. The Time-Honored Stone Wall I had learned of his fetishes through his dreams, the ones with sharp pricks, punishing him for his tresspasses as the barbs tresspassed him. It looked like a jellyfish, bent over, puckering out rust colored juices. He was pale, a cold and overwhelming pallor. It was more than an aversion to goofy shortpantaloons. He cleansed in full trouser, and relieved, and procreated, perhaps having inserted his relations into common trouser, or a larger trouser worn over the undertrouser. The weight of his undressed dreams compelled him, paying twofold penance for his wrested unrest. The eye in the middle of his invertibrate fissure sprang open, sending me leering back from an open claw. It had to have been a dream, perhaps my own dream, and I thought it might have been. When I closed my eyes, I would see this again, myself with eyes open face to face with that unsightly cyclops. He was a sick man, tangled as if his body and mind were stranded by a grindstone about himself. He had to stretch his arm a far length to turn the crank. "Do you think I've gone mad?" He asked me, the wire man. "Is it mad that a man wants to combine his legacy and his bloodline so that both may live forever?" I had no answers for him. I was after all, just a tangled mess of wire without a mouth for all my teeth, which pointed every which way so that I could chew, but it felt really awkward to chew that it could only be described and understood, but never imagined by those acclimated to teeth that align in rows and face against each other. I chewed like a saw, only against surfaces, and could never swallow, only sharpen to hack and chew more efficiently. "Why the wire has fused with you so seamlessly, I cannot be certain," he enviously ascertained. "It didn't take to me." I sadly looked on -- not that I was sad, but that the way I was looking was sad. My eyes were the size of pins, and they were made of pins. "Why didn't it take to me?" He gushed deparation and his disparate, all-purpose feature wept something, some venom I dared not touch with my hands rusty enough as they were. "Why you?" We were in the house, now. It was much darker than the barn, and he was powerless, captive to the same laws that bound the sleeping. Lucinda was beside him, and I between them; I between him and all, bound around him like a fly paralyzed in a vacant web. As a web, I, mindless, too, began to drift off, from nothing to something enough where there was something at all, slightly worse off than I would have otherwise been. I once possessed heart and mind and soul, and I still did to some degree, that they were all, like flesh, built from strands of fencing so that they felt as hands feeling foreign fingers felt. I was a particle, shaved off at ends, to nothingness, where logic is abided but not produced. I tasted not what I chewed, the nerves in my teeth rusted over, growing sharper by chiseling away the rounded edges, the humps and gristles that make a British Sea Captain's teeth. Meat softened into gum, and gum took so that wounds impressed but did not bleed. The frame reshaped around its enclosures, like a plaster hardening that never hardens, only looms until kneaded. I had thinned, long enough to slip through cracks, and when I did, I thought I had disappeared. V. And Everyone Said What Is Considering it foolish of me to lie, I fully admitted that I was dreaming to all save myself. For one must lie to himself before he can lie to others, or to be honest to others, for that matter. For one must lie to himself. It is necessary for survival. There must have been somewhere to go, to keep going on, unless it would have been right to stop, and to know if or when to stop is a can of worms in itself. Elva was there. I had been holding her hostage as a character in a dream, knowing that there would be no plausible end but escape. I looked into her fake, glassy eyes and knew she could not come with me, and I knew that I could not let her go. Her invention was more perfect than nature, too perfect, almost, to have grown from the seedling of a maniacal account of wire that she, too, was not made of ugly wire like I was. We had agreed to meet beside the tracks and hop aboard a passing freight, or rather I had designed such an agreement. She held a carrier, packed with whatever doilies I thought she thought might come in handy, me knowing nothing of handiness in the first place to befit her luggage with nonsense I thought might have been less useful than useless and oftentimes nonexistent things I held to mistaken standards. "Where are your bags?" She asked, but in her blank eyes, I could tell she was already piecing things together about the bagless man made from long wires. "I'm not coming." It needed not to be said, and yet it needed to be said. I had said it, but even if I hadn't, she had heard it. "I thought." "I'm not going to argue about it. You have to go." "But I don't understand. I thought you were coming with." "The train," I deflected. "It's coming." She turned and looked and grew louder over the chugging engine. "Why aren't you going with me?" "I can't. I belong here with your father." "He's going to kill you." "I'll escape." "How?" "Somehow." The train was here. "You have to jump on now." She looked at the train passing and then back at me briefly. "Will you write?" I nodded, and I could have sworn she recognized me beyond just being a figment, but that was just my psyche playing tricks is all. I was just watching it all, there from the center of it, and she jumped on and disappeared, better that she did it in front of my eyes than had I been forced to escape only to have her disappear when I'd wake up something sober. It was better off, too, that she had not seen who I was beneath the wire skin. It was the same inside, but what is outside sometimes does not belong within, just as what is within sometimes does not belong with out. If it was any consolation to her, I had no idea what that meant either, but it was a little too late for consolation at that point. VI. The Thorns On The Hedges I sat there on the third day, and he had gone unreasonably mad. His shotgun was aimed steady to my forehead, the barrel breathing on my face. "That's it!" He lifted his eye from level with the gun. "What's what?" "What's 'what's what?'" "'What's what's' what's it?" "It's the barrel." "What's the barrel?" "No. It's the barrel, so if 'what's what's' what's it, and it's the barrel, then 'what's what's' what's the barrel, then 'what's what's' what's the barrel. What's the barrel's it's the barrel, so what's it, and 'what's what's' what's what's." "What?" Even with this realization, these last thoughts were no more worthwhile than my first last thoughts, the thoughts I had thought to be my last before that. Truth be told, I had spent no more time working on the first last thought or any others in between them. A headful of thoughts is too fragile to withstand a bullet. It's too fragile to withstand pretty much anything. I was a ghost haunting a ghost, invisible spaces away from being a ghost of a ghost haunting a ghost, who had chosen me to haunt him. It was clearly so and not the other way around.
