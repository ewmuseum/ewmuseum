Arizona Skies

February 2008
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: Tuesday, February 2008 On a warm Friday night in, shit wherever we were, the lights were turned up bright. Bugs of all manners swarmed around the lights, each trying to get the best view of the game. The entire field was illuminated as if the sun had sat in the bleachers to watch the game. Everyone wanted to see this one play off, proven by the stacked and rowdy crowd in the stands. This was the kind of game that got circled on the calendar at the beginning of the year, the one coach preached about since everyone first stepped between the hash marks. For weeks the entire town had been buzzing with anticipation for today. The rivals were in town and that meant there would be tailgating, loud music, and far too much trash-talking to be health for high school sports. The stakes only helped to serve to bring the entire thing to a feverish pitch. The winner could be heading to the state playoffs, the loser would be heading back home to prepare for the next season. A lot of kids knew what was at stake for them. In this area the playoffs were littered with college scouts looking for the next big signing day name. A big hit in the playoffs, an amazing catch, the perfect throw, it could all make the different between having to take out loans for college and getting a free ride. Emotions ran high for everyone, regardless of if they would be taking a snap or not. This one would get physical, most likely before the coin was even tossed. Someone would end up suffering. When your world centered around the athletic adventures of high school kids, not even old enough to buy a drink, you lived and died by what happened on the gridiron. In some manners of perspective, I died. Eighteen years of work went out the window in a matter of minutes. Those eighteen years defined me, made me, and without them I was as good as dead. The other teams coach said he never told them to do it. It was supposed to have been a normal slant block he said. But one of the blockers forgot to slate and instead I went down like a sack of rocks. The trainers didn’t even need to get halfway across the field to see how bad it was. I didn’t even have to look at my knee. The collective gasp from the crowd told me just what I felt. Pain gripped me tight, choking me, and before I knew it, it had me seeing black. By the look out the window, a few hours had passed when I had finally woken up. The game had long since passed and I would have to read about the outcome in the newspaper. After just a few minutes of being awake the pain in my knee started to throb and brought my entire body into a state of agony that made even simple task like thinking impossible. It took all the focus I could muster just to ring the bell for the nurse. When she finally entered the room, she seemed to know just what to do. With syringe in hand, she punctured the IV drip and soon I could feel the pain washing away from my body. I could hear her talking to someone, though I couldn’t see who it was. “Poor child, his knee is just barely hanging on.” Voices started to get fuzzy, my vision soon joined it. “He should have been watching out for it” I wanted to see who was in the room with her, who said that, but sleep overtook me. I succumbed to the sweet lullaby of slumber. I don’t know how long had passed since I had last been awake, but I’m assuming it must have been for some time because it had been dark outside the window. Again it seemed like the pain was the cause of my insomnia. I instinctively reached for the button, pressing it rapidly in hopes to speed up the nurse. I needed my relief, my escape from the pain. I waited, and then pressed the button. I waited even more and again pressed the button. Nothing happened. After nearly breaking the button pressing it so much, the nurse finally showed up thankfully. She didn’t have the syringe with her though. Pain continued to rip through my body, scourging every nerve. “I’m sorry” she said sincerely. For just a second the pain seemed to disappear as I realized what she meant. Then, once that moment passed, the pain set back in. Trying to speak, all I could muster was a groan as I reached out for her. She took my hand, rubbing the back of it to try and sooth me. Though my vision was still blurred from the incredible pain I found myself in, I could see a man walk into the room. “You’re not giving my son any more of those damned narcotics!” The voice boomed throughout the small room, frightening the poor nurse who had came in just to help me. Right away I could tell that was my father’s voice. I wanted to look over to him, to try and explain to him that I needed them, that I needed anything that would take away the pain. Nothing came out and he left before I could form the words. It went on like that for days. The pain would wake me up, shaking me violently, but no matter how hard I pressed the button I could not get what I needed. They tried to give me medication to ease the pain, but nothing seemed to work. Whenever the nurses or doctors suggested I take something stronger, my father would always be there to deny any request. Each day that passed scrapped away at me. Each day that I knew the object that would cure my pain was being held from me, the more I grew upset. Regardless of the pain, after what seemed like a week, they started me on rehab. The physical activities they had me do felt like torture. Each afternoon I returned to my room by wheelchair, unable to even maintain enough energy to turn on the television. I would lay in bed, staring at the ceiling, wishing the pain would just go away. I’m assuming we had lost the game because in the weeks it seemed like I was in the hospital, I couldn’t recall anyone even coming to visit me. Not like I cared, I only torn up my knee beyond repair for them team. The one visitor I had each day was the nurse that had held my hand. She came by often for nothing more than to keep me company. She told me stories about how school was going, her brunette hair bouncing each time she laughed. Other times she just remained quiet and just stared at me with those big, bright, blue eyes. One day, after a particularly hard session of rehabilitation, I begged and begged for her to give me some stronger pain medication. She looked around, either to find someone to give her permission or to make sure no one would be around to see her. Leaving the room, she returned some five or so minutes later with a syringe in her hand. I smiled as I closed my eyes; waiting as the sweet relief came flowing through my body. My reprieve didn’t last long, however, as the booming sound of my father’s voice overtook the room. “What are you doing to my son!” She froze, the needle still sticking in my arm. Wanting to sit up and tell her I asked her, begged her, to do it, I found myself with my strength zapped from my body. The medication didn’t take long to take effect and my brain felt like it had just turned to mush. I couldn’t fight and I couldn’t protest. I could only close my eyes and fall asleep. She never came back to visit me again, something I feel horrid about. When the rest of the world had forgotten about me, she still cared. If it had not been for her, I couldn’t being to think of how things would have turned out. That night I made a decision. The next time my leg felt strong enough, I would rip my shackles off and escape this torturous prison. Each day there after I watched the routine my doctors and nurses made. I noted where they put my medicines and possible locations for other supplies. It took a few days but I eventually made enough sacrifices when it came to my pain killers. I had saved up enough to hopefully keep the pain at bay for long enough for me to make my escape. When the nurse had turned off my light to bid me ado, I hastily chugged my pills I had hidden behind my pillow and waited anxiously for them to take effect. Every few minutes I stretched my knee out to try and see how it felt, passing the time by looked at the patterns on the grotesque scarring from the multiple surgeries that were required. Not even the pale moon light could cast a romantic shadow over that disfigurement. When I felt my knee was as good as it would get and the medication was kicking in, I tossed the covers off my body and quietly slipped out of the bed. Having memorized the layout of the room, I stealthfully navigated my way around without the need of any external light sources. Using my pillow case as a bag, I filled it with as many pill bottles as I could come across. I didn’t care what it was for, I needed anything I could to try and ease this pain. Then, having grabbed as much supplies as I could fit in the bag, I turned and headed toward the window. The nurses never bothered to lock it despite being on the first floor. I guess it wasn’t procedure to do so unless I had a record for escape attempts. Jarring the window open, I dumped the bag first and fit my massive frame through second. At six foot seven and nearly three hundred pounds, it was hard to try and get through the small window. It was made even harder when I had to drag a busted wheel behind me. With some effort I finally freed myself and allowed a moment of peace as I let the soft misty air of the night embrace my face. It felt good to be out of that climate controlled hell-hole. Grabbing my bag, I took one last look at the hospital. Nearly all the lights were out, as one would expect from this hour. But up on the middle floor a single room stayed lit. Looking closely, I could make out the small frame of a female sitting in the window, watching. Her small hand pressed against the glass as she looked down upon me. Faintly I thought I saw glimmers of brunette hair. Instinctively I wanted to call out to her; to thank her for all she had did for me. But I knew better. The less attention I drew to myself, the easier it would be. I didn’t worry about her calling the guards. I knew she was just watching over me, as she did for those many weeks. I gave her a wave, to let her know I would be just fine, and set off on my way. I figured I must have had enough medications to last me as least a few months, maybe even a year if I rationed them right. Taking my first step of true freedom, I savored each moment of it. Colors and sounds came alive for me again. I had almost forgotten how much I loved to be outside in that cramp room. But I could never truly forget. It would just lie dormant inside me, waiting to come back out. Slinging the pillow case over my shoulder, I headed out to try and make something of my future. I didn’t know what would lie ahead of me on my trail, but I felt I could handle it. After all, I had a guardian angel looking over me from the middle floor. My name is no longer important. But for reference purposes, you can just call me Arizona.
