The Story Of Everything, Chapter 14: Neighbors

undated
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: Today A sharp pop thundered through me and dragged me along back to somewhere from nowhere; from where I wasn't aware there was a me to a recombined collection of vaguely acquainted thoughts, memories and ideas. It felt like a train and a train colliding, and it got so damn hot from all the friction and the metal that parts of one train became the other and the other way around. How long had I been out? It could have been hours. It could have been days. Did any of that matter? I couldn't remember what happened before it all stopped, for what I assumed would be forever, but had again ended up the tail end of an ass. I did remember some things; faces, names, rudimentary mathematics and a handful of codes of social conduct. It was more important to figure out where I was and how I'd gotten there. I remembered that someone was after me. I was reclining, and I looked around and all I saw was bookshelves where walls would be and diplomas framed where walls were and dusty old books and a bunch of shadows. I was couched. I turned, groping and gawking at what had been furnishing me. "It must be some kind of nylon polymer," I declared aloud, though I don't know why I did so. "What was that?" I twisted my body around violently to see, like some sort of invertebrate lizard who had been tapped on the right shoulder by a companion at his left. He paused his pen from scribbling and watched me over the crest of his notebook. "My accommodations," I clarified. "Who the fuck are you?" "I'm Doctor Wayne Gunn," he stated. The name sounded familiar, but I couldn't recall his face going with it. He had beady eyes, and his shirt was ironed to a rather unremarkable degree. "I'm your hypnotherapist." "Oh yeah?" I challenged, tactically suspicious. "What do you got in that notebook?" "Observations," came some cutesy bullshit. "You've got quite a lively imagination under the influence of hypnosis, but imaginary stories only serve to solve imaginary problems." "Let me see it," I reached out for the notebook, and the hypnotherapist clutched it defensively. "It won't do you any good," he handed it over reluctantly and with a defenseless, smirking guile. "Yeah, we'll see about that," I batted back like it was inflatable, skimming through the notes, flipping pages and trying to find some kind of overarching theme or at least a consistent direction. It was just a mess, though. It was books of names and some that would appear and then vanish without question. It was a meandering braid in a knot of mane. "A statistical improbability, perhaps, but I've seen worse. Much worse." "See?" the doctor was grinning. I wanted to smack him. "If you don't give me real answers, we'll never get to the bottom of what's causing you to subscribe to this perspective." "Doc, I don't even have a clue what you asked me," I admitted, and admittedly, I couldn't make anything of the notes. It's like each word made sense with the word before and after it, but the sentence as a whole didn't make any sense. "I asked you what you were scared of," he cleared his throat as if it were worth mentioning. "Well, I can't help what I say when I'm hypnotized." "So you weren't faking?" "I wasn't faking." "Because if you were faking, it's not going to help either of us out to think--" "I wasn't faking." "Well, good then." It seemed as if we'd reached an understanding. We were just kind of staring at each other for a while, like dogs aren't supposed to, or like you weren't supposed to with a dog, but it was mighty fine with people because of unique human devices like written language. Of course if you look a man in the eye, you can't see what he's doing with his hands. "Then what do you think it all means, Doc?" "How should I know?" "You're the doctor," I semi-mocked and pointed at the bookshelf. "Did you really read all those books?" "Not all of them," he said, turning around as if he hadn't noticed they were there. "I mean, I've read certain parts of all of them and all of some of them." "I don't associate with too many books," I said. "I don't trust them. You want to know why?" I stared at him with big owl eyes and my lips curled to an implied hooting. "Well, I'll tell you why anyway," I responded to the answer he gave by not giving me an answer. "Words tell you what to do. You read them. You see them on signs, and you think they have no ulterior motives. But words are weapons." "I think you look too much into things," swatted the hypnotherapist. "And that causes you to see things that aren't always actually there. Haven't you heard of sticks and stones?" "Weapons aren't just made to hurt, you know." He gulped, I'd imagine, at the gravity of this statement, and beads of sweat undoubtedly bubbled up over his brow, but I was focused on his hands. If you keep an eye on a man's hands, you know what he's going to do. "So what's this about me looking too much into things?" "I think you have some real troublesome issues with paranoia, Johnny." "How so?" "From what I could gather from your stream of consciousness babbling, you're convinced someone's trying to sabotage you or everyone's out to get you." "Well, which one is it?" "Which one is what?" "Is one person out to get me, or is everyone out to get me?" I asked, awaiting. "This kind of stuff matters, you know." "I really don't know," assessed the doctor. "Nothing you told me had any semblance of possibility in the real world. You were rattling off about your dead wife's bones one minute and magical hot sauce the next minute. You even suggested that I was plotting some kind of conspiracy against you." Then he chuckled; an amicable chuckle as if to let me know that he meant me no ill will, but perhaps he was plotting some kind of conspiracy against me. The silence was breaking him. Sometimes words are among the most powerful weapons when not used at all. "That's preposterous," he threw his hands up in the air. "Before you came in to see me, we'd never even met before." "So you're the kind of guy that would hypnotize a complete stranger?" I gave him the old stink-eye. "That's my job." I thought that maybe I'd be able to put things together in time, piece by piece as I passed a series of mnemonic triggers. It all seemed like such a robotic train of thought, carted along on some conveyor belt where you never know if there's a blade or a hammer at the end. Usually, it's nothing, and you just got somewhere faster and with less effort, but usually only works in horseshoes and hand grenades. "What are you after, Doc?" I stood up and started pacing, looking at some of the curious bindings. There was Randall's 'Mesmeric Forces And How To Develop Them.' There was Ewin's 'Ideomotor Signals for Rapid Hypnoanalysis: A How-to Manual.' There was 'Evaluations of Classical Biofeedback' by Rogers, Kimball and Raczynski. He had all the classics, and I was nearly shouting. They were all there. "What is it? Information? Money?" "I'm not after anything," the doctor stood up insulted and started fuming. "You came to me!" "I'd never do any such thing!" I protested and then spat symbolically on the floor. Then I rubbed it in with my boot even more symbolically. "The only thing I trust any less than words is the medical system." "Please don't spit on my carpet," whined the hypnotherapist. "It's going to harden the fibers." "Fuck the fibers!" I yelled, wishing I had something that I could grab and shake, but finding an object that was properly sized proved to be difficult. "You honestly don't remember?" the hypnotherapist, Doctor Wayne Gunn, stood in disbelief. "You came in here not more than an hour ago. You said they'd throw you in jail if you didn't start coming to therapy. I even have a copy of the court order you brought me if you--" "Yes, yes," I agreed, still searching. "They'd lock me up and throw away the key." To be quite honest, I still had no idea what he was talking about about what I was talking about. Aha! A potted plant, and none too soon! I gripped it firmly and shook the dirt out as leaves flapped and fluttered about, and the doctor stood, white as a ghost, holding his face in absolute horror. "That's enough! I want you the fuck out of my office!" The flabbergasted hypnotherapist had lost his cool. He pointed fiercely at the door; at his backwards name printed boldly across the opaque glass pane. If only you could still judge the worth of a man by the heft of his door, but times certainly had changed. "Believe me. I want me the fuck out of your office, too." I reached for the doorknob. "And I'll stay the fuck out." "If you don't show up to your sessions, they'll throw you in jail. It's the terms of your probation." I held firmly onto the doorknob, knowing I just didn't have the hand to call his bluff. He arched his eyebrows. "Same time next week then?" I answered by not answering as I had been answered by not having been answered before. We understood each other this way, as far as either of us could tell. "Good," he took a deep breath and put on a big smile. "Check out with my secretary, Marie. She'll be able to give you a receipt that you can present to your probation officer." *** We sat at a table and the way that we were seated, I could tell that they were going to come at me with whatever wasn't nailed down to the floor. I still wasn't aware of what I had done wrong, and I figured that violated what rights I had left. I couldn't recall having done anything to warrant having to sit here next to this foul, angry man who didn't even want to be here. If I had done something wrong, they must have changed the laws on me. But he was telling me things, and I've been told that this was his job. He was telling me things, then, too, but I wasn't listening to him. I was watching the three suits outside through the window as they rocked back and forth in triangular conversation with lukewarm, heavily sugared coffees teetering in paper cups. "Johnny, are you listening to me?" "No," I said, noting the irony of my appropriate response. Of course, had I responded affirmatively, I would have been lying. Perhaps I shouldn't have responded. It was a hell of a scapegoat, though, this loaded question. If worse came to worst, I could always trace it back to that inevitable blunder born from trickery, and I was haunted by the fact that I would have this and this alone to solace me. "If you agree to plead guilty and agree to apologize, I'm sure I can bargain them down to probation or community service and a fine." "If I plead guilty, that means admitting I broke the law." "You did break the law. You have no alibi and even if you did, you'd have nobody to corroborate it. You can't remember where you were when they say you did it, and for Christ's sake, an hour later, you show up at a restaurant covered in dirt and carrying a shovel." He wasn't angry, just disappointed. "Explain that to me." "Lots of people bring shovels to restaurants," I shrugged. "When they get done out there, you just sit there and let me handle this," he warned, with an unappreciated, heavily knuckled finger held aloft in caution. "You got that?" "I'm not apologizing." "I don't want to hear it, Johnny." "I'm not sorry." "Remorse is an absolute necessity, Johnny. You defaced a burial site." I tried to calculate the gravity of these accusations, and I was surprised that this was what all the fuss was about. "Several sites, actually," he continued as the door lever turned. The door looked like it would be cold to the touch. "Alright, they're coming back. Just don't fuck this up, whatever you do." The man next to me stood up as the three suits entered in single file from shortest to tallest. The short one was the youngest and the man of medium stature was the oldest. The tallest of the three looked like he didn't belong in a suit. I recognized him. The standing man who once sat next to me shook the hands of the first two. "Long," he greeted. "Jung." "Goldman," replied Long, the young, short one. "How do you do?" nodded Jung. "I don't believe we've met," Goldman said to the third entrant. "Joey Fitz," he chewed, seemingly as detached from the ritual etiquette of the meeting as I was. He kept his hands in his pockets like he had been instructed to do so. "Joey came to us with some new evidence that suggests your client might not have been working alone," Long wasted no time getting right to business. He was in his seat faster than anyone. "He says he saw your client stealing a pair of shovels from a garage across the road from one of the defaced sites." "That's right," Joey smugly nodded, sitting loosely, and something in his voice was familiar. The digging hadn't spawned the robbery of the shovels. It was the other way around, and it was all backwards. I remembered he was talking about how he used to dig out surgical screws and nails from his skin to pawn off for fixes or something. I had to see his hands. "I take it you read our plea bargain," Jung sleepily assumed, pulling his chair out and lowering himself in it. "One year plus a fine, right?" Goldman recalled. "Half that if he behaves?" "I thought you said no jail time," I turned to Goldman. "Relax, Johnny," Goldman professionally refocused his attention to the suits. "Given that my client has no previous convictions for any violent crimes and has only had a few run-ins on minor misdemeanor offenses, I don't think that jail time has to be in order. He's willing to offer an apology to the families whose loved ones' graves were disturbed." I glared at him, but before I could say anything, Long was on the defensive. "What is your counter?" "Two years probation, 120 hours of community service and payment for all damages caused. One year isn't much less than a judge would hand out for something like this." "That depends on what the estimated damage turns out to be. I'm thinking it might be enough to qualify this charge as a felony." I didn't like the way Long was operating. What a feather in the cap of his young career it would be to put me behind bars. "I'm not taking jail time out of the picture unless we get the name of his accomplice." "Accomplice?" I shook my head. "Don't have one." "Yeah?" sneered Long, "Then why would you bring two shovels?" "I can't say for certain," I ascertained. "Maybe to hide the evidence? Bury the shovel I used to dig with the extra shovel. That'd be my best guess." "That doesn't make any sense," Goldman growled at me, whispering through ground teeth. "You're only going to screw yourself if you don't agree to their terms." "Well, fuck!" I stood up. "I think I'd rather take my chances at a trial." "Are you crazy?" Goldman stood up after me as Long sat satisfied. "If this case goes to court, there's nothing I'm going to be able to do to save your ass." "Then you're fired, Jung," I pointed at Goldman, though a generally disinterested Jung perked up at the mismention of his name. "I'm representing myself." "I'm not Jung, I'm Goldman," he corrected, and up to that point, I had switched the names in my head. "Well, you're still fired." "You do whatever you want, but you do know that you're going to hang for this." "I figure I can't do any worse than you would've done, but at least I'll put up a decent fight before I go down." And with a half-insulted, half-relieved huff, Goldman slammed his chair back under the table and left the room, slamming the cold door behind him, and presumably from there, slamming a number of things in his path. I turned back to the panel of the three men in three-piece suits who could have just as easily been one man in a nine-piece suit. "I'll see you gentlemen in court." "Good luck, kid," Long laughed, and the son of a bitch wasn't afraid to be cocky about it. He folded his hands behind his head like some kind of hot shot. Fitz was still hiding his, so I spat on him, and sure enough, he lunged across the table at me, exposing his hands with jagged scabs and scars trailing across the spots where his metacarpals fused together or gapped apart. He got me good, right under the jaw there, but I slipped away as Jung and Long tried to restrain him. Little did they know an insane man trying to pass as sane would be far more dangerous than a sane man trying to pass as crazy, even if you fool everyone but yourself. *** I had argued my way to insanity, and I felt as if the argument had made me insane. Perhaps this was because I had argued so well, or conversely, argued poorly but convincingly enough to achieve the intended result. Argument was funny like that. You can't really argue the truth, because the truth is what it is. You can only argue it on one side or the other, and when you do too well, by virtue of doing too poorly or not, you confine yourself to the limitations of that argument. For me, that meant living in a small room, not too dissimilar from what I remembered my actual apartment to be like without any of my stuff in it. There was a bed and a pot to piss in and a man who checked in on me from time to time to make sure I was doing what I was supposed to do. He wanted to make sure that I didn't go sane too soon, because going sane would defy the logic of my confinement, and defying the logic of your confinement only confines you in new ways. As spartan as my conditions were, they could have been worse. I had a door where I could sit outside and have a cigarette. Sometimes there would be others, though. From the outside, I could see a line of similar doors and a square yard with no way out other than the doors. These doors were painted white, but someone sure as hell did an awful job. I chalked it up to lousy workmanship, but the whole project was damned from the beginning. The complex was designed as a labyrinth. Some people would retreat back to their doors if they saw you out there, and a lot of people would come try to talk to you and ask you for your cigarettes. I preferred the people that wouldn't take much notice of you one way or the other, but here, those people were few and far between. They were all running the same game I was, having defined a set of rules which led them there and kept them there. I liked to pass the time by counting the doors. I knew there would be 24 doors along each side, but it wasn't about figuring out the number of doors. It was about counting itself. I had gone down two sides and was halfway through the third when Ernie came stumbling out, walking with an exaggerated limp. He was alright. He had a backgammon board, and we'd play sometimes, if we were both out and he remembered to take his board with him. Neither of us ever learned each other's rules, but there were always a lot of pieces moving around and other pieces being captured and a lot of yelling at each other to negotiate the release of the captured pieces. "What the hell do you want, Ernie?" Talking to Ernie was like talking to a mirror, except your reflection is much older and uglier than you are. Ernie always wore sweatpants and a t-shirt, and when it was cold enough, he'd put on an extra sweatshirt, but his pants sagged and his belly hung over it, and he was coming at me, arching his back and picking wax out of his ear with a wedged, flanging finger. "Gimme a cigarette," Ernie shot back. I grumbled at the son of a bitch, but thumbed through my pack and reluctantly found one for him. He didn't bother thanking me or even pretending he wasn't inconveniencing me. He just stood there smoking it in front of me. "What are you still standing there for?" I asked, redundantly rather than rhetorically. "I gave you a cigarette. Go on. Get out of here." I shooed him away with my hand, but he stood his ground, ignoring me and rambling on about the same things he always rambled on about. "They gone and done it this time, Johnny. Let me tell you." He couldn't pronounce my name right, but he pronounced it in such a way that I couldn't even replicate or even represent the pronunciation. "Gone and did what?" "I was just minding my own business, and they came in, and they took me out there," he pointed off to the center of the square, so I knew where he was talking about. "I don't know what they did, but they hit me in the back of the head something fierce, and when I came to, it took me an hour to find out which door I was supposed to go into." "You're always just minding your own business," I remarked. That's always how it started out with Ernie. "Look here," he turned around, pointing to the back of his head. "You see anything?" "There's nothing there," I said, nothing out of the ordinary, at least. He had a lot of boils on him, but he was always covered in boils. He said it was some kind of medical condition; some deficiency of some amino acid or something like that. "Hey, Johnny. If I can guess how many brothers and sisters you have, you gotta give me three bucks." "Sure." "It's three, isn't it?" "No, it's two." I lied to him every time, even if he was wrong in the first place, and he never caught on. I don't know if he just wasn't keeping track, but I assumed he figured he'd eventually get it right if he guessed enough times. "Come on! Help me out!" "I don't even have three bucks, Ernie. Move along, now. I'm going inside." "You know they're going to come after you, too, Johnny," said a genuinely concerned Ernie. "They told me so." "They says they people, but they aliens. I've seen it." His eyes grew wide. "I was the first one the got to here, so everything they know about us came from me, but I had to tell them or they were going to do more tests on me." "Tell them what?" "Everything I know," he wiped his forehead. "I had to tell them about how I knew you, and they're probably going to try to do some tests on you. You ain't angry about it, are you?" "You gotta do what you gotta do," I guessed, trying not to prolong the conversation. There were all sorts here, and they all had their stories to tell, but nobody wanted to listen to them. I started to think that the only reason they ever told anyone else is because they got sick of hearing themselves speak. At night, when we were all supposed to be in our rooms after a certain time, you could hear them talking to themselves through the walls, and it was impossible to sleep. You'd have to find a rare lull and get all you're sleeping done then, because if you could get past the falling asleep part, you could stay asleep for a while, and then it wouldn't be so bad. "So you ain't mad at me, right boss?" "No, it's fine." "Can I get a cigarette from you, boss?" "I just gave you a cigarette. You're still smoking it." "I need one for later." I respected his shamelessness, though, so I obliged his request, albeit nonetheless begrudgingly. He stood there for a while, scratching at the back of his head. "I think this is where they put the wires in me." Then he shuffled off. Our conversations always ended awkwardly. All of my conversations seemed to do that, but I thought I explained things fairly clearly considering I still hadn't figured out much about why I was here and how long I had to stay. I supposed I only had to stay until I could find my way out, but I was too busy being chased around by the man who'd check in on me from time to time and search through my belongings and sometimes my bodily crevices. They didn't like when you had drugs here. There was some kind of punishment for it, and after three strikes, I think they just set you out in the courtyard and lit you on fire. I had seen it a few times, but I was told that those people lit themselves on fire of their own volition. To each his own, I guess. I was sure I'd escape unburnt, though. There were only 96 doors, and some of them had to lead out if they were to lead in, but you just had to get lucky. I tried going around knocking once, but that only caused more commotion. You can't go around knocking on too many doors, or they get wise to you and come after you, and I was running out of places to run. Maybe it would have been better if I would have just let them catch me. They wouldn't have had any idea what to do with me when they did catch me, and as soon as they let me go, I could just start running around again. It'd be just as pointless for them to keep running after me as it would be for me to keep running, but I figured I could wander around aimlessly longer than most men could stand. I had been doing it for quite some time. What I couldn't comprehend was the lack of shovels. Had I tried to bury them all, I would need another shovel to inter the last one into the dirt and then another shovel after that. But I had no shovels -- not a single one. They probably confiscated them all. If I had a shovel, I could dig my way out, but who's to say that outside these four walls of doors there weren't just other similar enclosures of doors, or concentric squares of field with walls in the middle. I'd best stay there, I thought. I'd best stay there with all the voices canceling each other out into a constant noise that was easy to ignore once it got to thick to discern.
