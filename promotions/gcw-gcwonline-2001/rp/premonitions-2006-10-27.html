Premonitions?

27 October 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 10/27/2006 “Come again?” Katie said into the phone, not quite sure she’d heard the principal right. “We need you to come pick up your daughter, Mrs. Rottonbottom,” repeated the Principal. Katie was instantly struck with deep concern and worry. “Is she in trouble?” “Yes, even though it’s not her fault, and she didn’t start it,” said the principal. Katie decided the story could wait until she got there. “Sure and I shall be right over,” she said. Katie’s dark green Saturn SL2 hummed to life as she turned the engine over. She drove the short distance, about a 20-minute drive, to the school that her adoptive daughter, Denise, attended. Her shamrock green eyes glittered with a hard light. It was hard for her to stay angry at Denise over anything, but her infamous Irish temper could be stoked hot under the right circumstances. Fortunately, it was able to cool down as quickly as it heated up. Katie walked into the principal’s office and found Denise sitting in a chair before the principal’s desk, her legs swinging slightly before her and her head down, reluctant to look her adoptive mother in the eyes. She knew that Katie wasn’t pleased. “Hello, Mrs. Rottonbottom... please sit down.” The greeting from Mr. Stanford, principal of the school, was civil and direct to the point. The diminutive Irishwoman sat beside Denise and put a dainty arm across the child’s shoulders in a gesture that was both comforting, but carried undertones of, “You’re still in trouble and will be treated as such.” She crossed her legs and nodded to the principal. “Good day, Mr. Stanford,” she answered back in the same, civil and direct-to-the-point tone. Mr. Stanford didn’t mince words. “Two boys in the school started picking on her,” he said to Katie, further emphasizing that Denise hadn’t started the issue. “They thought they could push her around. She proved them wrong.” “With ye fists, I take it,” Katie said to Denise. Denise nodded and said nothing. “In a nutshell, that’s it,” Mr. Stanford said. The boys started it, and their mothers will deal with them accordingly. But finishing it, so to speak, is of course, also against school policy. Denise, you know you should have come to us. We’d have made sure those boys didn’t bother you again. You stand up to them, yes, but with words, and a trip straight to me and my administrators. You don’t give them a bloody nose or a shiner or anything else that will have their parents threatening to get the SCHOOL in trouble for not protecting THEM.” Denise started to turn florid as she remembered just why the boys started to pick on her. How the school couldn’t understand her side of the story, the eleven-year-old girl couldn’t fathom. “They said ye would get beaten to a pulp at the next WorldWide,” Denise said to Katie; her voice starting to quaver with pent-up frustration, as tears started to well in her eyes. “They said that ye got Dies Irae angry at ye and that ye would--? But the Monarch Butterfly silenced her adoptive daughter with a small wave of her other hand; the one that wasn’t around Denise’s shoulders in a comforting gesture. “Sure and if I had an American dollar for every time I’ve been beaten black and blue during a wrestling card, and even had to spend time in the hospital, we would be wealthy enough that I could have taken that humdrum job cooking breakfast meals at the diner,” Katie said gently but firmly. “And if I had but an American quarter for everyone who’s laid claim to thousands of other beatings that I either fought off or simply never came’I might never have had to work a day in me life.” Of course, the thought of her adoptive mother having never been a wrestler’even because she never needed or wanted or money’didn’t excite Denise very much. She lowered her head again and put a hand on Katie’s hand which still rested on her shoulder. “Sure and school is no place for GCW, or worrying about GCW,” Katie reminded Denise. “If those boys want to spend so much time worrying about it, I fancy they’ll get poor grades on many an exam and have a bleak future ahead of them when they seek a job. Let that be ye justice for the insults they hurl at ye... when ye wear a white collar to work and they wear a blue one, working at minimum wage for a boss who thinks of them as a lower social class.” Denise giggled a bit at the thought of it. She nodded to Katie and turned to Mr. Stanford. “I’m sorry, Mr. Stanford,” she said, trying to sound contrite. “Sure and I shan’t do it again.” Mr. Stanford nodded. “See that you don’t,” he said. “I’ll see you back in school for the Halloween party on Tuesday.” Of course, the day being Friday, meant that Denise was being given a one-day suspension for Monday. He could have suspended her for Tuesday as well, making her miss that party, and knowing this brought a smile of gratitude to the young girl’s face. “We’ll talk about this when Percy comes home with John,” Katie said, though Denise knew her adoptive father would agree to grounding her. Hopefully it wouldn’t be too long. Katie stood, and Denise did as well. “Have a good day, then, Mr. Stanford,” Katie said. “Good day, Mrs. Rottonbottom. Thank you for coming down here promptly.” As Denise strapped herself in to her seat in Katie’s car, the diminutive Irishwoman noted the look on her face. “Don’t be scared for me, child,” she admonished, albeit gently. Denise tried to look brave. “I just hurt when ye get hurt,” she said. “Then I’ll just have to give a few people some of what ye gave those boys,” Katie teased with a wicked grin. Denise burst into giggles that floated lightly down the next two blocks on the trip home.
