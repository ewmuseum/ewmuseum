Sink

Date: 04/15/11 Sink A Garbage Bag Production *** I breathed a sigh of relief as I shut the door and slid further across the back seat of the cab. He looked at me through the rear view mirror, and it was only the slightest twitch of surprise, but when I saw it wash off as quickly as it appeared, I realized how I must've looked. He had one of those fancy headsets, and he said something to it in some language I didn't know. He seemed content pretending like he didn't notice me, but my head was somewhere else, and he was forced to acknowledge me, submitting to my command of unintentional patience. "Where are you going?" "125 West 26th." He pushed a button, and the tab began to roll, rising in even increments every so often, too fast for me to keep up with the math. I wondered if perhaps he was taking the long route, but then I thought better of it. There was really no way for him to know how little I knew of the city unless he tested me, unless he had tested me, and I had failed. We'd have to trust each other. We were as good as dead to the rest of the city. I heard it making a busily pittering noise outside that sounded at first like raindrops, then like footsteps, and lastly, the churning whir of traffic. There was an afterparty at some trendy place named after some concept or something, company sponsored and all that. I thought of going, but I knew I couldn't show up looking like I did. I guess I'd never thought the consequences all the way through and never expected how heavy they'd feel all cut up like I was. The driver rambled on, probably about nothing of particular importance in a language I didn't understand to someone I'd almost certainly never meet. The vehicle plodded along the road at whatever slow pace the other vehicles surrounding us would allow. I forgot if it had been raining, finding only my reflection when looking for a reminder, and I shied away from the window before I could confirm whether it was. "How long until we get there?" I don't think he heard me, or maybe he was too focused on the task at hand, gripping the wheel with locked elbows. It was hard enough to concentrate. Soon enough, I thought, Maybe he had responded. It didn't feel much like my own thought, though it could've been an adjunct to one decidedly of my own creation. I thought of some of the other thoughts I'd had to see if perhaps it belonged to any one of them, but it was vague enough to work with them all. I didn't know where we were going. That is to say, I had some idea where we going, but we could have been going somewhere else, and I wouldn't have been able to tell. I couldn't divert my attention from breathing, lacking the proper faith in my own automatic processes from having spent it all on the cab driver. My phone rang. I left it in my pocket, trying actively to forget it about it and desperately not to worry about who had called me. If they'd left me a message, I'd listen to it, but not in the cab. That way, I'd be prepared. The phone beeped. I'd just stopped paying attention to my breathing, to the traffic noise, the potential raindrops, the foreign language, and it beeped. The register clicked to a halt at $14.32. "This is it, no?" A bell clanked, and I could hear the rust on the bronze. I handed the driver a picture of Andrew Jackson and thanked him with a gesture. The rain had stopped. It must have if it was raining at all. I couldn't tell the difference between shadows and puddles. The automatic door opened, and I drew some glances from the receptionists and from some of the guests going the other way from their rooms, off into the fluorescent lit night. I must have looked crazy. I took the stairs, assuming that for some reason or another I had a history with elevators. The phone beeped again. "You have one new message. To play your messages, press one," said Sheila. "New message. Monday, March 14th at 12:21 AM." It was the outside rushing in to get me, the noise of the street buzzing over a thick voice mumbling loudly as it could over everything else. It was all in a language I didn't understand. Maybe it was the cab driver, but he had gone. I couldn't make out a word. "To replay this message, press one. To delete this message and move onto the next, press seven. To save this message, press nine." "Thanks, Sheila." I pressed seven and closed the phone. I'd lost count of how many flights of stairs I'd climbed. I'd have to start from the bottom and go back up, being sure to pay more attention. It was a lesson learned, I guess, but when I got back to the ground floor, I decided just to take the elevator, having found stairs to be the greater evil. Nothing out of sorts happened in the elevator. I headed straight to the bathroom and stared at myself for a long while in the mirror. She'd really done a number on me. There really wasn't no way to fix it but one. It hadn't even occured to me that I'd need a shaver. I left the bottle of whiskey on the nightstand, still in the paper bag. I unscrewed the cap and took a swig. It was room temperature. I headed back down to the lobby, either taking the stairs or the elevator, I suspect, enduring the same looks from the receptionists and from some of the guests going the other way towards their rooms, in from the fluorescent lit night. I thought about asking for directions somewhere but decided myself better off wandering about, almost hoping not to find a place that was open, and if I wasn't running so low on smokes, I might have stayed in and waited a day. I lit a cigarette and kept my head down, save for brief bouts of phototropism, mostly trying to convince myself that it wasn't a big deal. The night was alive, too busy to laugh at my laughable thoughts. I tried to laugh alone at them, but it was difficult when nobody was laughing with me. I wondered if it would change me at all. I couldn't imagine that it wouldn't. Most of the people I knew hadn't seen me without a beard. I'd had one mostly since it started growing in, and I'd cut it only a couple of times early on to unsatisfactory results. I wondered if people would look at me differently. That was what I thought would change me. The biggest part of what you are isn't up to you anyway. I'd gotten plenty of strange looks with my hair and beard all haphazardly sheared. I'd wander around for a few more decades, stating my case, and then whenever my ticket was punched, someone else would get in the last word, and I'd have no means of rebuttal. I thought about how much time had passed since I cut it last, and I couldn't remember much of my life before I had. The more I thought about it, the less it seemed like myself. They were memories of someone else, a bearded man trapped in an unbearded man's body. All that was left was a shed coat of skin, oblivious to what it would eventually become. A memory has no thoughts of its own, the living within it dead there, alive somewhere else, and dead somewhere else, too. I stopped and lit another cigarette. People were getting drunker, stumbling along and yelling back, and the rest of the world was fading into a blur and disappearing, and I felt apart from it all as it swirled around me. Somehow, it all managed to keep a loose order as pedestrians shuffled from one place to another in opposite directions through some double osmosis, semipermeable membranes collapsing past each other in waves, everyone walking like they were all unapologetically blindfolded. Red neon glowed off the drug store across the street. I thought twice about it. Beggars loitered outside, and I wasn't halfway done with the cigarette. The light changed, and I crossed the street, hustling past the entrance to avoid being noticed. I glanced quickly as I passed, whispering cuss words to myself. I was sure someone had seen me. I stopped at a safe distance to finish the cigarette and stood there for a while, my hands thrust in my pockets as I waited for people to clear out from in front of the entrance. I thought about smoking another one. It was the last one in the pack. I took it out and rolled it between my fingers, but I didn't feel like smoking it. My phone rang again. They were probably wondering where I was. I ignored the call and put the phone on silent. I just wanted to go back to my hotel. I felt somewhat obligated to go out, even if just for a short while to shake a few hands and bullshit around a little with the guys. I owed them all that much. "Help me get something to eat?" "Maybe on my way out, buddy." "Hey, you got a cigarette I can have?" He stood there undeterred, almost expectant, and I clenched my teeth to barricade the angry lump swelling in my throat. He looked like a statue, pieced together like a collage of scraps that had peeled off from the rest of the world, a bird's nest of decomposing patches. I caught myself just staring at him, giving him looks like I'd gotten, and I felt guilty enough to let him have my last smoke. He shielded himself from the wind to light it without offering another word to me, and I left him there. I wandered around the aisles in slow confused spirals until I found the right section and picked the cheapest electric shaver they had. I brought it to the line and searched the display of tobacco products for my brand while I waited, but I didn't see it there. "Next." I dragged myself to the register and slid the shaver to the cashier. She looked up and examined my face, seeing the half-done job before scanning the bar code on the back of the figured plastic packaging. "Will that be all for you?" "Do you guys happen to have American Spirits?" "No, we don't carry those." "What do you got that's like them?" "I don't know. I don't smoke." She did very little to hide her impatience. "Then give me what most people usually get." She turned to the shelf and paused to shrug at the boxes. "Anything but 27s." I paid the total, and she tossed the shaver and cigarettes into a plastic bag. Outside, the beggar was still smoking the cigarette I gave him. I brushed past him as he recognized me, hoping I'd have some change for him. I heard him call out to me but made sure not to turn around. "You said on your way out!" I kept walking. "Fuckin' liar!" I could've stopped and turned and explained the technicalities, but figuring that'd do no good, I didn't bother to do much of anything. I never promised him any change, but even maybe was a lie, and his words still hurt more than I probably should have let them. Perhaps if they'd stocked my brand, things would be different, but they weren't. That was reality for you, whether you liked it or not. While I hadn't always been it's most dedicated proponent, it was getting harder and harder not to acknowledge that it had always been there, reality, waiting to beat me down until I was spiritually homeless. The city had gone quiet, the noise all pumped into a vacuum and popped apart in the non-physical space inside it. I walked tunnels, the silhouetted world bending in a lens around me. I didn't know who I'd become, that maybe I'd all grown out of myself, and there I was, mutilated, hanging off the empty face of a losing gunfighter, dead finger stiffened around a heavy trigger. The age of inconsequence had run its course. Its winter spat me out a mound of disfigured bones. I heard its unmerciful laughter. It jittered against the movement of my aching bones knocking against each other, war starved and gypsy thin. I wondered who I was before, whether I was composed more of my actions as they happened or as I remembered them happening. There I was, nobody yet, and jealous of the shifting figment I imagined I was. At least it was something. I packed the box of cigarettes and then started to smoke one. I wondered who I was if I wasn't smoking my brand. It wasn't the taste. In fact, I couldn't taste anything. I stopped dead in my tracks, and I felt like I couldn't go on any further. Car horns honked, but they weren't honking at me and probably not quite at anything in particular. I had no idea how I got back to the hotel. I faded in and out of realization from there on out, the illusion of a pendulum swung from the magician's ghost hand, only barely visible where the light reflected in just the right way. I was neither the hand nor the pendulum, rather the space through which the pendulum oscillated. "Another round of whiskey on me! What do you say?" Liquid sloshed out of my raised glass, dripping down my arm, and the handful of the laughing doomed scattered around the hotel bar raised their glasses and made apish noise. There were eight of them, maybe fewer. I couldn't recall what any of them looked like. They had a man in playing the synthesizer, but that had stopped much earlier. The place smelled like chemical pine, and everything looked like it was so wet it was melting. I blacked out. I was urinating in the stairwell and didn't get in trouble for it. At some point, I managed the door open after having some great difficulty with the card reader. I dreamt that I had rid the world of its snakes, and when I woke up, bathroom tile impressed on my face, the sun was still a few turns from the horizon. I pushed up to my feet, as had become almost second nature when my equilibrium was at its least capable of doing so, and I shuffled from foot to foot until the room righted itself. I looked at my reflection and said farewell to an old friend and then down into the sink when it was all through with. It was all just old hair. The whiskey was lukewarm, just as I'd left it in the paper bag. I took a swig, and it tasted the same as it did before.
