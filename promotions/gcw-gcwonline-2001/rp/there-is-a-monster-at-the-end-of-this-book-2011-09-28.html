There Is A Monster At The End Of This Book

28 September 2011
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 09/28/11 The beach didn't have a name, but it was located near the break between the Carolinas. The summer crept toward its end, the Atlantic bluer than it was in his dreams, stretching past the bayous lighting the distant horizon. Orange hues were paintbrushed with gray wisps, feathering past comprehension. Hydrolics droned in the distance, his father and the rest of the carnies piecing together the genny and the big top and the other rides with their wrenches. He was still too young to help put them together. His hands hadn't yet tasted grease. He stood at the breech, where tall grass and cattail tendrils tickled what wasn't covered by a towel. His mother's toes dug into the white sands, the fabric of her flower dress clinging to her sunkissed skin. Eyes forward. Her shoulders and chest rising ever so faintly, as if savoring every last drop of this place. He remembered how she looked. How the wind toyed with her auburn hair, ruffling it behind her in a wild mane of curls. How she shined with a radiance he'd never see again. Pulling himself from his splay, he walked to her, to stand beside her. His head found a comfortable crook along her thigh. She looked down to him and smiled, taking his hand into hers. He remembered the taste of salty air, like the first drag of unfiltered nicotine, burning his lungs and making him feel alive in ways he hadn't imagined before. Bittersweet. A postcard from a time before everything he touched died. Together, they waited for the tide, their joined hands lightly sashaying as the sun set and the moon started to rise. Sheriff Wyatt Conyers pulled off his sunglasses, putting in his breast pocket. His hands sought his pockets, struggling to find them through nervous fidgets. He shuck his palms, tightening his knuckles until they crackled and popped. The night rain wasn't symbolic of anything, just a random occurrence that fit all too well. Chilled fall air roughed up his jowls as he swabbed his tobacco from his lip, flicking it away from walkway under the rural hospital's awning. “Maybe you should get somethin' to eat.” Terrence's gaze was outward, his eyes drifting in the distance. He reached into the pocket of his hoodie, his eyes blinking rapidly to try and whisk away the fluorescent light and wax tile sheen which burned his tired retinas. His spine rested against the cold hard steel, yielding to it with his slouch. He fingered the cardboard pack in his hands absentminded of who was around him, thumbing the lid up and drawing out a Marlboro before sparking it alive. One long drag. He held his breath until his limbs tingled and he became lightheaded. “You're sixteen.” The words didn't resonate, but Conyers didn't think there was much reason for them to. He hovered above the young boy, suddenly snatching the cigarettes from his hand. Exhaustion crumpled any resistance he might have had. He even offered the man his lighter, blowing smoke from his nostrils and immediately taking another harsh drag. Conyers obliged, reluctantly, taking one of the reds out and lighting up before handing the pack right back. “When I was a kid bout your age, the doctors used to lunge over top of you with one of these hanging out their lips. Different times.” He took a drag, nursing the butt close to his chest. No response. He stifled a cough with his fist, his smile dissipating and his thumbs snaking between his belt loops. “How bad is she?” Terrence glared at him, his fists clinching. Conyers reached for his shoulder. “If they'd of done their job--” Smoke oozed from his sneer. “Don't touch--” “Your father shouldn't of run--” He whipped his shoulder out of his grasp, his hands wildly combing through his hair as he shied away. “Maybe if you'd not of come for my old man...” but he trailed off, the finish lost in the exhaustion creeping too comfortably back in place. “I don't know where he is, alright? So if you came to ask me where he is, I don't know.” Conyers took a few drags, the stillness between the two creeping by and running nervous tension up the muscles of his neck. He sighed, his shoulder resting against one of the bannisters as he looked the young man over. “I know it's rough. It's a shame when bad things happen to good people.” “Yeah...” “Like, it makes you think if there is a God at all. If this is his plan, well, I dunno if it is worth all that much. Doesn't make any sense. Your mother was a good woman. She shouldn't have to suffer like this.” Cigarettes and mainlined coffee were all that kept him awake, and even then, he was running on fumes. He tossed his butt away. It didn't matter. He just needed to be there for her. For that one last sign that his mother was still here. He couldn't miss it. Rust bled into his skin, his eyes dry as dust, and if she had one last moment awake in this world, he needed to be there for it. Another spark of flint. His mind raced as the cheery grew,the hiss following the singeing of paper. “She's gone. Been asking out the plug for days. No point in lying about it anymore.” Conyers didn't know how to react. He merely stood there, gulping as he tossed his butt to the grass and peeling his hat from his head. He cupped the crown to his heart. Hiding his badge. “You sure?” Their eyes met, Terrence's shrinkwrapped in tears. “I'm all she's got left. Been waiting for a while. I just...” He couldn't feel his arms. “I can't do it anymore. And she shouldn't have to suffer anymore. It's gonna happen. It's gonna happen soon. At least she won't suffer anymore, right?” “I don't know.” “Right?” The blood drained from Conyers face. “You're right. Absolutely right.” Bitter, hateful affirmation. Terrence swallowed his cry, hiding it behind coughs. He dug his hands inside his jean pockets, his fingers clutching at his thighs until his nails broke through the skin. “Look,” he started, drawing his arm over the young man's shoulder so he didn't have to carry the burden all by himself anymore. “I didn't mean to put you through this. I know you have enough hate in your heart for my kind right now. But I know your family. I owe you something better than this.” Maybe he did. Terrence didn't care. The damage had already been done. But it wasn't Wyatt or the Jefferson County Police he hated. It was his father. For putting his family in this position. For stashing lord knows what in the basement of the Presbyterian Church. For running away when he should have faced his crimes like a man. His mother was the strong one, but she died the morning the tide washed over their flock in Rodney, leaving nothing in its wake. His father knew about his mother's condition for longer than anyone, that her insides were rotting her away from the inside. He should have been there. To do something. He hated him because he wasn't and he was the only one left with her, like he was the only one who'd ever cared. Conyers put the emblematic felt back on his crown, reaching into his pocket and pulling out a folded envelope, handing it to him. “I know the rest of your family is all strewn out along the county in foster care, but I figured you could use this.” He opened the crease, his fingertips running along a bundle of crinkled twenties. A piece of ripped paper stuck out from the edge, and as he took it into his hand, the sheriff made sure the young man kept it closed in his palm. “Enough for a bus ticket. Or two. But that's up to you.” He paused, looking back and forth to make sure they were still alone. “But once this is done, you need to go, alright? The sorts your father was around? They weren't good people. Alright?” There wasn't much choice for him, was there? “Alright.” “Take care of yourself.” This wasn't goodbye, but Conyers tipped his cap all the same. Terrence stayed out just a bit longer, listening to the pitter patter of raindrops as the need to smoke overtook him. It wasn't until he neared the glass doors leading to the hospital's entrance that he opened his hand, unfolding the piece of paper he'd pulled out. All there was was a phone number. The hand writing was unmistakable to him. He rushed to the nearest payphone. Autumn had to know what was going on. She opens her mouth to speak, but he doesn't remember what she says, or what she means. All that matters is the smile tickling her lips, the tranquility and the calm and the freedom of every piece of existence fitting together in such clarity. He fights the urge to cry whenever he thinks about this time and this place. The low sound of the tide. The rising sun vibrates in the wave's shimmer. The ocean leaks up their backs as they rest in its warm embrace. Summer is as youth, and as long as his tired heart remembers, it will last forever. He didn't know how long it took for Autumn to get there, just that she was there with him now, resting against him in the recliner the nursing staff had been kind enough to move into the room. They'd been kind enough to do a lot of things, letting him stay over like they'd done, feeding him without hesitation. He didn't eat any of it. They couldn't blame him. Their concern sickened him. He never wanted to be the center of attention, never wanted people to feel sorry for him. Feel sorry for her. She'd been withering away and dying for so long it pained him to think about it. His fingers wrapped around the hem of Autumn's floral shirt, her nervous, black chipped fingernails seeking his hand out just to let him know he wasn't alone. The living only grieve the living. It wasn't like he'd had anyplace else to go. Home had always belonged to someone else, even if it was nature. Anywhere was better than the hospital. He rose from his recliner, thumbing the flint wheel of the lighter in his pocket as his eyes hovered past his mother's bed. Autumn cupped his chest, tried to ease him back into the chair. Her eyes were on the clock, its hands spinning far too fast for the both of them. There wasn't much left time left. She kept her eyes averted, doing what she had to do so she wouldn't see her because she didn't know if she could handle seeing her like this. But he'd seen her. Seen her for too long but not long enough. His hands clutched the blankets at the edge of her mattress, tugging down slightly. Time froze, machines he didn't know the names of constant with their whine like a thousand cut kill. Lymphoma had rotted her spectral, her paper skin amalgam with catheter bruises and other prodding cords he wanted to tear away. Her hair was flaxen, stringy and splayed in a mane around the rut her brainpan had dug into the pillow. It'd been weeks since she'd opened her eyes without a morphine drip. At least they knew what had been wrong with her now, even if it was too late. It wasn't all her family, but it was the ones she might have cherished most before she'd faded. She'd wanted to sew Autumn's wedding dress. She'd even had a design. Begun collecting fabric. It took too much energy to cry. He just hovered over her, taking her hand into his. As time drew closer, Autumn felt claustrophobic. He did too, but he had to stay. He reached for his mother's hand, tightening his grip around the coldness of hers. Just one sign. It was all he needed. He felt phantom twitches and his heart sank because he knew they weren't real but he couldn't forgive himself if they pulled the plug and she was going to open her eyes any moment. Any moment. Any damn moment. He clutched her hand until zero hour. Numb to the world. Save for her touch. All the way until the last of her left her body and the machines grew silent. Warmth. Riding along heroin vines, except too filling and bittersweet. Too many blows to the head, scars laced too close, throbbing along the closed stitches he's threaded far too many times to be surprised by the sensation. Her fingers clasping between the webbing of his. His palms are calloused. Cremation. His mother never said what she'd wanted done with her remains, but she'd never been a woman who liked to stay somewhere for long. The funeral parlor's lights were somber, the air choked with lilac. The keeper, Mr. Cotton, wasn't but a wisp of a man, his gnarled knuckles clinched tight and his gaunt cheeks shining a lipstick red. Amongst the dead, appearances didn't matter. Before they'd even entered his office, they'd seen him try to straighten the few remaining strands of his comb over. When they came in, he was quick to stutter his way to his sales pitch, his palms making theirs oily from his handshake. He didn't wait for them to sit down before he was digging through pamphlets and binders in his desk, stopping when he found the right one. Flipping through his booklet, he stopped at the middle point, his eyes darting along the print and coinciding pictures. “This is our most modest receptacle,” he turned the book around, pushing it across the mahogany so Autumn and he could take a look. “This is for basic transportation.” The urn wasn't anything remarkable, just a tapered copper vase with movable handles fixed on brackets. There wasn't any reason to be here longer than they had to be. They knew what they had come in for. “How much?” Terrence asked, digging his elbows into the wood finish. Cotton looked the pair up and down, saw the dark, puffy circles underneath both their eyes and the dirt on their cheeks. “One-hundred twenty sounds about right.” He'd shaved all the way to the point where there would be no profit. It was better than them asking about using a coffee can. Terrence reached in his back pocket, pulling out his wallet and rifling through its contents. Conyers had left him two hundred dollars. Barely enough to survive, but the both of them had survived on less. There wasn't a handshake to complete the sale, just a dollar exchange and a promise that tomorrow would be the closest available time to pick his mother up. They left the funeral parlor, making their way to the aged Speedster Terrence had fixed before the drug bust at the Church. Before his father had run off into the early morning dew like the coward he'd always been. Boosting her up on the seat, she quickly clasped her arms around his waist as he revved up the engine. There wasn't much time to think, and the noise between them was rough on their ears, but he leaned back and spoke so she could hear him. “Ever seen the ocean?” “No.” Another rev. “You will. Soon.” Remember the color of her eyes. They're blue. Sky blue. Remember the sensation he tells himself because the monster inside him has snatched her away, its greedy maw hungering for more. Green. They were green. The color doesn't matter anymore. It's why all he wants to do is die. They'd gathered what they could in knapsacks, taking off in the dead of night because there wasn't any better time. He drove north, the Speedster keeping together through strength of duct tape and her driver's sheer determination. Terrence never took good care of his bikes. He just pushed them to their limits and put them back together again with a little oil and a wrench. Autumn held on tight as they gained speed along the small crests and hills, the helmet on her head sashaying ever so slightly along her chinstrap. The bumps in the road took their toll on her lower back and her tightening thighs. He didn't know the state, didn't know where he was because nothing was registering beyond the hum of the engine and the arms draped around him and the taste of salt in the air. The closer they drew, the more the road tapered off, until concrete was replaced by asphalt, which soon after was replaced by dirt. The moon fell and the sun painted the horizon violet, the promise of warm orange following close behind. Once away from private property lines, tall grass blinded their view, scant lighthouses strewn along the ocean's bed the only things lighting their way. All they needed was an inlet. A break in the forage. They finally found one, a gravel lead-in fenced off with two hollowed stumps and a rusted chain. He ambled the bike along the patches of rock, using his toes to guide along their chalk until he was close enough to park and rest the bike on its kickstand. She released her grip, and he helped her down and took the pack she'd nestled against her side for the entire ride. Her helmet came next, the strap rested on the overhang of the handlebars. As they walked up, they both untied their shoes, kicking them off once rock turned to pure white sands. The wind beat back against them harshly, her long, bunched amber locks pushing out in a wild, untamed mane. He kept one foot in front of the other, drawing closer and closer to the vast Atlantic, and while it wasn't exactly how he remembered it, the sea was big enough to forgive and forget. He kept in front, walking until he was thigh deep in the water. She lingered behind, sitting on the beach as she rolled her pant legs up past her calves. Part of her wanted to be there with him, for him, but she stayed back, lending him the privacy to say his goodbyes. Words were fickle things. They hardly worked to convey true meaning, and in this moment, he couldn't find any that would make sense. None of this made sense. They'd had a faith, but in it, once you left this world, you never returned. Your soul gone forever. He didn't want to believe that. He unzipped the pack, resting the strap on his shoulder as he pulled out the copper urn. The top of it was scotch taped to the body, to make sure there was some kind of seal. Dirty fingernails dug at the tape, and when that didn't work, he gnashed at it until the was a slight tear to pull at. The wind was too harsh, so he opened the lid and dunked the urn into the blue. He was quick to step away, letting the ash expand and settle and be carried away in the current. Free to do whatever she wanted. He kept his head down, his hand combing through his hair as he trudging out of the water. It wasn't until he was halfway up the beach that he turned to see Autumn. She'd walked along the bank, her toes digging into the sand as the waves licked up her ankles. The pack across her back kept her flannel tight against her faintly sunkissed skin. A few steps forward, and they were side by side, their eyes toward the red bayous bobbing in the distance. Her hand met his, their fingers lacing between one another. “It's beautiful,” she said with quiet wonderment, her eyes turning towards his. “Yeah,” he nodded. When he turned to face her, he saw that her dimples showing through the corners of her candy apple cheeks. It was okay to smile again. “Home is wherever we want to make it. So where do you want to go?” The Atlantic was alluring to her in ways she couldn't describe. “Maybe we just rest here for a little while.” They both eased onto the sand, the waves warming them from the chill of the breeze. Their hands locked together. A promise made to himself to never forget the effervescent feeling that they were finally complete.
