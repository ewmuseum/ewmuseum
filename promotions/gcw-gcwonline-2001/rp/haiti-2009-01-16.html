Haiti

16 January 2009
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 1/16/2009 Based on a true story. “Hope has been in short supply in Haiti since a 7.0-magnitude earthquake devastated this impoverished island nation on Tuesday… While there has not been an official count, estimates of the number of casualties in the capital alone range from 100,000 to 150,000.” - CNN.com Cheap Labor sat in a murky room with stagnant air. The light from a computer monitor illuminated his face; the colors shifting across his skin with every new article he read. Each page was another eulogy. Minus the forced clickclick of the computer mouse, the room remained silent. A U.N. humanitarian spokeswoman declared the quake the worst disaster the international organization has ever faced, since so much government and U.N. capacity in the country was demolished. In that way, Elisabeth Byrs said in Geneva, it's worse than the cataclysmic Asian tsunami of 2004: "Everything is damaged... Truckloads of corpses were being trundled to mass graves Saturday.” - MSNBC.com His mind was a demolition site. It’s amazing how a catastrophic event is sometimes required to jar certain people or experiences back into your conscious memory. The earthquake in Haiti was one such event for Cheap Labor. Before his dreadful radio career or before meeting his future ex-wife or before having a kid or before wrestling, he had been to Haiti. He had done great work and touched many folks. All for naught or so it now seemed. The space was no longer silent. The noise from its lone occupant echoed off the walls. It sounded like the entire room was weeping. +++ February 16, 2008 Four months before he’d ever set foot in a broadcast booth, Cheap Labor (although his name was still Mike back then) stepped off an ancient rattling tour bus and planted his sandals into the grimy landscape of Gonaives, Haiti. A moped sputtered by puking thick oily smoke from its exhaust pipe. The town’s air quality existed as a yin-yang. The primary transportation throughout the town was the moped, and none of them seemed to be tuned up correctly. They all coughed black smoke and thick odor. However, even more prominently in the air than smog was the soothing smell of water. Gonaives sat directly on a gulf of the Caribbean Sea. The water from the sea rushed up along a series of rocky precipices not far beyond the town. There were quite a few people around. They all looked like Old Navy mannequins made out of chocolate. Their dark-skin served as a contrast to their brightly plain clothing. Some carried baskets on their shoulders or on top of their heads. None of them wore shoes. Behind Labor, another person stepped off of the bus. The second figure was a blonde, thinly bearded boy who thumped his Bible like John Bonham did a drum set. “How blessed are we to be here, Mike? How blessed are we? Praise be to God!” Mike, who never foresaw himself behind called Cheap Labor less than a year later, uncomfortably shifted his backpack from shoulder to shoulder. He looked at the house and business structures lining the street. They were all made of concrete and sheet metal. Power lines were strung to all of these structures, yet there was no presence of electricity. At this particular moment, there really was no need for it. For February, the sun hung high and Mike was comfortable in his shorts, sandals, and t-shirt. Still, it was a dramatic shift from his technology-laden pad back in Michigan. It was just going to take a minute or two to adjust. He nodded to his overly enthusiastic Missions partner. “Yeah Noah, we are very blessed to be here.” The duo’s presence in Haiti existed solely as a church mission. Most of the townspeople spoke broken English, which was a lucky considering neither of the Americans knew how to speak the Haitian language of Creole. When asked, and Noah was questioning everyone like a proper evangelist, the townsfolk often replied that they were Christian. They provided little elaboration to this answer before scampering away back to their daily lives. Noah knew why. “The primary religion here is Voodou. It’s a holdover from their African roots. The Haitians came to inhabit this land as slaves by the French. They were forced to believe in Christianity and were persecuted if they did not. That’s why everyone says they’re Christian but not many of them really are. We are here to bring Christianity to the forefront, Mike. We want God to bless this country! We want these people to stop worshipping their false idols so the Lord’s glory may spread across this land. They need it, Mike. They need God’s grace and mercy to fall upon this ground!” Mike and Noah hoofed it to the heart of the town. The entrances to houses and buildings existed as open, welcoming portals. There were no obstructive coverings over the vacant doorways. As the Americans would come to find out, the Haitians lived with a charming sense of community. There was no dire need for privacy amongst these people. They congregated in the street during the daylight hours and all worked together to support the children and the elderly. When the two Christians finally came to their assigned dwelling, which was a chore because there was no semblance of an address system, the structure was unsurprisingly empty. Unsure on how to locate their designated family, the duo continued uptown to “HQ” so they may meet their contact in the country, Pastor Maula. Pastor Maula was a short athletic man who spoke fluent Creole, French, Spanish, and English. He dressed exclusively in khaki pants and Hawaiian shirts. He was so highly regarded in his position that the missionaries would never learn his first name, opting only to call him Pastor. The Pastor’s office existed in a dilapidated building that was being gutted and then rebuilt brick by brick. They were even adding on. As Pastor gave the Americans a quick tour of the facility, he explained his vision to them. “Gentlemen, this will be the Christian stronghold for the city of Gonaive. On the bottom floor of this building, we are holding worship services on a weekly basis and the congregation continues to grow with regularity. Our city is anxious to embrace the Word of the true God. The second floor is comprised of offices. My office is located there as are other storage facilities. The third floor is now being converted to temporary dwellings for Christians in need. Had you come six months later, you would be able to stay in this very building. Don't be fooled, though. We are so blessed to have you come now, my friends. And the fourth floor, where the men are stacking the bricks now, those will be classrooms where blind children can be made to see The Light. Exciting prospect, isn’t it?” The Americans nodded in agreement and astonishment. Pastor Maula smiled brightly. “Come, fellow Christians. We have much to speak of.” The trio ascended the stairs to Pastor’s office. The workplace was mostly barren with the exception of a desk and folding chairs. Unlike most American priests, Pastor Maula lacked a college degree to hang on his wall. However, as he would often defend himself, a degree does not declare a clear relationship with The Alpha and The Omega. In the folding chairs, the Americans sat before him. “Gentlemen, please understand that you come here for a dual purpose: one of spiritual importance and one of physical importance. My goal is to bring Christianity to this land so that God may help it prosper. You will be working hand-in-hand with the congregation, mostly the children, and educate them on the many splendors of The Lord. This is your spiritual calling. Your physical calling is equally important, my missionaries. Look out of this window and tell me what you see.” Pastor extended his arm to his right. The view was spectacular, overlooking many large hills covered in lush greenery. “What you see now is nothing as it used to be. These plentiful forests are the victim of much deforestation, which has left our low-lying town as a floodplain. Our worst experience came in the year of 2008. The flood waters rose and rose. Most of the houses in our town are single-level structures. Most of these children of God did not survive. Only those with two-level houses were able to live; moving all of their belongings to the second floor until the flood waters receded. We are still recovering. With our God-given strength, we may help some of these families finally recover from the wrath of God we have brought upon ourselves. By helping them establish normalcy, we may introduce the mercy of our Savior. Go, my friends, and meet your adjunct family. They will treat you as one of their own. Rest, gentlemen, for we will need our collective strength in coming days.” As the Americans walked back into town, where the people of Gonaive had begun to separate back into their respective homes, Mike and Noah had talked briefly. “This is the Lord’s work, Mike. We are privileged to be called to perform it.” Mike guffawed to himself. “It sounds a little like ‘cheap labor’ to me.” Noah considered. “Yes, perhaps. But at least we have a dedicated purpose to fulfill.” Back in 2010, where Cheap Labor sat in front of a computer screen with his head in hands, the recollection of this conversation only intensified his sadness. Where had his purpose gone? +++ Mike and Noah met their impermanent family later on that evening. The differences in culture became increasingly more apparent as the night wore on. Despite the power lines, electricity only existed between one to five hours a day. Showers consisted of dumping jugs of water over oneself until you felt clean. Dinner was prepared exclusively by the youngest mother of the household. Haitian meals consisted of plentiful portions of rice with beans mixed in. Meat was rare in Haitian cuisine, a consequence of stark availability. The Americans quickly took notice that their plates always had meat while the other family members went without. After their first dinner, everyone briefly prayed together and then retired. Noah and Mike, wired from the enormity of their undertaking, lay awake on their cots and rehashed the day. This would be the only night in Haiti that they were not asleep before their American heads hit their pillows. The next morning, the Americans met up with a chipper-looking Pastor Maula and a small Christian faction. The group, consisting of about a dozen people in all, were piled into the back of a rusty old pick-up truck and given a bumpy ride to a few towns over. At their feet, metal spades clanged noisily against the truck bed as they tumbled through myriad potholes. On the outskirts of the neighboring town, every vacant inch of space was piled high with dirt. In between the building there were towering piles of sandy soil. In the backyards of families were mounds of earth tall enough to cast shadows over the houses. This landscape was surreal; infinitely more concerning and worrisome than the SurReal that Cheap Labor would face in a future skirmish. Pastor Maula spoke to his mini-congregation. “Praise be to God! The Bible tells us who we are, what we are here for, and what we are capable of doing. We are children of The Lord and he has given us a daunting mission that will only be possible with His grace. My friends, you may notice these large mountains of soil behind me. This is the loam that has been removed from these surrounding houses. When the floodwaters receded, houses were left polluted with earth and debris. Two years later, as you look at the work that has been done, we realize that a large amount of work remains. We are here to continue God’s work. Please grab a spade, fellow Christians. We will work in three teams.” Mike, Noah, and their third team member worked in one house for the entirety of their day. The house was a single story abode with about as much space as a standard one-bedroom apartment. As the missionaries worked, they had to hunch over to scoop out the debris. The floor had raised no less than four inches due to the amount of soil that had washed into the house through its open doorway. As the Christians worked, they were often approached outside by other townsfolk who pleaded with them to assist them also. These requests were politely declined. Each refusal, no matter how gentle, trampled silently on the broken spirit of an all ready devestated Haitian citizen. Cheap Labor would force his shovel into the dirt. He’d pull up compressed sand and clothes, furniture remnants, pieces of broken dishes, lost family heirlooms and other meaningful things. By the time the sun had fallen, this one house had been cleaned out completely. By the time their task was finished, the pile of dirt dwarfed the Christians considerably. Their backs ached and their hearts pounded. Sweat jumped from their brows and cascaded into their eyes. From inside the house, the Haitian mother came out with offerings of food. She collapsed onto bare knees in front of the missionaries. Tears flowed shamelessly from her eyes. She was so thankful and so relieved. Noah looked at Mike. “See what I told you? This is God’s work. Do you need further evidence?” Over the next three weeks of their work, as they did the same exhausting chore several days in a row, this scene would become commonplace. By the end of their mission, Noah and Mike had discussed how they were happy for their accomplishments. They also talked about how they needed to be careful with their own pride so that it may not become a vessel for their sin. When it came down to it, they realized together that they had only affected a drop of water in the vast ocean of humanitarianism and faith. +++ It felt erased now. Haiti, as a country, had stumbled back several steps. Their devastation had worsened tenfold. Was it the wrath of God? Was it their practice of Voodou and their worship of false idols? Labor didn’t care. Upon learning of the situation in Haiti, Labor had sent a desperate e-mail to Pastor Maula. "Please Pastor, respond to me and let me know you're okay." The response had not yet come. Labor was a mortal man. He feared plenty. He had limited strength and limited resourced. At this point in time, Cheap Labor did not fear SurReal or losing the GCW Television Title. He had power to control those circumstances. However, he was powerless now to help Pastor Maula. He feared for his Christian confidant. Actually, his fear extended even further than that: Cheap Labor feared for a nation in ruin.
