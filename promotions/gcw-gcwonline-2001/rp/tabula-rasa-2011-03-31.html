Tabula Rasa

31 March 2011
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 03/31/11 At some point in your life you were happy. Or you thought you happy. Or someone told you that you were happy. That would be good enough for you, nowadays. But that would take someone wanting to talk to you. One Ingrid Wolfenden’s eyes began her surrender. Draped in the warm amber blanket of the setting sun, she took one last look across the Atlantic ocean - the waters her parents had crossed some 80 years ago - before her eyelids gave in to their final, tender embrace. The softest of kisses from the evening breeze brushed her lips and she smiled, content. “Monty,” she fondly whispered. Under the eyes of God, Montgomery Wolfenden had vowed to be faithful to his wife, always, and she slowly turned her shrunken hand for him to take, to guide. Purple clouds bruised the glowing peach sky above as her ears filled with birdsong and her nostrils filled with the heady scent of lavender. From the bushes, the neighbours’ golden retriever emerged, trotting up the porch steps before sitting dutifully at Ingrid’s feet. He betrayed himself but one whine. The house in front of which she sat offered her its parting gift as it squeezed decades of memories out of its bricks and mortar; every Christmas, every birthday, every family vacation. The laughter which had echoed within its walls finally took its leave and was carried away on the sea breeze. As the last breath of Ingrid Wolfenden was expelled, the retriever turned to look down the garden path, barked twice, then departed back through the bushes, safe that he was leaving her in good hands. Two The face in the mirror, pale and dripping with water, was one from which the memories of your fall had been surgically removed. Your right cheek, butchered with broken glass by the first to see underneath Rasa’s grotesque mask, no longer sported its jagged centrepiece. The scar tissue which you’d displayed as penance for your sins had been accepted by way of surgeon’s blade. You’d finally begun to forgive yourself. Three The secretary’s eyes tried their best to avoid you, so as not to soil her gaze. Every so often, her rat-a-tat-tat typing slowed as she snuck the sneakiest of peeks, her fresh face souring as if your very presence cheapened her. There was no offer of coffee, tea or water; any word spoken to you would have left an unpleasant taste on her tongue. While every glossy strand of her strawberry blonde hair was pristinely pinned in place, yours reflected the emotions which had ricocheted around your head the past few days. It was dry, frazzled, and dyed a choppy mess of black, purple, blue and peach. Her make-up was painstakingly applied and touched up twice in the time you’d been kept waiting, while you eyeshadow had been applied courtesy of Vivica J. Valentine’s fists. You were the reflection of everything she hoped that she would never become… as she was to you. Office life was not your calling. Even the most breathtaking of views were framed by uniform windows and exhibited behind glass and the air you’d breath regurgitated from countless air-conditioning units. “Miss Wolfenden.” Ivan Harris stood in the doorway to his office, the welcoming tone of his words exorcising the frigid atmosphere from the reception area. “Sorry to keep you waiting. Please come in.” As you walked into the lawyer’s office, you felt his secretary’s stare burn into your back, a sensation you tried to douse by scratching it with a stiffly-raised middle finger. “As you’re aware, the reading of your grandmother’s will was conducted yesterday,” he began. Your mother was the one to tell you that they thought it was ‘for the best’ that you attended your own reading, just as you’d occupied you own pariah’s pew at the funeral. Death, apparently, wasn’t always enough to bring a family back together. “I’ll forgo a full reading and just cover the parts pertaining to you, if that’s okay with you, Miss Wolfenden?” “That’s fine,” you answered. It sickened you to think of what Alice would get her greasy fingers on. Every fibre of your childhood memories would unravel upon her touch. You closed your eyes and tried your best to hear it in your grandmother‘s voice “To my granddaughter Karina I leave my home,” he read. “I never did ask you what it was that took your beautiful smile away. I know you are fiercely protective of your privacy, but maybe I should of. For that I am sorry. So what I leave you isn’t bricks and mortar, it is the last place that I remember seeing you truly happy. I truly, truly hope that one day, it will be a family home once again.” Four “Bayard!” Jack Carpenter hollered, a sharp whistle chasing his words down the breeze. “Here, boy!” Shielding his eyes from the sunlight which skipped off of the rippling ocean, he peered into the undergrowth, where the last few inches of a creamy-gold tail slithered out of sight as if repelled by your gaze. “Bayard!” With repetition, patience diminished. “Don’t go blaming me if you get another grass seed stuck your paw, you silly old thing.” As their walks tended to go, Jack followed Bayard’s trail through the overgrown, weed-riddled grass. He caught sight of his golden retriever, but when he went to call out, his plummeting stomach deflated his lungs. Bayard had emerged outside the house once owned by Ingrid Wolfenden, and was in the process of licking its new owner’s face when he finally dredged up the words. “Kari?” In just two syllables his voice fluctuated with emotion, from shock to joy and all the nervy tones in between. “That really you?” Five There was a civility to it all which made you feel uncomfortable. The chitter-chatter of 25 tables blanketed your senses, while the body heat of your fellow diners prickled your clammy skin. The fingers of your left hand drummed at the side of your wine glass while those of your right subconsciously traced your right cheek. “You look, um…” Jack tried to wade through the awkward verbal fondling. He’d made an effort; even though his suit jacket seemed a size too big around the chest and a size too small on the sleeves and underneath the clumsy Windsor-knot of his tie his collar was unbuttoned. He was trying to impress you, which he did, albeit with the misguided effort rather than the result. “Different?” you tacked onto the stray ends of his words. “Grown up.” It was a polite mistranslation. “Has it really been ten years?” he pondered in disbelief. “How the hell has it been ten years already? It still seems like yesterday that we used to race each other along the beach, or try and sneak into The Duchess; I‘d try and get in with an ID I swiped from Max and you, well, girls didn‘t get carded.” You were reserved with your words, instead offering some sounds of agreement. To you, ten years ago was ten years ago; sentimentality wasn’t your thing. You were no longer the teenage girl who used to talk to him via messages left etched into the sand at your own little corner of the beach. He was still the young-faced boy with blonde curls that he was ten years ago, while you couldn’t help but feel your very presence tainted the innocence around you. “You used to talk more,” he said, a little hesitant in his delivery. His words wilted; as if this conversation were an unfulfilling prize for a decade’s patience. “I…” A lot of people in your life had done bad by you; but Jack Carpenter was not one of them. You were there with him because someone had wanted to see you smile again. “I have a habit of saying the wrong thing,” you explained. “Practically all the time. I guess that’s why we got along so well… you kinda knew what I meant even if I got half the words in the wrong order. I didn‘t have to think around you, which, well, made life a whole lot easier. Thinking‘s not exactly my forte either.” “Why did you stop coming here?” he then asked, abruptly. You were as poor with your words as you were with your thoughts, but if anyone deserved an explanation, it was him. “Because there’s not a single thing in my life that I haven’t fucked up.” It was an outpouring of honesty which broke you halfway through its delivery. The barriers that you put up were easily blown down by the ocean breeze. “How does someone who is really, really fucking stupid react to having something perfect? I told myself that there was so much more than this… then convinced myself. But those ten years? I have nothing at all to show for them. Nothing that matters. Nothing that’ll last. All I have is the house and I only have that because… it doesn’t matter. I’m sorry. You need to talk about you now.” He could always read your mood, but he’d never seen it in such extremes. On the outside you barely resembled your old self, and when he looked inside you all he saw was a fading echo of what you once were. “I’d been away for about a year when Dad got sick.” He sensed you were about to jump in. “He’s okay, he’s fine. But he’s just not in boat-builder’s shape anymore, so I took over the business. I guess I needed that little bit of time away to know that this place will always be home. Nothing really tastes right without salt in the air.” You used to talk for hours about absolutely nothing, words flowing as freely as the time they passed. But now the conversation was staggered and disjointed, and it angered you. One of the few people in the world you ever opened up to, and you still couldn’t hold a conversation. A membrane had grown between yourself and society which dulled the sensations. There was nothing for you back home; not even a home. “So…” Jack dangled a hook off of his tongue, trying to catch a bite. He sensed the fragility in you, and his every instinct told him to put you back together. He was a craftsman and you were in urgent need of repair. “How long are you thinking of staying? Any plans for the house? I helped your grandma out with some repairs a couple of years back and that place is solid, as good as the day it was built. A few of the places here have been passed down and let out to tourists. Come the holidays it could be a goldmine.” The conviction drained from his words as they trailed off. “I think I’m going to stay,” left your lips without you being consulted. “I think.”
