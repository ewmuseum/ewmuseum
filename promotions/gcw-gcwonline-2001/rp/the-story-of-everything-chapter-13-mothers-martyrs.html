The Story Of Everything, Chapter 13: Mothers / Martyrs

undated
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: Playoff Season Somehow, I had collected an overabundance of shovels, and my cache had grown so sizeable that modern logic could not explain how I had unknowingly collected all those shovels. I had always felt that you could never have too many shovels, but I couldn't recall acquiring any of the shovels in particular. The process of buying tools had been so mundane to me, perhaps, that I had cleared all prior purchases from my memory and assumed that I needed to buy a shovel any time I came across one. Or perhaps it was something subconsciously deeper. Regardless, Wayne and I dug out some shovels buried under other shovels, selecting those that looked the most suitable for the tasks at hand. You see; I had all sorts. I had shovels for digging dirt and shovels for clearing snow and shovels for chucking coal. I would've remembered chucking coal, and I hadn't done it, but it was reassuring to know that if the situation arose, I would be prepared unless I forgot about my ownership of so many different varieties of shovel. It seemed like it had been ages since either of us had tried Undead Sauce, and upon ingestion, we both tried to remember what effects were sure to come, and in doing so, deciding if they were the same as the effects we were feeling. I can't recall how we had gotten back there to that garage. It just didn't connect that we were here, so far off from where we were, and on top of that, the place had provided haven to such an impressive pile of shovels. Through dusty, tainted windows, I watched as a firestorm brewed outside. Wayne, crippled by laughter or rendered immobile by the expectation of being crippled by laughter sat against the wall, his legs thrust remarkably forward as if he, too, was nothing more than an angle decorated with identifiers. "Stop fooling around, Doctor Wayne!" I barked. "The tractor's running." The triumphant and timely return of the Lanz Bulldog realized, the haggard doctor showed no sign of ceasing his charade, a haze of laughter melted over his face. "I'm going to sit this one out," he said through it. "We've been wandering around for, like, two days, and we're only getting further from where we need to be." "No, this is where we need to be right now," I rebutted, but honestly, I didn't know what now was. I couldn't tell if the sky was dim because it was getting dark or because it was getting light. "We just have one more place to go, and then we can get your car." "I don't think I can last one more stop," piped the doctor with a great perpendicularity. "You're coming with me if I have to drag you kicking and screaming," I refused. But dragging the doctor proved to be quite a feat. Kicking would have made the act of dragging only slightly more laborious and screaming added no further difficulty to the equation. If anything, it would wear Wayne out faster, making him easier to drag. Instead, he kept a perfect stillness, staying true to his right form and pivoting like an oblong wheel, turning over himself rather than sliding against an overcome friction. "I just can't trust you here by yourself." "If I have any more Undead Sauce, my heart's going to stop," feigned Wayne. "Honest." His poorly veiled protest, masked behind buckets of laughter, forced as if he was jamming his fingers down his own throat, did nothing to stop me from hurling the sauced up doctor over my shoulder, his shape allowing me to better wear his weight against itself as if two equal amounts of him were being measured on a balance. "Hey, you asshole!" burst a shocked Wayne. "Put me down." There was something heroic about it, as if I were rescuing him, save for the fact that I was only bringing him to rescue the Undead Sauce. I hurried Wayne over to the Lanz Bulldog and set him inside before loading a shovel, the bag of money and one of the barrels of Undead Sauce in a neat stack on the other side of the seat. I boarded the Lanz and sat at the wheel, all the while operating as a barrier between the barrel and Wayne, who would have surely awakened from his bending to seize the barrel had I not been between them. Operating the land tractor was easier said than done. I was much more suited for a sea tractor. I shared the same sea-worn disposition though I've never even so much as touched a whip of ocean. On land, there were all sorts of clutches and breaks to account for, but the only obstacle of the sea was the loneliness of unchanging scenery. Still, I jilted forth, as fast as the Bulldog's septuagenarian motor could take me. "After we get to my car, I want to split up the money and split up," angled Wayne. "I can't take any more of this shit." We had three quarters of a million dollars sitting in a burlap sack with a large, bold dollar sign printed on its side. I felt such a large, bold choice in baggage design took a certain amount of bravado that would ward away potential thieves and any other curiously general riffraff. Of that three quarters of a million dollars, two-thirds came from Westonbrook's up front payment and the other third of the three quarters of a million dollars came from the locker where we exchanged the letter in lieu of the detachable unit. This is where it gets convoluted. "I'm going to need you to stay there with me for a while once we go back for your car." "Why?' "Numbers." "What?" "I told Westonbrook to meet us at the church, and I told Emery that I was telling Westonbrook to meet us there." The fiery sky spat down around us as I slalomed through across the road. "Emery's going to take care of Westonbrook for us, and he's going to bury him in the cemetery out back." "So we are picking up the rest of the money?" "No," I regretted to inform. "I told Emery he could keep it in exchange for putting Westonbrook underground." "So there is no money?" Wayne asked rhetorically, dumbstruck. "Then what the hell are we going back there for? We should take what we got and get as far away from here as possible." "There are still loose ends that need tying up," I reminded the corner of Wayne. "I've got to let Emery know the new locker number and combination so one of his men can pick up what we left for him at the airport, and before I go around giving that away, I want to make sure that Westonbrook's actually buried. Thus, numbers." "I'm not helping you dig up a body," Wayne washed his hands. "I'm not down with that." "Then just play lookout so I can dig without having to worry about any funny business." "I don't know," Wayne uneasily chewed, choosing his next words carefully. "I don't think so, Johnny. I'm just going to take my money, get in my car and drive. I'm not going to look back, and maybe it'd be wise if you did the same thing." "We've got to give Emery the combination. We made a deal." "You made a deal." "If you want any of the money, so did you." Wayne didn't have much to say to that, and the embittering amount of time we'd been plastered together by circumstance and the amount of money at stake really soured us at each other. "So we're headed there now?" Wayne asked. "Nope," I declined. "We're going to a different cemetery. I've got another grave to dig up." "Christ, Johnny. How many graves are you planning on digging up today?" "As many as I have to." All Saints Cemetery wasn't much like I remembered it. Of course, the last time I was there, I was drunk and uninvited. I didn't know how I had caught wind of the funeral details, or if I had just merely stumbled upon them, but the newspapers report that a man who identified himself as Johnny Watt, age unknown, was quickly ejected and placed under arrest for disturbing a gravesite. It was a serious crime, but it was only as serious as the yellow journalism of the police blotter made it out to be. If I hadn't served the sentence firsthand, I never would've believed the sensationalized media account of the events. Eryn was buried there, and I was going to see if it was true, what that voice had said about Undead Sauce. It raises the dead. Still, by inflection, I couldn't tell if it was some sort of slogan or some sort of necromantic sorcery. I was grasping at straws, but at the time, the idea of magically resuscitating the long dead didn't seem that particularly farfetched. Then again, the skies rained fire. "I think I'm going to handle this one myself," I said to Wayne towards nobody. "Sure," Wayne perked up, somehow still angled, but his excitement waned when I took the Undead Sauce with me along with one of the shovels and walked off through the gates. It took some searching, cause there were a lot of markers, and hers didn't stick out any more than any of the rest of them. Once I did find it, I swallowed hard and rested the jug of sauce against the headstone. The ground was hard and cold, and it took some wrestling and jabbing to break dirt, but once I was through, time and soil alike flew. It took hours actually to clear away enough earth to get at the coffin. I reached out from the hole and grabbed the jug of Undead Sauce. Then, I unhinged the top half of the coffin and cringed. All that was left was bones. "All that's left is bones." I whispered it over and over again, and I wasn't sure what I was expecting, but for some reason or another, it wasn't this. I about lost it there, and I think anyone would if they were in my place. I about lost it, and I started slathering the dirty bones in psychedelic hot sauce, expecting them to be revived, but nothing happened. "Fuck!" I screamed, and I grabbed the bones and started shaking them. I fought back tears, and I really didn't know what else I was expecting. "Fuck! Fuck! Fuck!" in rapid-fire succession. I dropped the bones back into the heap, and these weren't clean bones like a science class skeleton. These were darkened, dirty bones, in a moderate state of decay. These were the old, rotting bones of what used to be a young, rotting woman, and I had covered them in a hot sauce with severely hallucinogenic properties. "Fuck." I gave up, and I broke down, and, perhaps worst of all, I started licking the sauce back off the bones, thinking that maybe refilling the sky with fire would set my head right. It started to rain, and I pulled back up from my supping, rose to my knees, knelt on the closed bottom half of the coffin's lid and started to wonder what the fuck I was doing. *** I didn't even bother filling the hole back in. I don't what I thought saucing up the bones would do, but I had used the whole jug anyway, and Wayne was pretty miffed about that. He no longer held position, exhibiting his usual limberness. I didn't mind, though. I didn't feel much like talking. All that was left was bones. I still kept that whispering through my mind. There were only bones. There had always only been bones. The Lanz Bulldog was not built for city driving, and the other drivers on the road were sure to point that out to me. Some honked irascibly, others swore or gestured and the most passive simply passed me. I didn't even take the time to appreciate the sensibility of that observation. "Can't this thing go any faster?" complained Doctor Wayne. "All that was left was bones," I replied, not necessarily to the doctor's question, but to getting acclimated to words again. "Forget about her," groaned Wayne. "You met her once. You probably never would've seen her again if she hadn't died. You probably wouldn't have even wanted to see her." I made a sound, but it wasn't a word, so it didn't matter, and it only served to confirm the doctor's suspicion. Even though he had no medical training, Wayne was astute, and though he couldn't have figured out the workings of my mind, he knew how my mind didn't work. "Don't talk about her like that," I mumbled, defeated but defiant to admit that. "Whatever," scoffed Wayne. "Let's just get this over with." Neither of us said anything, but at the tractor's pace of the Lanz Bulldog, our silent progress was awkward almost added a degree of hilarity, certainly to the casual onlooker. We were two men, neither really disposed to looking necessarily angry, trying to appear menacing and at ends with each other, cramped together in the unaccommodatingly intimate seat of an antique land tractor. Still, I was tired of digging and tired of shovels. They had run their course for me, and after this, I'd swear it off forever. I was getting by on a narrow set of skills, honed by repetition and also mired by it. Nobody seemed to notice, but it was wearing me down. I was surviving, but it was wearing me down, and eventually it'd kill me. We approached Ascension Church, supposedly attended by Father John Thomas Meyer, according to the sign. Today, it wouldn't be. I had written Emery that I didn't want anybody there. I told him I'd dig up the grave, confirm Westonbrook's burial and leave the locker information in between the pages of the bible on the pulpit. Wayne and I exited the tractor and stopped at his car to load the bags we already had in the trunk. "You sure you don't want to just bail now?" asked Wayne, shutting the trunk on the money. "We can't do that," I sighed, pulling out a card measured specifically to carry the five letters MMXMM. Or maybe it was some kind of imaginary Roman numeral. It had some significance, but no significance to me. Latin was a dead language, so Roman numerals were dead to me, and to me the dead had no significance. "I've got to leave a number, but I'm not leaving anything without assurances." I started walking towards, shovel over shoulder like a continental soldier, but Wayne held by his car. "Come on," I impatiently coaxed. "We've got to do this." It was enough to prompt Wayne to lollygag behind, a sheepish shadow creeping after me. It was bad luck to walk into a church with a shadow following you, but I didn't know that. It was even worse luck to walk into an empty church in the same manner, and Ascension was vacant and eerily resonant. I thumbed through the bible at the pulpit as Wayne dragged himself through the door. I thrust out my palm from the stage. "I'm going out back to dig. I want you to keep watch from here, and if you see anyone coming or hear anything, you come get me. Got that?" "Sure thing," said Wayne, looking back towards the entrance, which had somehow metamorphosized into an exit. It was all about angles. "And give me your keys," I added. "What?" "Your car keys." I held out palm in a scooping motion from it having been thrust forward to being bowled upward. Wayne walked forward and dropped the keys into my hand, and I pocketed them, but I didn't like the look in his eye. He was going to leave me in the dust. "Anything else?" "Yeah," I snared, half-joking. "Say a prayer for me, would you?" Wayne looked apologetic. I knew he wasn't going to run, but I kept his keys so he couldn't, and I went on out the back to dig. I found the freshest dirt and sank the shovel in, and it was still loose and easily penetrable. It was like a species of quicksand with a sense of cheap gloom over imminent danger, but it was a deceptively cheap gloom and was more like a doom disguised as a gloom. I had sunk so far and cleared so much away that I found myself stuck in the nozzle of a funnel. The ground hardened beneath me. I scraped around like a madman with a love for soup, scraping his spoon for the last pip of broth. "Shit," I said mid-epiphany. "I've got to get the fuck out of here." "I'm afraid this is where the game has to end." I recognized the voice, and it didn't matter whose it was. I heard the hammer click and lock, and I knew I'd been had. It was all of them rolled into one and I'd been had. "I hope you know you're a miserable piece of shit," I departed. "Go ahead. Do it." I had dug so many graves, it's no wonder that I'd eventually dig my own. But as I waited there, I knew that I'd never hear the gunfire, if it did, but it hadn't, and it couldn't. A blow to the head with a shovel wouldn't have the same effect, but it would have to do. *** I had escaped. I had somehow squeezed out the other end of the funnel, and it was like being born again in a decidedly reverse fashion. It was peaceful, though, once you could get past the feeling of the worms crawling around on your body. You had to just let go of it, detach yourself from the body and let it fertilize the soil. There was a voice underground; a low, lone frequency that hummed a lullaby that was sound but not song. The tone was like a key, and I could hear it again, murmuring unpronounced with precise vibrations that kept balanced things we'd never know were off-kilter until we were all thrown sliding off the planet and into space. "Johnny." I sank in this kin of quicksand. It continued sinking in theory where it stopped in practice. I was digging without a shovel, and all I had left to burrow away from were the words, the names, all of them that had been forged for the sake of codes. "Johnny, I know you're there." I couldn't help but wonder if our names had all been codes, if we were written down on cards somewhere, beginning as arbitrary combinations and rearrangements of letters. Is that all that life was? "Johnny, I'm only here to help you." "Is that all that life was?" I shouted. And I heard it echo and rebound back at me, but I was stuck, done with spatial affairs and left to the will of angles. "What do you mean, 'is that all?' That wasn't enough?" "Maybe it was a bit too much. It's just that you only get one shot. You do what you do, and you don't get any second chances to do it again if it all falls apart." "Well, what would you have done differently knowing what you know now?" "I didn't have much choice in the matter. It was all a chain reaction, and anything I did only made it that much more volatile. Once the cards started falling, I just had to keep going or the whole structure would collapse on top of me. It was inevitable and I should've seen it coming," I laughed. "So who are supposed to be? Are you me, too?" "I'm just the earth. These are just vibrations, meaningless vibrations. Though you've put meaning to them." They were all after me, but I couldn't tell if it was the words or the names. They were all just vibrations or representations of vibrations, each marked by peaks and valleys whether heard or written. They had buried me, the words, not the shovels themselves but the recollections of shovels. The words were the ones trapping me. It was the threats and the promises and the jaded, often forgotten descriptions, not the men behind the guns. It was the letters and the numbers that had ultimately condemned me. Everything else was just alphanumeric mathematics. "So what happens next?" I asked. "It's all just over like that?" "Yeah," the frequency droned. "As soon as I snap my fingers, it'll all be over." "Fair enough," I acquiesced. There was no point in fighting it now. I was tired of fighting and tired of digging. I was always fighting or digging, and besides, I wasn't blindfolded.
