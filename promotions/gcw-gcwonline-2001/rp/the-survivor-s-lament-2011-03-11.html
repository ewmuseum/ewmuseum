The Survivor's Lament

11 March 2011
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 03/11/11 As I peered through the window I gazed upon a dull sky, threatening rain. The streets were silent, even the squirrels humbled. That first drop of water splashed across the window pane a half second before its brethren dove toward the ground and everything between the sky and the ground. At least, that was always how I thought it’d be if ever I got the news. It wasn’t. Sitting at a booth, delicately balancing my newspaper as to not dip it into the previous patron’s coffee spilt across half the table top, the ink blackened more than my fingertips. “Second Lieutenant Lee Redding, Three Others Killed in Roadside Bomb Attack,” it screamed. I hated my eyes for the moment that they dared to deliver such a story. But you know what they say about killing the messenger. “… returning home March 9th … to be buried at … National Military Cemetery in Petawawa. Redding, a local ,grew up in Kitchener, Ontario. … He is survived by … Mother Helen, Father David, Sister Grace and Brother Alexander.” Not exactly how I wanted to get my name into the paper. And while my world had been dealt a dizzying blow, the world continued to turn. The smokers continued to pollute the air, the caffeine addicts got their fixes. Even the sky refused to sympathize: clear blue and sunshine. You were their brother just as much as mine. The village that raised a child couldn’t think to pause for the briefest of moments to say goodbye, thank-you. It was all a little much for me, and I slipped out of the hard plastic chair, my back happy to be standing again, just needing to get away from this. I couldn’t take this - life - for the moment. I needed to be hid away in some place that I could close the blinds to an indifferent sky, lock my door from the non-grievers. I know you’d want me to be stronger than this, but what right do you have to govern my emotion? I never did tell anyone that you cried at Bambi when you were ten. As I pull open the door and let that dank air kiss my face, an indigent bastard brushed past me and on with his busy day. Not so much as an excuse me, pardon me, piss off. Nothing. Asshole. Killed by a roadside bomb. Killed a five thousand miles away from home. My little brother had to be so tough. My little brother went and joined the military. Army? But then you always did say that the Navy was for sailors and at least it wasn’t like you were killing people from five thousand feet above. Killed. What made you think war did anything but kill? Shit, I could have told you that. Was it the education that hooked you in? So, I still have student debts; I am alive to pay it. And you, you’re dead. Didn’t we get taught the same lessons? We both grew up in Catholic schools. You heard those commandments just as often as I did. Probably more, since you didn’t spend nearly enough of your education in the pool halls. Number 5: Thou shall not kill. What? You thought it was talking to some other ‘thou’? What exactly did you think you were signing up for when you signed your life over to the military? For the love of fuck, we don’t even have 5 submarines in our Navy. You rushed to sign up for that proud tradition? Thou shall not kill. Least you be killed. I didn’t think you’d have it in you, little brother. But then again, you always did want to show your toughness. You always had to try and measure up to me, look stronger than me, better than me. I thought I told you that you won when the family split? “I’m sorry sir; we don’t start serving alcohol until 11.” She looks pretty, about your age. Come to think of it, you’d probably remember her as a high school crush, friend, rival … fuck, something. But that’s the thing, she’s nothing to me. Nothing but a blonde with a decent rack telling me that my twenty isn’t worth shit compared to her rules. How did people see you? Just another GI with a gun and bad haircut? The enemy? You were more than that. You were blood. The kid with the most promise in this family and you threw that all away to do your nation proud. And how do they see you now? Hero? Just another name in the paper? “Sir, I told you: we cannot serve you alcohol for another hour and a half.” And like that, she is kicking me out of her fine establishment. Apparently I was too sad a case to sit alone in a bar that was open at 9:30 in the morning, but didn’t start selling until 11. I can take a hint. I start out the door and figure I could use a nice walk to clear my head, since I wasn’t going to be able to drown it. I forget my twenty on the counter, and she doesn’t remind me. My little brother, and the first time in a year that I see him is in the newspaper telling me that he is dead. I don’t know who to blame there. I was off working around the world and you were half a world away fighting the good fight, or whatever they wanted to call that bit of savagery these days. It didn’t use to be like this. Back when we was kids. You remember, right? Back in the day when we’d be out throwing ball on the street until the sun ‘tanned’ the outline of our shades across our faces, and Mom called us in for that God-awful stuff she dared to call supper. Mom; I know it is her fault. She was the one that split up the family. She was the one that moved you out to Alberta. Idiots out there think they are cowboys. And Mommy Dearest went and took an impressionable 16 year old to Calgary. Right then and there she signed you up to die in the service of your nation. She stripped you away from your big brother. She stripped me of my little brother. For what? Because it was too hard to live here? Because she couldn’t take the looks that everyone gave her, married to a man in prison. Or maybe it was just to dig her talons into a new man’s flesh and drain his bank accounts too. How long was it before Mom brought Jerry around to meet you and Gracie? I will never forgive her for taking away from me what precious little time we brothers would have to spend together. Rest knowing that you not the reason that she and I will never talk again. You weren’t there to know the first reason. It was the only one I’d ever need. And the bottles clang as I set the box on the kitchen counter. I pop open the door and slide it into the fridge. The hour it’d take to cool was a touch to long. It wasn’t until it noticed the bottle of Grey Goose peaking behind the milk that I thought about smiling for the first time today. I hurriedly twisted the cap off and pulled out a short glass. I poured a finger into it, rushed it down my pallet and put the glass in the sink. The bottle and I made our way to the lay-z-boy. We kiss and I feel warm. I don’t think you’ve ever seen the place. It’s nothing special, but it does me fine. It’s got this comfy chair that I can melt into and the flat screen to kill my time, and this annoying flickering red light. It’s an awkward reach to press play on the answering machine. “BEEP. Yeah, Alex, boy o, where’ve ya been? I’ve been trying to get a hold of you for a few days now. Nothing from Palmer either. You boys ain’t trying to give this honest man a heart attack, are ya?” Sorry Grady, today doesn’t look like the day I’m going to deal with your crap. It’s a day like today that I need a friend, not a manager. I need a brother. Not a brother in arms. Not a brother in a damn box. You are missed, Lee. You might have liked Grady. He is full of shit, don’t get me wrong, but it’s just so comically so. He’d just as soon invite you in on the scam as try to get you with a different scam. A true snake oil salesman, the type you thought had died out three decades ago. I’d say you guys would meet some day, but I don’t think he destined for those fluffy clouds you call home now. I won’t ask what death was like, because I am rather certain that I will find out one day. But what is Heaven? I mean was there this bright white light you walked to, met up with Grandpa and Lucky? We were so creative in naming the dog, weren’t we? I guess it is funny to think of the guy with the crucifix tattooed to his arm is marked for Hell, huh? Like I told you, you always were the better, brother. Just please do not tell me it is nothing. I’ve already got plenty of nothing now. Tell me there is that bright, white light and not just eternal darkness. Please Lee, give me some sign that there is more to all of this. Tell me you did not toss your life away in a useless war to be rewarded with a nothing of an afterlife. I’d kill God myself if this is it. But I open my eyes again and it’s pitch black, stay a stray sliver of street lamp light that makes its way across the living room floor. The bottle is lying at my side, empty and pointing at a pool of liquor soaked in my jeans. I search for the clock for a second, and then pray my head to stop spinning for the next several. The room and my stomach finally settle and I look at the LED dashes that read, “1:26 AM.” The immediate reaction is disbelief that I had drank a day away, but that passes into an acceptance. I would have done nothing with that time except moan and morn. Walking around in my boxers, I toss the jeans into the washer. The only other clean thing I have are my shorts. They’ll have to do, I think as I slip them on. The next thing is to feel hungry, then frustrated. I’m pretty sure the only thing I have in the fridge expired days ago. I look out the window and now it decides to rain? Don’t I look the height of fashion when I stride down the streets in my black jacket, cotton shorts and sneakers? Like I give a damn. I dare them to press me. Was that what you thought? Just a good fight to get that primal aggression gone? Notch it up to a war and think you’ll be done with the thing forever? Psychopaths and suckers: that’s what they tell me the army is made up of. Just psychopaths who smile at the thought of tearing apart the whole and suckers who didn’t know what they’d gotten themselves into. I didn’t raise no sucker of a brother, did I? A dirty business overthrowing a government. It takes blood, sweat and tears, but mostly blood. Kill the men that dare stand up for the evil dictator and move on to storm the castle. Pull the bad man out of his palace and then place his neck in a noose for everyone to see. But then you have to fend off all the power hungry that see this great void they’re more than willing to occupy. And that’s how the people see you, just an agent of the power hungry, and occupier. And then after a while, the politicians back home decide that the effort is not working, and we should pull out. The bastards decide to put a puppet in the government and call it a victory. Your death was just a part of their victory. I give it five years before this whole thing crumbles back into a government that the politicians will scream to be overthrown. It is a damn dirty business. “That’ll be $4.92,” she spits, luckily, not on my fries. I hand her a five and don’t wait for the change. I don’t need her touch another thing of mine. I can’t say I blame them for rolling out the B team on this overnight deal, and must say I was quite glad. McDonalds had always had this nasty way of making you feel like a dirty old man when you think about the girl at the till like that, because she is a girl. A fucking 17 year old that will chase you off to a strip club to look at some 30 something single mothers to just even things out. I choke through the McDeal all the while thinking about how I shouldn’t cross my thoughts between strippers and 17 year olds. Promise and whores. Honour doesn’t seem to make its way into either of those. How in the hell did it ever get associated with the business of killing? What great propagandist ever told young boys that there was honour in taking the life of another and justifying it as mutually accepted? How long have you confused glory with honour? What glory is there even left in war? You were little more than a paragraph in a third rate newspaper and the people didn’t even seem to care. They might salute your casket, but with every passing thought, I cannot. I cannot. I will not. You were my brother, but you are dead. You are off on some silver cloud laughing down at your big brother strolling in the middle of the night looking like a bum or burning in Hell for breaking the big number 5. Don’t tell me you’ve sifted off into the ether. Either way, my brother is gone. On that parade route will be a corpse in a box that represents everything I hate right now. I wouldn’t be saluting the man; I’d be okaying the soldier, giving a thumbs up to the war. I’m sorry, but I sure you get this. That’s why I am explaining this to you know. There is no nameless, faceless mass that we call an army. We’d like to think of it that way because it is just that much easier, but there is no such thing. It’s a collection of young men (and now women) who agree to kill and be killed that we call soldiers. It is these soldiers committing individual actions, sins and crimes. You do not get the privilege to turn off your conscience and become a machine. You must be held accountable for your actions and your actions alone. Brother, sweet brother, you don’t get to forget that I taught you there was always a choice. Always. A few days roll past, and the air I am breathing changes from fresh to smoggy with the wind. The smell of the big city wafts westward and the exhaust streams up from the busy highway. I don’t know what I am doing here, but something told me I had to be here. I cast my gaze to the crowd gathered on this overpass and see a mix of people. Sticking out the most is the Fire Department. Not lost on the subtly of the grey of the bridge and road below are the flags that hang limp, all red and white and proud. A dull murmur picks up as the long black cars make their way into view from around that corner. This was the Highway of Heroes and I took solace in the faces of the old and young alike here to send you off, one last time. It wasn’t until I moved my hand to wipe away a solitary tear that I noticed it planted firmly on the ridge of my forehead. Dead soldier, I salute you. Brother, I’ll always miss you. And it is that War will never disappear so long as young boys understand that the way to power was through violence and the threat of violence. This is the soldier’s lament. “He is survived by his fiancée, Mary, Mother Helen, Father David, Sister Grace, and Brother Alexander.” I didn’t even know you were getting married.
