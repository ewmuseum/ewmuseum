The Ballad of Benny Johnson

10 February 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2-10-06 Benny was one of those guys who could make a smoked-out bingo hall seem somehow less carcinogenic just by walking through the room. Everybody knew the guy. He was the hometown football hero in high school, that kid who fell madly in love with the prom queen and promised to run away to Miami or the Keys or anywhere other than Edmond, Oklahoma. He married his queen right out of school, and unlike most overly romanticized fables of love and loss, they’ve actually stayed together these past seven years. They even got a six-year-old junior quarterback just learning to tie his own shoes. Skin the rabbit style, just like daddy taught him. He was a class act, that Benny Johnson. Everybody loved Benny because he was an abnormality in our quiet little community; he didn’t swear, he didn’t take the Lord’s name in vain, didn’t drink or smoke or consume anything more toxic than a glass of extra pulpy orange juice. It was just his style to be everybody’s best friend, and the funny thing was, he was sincere about everything. A real abnormality these days. No, he didn’t go on to become the doctor who cured cancer or found a vaccine for HIV. He wasn’t the world’s fastest hundred-meter sprinter, but he did own a coffee mug with World’s Greatest Dad emblazoned on the side. Benny tore himself a groin when he went out for the Sooner’s football squad, which squashed his pro dreams quicker than the time it took to hike a pigskin. He didn’t even dwell on it or mope about how his life was ruined, not Benny. He simply shrugged it off (well, not literally since it was his nuts that did the tearing) and jumped on a new bandwagon, one that he feel in love with immediately. Let me tell you a little something about Oklahomans and professional wrestling. We practically invented the sport, depending on who you ask. You can call us a bunch of backwater cow-tippers until you’re pink in the cheeks, but you never met Benny Johnson, now did you? So how could you know how important it was to Edmond that our hometown hero become something special, something our own children could look up to? He was a rock, and he was our strength whenever we felt down about our own little insignificant Oakie lives. Sounds silly to you city folk, don’t it? If you think any of this is funny, just try to hold on to the humor. Whenever Benny had a show, you could bet on at least half that audience being from Edmond. He had his own cheering section wherever he went, and as an independent wrestler he was able to find gigs from Chicago all the way down to Florida. I joked about with him, saying that he finally made it to Miami but he forgot to bring the town with him. He mostly fought other no-name indy workers from the area, sometimes down into Texas he’d hook up with a smaller promotion and work a match with some guy from TV, but that was rare. So as you can imagine, the day that GCW came rolling into Oklahoma City and Benny got selected to work a match with Rich Rollins and Jason Dread, well, the entire state knew about it. We put up posters and made tee-shirts in the community, letting the world know how proud we were of our Benny and how no big shot city boy was going to get the chance to pin him down. Sometimes I think it was our constant and obsessive blind faith in Benny that led to his breakdown, but I’ll get to that in a minute. It was a frigid Friday night when I walked into the Ford Center in downtown Oak City, bought myself a hot dog, and found my seat at ringside next to Benny’s wife and kid. Gina was a sweet girl, I always liked her. She had this ritual before every one of Benny’s shows where she’d turn her wedding ring upside-down with the diamond tucked into her palm, just so she could slap the shit out of anybody who hurt her Benny who was unfortunate enough to wander in her direction. Many a wrestler had come away from a Benny Johnson match with a princess pearl-shaped cut seared into his cheek. Lots of guys in the back had come to know it as “Gina’s Touch,” and if you got one, it meant you did something uncalled for on poor Benny Johnson. Then it was your ass. Such was life in the wrestling rings of Oklahoma. The show was a riot all the way up to the main event. Since it was an untelevised house show Benny was the star of the night as he teamed with some slick little lucha fucker from Arizona to take on the duo of Rich Rollins and Jason Dread. I remember when the Global Champion came out the whole arena wanted to smother the barricade and squash him like a squirrel under a semi, but the only thing that stopped us was the fact that we knew Benny had it covered. He never got scared, not even in the face of two of professional wrestling’s biggest and meanest faces. He just waited patiently in the ring, blowing a kiss to his six-year-old son who was too busy beating a plastic Rollins into the guardrail to take notice. Benny started it off and locked up with Dread, and I was out of my seat with excitement. Here he was, little town Benny Johnson, brushing elbows and swapping swings with two of the most recognized names in sports entertainment, and it was all happening right in front of me. Gina fiddled with her wedding ring as Dread planted her husband with a military press, but he recouped fairly quickly. A few Mongolian chops and a head-scissors takedown later and Dread tagged out to the Renegade. Taking in the sound was like trying to shove a watermelon through your trachea; it was too much to handle, but goddamn if it wasn’t sweet all the same. I could tell that Benny was nervous as he looked across the canvas at Rollins. The Renegade had one of those cocky little half-smiles on his face, sizing up our hometown hero as if he were a stray dog that needed to be put down, but Benny wasn’t going to be an easy mark. Not tonight. They did the cat-and-mouse thing for a bit, trading punches and exchanging holds. Whenever Rollins would try and put Benny in his place, good “ol Benny’d just stand back up and stick a forearm in his face. It wasn’t until the Global Champion caught Benny with that belly-to-back suplex that it all went screwy. He did it on purpose. I know he did, just to keep Benny from looking good in front of his home crowd. He released Benny way too early, and just like that, our very own Benny Johnson was a paraplegic. I’ll never forget the look on Gina’s face when Benny’s head snapped back on the canvas. You could hear the bone break from our seats, it was that bad. Her son didn’t understand when he asked, “Mommy, why isn’t daddy blinking?” That was our signal. Whenever Benny took a spot that looked worse than it really was, he’d look over at us and blink three times, letting us know he was fine. But he wasn’t blinking. The referee knew something was wrong, but he couldn’t call the match on account of foul play, not when that bastard Rich kingshit Rollins was involved. Medics came to cart Benny away while Gina screamed in horror, but security wouldn’t let her jump the rail. More than one of those yellow shirts felt her Touch that night, let me tell you. The match continued for another couple of minutes before the Arizona luchadore got pinned by Jason Dread. Rollins watched from his corner the entire time, completely emotionless as my brother was wheeled away on a stretcher. The heartless bastard. Benny knew how to take a fall. He knew, damnit. He would have never jeopardized himself like he did if we hadn’t have pumped him up before the show. As Dread and Rollins posed in the ring, an ocean of wadded Benny Johnson tee-shirts began to cover the canvas as the Oklahoman crowd teetered on the brink of anarchy, demanding assurance that it was all just a work. A ruse. A cruel practical joke. Sure, Benny lost the match, but he couldn’t really be hurt. Not Benny. This was one week ago. I sat in a hospital combing my brother’s hair as his wife rustled in a restless sleep on the tiny sofa next to him. I was there when the doctors showed us the CAT scans and analyst sheets, hearing the sound of his snapping vertebrae as the examiner pointed out the fractures in Benny’s spine. I helped Gina look at wheel chairs, deciding whether or not to go with a mechanized model or let Benny build up his arm strength with a traditional chair. When he woke up, Benny let his wife know that his arms would always be there to hold her, even if his legs could no longer take them to Miami. He wasn’t even mad about the accident. That was just his style. ~Fin~
