Labour's Lost Love

16 May 2010
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 05/16/10 It was typical, really, that after a lifetime of false starts, Tim Shipley’s first foray into the world of being a winner should be so unapologetically struck through by the news from back home. "Come quickly," his mother’s frightened voice had told his voicemail. Only half-listening until then, still buzzing with the afterglow of winning the Rampage Royal, Tim’s ear pricked up: his mother was never frightened. "Kate has gone into labour." He didn’t, in fact, come quickly. Notwithstanding the realities of geography -- he was in Hawaii, the US mainland half an ocean away -- there was a lot intruding on the mind of Tim Shipley. He had never been the sort of person to fight blindly through a day. To wake up without knowing exactly how he would spend his morning was alien to such a meticulous planner. For the most part, he felt it heartening to know that he rarely wasted a moment; after all, our stock of them is limited, and worse, could run out without warning. But on a day like today, he couldn’t process it all at once, and this was costing him rather severely. The nagging suspicion -- that the self he had finally found was not, at all, what he wanted it to be -- had probably been there for some time, he had grown to realise. But it had been brought into sharp relief when all his best laid plans came to fruition, on that night when he and hired hand Cheap Labor had put Andy Murray on the shelf. He could tell himself, again and again, that what they had done was for the best -- indeed, he did. Each night he ran through the rationale, not allowing a single logical link to go unexamined. Only in this way could he sleep. Inescapably, he had found himself to be cold at heart, selfish of mind, and rotten somewhere deep within his soul. This chilled him. Yet, simultaneously, it gave him a paradoxical sense of relief. To know that he was not the man he had once imagined drew a huge weight off his shoulders. He would never right the wrongs of capitalism, he would never build schools in the godforsaken wastes of Africa, he would never volunteer at a Harlem soup kitchen -- and he was OK with that. Tim Shipley could only fight the battles he’d been equipped for. And with this realisation came a new approach to morality. He hadn’t thought it through just yet, but he suspected that he might not want to raise this baby after all. The ethics of it were unfathomable. Technically, of course, he was a rapist. There was no denying it. It was his closest guarded secret, and for good reason; one false word and his career, his life, his freedom would all disappear, as irreversibly as the puffing out of a flame. And the facts of his darkest hour had scratched scars into Tim Shipley’s very soul, scars that would never leave him, scars that could never fade. Yet fundamentally he could not bring himself to see this as evidence that he was a bad person. Because though he might well be a bad person, this wasn’t what made him so. To give an action a name and speak of it in such fearful tones is to condemn a man to a lifetime of self-hatred. Tim Shipley would not sentence himself to that. He knew that he was bad, and he knew what made him bad, and it wasn’t that. Good people could make mistakes, he had reasoned across innumerable sleepless nights after the incident. And when he had rolled over in the mornings and seen her next to him -- her pale skin -- her blue eyes -- her arms drawn up around her, hugging the duvet close -- he had felt something close to love. And so they had lived; in love. For a time. Even if he hadn’t been bad. Even if they had lived, and loved, and smiled at each other over croissants and orange juice in the mornings -- they were always sinking into the quicksand. No matter how fervently they pulled themselves up, they still sank; unstoppably, tragically. For even before the most indecent, indeed criminal, expression of his love for her had come the betrayal. Just as he could never take back a sequence of actions that would hang over him until his dying day -- through marriage, illness, love and decay -- she could never take back her betrayal. And with every minute it grew within her. If Tim Shipley was a man of lesser stature, he would tell you that all this was fucked up. Because, more than anything, it was. A lifetime of education had not prepared him for this. Discrete doses of awkward hands-off English parenting had never even threatened to. This was something that could not be learnt other than from experience. And so he had fumbled through. The lives of Tim Shipley and Kate Barnard had been conjoined from their school days, but never could either have imagined they would be so inscrutably entwined. He avoided having to introduce her to anyone, because he simply did not know how to refer to her. My high school crush turned live-in (occasional) lover, fresh out of the psych ward, pregnant with my once-friend’s child -- oh, yeah, and I raped her, once. Meet Kate. You know what -- he would think. I’m not a man of stature after all. This is fucked up. * * * "You missed the birth," she told me, her face blank. "I’m sorry," I said, though I’m not sure I hid my ambivalence. I had always hated the thought of childbirth -- hell, I had always hated babies. My mother used to tell me that when it was my turn, it would be different. Well, it was my turn now, and I wasn’t feeling it. I guess it wasn’t my child, so. Either way, it didn’t make a blind bit of difference. I wanted to be sick. "It was nice of you to come." She looked different, yet the same. The flush of her cheeks hid a pallor that snaked under the rest of her skin. The nurses said she’s been through a lot. The nurses said what took you so long. The nurses said let her sleep. "I thought of you," she said with half a smile. "All the time." I moved closer, and stroked her hair out of her eyes. It was sodden with sweat. I’d say I couldn’t imagine what she’d been through, but in some ways I could. She looked just like any one of the guys in the back after a tough match. She probably looked exactly like I had when I’d made my bleary way to the showers after Andy Murray had given me the Hex Breaker only the day before. In this moment, I felt like I understood her. But I still couldn’t think of anything to say. "I’ve missed you," she breathed, quieter now that I was close. Her eyes were wet. With the drugs, she could have been saying anything. I took her hand, and she squeezed my fingers between hers. There was this glimmer in her eyes, a glimmer I’d remember for years to come. It was the glimmer of hope. "I love you," she whispered, folding the countless emotional peaks and troughs of the last 24 hours into her words. I closed my eyes. I kissed her on the forehead. "You should rest," I said, and turned, letting her hand fall. My lips tasted of salt and someone else’s baby. I heard her say my name. I didn’t look back. * * * The father is called Owen Compliments, and that is his real name, and the reason for that is not so important right now. How he knows Tim, and when he met Kate, and why they ended up in bed together that fateful night – candle flames burning up her dreams around them -- that, too, can wait. For now, just know that he’s tall, and tanned, and blond, and that none of these details matter to Margery Shipley, who’s staring at him with a fearfully palpable hatred. The room has been silent for some time. Clemence sits, in his chair, listening to the clock beating out the seconds. Gin and tonic sit, enmeshed in one another, untouched. Maggie’s face is burning. Owen swallows into a dry throat. He studies the carpet. It’s blue. Maggie purses her lips. She doesn’t believe Owen Compliments worthy of a cup of tea in her home. "Well, speak, boy," she growls. He takes a second or two to react, before raising his head with an ungainly jerk. "I thought I’d better check in with you guys. Uh, before heading to the... hospital." Clemence breathes out with some force, and Maggie turns to look at him, unused to her husband making any such interjection in social matters. It is a testament to the delicacy of the situation that Clemence is deigning to listen. However, not wishing to misrepresent how far he will contribute to the ensuing discussion, Clemence scratches at the side of his cheek, and turns his gaze to the pigeon perched at the edge of the gutter. "I mean," Owen continues, unable to meet Maggie’s eyes, "if that’s alright with you." "Why," she says softly, having completed the course of heavy breathing that she feels will enable her to speak rationally, "should it be any concern of ours?" "Uh." He chances a glance at Clemence, who offers no way out of the question, keenly observing as the bird pecks hopefully at a dead leaf. The relentless swinging of the young man’s arms does him no favours. "I just." "Of course, Clemence and I both were disappointed," Maggie continues obliquely, straightening several picture frames so as not to provoke herself by meeting Owen’s eyes, "very disappointed to hear the truth from Kate. We were, weren’t we, Clemence, darling?" Her piercing eyes ensure that Clemence has little choice but to nod gravely. Owen closes his eyes, welcoming the blackness they bring. He has done nothing to deserve this. But he made a promise, and he will see it out. He will let Tim’s parents continue to believe the sanitised account that they heard. He will let them believe him a scoundrel, a liar, a waster, and all manner of other words he has heard himself called in this neighbourhood. He will let them believe him the sort of man who would so completely, so calculatedly betray a friend’s trust. He isn’t doing it for Tim, though all too raw are the consequences for his old friend if the real truth should come out. No, he is doing it for her. For Kate, who -- though all tangible evidence points to the contrary -- he now believes he loves. And now he is doing it for the child. His child. And what’s worst of all is that in the hours he spent deliberating over whether he would be welcome in the maternity ward -- in the hours spent pacing his bedroom with only his thoughts for company, before coming here in some kind of self-indulgent quest for absolution -- he has yet to discover whether the child, who he is so desperate to be a real father to, is a boy or a girl. "I know, ma’am," Owen says, as sincerely as he can find the heart for. "I don’t ask for your forgiveness, nor for your blessing. I know I can never ask for your friendship--" "Then what are you asking for?" Maggie interrupts fiercely. "Because I’ve a good mind to give you a clip on the ear and send you packing!" Owen smiles grimly. "I guess I -- I don’t know what I’m asking you for, ma’am. Because I don’t... I don’t know what to expect when I get there. Or what happens next." He runs a rueful hand through his fluffy blond hair. "I don’t know how to bring up a child, or to be a husband either, if it comes to that. I don’t know anything. But I want to be there. I do. I guess what I’m asking for is... is you to wish me good luck. If it’s not too much trouble for you, ma’am." Her eyes are wide; a vein pulses angrily in her temple. She’s speechless. But before she can find the words to tell Owen Compliments exactly what she thinks of this latest dramatic performance, a cough from her husband diverts both their attention. "Go, son," Clemence says gruffly. "Go, and the best of luck to you." Owen crosses the room, and squats down where Clemence sits in his chair. The pigeon has flown away. "Thank you, sir. I appreciate that." "I’m not forgiving what you did, son," the old man says, measuring out each of his words. "Nobody ever will, not until you yourself can. But every little boy in this world needs his father. And you can make up for what you did -- slowly. Be that father to your son." Maggie coughs. She can’t help feel betrayed by her husband’s words, not least because it’s the longest speech he’s made in months. "We don’t know if it’s a boy or a girl," she interrupts acidly. Owen turns to her, and there’s a broad smile on his face. He’s unrecognisable from the tortured soul that came in. He’s seen the goodness in his future. "It’s a boy, ma’am," he calls, already halfway out the door. "Just call it a gut feeling!" The front door slams shut, but even the breaking of the spell cannot take the look of deep contentment off Clemence Shipley’s face. "You realise what that boy did to our son?" Maggie sits alongside Clemence, repatriating his untouched gin and tonic. Her husband nods slowly. "Yes. I do. But what matters now..." He reaches out his hand, arthritic fingers closing episodically around hers. "What matters now, my darling, is what that boy does for his son." She cooks his favourite meal that evening. Though she may curse the useless husk that sits in his study all day, pretending to read his books -- though she may resent washing his clothes, and helping him dress in the mornings and undress at night, and ignoring the evidence of mental deterioration that sits so starkly before her -- never again will she forget that the man she loves is inside.
