Terry

Date: 04/15/11 I. Ev'ry sinner looks for something That will put his heart at ease There is only one true answer He must get down on his knees - "Crying at the Chapel" by Elvis Presley "Ain’t this a kick..." Terry Frost hovered over his sister’s headstone in that San Fransiscan graveyard for longer than he had ever hoped. Clutching a bottle of Jameson’s and a somber disposition, this type of thing wasn’t really Terry’s game. Romancin’ a bevy of inebriated broads just turned twenty-one and don’t know a Sex on the Beach from some sex on the beach? Why, that was typical Saturday evenin’ fare. But this weren’t loose women and adult situations. For here, staring down the gravesite of his sister not less than a week after the fact - that same sister who once told him that she’d rather see him in hell than ever speak to him again - it mighta been straight beyond the pale. Even after he stood for a spell, and rubbed his hands over the engraving of her memory, fingers tip-toeing in and out of every crevice and curve. Try as he might, words just would not come. And so it was him, a bottle of whiskey, and the remains of the sibling that used to squeal in delight as a young’un when he’d call out her name, Teresa Frost. II. You can't leave 'cause your heart is there Sure you can't stay 'cause you been somewhere else You can't cry 'cause you'll look broke down But you're cryin' anyway 'cause you're all broke down - "Family Affair" by Sly and the Family Stone Callin’ the Frost clan nomads would surely be one helluva’n affront to nomads. Descended from French peasant stock that survived the plague no wiser, they were more of the manner of gypsies. The original family name itself was nothing short of a bastardization of the norm - LaFluer - that went in the trash when at the end of the 19th century young Ian LaFluer made the unusually bright move of a straight break for the new country, and took up the last name Frost as he was cattle-herded through Ellis Island. Ian worked himself to the bone in Hell’s kitchen, picking up any odd job he might find along the way, were it shovellin’ coal or some form or another of backbreakin’ manual labor. Though he barely spoke a word of English, he made along fine, doin’ better than anyone else of his bloodline had ever done before. But poor ol’ Ian saw that the old genetic habits die hard. Ian liked the drink, and he damn sure liked the women. Didn’t matter what kind or who were, it was all the same to him. And before long, the boy found hisself shackled to a particularly obese woman whose only job qualifications amounted to berating him something fierce and spittin’ out kids that may or may not have really been of Ian’s own. And that’s how things went ‘long the Frost folk for awhile. The wrong kinda people tangled up with the wrong kinda people. Hell, if you looked at the family’s crest, it might as well’ve been two downtrodden Frost men carryin’ on somethin’ somber, with two Frost matriarchs lookin’ down upon them with shame and judgment. And as the years passed, their genetic code went about spreading ‘cross these United States. Surely not the type of thing John L. O’Sullivan had in mind. Just like name-brand coffee shops, they were everywhere. Cleveland. Biloxi. Cheyenne. East and west, north and south, and all points in between. None of ‘em really ever provided anything important to society other than addin’ another household for the census bureau to visit. At least not ‘til Marlon Frost found himself inevitably tethered to Alice Bates, a homely woman if there ever were one. Together, they produced six children, three of each sex, including twins with the girls. Todd was the oldest boy, a string bean of a fellow, and Josh the youngest, who found more solace in a joint than any other. Karen and Sam were the twin girls, and treated like royalty by mom. Hell, mom treated all the kids like they walked on water. Except the two that mattered, that is. Teresa Frost was pretty as a picture and smart to boot. Girl had ambitions, too. Lofty ambitions. In middle school, she obsessed over acting. She auditioned for every play under the sun. Got the lead in all of ‘em, too. But take a peek out into that sea of people in the audience as she’s beltin’ out lines from "Annie", and she’d only recognize one face of a familial kind. But that’s how it went. It killed her that her mom took no interest in her day-to-daily. When they were livin’ off the beaten path in the Missouri flatlands, she asked Alice if they could go all the way to Kansas City so she could take part in a beauty contest. Alice just scoffed and said, "you should be looking for a man, that’s what you should be focusing on." Teresa was ten. And when Teresa argued otherwise, all she got in return was "never too late to start. You’re not going to be desirable for long." Never did surprise anyone when she turned sixteen and three days later was on a bus to New York City. But she weren’t the only one plottin’ their escape. And she sure wasn’t the only black sheep. Hell, she weren’t even the worst of the bunch, for that infamous distinction laid at the feet of one Terrence Marcus Frost. III. Never know what I came for Seems that I've forgotten Never ask what I came for Or how I was begotten. - "Poor Boy" by Nick Drake You better believe the man was a bastard through and through, though ‘bastard’ may not be harsh enough a word to describe such a man. He was a schemin’ moral-less sonuvabitch that’d sell you a dollar for a dollar-twenty-five and leave you feelin’ like you made out the bandit. The type that’d lie straight to your face, smilin’ all the while. A man that’d smoke, drink, fuck and gamble, though the order of importance varied dependin’ on the time of day and his general frame of mind. Terry knew well enough to shy away from the harder stuff - in his first and last toe-dip into the crystal meth game let him bear witness to a basehead pal jump in front of a moving train to prove his bones were made of steel - but he had a drug all the same, one that took hold every time. For a cold beer tastes good, a Chesterfield calms the nerves, and you always double down on eleven, but there ain’t nothin’ else in the way of chasin’ skirt. And Terry excelled. You see, to women, he was a golden God, the type that could do no wrong even when all he did was just that. Even with the receding hairline and the anything-but-toned body, the boy could talk a sweet enough game to have the gals swoonin’ and hangin’ on every-which word. And did they ever, at least ‘til they found him in bed with their sister and their best friend. To the gamblin’ community, though, he was little more than poison in the well water. But we’ll get to that a little bit later. Now, Terry didn’t grow up in the best of circumstances. Marlon was a contractor, and that meant movin’ along every year or so to a new spot. And though he was second-oldest, you’d never know it the way he took care of the others. Seein’ how dad only worked, mom only gave a damn about the twins and Todd was as useless as tits on a bull, it was Terry left to pick up the slack. Keepin’ everyone clean and presentable? That was him. Gettin’ the kids to school on time? Him, too. Cooking? That was Todd, even if his palette never extended past "white" and "trash". But the rest? That was Terry. Of course, just ‘cause Terry had done right by his family early on don’t mean he did wrong everywhere else the rest of the time. Terry had his own special reserved seat for detention and Saturday school, at least when he bothered showin’ up to either. In the hierarchy of the education system, he didn’t need to be the playground bully. He was a chunky Marlon Brando, twirlin’ his mustache and having respect paid. Even when they moved from place to place, it didn’t take long before he was patrollin’ the halls and just waiting for his tributes. But the respect and fear of his peers meant nothin’ to the authoritative figures who bounced him out of schools like nobody’s business. He musta been suspended countless times, though all that amounted to was an extended vacation. He even got expelled from high school in the family’s brief layover in Kentucky, but Terry kinda forced that one himself. Why else would he go about bangin’ the principal’s daughter, in the man’s own bed, while the poor fool was downstairs watchin’ TV? And with each punishment, Terry helped ‘round the house less and less, findin’ more solace in the dark holes where his vices laid. Finally, when Terry turned 18, his mother told him to get outta the house and never come back. He didn’t understand it at the time. "Who was this woman, thinkin’ she could tell him what’s what?" But it didn’t matter much. It went down much like you’d expect: a scuffle, punches thrown, Terry breakin’ Todd’s nose, dad breakin’ Terry’s arm, and three cops draggin’ him off and tossin’ him into a holding cell was all it took for Terry to realize that Alice still held the sway and that he never meant shit to her. To any of ‘em. Only Teresa paid a visit to make sure he was okay. And when he saw her, all he could do was flash a broken, bloody smile and tell her to run like hell the first chance she got. Out on his own for the first time in his life, Terry found the real world to be nothin’ short of his playground dealings. Only this playground was endless, and the games folks played were for keeps. And, boy, did he love it all. The card games under a cloud of cigar smoke. Fast cars, faster women, breakneck speed twenty-four-seven. The man knew no other way to go. He shacked up with folks he could find along the way. A stripper in Portland. A degenerate gambler in Madison. His uncle Charlie in 29 Palms, who fought at Khe Sanh and whose hand shook so bad he couldn’t sign his own name anymore and spent his days cursin’ Agent Orange and the Dodgers. And he’d lie and steal ‘til he wore out his welcome and they tired of his games and send his ass to the streets, just so he could go searchin’ for another bridge to burn. But even with all his deceit and his audacity, one bridge he tried not to burn was the link to the nephew he adored so just for bein’ as fun as he was. So it only made it that much worse when he’d do just that. IV. I'm goin' way down south, Way down to Mexico way Alright! I'm goin' way down south, Way down where I can be free Ain't no one gonna find me - "Hey Joe" by Jimi Hendrix "What is wrong with you?!?" Teresa was all grown up by then. It was the first time in a long time they spoke face-to-face. But standing in her kitchen, in her house, and bein’ yelled at about what Terry did to her boy, it was somethin’ else. Of course, Terry didn’t see what he did as wrong. I mean, what’s the harm in bringing a twelve-year-old boy to Tijuana and showin’ him the time of his life? "It’s not that big a deal," Terry reasoned. "I mean, he’s safe now, right?" But that wasn’t the point, a thing he so often struggled to get. She shook her head with disbelief. "He could have got hurt, or kidnapped, or…or…" Terry laughed, as he did in these situations. "Kidnapped? I didn’t take the damn kid to Islamabad. It’s just Mexico, for chrissake." Her head shot straight up into the air, her eyes lightin’ up like firecrackers. "You didn’t tell anyone you were taking him!" Terry shrugged. "Spur of the moment thing." Terry approached her slowly. "Just think of it like I took him out for ice cream. But for three days and in a whole other country." And when he got close enough to put his hand on her shoulder to reassure her that everything was okay, just like it used to be, she swatted his hand away and brushed past him over to a waiting bottle of red and a glass. She poured a small bit, and chugged it down like water. And Terry didn’t utter a single word ‘til she turned to face him and offered her own. "You haven’t grown up in all these years." Terry smirked. "More things change…" She rubbed away at the bridge of her nose like it housed some unitchable twitch. When that itch finally seemed to be scratched, she lowered her hand. "What did you let him do? Drink? Bang some whore?" And all of a sudden, Terry got defensive, throwin’ up his hands and providin’ the whole show. "Whoa! I didn’t let him drink anything or bang anyone!" Her sentences became a series of snarls. "Then what?" And that’s where Terry got sloppy. Rubbin’ the back of his neck, hemmin’ and hawin’. If this were any other broad, he’d have her eating out of the palm of his hand. But Teresa was his chink in the armor. "You know, just…uhh…" He scratched his neck furiously. "Some food. The beach." And then his voice got real soft. "A show, maybe." "No," she barked out. "You did not." Terry got coy. "It just happened, Teresa. It’s not a big deal--" "It is!" Her finger extended outward, pointing, wagging. "I lost one man to that kind of life. I will not lose another to that kind of life." And so Terry did what he was wont to do, not realizin’ he was a damnfool for doing so, and muttered under his breath, "then why’d you not keep the guy’s last name?" The room went dead. Teresa’s blood boiled and her eyes filled with rage. Terry’s veins pumped ice water when he saw her eyes. That look. That damn look, the way she was lookin’ at him right now, it was like nails scrapin’ on his soul. It was the kinda moment that told you all you needed to know, that nothin’ you ever say or do will ever bring things back to the ten seconds before you opened your mouth and went and said the stupidest thing you could say. He would only see that look twice in his life. And Terry opened his mouth, and tried to say somethin’. An "I’m sorry," maybe even an "I didn’t mean it." But he didn’t. He couldn’t. Neither of ‘em said anything for a spell. But they both knew that was that. Teresa calmed down, her body no longer tense, and she poured herself another glass of wine and took a sip. She reached into her purse, and pulled out a checkbook. Terry watched in stunned silence as she tore out a check and handed it to him. $30,000. He would have said something, anything, but again, he didn’t know the right thing to say, if there were even such a thing. And in that moment, he didn’t know what to feel. Shock? Happiness? Dismay? But all Teresa did was part her crimson-stained lips to tell Terry that it would be best if he never came ‘round again. And first time in a long time became the last time ever. Terry pocketed the check and took his leave, but there waitin’ for him in the hall outside the kitchen was the young Triumph, and though he was eavesdropping he was no less confused. He spoke as innocuously as ever. "Don’t go. Why do you have to go?" Terry turned and saw Teresa, and she coulda easily stepped in, told Triumph to go to his room or pay it no mind. But she didn’t, and Terry was always kinda thankful of that. And as he crouched down to speak to Triumph, he flashed that winnin’ smile. "I was only in town for a few days," he said. But Triumph still didn’t get it. "Did I do something?" Terry wasn’t good around kids, that was certain. But around people that cared about him, child or adult, he was often out of his element. "Nah, buddy. I just have to go take care of some stuff." Triumph fired back immediately. "When you coming back?" And for a bit, Terry didn’t respond. He turned to see Teresa, who just took a sip of wine, and he just rubbed Triumph’s head. "I’ll see you soon. Promise." It was the one time Terry truly hated to lie. V. Silent life of crime A man of odd circumstance A victim of ghetto demands Feed me money for style And I’ll let you trip for awhile - "Pusher Man" by Curtis Mayfield Terry used that $30,000 and set up shop in the Sunshine state, and within two months he was fifteen-large in the hole to a local loan shark that wasn’t big on untimely payment. When the large men with guns would come ‘round to find Terry, his girl-of-the-hour would divert attention while he’d climb out the bathroom window and hole up in some Lauderdale dive bar. But the thing about runnin’ away from problems, you end up running right into ‘em. And Alabaster Crayfish was not the type of problem that was worth findin’. Alabaster Crayfish earned his reputation breaking kneecaps for a portly bijou bigshot gangster that he ended up replacin’ via a .22 shell to the forehead. But though he was feared throughout the swamplands, it weren’t without a helluva climb. You see, Alabaster was born Simon Fredrick Crayfish, one of eight children, but the only albino of the bunch. Now, being an albino ain’t exactly uncommon. Being a mulatto albino, well…that took some gettin’ used to. Life was anything but spectacular. Daily schoolyard beatings. Nightly fatherly beatings. An older brother that molested him. A mother that told him she didn’t love him. Three dead siblings within two months of his fourteenth birthday, one to a drug overdose, two to gang violence. On the streets a month after that. But through the bullshit, Alabaster had a calling, and his came in car theft. Around a Mercedes-Benz or a Lexus, the man was Vermeer, and the type of folks that take an interest in that kinda exceptional skill would come calling. But for Alabaster, it wasn’t the money or the infamy or the girls. It was the fear. The fear that came when he had that gun in his hand and he pointed it right between the eyes of that bastard, his mentor, that laughed at his condition, the one that called him "God’s biggest muthafuckin’ mistake." And when he squeezed that trigger and blew the top off of that fat swampland-bred sonuvabitch and began his ascent into notoriety, no one would laugh at him again. So it wouldn’t come to the shock of any sensible human being that Terry would end up under the thumb of such a man. Terry had run-ins with similar folk, and always managed to be one or two steps ahead. And as these situations often do, the whole mess slowly spiraled out of control as each day passed. When Terry came home to found his crash pad busted up and turned inside-out, he shacked up with a well-to-do teenage coke addict. When he found her beat to shit, he got her to front him some cash and holed up in a swanky hotel on Miami Beach. When he found himself dangling out over the balcony of his hotel room, Alabaster holding a gun to his temple and told him he now wanted fifty-k, he blurted out he’d get the money and that he needed a few days. And when Alabaster told him he had twenty-four hours, he agreed. And after they left, Terry packed his bag, took the elevator to the lobby, hailed a cab, and then took the first flight out of Miami International. VI. But that's what makes the world go `round The up and down, the carousel Changing people, they'll go around Go underground, young man - "People Make The World Go ‘Round" by the Stylistics For maybe only the second time in his entire life, Terry hadn’t the slightest inklin’ of the proper thing to say. Seein’ his nephew drunk and despondent in that dank alleyway, tears streamin’ down his face, knowin’ full well their sister and mother was hours away from takin’ her last breath… It hurt him like nothin’ else. More than Alice’s poor parenting, more than Alabaster’s thugs. It was the knife in his gut that just kept twistin’ and cuttin’. So it only provided him that slight moment of solace when Triumph asked to take him home because it was the one thing he knew for sure he could do for the kid without fail. And when they arrived, Terry hugged him. "Stay out of trouble, yeah?" He pulled Triumph away from him and flashed that winnin’ smile one last time. "You don’t wanna end up like me." And Triumph chuckled, and he sent him on his way. In that taxi ride back, though, Terry’s insides churned. Teresa wasn’t why he came here. He’d been sitting in a hotel room in the Haight for three days, drinkin’ and watchin’ cable TV, buildin’ up the courage to chow down the biggest servin’ of shit pie he’d ever eat. Three days of burnin’ daylight. Hell, he didn’t know ‘til Todd of all people called him and broke the news. So he’d put on his brave face and go through the motions, and maybe find that moment when to ask. But when he sat with Triumph in Teresa’s hospital room, after Alice’s venomous barrage, he knew that moment’d never come and present itself. And on that drive, he wondered to himself. If that night had been about making Triumph take his mind off the whole thing, or making himself forget about what he had fully been prepared to do. If it ever would have been worth it. If he could have ever lived with himself afterwards. If the answer to both of those things would really have been ‘yes.’ Terry stayed in town for a few days after that. He went and bought himself a suit, got a shave and a haircut. Prepared himself to see off the one flesh and blood person he cared so much for, and console the nephew he admired so. But he just stood outside that church. He couldn’t go inside. How could he? What kinda person would he be to do so? And when they lowered Teresa into the ground, he merely watched from afar. VII. I've been first and last Look at how the time goes past. But I'm all alone at last. Rolling home to you. - "Old Man" by Neil Young Terry stammered and stuttered ‘til he finally got the courage to say the things he couldn’t bring himself to say to Teresa’s face. "I’m sorry," he started, breath labored. "I’m sorry for a lotta things. I’m sorry for mom. I’m sorry for not being there for you. I’m sorry for Triumph." He shook his head and laughed. "Shit, I’m just plain sorry." As the gentle wind rustled some nearby leaves, Terry gazed down at that bottle of Jameson’s, and popped off the top. He kicked it up, and took a long sip. And after he forced it down his gullet, he wiped his mouth with the sleeve of his sweater. "You raised one helluva kid," he admitted. An admission that brought one helluva smile upon his face, and he poured her a drink, bottle tilted at a slight enough angle, but an angle enough for her to partake in the party. When he felt she had enough, he took one more for himself. And so he continued on for a right amount of time. He covered every subject he could think of. Seeing her on stage, illuminatin’ the crowd. The first time they both came back to visit the folks, five years after leaving, and Teresa called mom a "cunt" and Terry got into a fist-fight with Todd. About how they talked about hearin’ dad left mom and how the first thought they both had was "about damn time." He brought up Mexico, though it pained him like no other. And he even apologized about why he had really come into town, even if he danced ‘round the subject like no other for no reason other than to just plain not havin’ to admit to the damn thing. And when a good hour had passed and he said all he could think of sayin’, he poured the remnants of the bottle for that one final drink, he made the "sign of the cross" even though he weren’t religious in the least, and he said his final goodbye and turned and walked away. Just like that, he was gone once again. No one rightly knows what became of Terry Frost after that. Some say he scuffled back to Mexico, his adopted home-away-from-home. Some believe that he gave up his old downtrodden ways and blended in with some crowd somewhere out in the unknowns. There are even some that say that he foolishly hustled back to Florida for one last caper, one that left a man of his description face down in the swamps, though that one’s hard to peg, seein’ as how the gators didn’t leave much to say otherwise. Ask his mom and her kind, they’d tell you they’d hope he was dead. Ask Alabaster, he’d take all the credit for the most nefarious of deeds. Ask Triumph, he’d greet the question with a shrug. Anyone else might offer you a hundred different answers. But when you stop and really think, think about the man he was, think about the man that would never come to be… Maybe it’s best not to think about it at all.
