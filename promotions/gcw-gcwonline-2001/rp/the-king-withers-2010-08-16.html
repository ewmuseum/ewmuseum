The King Withers

16 August 2010
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 08/16/10 Dense fog hung low under the morning sky making Houston's Park Plaza Hospital's tawny brickwork even less inspiiring than usual. It was a lopsided building of gentle curves and cylindrical towers built on either side of it's Ewing Street thoroughfare; a building lofty enough to dominate the surrounding skyline, but with a hue earthy enough to blend seamlessly with it's green surroundings. Sidney Murray left his rental car in the lot across the street and headed for the nearest entrance. He'd never understood why they built these buildings to look so depressing. Surely, he figured, they should make them as bright and cheery as possible, given the amount of bad news strewn within. He finally found the main entrance after ten minutes of wandering around the block with no idea of where he was going. The hallway's clean white walls and light ash panelling belied the building's drab exterior, and Sid soon felt his head clearing with the first two lungfuls of sanitised hospital air. The waiting area was empty bar a handful of scattered visitors, and Sid stepped quickly passed it to a seemingly abandoned reception desk. Calloused guitarist's fingertips tapped gently on the desk's top. There was no “attention” bell, so the best Sid could do to attract the attention of the staff in the office beyond was to loudly clear his phlegm-lined throat. A lady came through in her pastel blue uniform – Hispanic, mid-thirties, not wholly unattractive – and smiled. “Sorry for your wait, sir,” she said, sitting down at the computer and quickly punching-in a password. Sid chose not to say he'd only been waiting for a few seconds. “I'm here to see my brother,” he said, leaning against the elbow-height desk. “Andy Murray. Black hair, blue eyes, about yay high.” Sid raised a hand above his head, exaggerating his brother's great height. The receptionist nodded her long head as she typed the name. It wasn't until “Andy Murray” was up on her screen that she realised who Sid was talking about. “The wrestler guy?” she asked like she knew something Sid didn't. “Yeah. Collapsed the other day and signed himself in after the GCW show. Think he was supposed to have a scan or somethin' this morning.” “He's not here.” Sid's brow tautened. He stood straight, pushing his weight away from the desk. “Huh?” “He, uhhhh.” The receptionist paused, her frustration manifesting with a sigh. “The doctors went through before his MRI scan and he wasn't there. He left just enough money to cover his bills behind, but that was all. All his stuff was gone.” “So he just disappeared?” Sid asked, struggling to find any sense in her words. It was Andy's idea to sign himself into the hospital in the first place; why on Earth wouldn't he stick around for the scan. His brother's career meant he'd spent enough time in America's hospitals to become sick of them twice over, but this was just a scan. A simple, painless procedure that would be done within an hour. What did he have to run from? The receptionist nodded. “Yessir. A few of the doctors searched the ward thoroughly but couldn't find him anywhere.” “And you have no idea where he's gone?” Obviously, she wanted to say. “No, sir. I'm sorry,” she said instead, her face forming a well-rehearsed mask of sympathy. “Huh.” Sid suddenly became conscious of his gormless expression. He walked away from the desk without saying “thank you,” and stepped back out into the balmy Texan day. Where the hell did you go, Andy? * * * * * “Really ain't one fer talkin', aye ya?”. Ronnie Sobchek's face was just a few inches from the cell's grimy mirror. Tiny greying bristles floated to the sink below as he trimmed his thick moustache with a pair of scissors one of his sons had smuggled in for him a few months ago. “I know this is prob'ly yer first time in th' big house an' all, but ya ain't made a peep since ya got here.” John may very well have “made a peep” if he had any interest whatsoever in communicating with the burly fifty-something, but he didn't. Instead he lay silently on the bottom bunk, staring at the bare breeze-block wall, praying that his cellmate would eventually realise he was talking to himself and decide that boring John to death wasn't as fulfilling an activity as he first thought. Hood had only been there twenty-four hours, but already he'd learned everything he could ever want to know about Ronnie. He'd heard all about Sobchek's three failed marriages and his two illegitimate children, about how he'd given up on High School aged twelve and not spent a day worrying about education since, and about how he'd lived in his Ford Taurus since the failing of his auto parts business in 2007. John knew more about Ronnie than he knew about some of his employees. Place of birth, family members' names, favourite grocery brands, shoe size, waistline, inside leg. Everything. And that, it seemed, was just the tip of the iceberg. Ronnie's mouth had been motoring since the break of dawn, and he wasn't showing any signs of shutting up just yet. “Well, from me t'you, you might wanna think 'bout livenin' up real soon,” Ronnie said as he ran the scissors under cold water. “I dunno how long yer stuck here for but folks aroun' here'll notice that shit straight away. Them folks is like vultures, man. They'll see yer weakness and pick ya apart like yer a dyin' animal.” He slid the scissors into the pocket of his orange prison fatigues and splashed a cupped handful of water across his weathered features. “Just 'cause this ain't high security don't mean there ain't some bad dudes 'round here.” Having turned the tap off, Ronnie waddled towards the bed and slung his bulky frame onto the top bunk. Shockwaves rippled down the steel frame as Ronnie made himself comfortable. A deep, rumbling burst of wind suddenly escaped his buttocks and soon the noxious pong of Ronnie Sobchek's digestive tract filled the tiny cell. “Phee-eww!” Ronnie exclaimed jovially. “Excuuuuse me.” Clenching his teeth and closing his eyes, John Hood had found himself in the last place he wanted to be. Two days had elapsed since his arrest and his bail hadn't even been set. Hopefully they'd drag him to court before the end of the week and he'd be free of Ronnie's failing bodily functions and excruciating banter, at least for a day. Prison had been quite the culture shock for a man subjected to a lifetime of laid-back suburban living, and John estimated that it'd be some time yet before he'd even muster the willpower to venture beyond his cell. The prisoners were free to roam the communal areas between lunch and dinner every day, but John hadn't even stepped-out for his meal breaks. He was confined, a prisoner in the truest sense of the word, and Ronnie's incessant nuisance was only making him sink further inside his shell. “Ya like books, buddy?” Ronnie asked. John could hear him thumbing through pages of the dog-eared paperback he'd taken from the library cart earlier that morning. “Always figured books were for th' boffins b'fore I came 'ere, but they alright. This Dan Brown guy's ont' somethin', man. Clever shit.” And then came salvation. Footsteps came along the walkway just as John had resigned himself to enduring another tumbleweed-inducing diatribe from his pest of a cohort. They stopped. “John Hood?” asked a shrill, nasally voice. John rolled onto his back and squinted at the young guard through a curtain of dishevelled black hair. “Yeah,” he croaked. “C'mon, there's somebody here to see you,” the guard explained, motioning for John to get up. “Who is it?” “Your wife.” And that was all the encouragement John Hood needed. He stood on wobbly legs and strode towards the security guard. Ronnie watched the two of them disappear down the walkway. “John,” he mumbled. “So that's what'cher name is.” * * * * * It had been a while since Floyd Holland had felt so peaceful, and Hermann Park's leafy surroundings were the perfect conduit for his serenity. Two months of internal strife had finally ended with John Hood's arrest and the lawyer could finally breathe again. Working with Hood was something he'd had doubts about from day one. John's reputation preceded itself and his fiery temperament endured himself to few, and Floyd found himself on the receiving end of Hood's “humour” just hours after their working relationship had begun. His plus-sized form and unsightly exterior meant that Floyd was always going to encounter the odd insult every now and then, but never in a million years did he think he'd experience such mockery from an employer. Nonetheless, John Hood's money was as good as anything Floyd had earned in twenty years, and the job was simple enough. He'd draw-up a contract, oversee its formation, witness the signing, and seal the deal. Quick turnover, no stress. Easy. Or so he thought. Rueben Dallas and his battle with cancer had complicated matters. John, frustrated by Dallas' inability to grip a pen and ink the deal, dragged Floyd to a hospital late one evening. It was only then that the true extent of Rueben's physical decline became apparent. Hooked-up to thousands of dollars worth of machines charged with keeping him alive, Dallas was more dead skin hanging from brittle bones than a human being. Floyd could only watch helplessly as John forced a pen between Rueben's fingers, tightened his fist, and forced the transaction. Figuring the money would help him overcome his moral objections, Floyd said nothing. He slinked back to his home without a single complaint and John Hood's final paycheque arrived in his mailbox the very next day. He'd seal his lips and forget all about what he'd seen in that hospital room, before moving on to the next job. If only things were so easy. Stricken by guilt and spurred by Hood's constant mockery, Floyd was edging slowly towards breaking point. Inspired by the words of an old mentor, Roy Lahenne, Floyd finally realised what he had to do. Months after first being introduced to John Hood, Floyd was reporting his crime to the police and wiping clean his moral slate. Guilt's terrible weight had been lifted from his shoulders, but Floyd Holland wasn't quite finished yet. He needed to ensure that Hood wouldn't get away with what he'd done. The man was in jail, sure, but if he was to stay there then a strong case would have to be made against him. The police would surely have examined the hospital's CCTV reels already, but there was little they'd be able to do without finding somebody to testify. The wife was out of the question. Stricken by grief and wanting nothing but to move-on with her life, Cherise had spurned Floyd's advances before the first sentence had even left his mouth. Floyd couldn't testify himself: the'd spent the past eight weeks working directly with John Hood, and that meant that his credibility in a court of law was severely diminished. And John Hood was not a poor man. With his relative wealth, John would surely bring the finest legal team money could by to court. Their case would be rock solid, so the case against them needed to be nothing less than watertight. There was only one man for the job. Floyd had already been waiting for half an hour when he finally turned-up, but the day was pleasant enough for him not to mind. He saw him approaching from fifty yards away; a Scottish giant, casting his gaze around his leafy surroundings, not sure who to look for. It took considerable effort and a hefty wheeze for Floyd to rise from the bench. He waddled awkwardly towards the GCW wrestler, meeting him in the clearing as two youngsters raced past on brightly-coloured mountain bikes. “Mr. Murray?” Floyd asked, squinting as beams of sunlight finally began to poke between the thick cloud. “Uh, yeah,” Andy said. Much to Floyd's surprise, Andy's first glance was not one of revulsion. “You the guy that called me?” “That's me,” Floyd said as he scanned Murray from head to toe. In-person, the grappler was even taller than he seemed on television, and a frame that often looked gaunt in-comparison with Shaman and Bryan Mayhem was considerably more imposing in the flesh. Floyd extended a hand and Murray shook it firmly. “Floyd Holland.” “I guess you already know who I am, given you were somehow able to get hold of my private phone number,” Andy croaked wearily. He'd always made a conscious effort to be as accommodating as possible to every new person he met, but the world hadn't been kind to him lately and maintaining a friendly demeanour was proving difficult. “Guilty,” Floyd said. He'd barely been standing for a minute but his troublesome body was already uncomfortable. He sat back down and Andy followed. “I can't spare too long so this had better be important,” Murray said matter-of-factly. “I'm not trying to be rude, I just don't usually do the whole 'meet a stranger in the park' thing. But you mentioned John, so...” Andy's words fizzled out and it was Floyd's turn to talk. He struggled to get the words out, having not expected to have had to play his cards so early. “Yeah, John Hood. I was his lawyer. I represented him. I understand he was your agent?” “Still is, I guess. If it's possible to manager a guy's career from behind bars.” Floyd forced laughter at what he perceived was an attempt at humour. “Can't say I like the guy,” Andy continued. “Can't really stand the prick, in fact. There's been a bit of bad blood between us for a long time. To be honest, I'd almost forgotten about it 'til he stole Rueben's agency, but he obviously hadn't.” “I know all about that,” Floyd said. “I'm the guy who drew the contract.” He noticed Andy's expression sour immediately – the mere implication that Floyd had had anything to do with John's crimes against Rueben was enough to draw disgust from the Scot. “I was there when it happened, Andy, I saw everything. That's why I reported him to the police.” Murray turned away from the blubbery lawyer and nodded his head. “Heh, how ironic. A man like John Hood gets double-crossed by his own lawyer.” “I couldn't live with it, sir. I just couldn't. I thought this was going to be just another job, but when I found out there was a dying man involved, and when I saw what John did...” Floyd found his voice failing him. It took a few seconds for it to return. “Well, I had to do something about it.” “I guess I owe you my gratitude,” Andy said, sighing. His eyes met Floyd's again. “I wouldn't have found out otherwise.” “It was the least I could do.” A silence fell between the two. Figuring that Floyd Holland was just a guilt-stricken man in need of closure, Andy rose to his feet, assuming they were done. “Well thank you, Floyd. I appreciate it.” “Andy, wait.” The words stopped Andy mid-turn. He looked back and Floyd was already rising. “There's something else.” Andy felt a buzzing from his pocket. “Sid,” his cell phone read. He rejected the call and looked back at Floyd. “Well spit it out.” “Uhhh, listen,” Floyd said, fumbling around in his jacket for something. “I want to offer you something.” Floyd's hand escaped the folds of his jacket and he handed Murray a business card. “Representation.” “Representation? For what?” “The case.” “What case?” Andy shook his head. “Stop talking in code, man.” “I want to build a case against John Hood,” Floyd said. “Put him away for a long time. But I need someone to testify against him, and you're my only option.” Andy took a moment to contemplate his options. The idea of John Hood rotting in jail was a pleasant one indeed, but he was a busy man with a lot on his plate. “I don't know, man. I've got a losing streak to overturn, a son to look after, and an agent to replace. I don't know if I can deal with the stress of a trial right now.” “At least promise me you'll think about it,” Floyd said, not wanting to sound too desperate. “My number's on the card.” Andy looked at the card in his palm and slowly nodded. He slid it into his pocket. “I'll think about it. No promises, though.” And with the slightest of nods Andy Murray was gone. Floyd watched him as he strolled through the trees, back to wherever he came from. There was nothing he wanted more than to see Hood put away for good, but it was in Andy's hands. All he could do was cross his fingers and hope that Murray's obvious disdain for Hood would force his hand. He didn't even want to think what might happen if John was released and found out it was Floyd who'd ratted him out. * * * * * Alison Hood had never been the most patient of women, and waiting for her disgraced husband in such a busy room was wearing thin. Tens of convicts gathered in the sweaty enclave, exaggerating expressions and stressing words so that their loved ones knew then they meant it when they said things were gonna be different when they got out. The air was thick with sweat and buzzing with the sound of thirty different conversations reverberating around the room's barren walls. Not the most soothing of environments, but fortunately John's enthusiasm ensured her wait was short. She watched him push through a crowd of guards to enter the room, his walk quickening to a jog when he laid eyes on her. Alison cleared her throat and straightened her blouse, then tightened her facial muscles to create a frosty façade. Though John was her husband, this was serious business. The distance between them had only grown since John's acquisition of the Dallas Group and now they were at the precipice. She needed to know that her husband was coping, but she needed the truth more. Short of breath, John dropped onto the chair and leaned forward with clasped hands. He looked terrible: the bags under his eyes told Alison that he hadn't been sleeping, and the layers of black hair John took great pride in perfectly manicuring hung straggly around his temples. There was a thin layer of stubble that he wouldn't have allowed outside the slammer, and his nose was running. Eww, she thought. “You didn't bring the kids?” was how John broke the silence. Alison's jaw slackened and she stopped her eyes mid-roll. “That's the first thing you say to me? Really, John?! No 'oh honey, I'm so sorry'? No 'I've missed you'? Not even a 'hi, how are you'?”. She closed her mouth. Sighed. “Real nice, John.” John closed his eyes and took one of her hands. “I'm sorry, Al. I didn't mean it like that.” “They're at school,” she said. “It wouldn't have been appropriate to drag them out of class just to sit in a room full of murderers and rapists.” “This isn't high-security. We got a couple of drug dealers but that's about the extent of it.” Her response was silent. She knew John's facility wasn't exactly Alcatraz: it was a deliberately incendiary statement, but her husband hadn't taken the bait. “How are they doing, hon?” John asked, stroking his thumb against her knuckles, “and how are you?” “They're having a little trouble understanding why their daddy's mugshot's on television every night,” she said, shaking her head. “Toby was up half the night crying his eyes out, and little John's barely said a word since he found out you were gone. It's been brilliant, John. Just brilliant.” The pressure grew heavier as John's wife seeped her sarcasm onto him. “I know it's bad, Al, and I'm sorry you guys have to go through this, but bail'll be set before the end of the week; I'll be back before you know it.” He looked into her eyes, his gaze never faltering. “You do know how to access the funds, right?” Alison breathed deeply, looking away from her husband for a couple of seconds. She was deliberately ducking the question, and John knew it. “John,” she began, looking back at him, “I need to know something.” A lump grew in his throat. “What, honey?” he asked, although he already knew the question. “Is it true?”. Her tone was cold, but her bubbling eyes were full of hope. “Did you do it, John?” John looked away from the first time, his bowed head staring at the scratched table that separated them. His top lip quivered. “Alison, I--” “Just tell me, John,” she said. “You couldn't have taken advantage of that poor man; that's not you. You didn't do it, John. Did you?” Still he couldn't look up. The answer was staring him in the face, but he wasn't ready to confront it. Alison's eyes were welling-up. Her husband's silence told her everything she needed to know. “I knew it,” she said. “I fucking knew it.” Her heavy chair scraped loudly on the concrete floor as she kicked it back. When John looked up she was already halfway across the room and he leapt up, barrelling after her. “Alison!” he yelled, drawing the attention of most in the room as she headed for the exit. “Alison, wait! I can explain!” But it was too late. Alison had vanished through a crowd of guards in navy blue uniforms; the same crowd that restrained him as he tried desperately to follow her from the room. And he struggled. He kicked, pushed, and pulled at the guards but their grip wouldn't slacken. Soon John's body went limp. He gave up. * * * * * Andy's car had just passed an elementary school when Sid's name appeared on his phone's screen for the second time that day. He took the call and sandwiched the phone between his shoulder and ear: he didn't have time to pull over and stop the car. “Hello,” he said, emotionless. “Andy, where the hell are you?” Sid demanded. “The hospital think you've vanished off the face of the Ea--” “I'm fine, Sid. I'm still in Houston.” “You're NOT fine though, Andy! You checked yourself into hospital; you know you're not fine! Now tell me where you are so I can come pick you up and take you back to the hospital.” Andy closed his eyes and rubbed his temples as he stopped the car at a quiet intersection. “I'm not going back there, man. I don't need to.” The lights quickly changed from red to green and he rounded a corner onto a tree-lined suburban road. He was far away from the hustle and bustle of downtown Houston, now. “So I got a little dizzy a couple of days ago, big deal.” “You fucking collapsed!” Sid had stumped him. The truth was that yes, Andy had collapsed, and he hadn't even remembered driving to Hood's office until his brother slapped him on the cheek and told him where he was about five minutes after falling to the ground. But he wasn't concerned – he couldn't be concerned. Rueben Dallas was dead, he'd lost his last two matches, and his Dangerous Games charge was disintegrating before his own eyes. He couldn't have something like this weighing on his mind. “I don't need the scan, Sid,” Andy said solemnly. “Would you accept help for once in your fucking life?! Alright, the chances are that it's nothing to worry about, but we can't rule out the possibility of it being something a little more severe unless you have the MRI.” “Exactly, brother: it's nothing.” Andy's voice remained calm and crisp. He didn't want to snap at his brother; Sid was only trying to help, but Andy wasn't going back to the hospital. “I'll talk to my doctor when I get back to Tallahassee, but chill, man. It was probably just a migraine.” “Andy, look,” Sid began, the frustration in his voice replaced by concern, “I talked to one of the doctors last night after I dropped you off. They think it might be something neurological. Something similar to what Mum had.” “Look, I gotta go,” Andy said. He'd been looking for a way to end the conversation and fate had handed him a Get Out Of Jail Free card. The car pulled towards the pavement and Andy killed the engine. “I'll talk to you later.” “Andy, c--” Beep and Sid was gone. Andy gripped the steering wheel tightly and steadied his breath. Sid was right; if he was thinking logically, Andy wouldn't have left the hospital without telling anybody. He'd have stuck around for his MRI scan and the eventual confirmation that there was in fact nothing wrong with him, but Andy was too bullheaded to listen to his brother's words. It was only in moments like this that he realised that. Andy stepped out of the car and looked at the two-storey family home before him. The brickwork was brown and the mailbox read “Dallas”. He'd arrived. * * * * * Ronnie sat-up in his bunk, his interest perked by the sound of footsteps outside the cell. Eventually his cellmate blustered back into the tiny room with a face like death. “Bad news, then?” Ronnie asked, brows raised. John paid him no heed, though. Instead, under the frowning gaze of the guards who'd accompanied him, John headed straight for his bunk and let his head sink into the pillow. The guards quickly vanished, knowing that the sports agent was no longer a threat. Ronnie just pushed his glasses up the bridge of his nose and returned to his novel. “Suit yourself,” he said. Five minutes later, Ronnie thought he heard sobbing from the bunk below. He thought better of interfering. * * * * * Andy knocked a heavy hand against the door’s hard wood and waited. And waited. And waited. Ten seconds passed. Maybe I’ve made a mistake. Maybe I should go before she comes to do the door. Twenty, and the gentle pitter-patter of feet on the stairs. The first sign of life from within. He swallowed. Too late now. Thirty. A key in the lock and the door creeping open. Definitely too late. He inhaled sharply as Cherise Dallas appeared in the doorframe. Parted his lips to speak but nothing came out. Fortunately she saved him the trouble. “Andy,” she said softly, then cleared her throat. Their relationship had always been a pleasant one. In the past there had been little reason for them to communicate outside of public events, but they’d never had a reason not to get along. Not until a couple of days ago. “Hello.” “I, uhh. I think we should talk.” Andy’s voice was stronger and more composed than he thought it’d be. Many in his position saw apologising as a sign of weakness or a dent in their pride, but Andy had never had that problem. He watched Cherise fold her arms expectantly. “I'm sorry, Cherise. I was out of line; I shouldn't have barked at your like that, especially on the day of Rueben's funeral.” “You certainly didn't time it very well.” “No, I didn't time it at all. Something just clicked in my head and all of the sudden the only emotion I knew how to feel was anger.” He might have defended himself by pointing out that he was in a high-pressure situation at the time, but chose not to devalue his apology. “I should have phoned you a couple of days later and kept my cool. I'm sorry, I didn't mean to put another black cloud over an already grey day.” Cherise forced a smile though. “It's fine, Andy. Honestly. You've been so good to Rueben over the years; I can't just shut you out for seeing red, but thank you for the apology. It's appreciated.” She stood to the side. “Come on, I can't keep you standing outside.” Andy nodded and walked gingerly into Rueben's house. The surroundings were familiar with cream-painted walls and light laminate flooring, but a few new trinkets and pieces of furniture had appeared since his last visit. Cherise guided him through to the open plan living room and he sat down on the edge of one of the couches. He leaned forward, elbows resting on his calves. “So how are you holding up?” he asked. “All things considering, I think we're doing alright.” Cherise paused, her gaze drifting out the window. Her slight frame heaved a sigh. “It's hard, and if it ever stopped being hard then I'd start worrying about myself. I don't think I'll ever get used to this house being so empty, but we've got to survive. We've got to keep living. Knowing that that bastard's in jail makes it a little bit easier.” “I met the guy who ratted him, Cherise.” She turned her head suddenly. “Seemed genuine enough. Asked me to testify.” “The, ahem, larger gentleman?” “Holland, yeah.” Cherise shook her head. “It's not worth your time.” “But you just said you were glad Hood was in jail?” “I am. How could I not be? He deserves punishment for the way he took Rueben's company, but at the end of the day, Andy, I made the decision to sell to him.” She looked away again. “The method he chose was crooked, but an agreement was in-place. He doesn't need to rot in jail to suffer, though. The news is already spreading through town like wildfire. He won't make anything like the money he was making before this happened: nobody's going to touch him with a barge pole now. Why waste your money in court?” Andy was ponderous. Her strength was admirable and he was surprised by her ability to keep a clear head just days after her husband's funeral, but he couldn't grasp how she could be so philosophical about Hood's predicament. “I guess there are other ways he can pay his penance,” Andy said, swallowing back his true feelings. He wouldn't get away with upsetting her again. “Exactly,” she said. “Things will come back to bite him: they always do. I'm trying not to think about him, though. I don't want him tarnishing my memories.” “He's a piece of work, Cherise. Has been for years, and he ain't gonna change.” She nodded. “What happens to you, though? I mean, he's your agent and he's banged-up. There's no way he'll be able to keep hold of the agency if he gets out.” He was lost for words, Andy: the thought hadn't even crossed his mind. He shook his head. “I guess this is my way out. You've know idea how sick it makes me to know that this guy gets a cut of my paycheque, and some of the shit he's had me doing...” Cherise couldn't stifle the chuckle. “Last I heard you were running around town in a chicken costume.” A snort. “Yeah, exactly.” “Well I guess that's one less thing for you to worry about.” She moved around the room, sitting down on an armchair opposite the GCW wrestler. “You must have a lot on your mind just now.” “Not nearly as much as you, I bet.” Andy sensed where the conversation was going. He didn't like it. “I heard about what happened, Andy,” she said in a softer tone. “They said you just... collapsed.” “Who said that?” “The news: you've been on ESPN a couple of times today already. They said you ran out of the hospital this morning.” Momentarily Cherise's gaze turned to the floor, but Andy had done her a favour by coming to apologise and now it was her turn. She looked back up. “Is everything alright with you, Andy? Men of your age and physical condition aren't supposed to black-out like that.” Andy groaned. “I'm fine, really. Like you said, I have a lot on my mind. It was just a bad migraine: I don't need an MRI scan to tell me that.” “A migraine will floor you, Andy, but it won't knock you out. I really think you should go back to the hospital and get yourself checked out. Rueben said you were never one to accept help readily, but the last thing he would've have wanted was for something like this to happen.” He was wondering how long it would take for Cherise to play the Rueben guilt card and he stood-up almost immediately. There was no way he could earnestly convince this woman that he was fine. No way at all. “I really appreciate the concern, Cherise. Honestly. But I'm fine, and I'm not going to waste even more of my money by going back to that place.” He watched her rise from her own chair. “It's nothing a few pills won't sort out.” “But An--” “I gotta get going, Cherise,” he said, turning. “I've got a big match this week and I gotta prepare. I'll talk to you again soon.” “Wait, Andy.” But he words fell on no ears. Andy Murray was out the door before she could finish, and he paced his way down to his car like a man on a mission. A button press activated the central locking system, and within seconds he'd sat down, buckled-up, and turned the ignition. And then it came to him. Something in his head. Thud. Thud. Thud. A bass drum reverberating around his skull. Pressure in his sinuses and pain growing with every heartbeat. He'd felt it before. Through winces, Andy tried to shake away the pain as he put the car into gear and drove-off down the street. He was fine. There was nothing wrong with him. It was just a migraine.
