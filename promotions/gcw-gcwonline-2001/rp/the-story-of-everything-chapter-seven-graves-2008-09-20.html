The Story Of Everything, Chapter Seven: Graves

20 September 2008
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 9.20.08 The concoction of baking soda and hot sauce was nowhere near as debilitating, at least navigationally, as pure, unadulterated Undead Sauce. The feeling was similar, but the ability to occupy multiplanar points over multiple planes felt more mental than physical; ghostly more so than godly. We knew that there would be fireballs chasing us in no time, and we knew there would probably be some side effects we hadn't quite accounted for. Wayne suggested that we steal Mathais' 1935 Lanz Bulldog Model D9531 Road Tractor, and as the feeling of imminent danger dissipated, we had slowly gained the courage to make it back to the porch, where Matthais still motionlessly lied. "You think he's dead?" Wayne wondered aloud. "Keep your voice down," I hushed, urging Wayne to wonder aquiet. "What if his wife hears us and comes out here?" "I don't think he's married." "She prepared our dinner, remember?" "That's what he said, but did you actually see her?" "No," I scratched my head. "No, I didn't." "When you guys went outside, I searched around the house to make sure the coast was clear. I was craving the sauce, craving it real bad. I searched every room in the house for ingredients. That's how I found those cans!" Wayne laboriously spat. "He had a basement full of cans. There was a big pile of empties. I didn't think he'd mind. You were just shooting them anyway, right?" "Get a hold of yourself, Wayne!" I could see he was getting riled up. "Do you know what was in the cans?" "No idea," he responded. "But we can go check." "Lead the way," I commanded. "What about him?" Wayne pointed at the still body of Matthais at our feet. "You think he's dead?" "Give him a poke." "I don't want to do it. You do it," Wayne refused. His face contorted into a maze of disgusted wrinkles. I sighed and looked down at Matthais' body. He sure wasn't breathing. My gaze trailed off to where his shotgun had dropped, and I decided to trade up for some heavier artillery. "What are you doing?" Wayne asked, half fearing that I was gong to paint the old wooden porch with the farmer's brains, just to be safe. It wasn't a bad idea, and I couldn't say that I wasn't considering it. Under the influence of the hot sauce cooked with baking soda, I was more than capable of doing so without considering the repercussions. Morals were foreign to me. It was not as though I was ill willed; I just had no relative sense of behavioral norms and anomalies. I poked Matthais with the barrel of the shotgun, and he was unresponsive. It was a pretty safe diagnosis. "I think he's dead." "We can't just leave him here." "Why not? You were cooking hot sauce with baking soda, and he had a gun to your head. Any judge would see that as self-defense." "Why don't you leave the lawyer stuff to me, alright?" There went Doctor Wayne, attorney-at-law, fluffing his titles at me. "We were on his property. The judges wouldn't buy it; not out here." "Still, even if they find him, it's obvious that he overdosed on too much hot sauce and baking soda." "First, we should come up with a shorter name for that," suggested Wayne. "Hot sauce and baking soda just takes too damn long to say." I nodded in agreement. "And second, it might look like an overdose, but there are signs of foul play all around. You fired a gun in the house. Matthais let off a round of shotgun blasts, and there are no firearms around that you aren't covered in your finger-" he screeched to a halt. "My god. Our fingerprints are all over the house." "Since you're the lawyer, what's our best legal recourse?" "Let's drag the corpse inside the house and burn it to the ground," Wayne had a distantly grave look in his eyes. "Get his keys. We're going to have to tractor our asses out of here as fast as we can. Bring the guns." Wayne was already in motion, one foot out the door. "Can we check the cans first?" I held. "I want to see what's in them." "Fine," Wayne acquiesced -- and the sooner the better. It'd take longer to argue me out of it. "Let's make it quick." "And what about Hobo Sauce?" "I was actually thinking Red Zombie," Wayne passive-aggressively rejected. "Yeah," I admitted. "I kind of like that." Wayne led me around a gnarled twist of hallway and into what appeared to be a normal spandrel, however, I hadn't been inside many spandrels, so who was I to judge? After some cumbersome maneuvering (how silly it was to try and fit two men in such a dark, cramped spandrel) Wayne was able to find and pull the chain on the light fixture. There was a very indistinct latch on the ground, and Wayne had luckily, and likely literally, stumbled upon it. We both got near the floor and unfastened the hinge. The board flapped open, revealing another set of stairs leading to a hidden basement. We each felt along the wall for some kind of switch, our feet noisily clanking into the empty cans that lined the floors. Doctor Wayne found the switch first, and flipped the lights on. The basement was full of cans, full cans unmarked on shelves and empty, opened cans spilt throughout the aisles. "You think the old guy had enough cans?" smirked Doctor Wayne. I was too transfixed to respond. I walked to the shelf and picked off the nearest can, taking the handgun from my waistband and banging the handle into the top of the can. It started to dent and then to give, and I was pounding so hard my palms were scraped. I ripped off the jagged tin lid and threw it on the floor with a clang. "What's in it?" inquired the good doctor. The odor of the can's contents drifted into my nostrils, and it was strong; pungent at first with a very aromatically spicy afterthought. I peeled back the lid a little more and shifted to let more light into it. "I've never seen anything like this before." It was Jenkem, all right, but none like I'd ever seen. This shit had been tinkered with, expertly cultivated and even seasoned. It didn't even look all too unpleasant, colored with a warmer, smoother set of hues that reminded me of sunset. I felt a tag on the bottom of the can and lifted it high to see it. A tiny, round sticker read 'Desert Stretch.' "Unbelievable!" I exclaimed, scurrying from aisle to aisle, flipping over cans and reading their discrete labels. They had names like Campfire Clench, Ocean Flush, Plumbed Plum, even an unscented variety. "Come on, Johnny. We have to get going. What is it?" Wayne asked again. "Jenkem!" I proclaimed staccato. "All kinds of boutique strains!" "Well, take what you can, and let's move." "I think I'm going to stay here," I said slowly through gaps in calculation. "I'm going to try to get all these cans out of here." "Are you crazy?" Wayne threw his arms up in the air and started for the stairs. "Come on, man. It'll take hours and hours to get these cans out of the basement, and once you get them all upstairs, how are you going to get them anywhere?" "Look, if you want to go, then go." I said. "I'll figure something out." "There are hundreds, if not thousands, of cans," doubted Wayne, well aware of my ignorance to his dissuasion. This only tested his patience. "You know I'm taking the tractor now, whether you're coming with me or not." "Take it," I replied, waving him off with a gesture of dismissal. "Come up and help me look for the key." I stood up, fully displaying my disapproval of this interruption. I huffed up the stairs behind him and scanned the scant furnishings of the humble farmhouse. There wasn't much to do here to pass the time; that much was for certain. "It's probably in his pocket," Wayne inserted. "Check it." "I don't want to touch him." I shook my head in disbelief and walked out the door towards Matthais, whose face was now stained red as his body stretched lifeless across equally lifeless planks of porch. Still, there was a look of peace on his old, red face. He looked free; complete in death. He had used Jenkem, too, maybe to visit his own dead wife. I thought of Eryn. It seemed like forever since I had seen her last. It was probably only a matter of days, but time had been such a fluxing variable that taking measurements of it meant very little to me. I couldn't burn him. I had to give him a proper burial. It even made me feel guilty to scavenge through his pockets for his tractor keys, but it was necessary. His pockets were mostly bare. He had a tin of chew and a thin billfold, both of which I quickly tossed aside before finding the keys. I brought the keys in and handed them to Wayne. "Are you alright to drive a tractor on Red Zombie?" I tested, reluctant to hand over the keys. "Yeah." "Have you ever even driven a tractor before?" "No, but I'll manage," Wayne flapped back his fingertips, his arm extended in reach for the keys. I looked him in the eyes, and I figured he'd fail. I figured that'd make us equal, and we'd see who could prove the other one wrong. I handed them over. "You have your phone on you, right?" "Yeah," Wayne shrugged. "Why?" "Look for your car. If you find it, give me a call. I'll be long gone from here, and I'll need you to pick me up." "Yeah," Wayne nodded. "Just make sure this place is smoldering when you leave, or we're going to be royally fucked." "Don't worry about it," I said. "Burn it to the ground, got it?" "I got it." Wayne seemed satisfied. He walked towards the tractor, and I grabbed Matthais' wallet and ducked back inside, waiting for the roar of the 1935 Lanz Bulldog Model D9531 to break the eerie calm quiet of desolation. The billfold only had one card on it, a plain business card. The only thing printed on the front was a name: Graham Nabors. On the reverse side of the card, Matthais had scribbled something in pen: 59 E. Beech Rd. #70. I flipped the card over and back, wondering if it held any importance whatsoever. I half-expected new information to appear with every turn, but I heard the engine of the road tractor start to buck and slipped the card into my pocket to watch through the window as Wayne clumsily steered and swerved the slow moving vehicle, snaking its way through the grass and out past the hilly horizon. I didn't much know where I was, but the address gave me at least an arbitrary place to try and wind up. First thing's first, though. I had to bury Matthais Henrik. I remembered the spot of field he was staring off into when we were chewing together on the porch. I didn't remember the exact location, but I could just approximate it, and I'm sure that gesture would have been appreciated enough. I headed to the shed to inventory the available supplies and quickly found a shovel and a large wheelbarrow; large enough to fit an acceptable bulk of the premium Jenkem and large enough to cart Matthais to his final resting place. I embarked on the latter of these tasks, which proved more painstaking than I had imagined. His unevenly distributable body slid around the cart, throwing the balance of the barrow's single wheel. When I arrived at the newly established burial plot, I knew it was foolish to try to dig the traditional six feet by myself. I only had to uncover enough dirt so none of the body was sticking out. "The cans won't talk to you here, big guy." I broke the dirt and made quick, enjoyable work about digging. I figured a couple of feet would do the trick, just above half the length of the shovel or so. I never figured that I'd come across what appeared to be a mammoth of a worm, writhing in the dirt. I tried to dig around it, but I came across another similar beast, and I tapped at it with my shovel to see how it would react. The end of the protrusion recoiled and tiny flaps retracted, giving way to a widening, multi-pointed opening that spewed out an offensively odiferous, gelatinous residue. "Jesus!" I snapped. "Are these intestines?" I turned and looked over to the manure pile downfield. I had never put together that there were no animals anywhere in sight. There was nothing here that could produce such an unimaginable heap. It was far too large to be the work of Matthais alone. I dropped to my knees and grabbed one of the slippery tentacles. It disgusted me to no end, but I gripped with all the might I could muster, and I tugged, wresting at the tangled mass of intestines buried somewhere deeper down. Progress was slow, but I could see the dirt shifting, and the tubing inched its way through and out from the earth. It was too much to process to identify the intense emotional flourish that coursed through me and lingered like shadows cast from circumferential luminescence. He was a farmer, and this is what he farmed. *** I regretted the decision to push the wheelbarrow all the way to the address written on the card. I had been able to snag a foldout map from the farmhouse before I left, and on paper, the distance to my destination seemed laughably minimal. I had managed to fit a heaping number of cans onto the wheelbarrow; not as many as I'd liked, but I could always come back for the rest later. I'd say in all, there must have been about 250 to 300 cans piled up in that wheelbarrow when I set out. However, navigating was still a chore, especially with all of those fireballs that were shooting off everywhere in the first couple of hours, and the sum of cans was a lot heavier than I had expected. It was at least twice as heavy as Matthais' corpse. I had been pushing for hours, cursing the whole way. I pushed along until it got dark, and then I kept pushing. I had to jettison several cans, one at a time, along the way. The shotgun holstered down the back of my pants made it even more difficult to walk, and by the time I got to Beech Road, my arms were shaking violently from weakness as I rolled along down the old pavement, but this was it. The building on the corner was addressed 59 E. Beech, but it didn't appear that there was any apartments or offices and nothing labeled with the number 70. It was all just one large building, and there were no lights on. If I hadn't noticed that there were several matching black sedans parked in front of the building, I would've assumed the place was abandoned. As I walked up to the front door, I noticed that two men were standing at either side of the entrance, guarding it. One of them pointed at me and whispered something to the other. The other nodded and walked into the building. The one who spotted me started walking towards me, and I stopped and waited. "Excuse me," I called as he briskly approached. "Are you Graham Nabors?" He reached into his suit and pulled out a handgun. He kept walking, the firearm never once swaying from its aim at my chest. "I think I'm at the wrong place," I said. "I'm just going to turn around. Sorry to bother you." "Hold it right there." I froze, and he stopped a few feet away from me, examining me and examining the wheelbarrow full of cans. "Now let's get one thing straight," he cautioned me. "You keep your voice down, and don't you ever speak that name in public where people can hear you." "Easy," I pleaded. "I haven't seen anyone else in hours." "Are you trying to be wise with me?" "No, sir." "Good," he barked. "Is everything secure?" crackled a voice, coming from a transponder of some sort. "It's not him," the guard replied into a clip on his jacket, and I listened to the conversation, wondering if I was quick enough to reach for the shotgun trigger behind me, kick my leg up in the air and fire. "What do you mean it's not him? I thought he had the stuff." "He does," replied the guard, aim steady. "But it's not the same guy. Does Your Honor want me to shoot him?" "Let me ask him." The guard stood there with eyes locked on me as he awaited the answer. I slid my arm around my back very slowly and clasped my hand around the shotgun. I slid my finger against the trigger, with my eyes locked on his hand on his gun. After pushing all those cans all that distance, I strongly questioned whether or not I had the strength even to kick my leg up high and fast enough to matter. Any sign of tension in his wrist, though, and I wouldn't have much of a choice. "No," came the reply over the transponder. "Bring him in. Your Honor wants to meet him." "Copy that." I breathed a sigh of relief and released my grasp on the trigger. The guard kept his gun on me, though, and I wasn't sure that I wanted any part of the business inside the building. The guard then circled around me and held the gun to my spine. "Walk." The twenty feet to the door were the heaviest steps I took the whole way. This time it wasn't the weight of the cans. "Before you enter the building, I'm going to have to search you. Any weapon I find on you will be returned so long as things go without incident. Any artillery confiscated will be returned to you before you leave. Once you are cleared for entry, you will walk in first, and I will walk in behind you. As long as you comply with these instructions, you will be properly compensated. If you do not follow these instructions perfectly, I will shoot you on the spot." It was the weight on my back. *** The inside of the building was pitch black. The guard needed to shine a flashlight to lead the way until I followed the curve of its beam to an old, boxy freight elevator at the end of a long, wide hall. He pulled the elevator open and I rolled the wheelbarrow inside. The doors slammed shut together, and we rode down to the basement. The basement itself was largely undecorated, and there were no furnishings aside from a large wooden table and some folding chairs around it. The room was just dim enough to see what you needed to without seeing everything else. Two more guards formed from shadows, escorting a third man who was carrying a silver briefcase in his left hand. The man with the briefcase was dressed in a robe, and he was much less imposing than the impeccably dressed private security guards that surrounded him. The two guards stopped in front of me, looking down at me with arrogant disapproval, pulling their jackets aside to show me their holstered handguns. They parted, and the man with the briefcase stepped through. There was something insincerely sleek about him. Maybe it was his hair. It was slicked into a single entity that covered his head instead of individual strands. "Put your guns away. This is no way to treat a guest." The man with the suitcase used his free hand to gesture to his men. They took their fingers off their triggers, but their posture remained just as rigid. He then turned his hand towards me for a handshake. "Graham Nabors?" I asked, and I could see the security guards tense up at the mention of the name, but the man just chuckled a bit as the handshake released. "No," he said smiling. "What's your name, kid?" "Johnny." "Johnny what?" "Johnny Dough." He laughed again, harder this time, though I failed to see the humor in it all. "Let's get one thing straight," he started. "I'm the guy with all the dough. I'm going to call you . . ." he trailed off in thought. "Johnny Don't!" He started laughing again, like a hyena shark. I didn't mind the new name, though. It had an apostrophe in it, which I felt gave it some flare; distinction. "Matthais told me that someone would be taking his place tonight." I'd been set up! I should have finished burying that son of a bitch when I had the chance. I should have buried him alive. "Tell me, Mr. Don't. What was so pressing that Matthais couldn't make it?" I could never come up with good excuses, and I had a terrible poker face, especially when the cards were stacked against me like they were. "He had plans with his wife." "Is that so?" He raised his eyebrows at me. "I find that hard to believe. Matthais has always been very face to face with me. He hasn't personally missed a delivery in the seventeen years I've worked with him." I couldn't think of a way to respond without straying away from my original story. I figured the best route would be to keep my mouth shut and wait for my interrogator to leak a little more information. I saw him looking down at the wheelbarrow. "That's a lot more than I asked for." "You can never be too prepared." "I only asked for 70 cans." I had somehow landed myself smack dab in the middle of a Jenkem deal. None of the schematics made sense to me. I was never much of a salesman at anything. Maybe this was all part of my job. I had so much going on that I neglected finding out any further job details and had amassed several projects to occupy me on the side. Still, I felt as though I could bullshit my way out of this one a little more adequately. "I'll count them out for you," I said. "Matthais told me to throw in a little extra on account of his absence." "I'm sure he did, kid," laughed my customer. I counted out the cans and stacked them on the table as the customer sat down and watched. I threw in an additional five cans, hoping that would be enough of an incentive to let me go without further ado. "That's 75 cans for the price of 70," I said, but the cocky bastard didn't even bat an eye of appreciation. He unclipped the briefcase and slid it across the table to me. "It's all there -- $62,720." He stated, as if 62,720 was a regular number like 10 or 20. "You want to count that, too?" "I trust you," I lied, not wanting to stay there any longer than necessary. It was more money than I had ever seen in one place. "If that's all, I've got a long way back, and it's getting late." I closed the briefcase, but before I could turn to leave, the man stood up from the table and held his palm up at me. "Not so fast," he started. "How do I know if this stuff is any good?" "It's the best," I blindly assured him. "Then you wouldn't mind trying some before you go running off with the money," he beckoned me to take a seat before turning to his bodyguards. "Gentlemen, will you get a seat for Mr. Don't?" One of the guards pulled out a chair for me, and the other two drew their guns and pointed them at me. Needless to say, I obeyed. I had a secret weakness for bullets. "Tank, will you go get the mask?" One of the guards walked off into the darkness in the most distant part of the room, and I heard him unlock something. He came back bearing a gas mask with a crank at the end of the nozzle. "Here it is, Your Honor." "Give it to Johnny Don't, over there," he pointed at me with a sly grin on his face. The guard handed me the mask, and I sat there, looking at it and wondering how to use it. "It's got a can opener built into the nozzle, and it's custom fit to the can. I had it made for me in Sweden." I snapped the can into it and began turning the crank, and the can spun around, grinding against the nozzle as a blade inside the nozzle seared through the top. "Now breathe." I took a deep breath and inhaled the can of boutique Jenkem. It was a can of the Plumbed Plum, and the very rich, hearty fumes broke up to a slightly fruity aftertaste. It was richer than anything I'd huffed, and I feared what was waiting for me on the other side of the mask until I couldn't take it anymore. I started coughing, and had to pull the mask off for air. The customer was laughing and clapping, and even his guards broke a rare smile. They waited and watched as I tried to come to my senses, and I looked around the room at their grinning faces, but I counted one more face that hadn't been there before. It was Matthais. "You!" I growled, pointing. "I thought you were dead!" "He must not be lying about this stuff," I heard from the customer. "He's fucked up bad." "I am. Now, shut up and listen," said Matthais. "As soon as he huffs up, he's going to be able to see me, too. You agree with whatever he tells you to do and get out of here. Then you come see me when you're alone, got it? I can't help you out unless there's nobody around." "Wait a minute, man. What the hell have you gotten me into?" "Tank, bring me the mask. It's my turn." "You got yourself into this one, my friend, but it helped me out in the end. I can finally get some peace and quiet now that those cans are off my hand, and I can finally be with my wife again. I'm going to repay the favor, and then that's it. We're even, you got it?" "Why should I trust you?" I asked. "Curtis Westonbrook," replied Matthais. I heard the mechanics of the can and the Jenkem mask in operation. "What?" "Act sharp," Matthais cautioned. "Matthais, you dead son of a gun. It looks like your attendance sheet remains exemplary. You've outdone yourself once again." "Thank you, Your Honor. I'm glad you're enjoying it." "It looks like I had no reason to doubt this kid you vouched for. I got a little anxious because he lied to me about where you were, but I'm sure that was just a miscommunication," Your Honor flashed his shark-toothed grin at me. "I'm sure it was," Matthais applied. "Now, Johnny, it appears I've got a spot that just opened up on my staff. I hope you're looking for employment." "Actually, sir, I'm already gainfully employed," I declined, unsure if I was lying or not. "I'm not offering this spot to you, Johnny." I breathed a sigh of relief, but Matthais looked none too relieved. "You're taking it whether you like it or not, or you can take a nice, deep dirt nap and get a job feeding the worms. Believe me, Johnny. My job pays a lot more." "Where do I sign?" "That's what I like to hear, Johnny. You know, I knew you were a much smarter guy than you looked when I first saw you." He took a card out of his robe pocket and handed it to me across the table. "That's your first assignment, Johnny. We'll see how smart you really are." It was a plain white card with the same print on it as the one I had found in Matthais' wallet. Except this one had a different name. "Andre Smiley?" "Good luck, kid." He got up from his seat and shepherded his men. One of his bodyguards opened the elevator, and the man and one of his guards boarded. "Once we take off, see to it that you help Mr. Don't get on his way back." One guard nodded. The other released his hold on the freight elevator's doors, and it snapped shut like the hungry jaws of a set trap. I had gotten used to dodging the teeth. All I had to do was keep away from the throat.
