The Layover (pt.1)

Date: Several weeks ago. ---- Several weeks ago. Biddenham, England. Late afternoon. ---- It had been several years since Thom LeStrange had left Bedford. After graduating from St. Catharine's College, Cambridge, with a degree in Modern Languages, he had not only left the family home in Bedford, but left the country altogether. Life as he knew it had thoroughly bored him. Whatever ambit he had applied himself too, he had sauntered out again with unmitigated success. Academically, he was gifted; athletically, he was an Adonis (somewhat of an exaggeration, although one cannot deny he was a strapping young brute), and financially, he was set to inherit more than he knew what do with. The only reaction available to him faced with a world which offered him few challenges was an all-consuming sense of ennui; of world-weariness; of indifference. And so it was to America that Thom had travelled, to train to become a wrestler. How could the wrestling world not appeal? Constant excitement, exposure and fame to feed his narcissistic side, a healthy measure of pain to remind himself that he was human after all (the temptation to believe himself as some kind of demi-god was ever present, it must be understood), and a chance to mete out a little of the pent-up frustration engendered by a life of unbridled success that made life seem mundane. His decision was met, however, by a considerable degree of chagrin from his family, but as always, time played the part of the healer. Following WorldWide 83, aired live from the Edward Jones Dome in St. Louis, Missouri, the entire GCW ensemble was scheduled to travel to Japan for a run of shows. Taking the opportunity to make a long-overdue visit home, Thom flew east instead of west, flying to London Heathrow for an extended layover before flying on to Tokyo. From Heathrow Airport, George, the LeStrange family chaffeur had met Thom with the Bentley, and had transported him back to his lieu de naissance, the Manor House at Biddenham, a village just outside Bedford, a place which the LeStrange family had called home for generations. Thom had bittersweet memories of the house. Aesthetically, it was a fine a home to grow up in as one could ask for; the oldest parts of the house dated back to the Tudor period, and was a wonder of English heritage architecture. Guests were often struck speechless by the splendour of the entrance hall, all oak panelled walls and balustrated double staircases. A beautiful garden with its own apple orchard provided a place of serene tranquility. It was a house in which Thom's every need and whim were catered for. He could remember fine dinner parties his parents had thrown with various other of the Bedford dilettantes; epic in length, with numerous courses each punctuated with a light mouthful of sorbet to cleanse the palete, and all lubricated with the finest wines from the LeStrange cellar. His upbringing had been one of unmitigated success; his parents were charming, decent, generous people, and throughout his childhood and adolescence he had not met with a single setback or dilemma. He had fulfilled all expectations set of him, and life had more or less satisfied him, but he couldn't help feeling that there had been nothing more than that. Every time that he had achieved something, his father had just nodded, as if Thom had merely ticked another box on the list of what it was assumed he would do. Feeling like he had achieved everything possible but it being nothing out-of-the-ordinary was a singularly frustrating experience. Simply put, he had yearned to achieve something more, but it appeared there was little more he could. And it was from this jaded tedium and monotony that the notion of becoming a wrestler was born, and the raised eyebrows and dissenting voices of his disapproving parents had provided a glee he had not experienced in years. "Thom, darling, it's so lovely to see you again, you've been gone for so long." Elizabeth LeStrange, her face a picture of refined beauty and elegance which belied her sixty-one years, moved gracefully towards her first and only son, arms outstretched for an embrace. Thom obliged, kissing her lovingly on each cheek. "Mother, you look as beautiful as ever." Behind Elizabeth, standing in the doorway of the sitting room, stood Harry LeStrange, Thom's father. Some may have mistaken his languishing in the background as sheepish. They would have been wrong. His was not a sheepish, self-conscious sort of awkward cowering, it was an imposing, decisive looming. It was clear from where Thom had inherited his build and frame; everything Thom was, his father was double. Whereas Thom was naturally lean, athletic and well-built, Harry was a couple of inches taller and practically square; not a gym-goer, he was not toned like his son, but made of pure brawn. Atlas-like shoulders sat astride a massive chest and torso, all supported by tree-trunk like legs. When Thom was a child, he had seen his father push a six-inch nail into a piece of wood with his own hands, clasping the wood with his palms and fingers and using both thumbs to drive the nail home. Although largely an easy-going man, with a rather wicked sense of humour, Harry LeStrange was imbued a song sense of tradition and of noblesse oblige, and when confronted with matters he felt discourteous or ill-mannered behaviour, he could suddenly become not only austere, but downright intimidating in his silent indignation. "Son..." Harry LeStrange nodded curtly, and stepped forward towards his son. He stretched out a hand, which Thom took, and they shook, each squeezing the other's hand as hard as possible, trying to assert themself as the stronger man. "How is America treating you? Well?" "Very well, thank you father. You'll be thankful to know that I'm adapting to their ways swiftly." Harry LeStrange nodded, his eyes narrowing. "Still involved in this wrestling malarky are we?" A slight grin flickered across Thom's face, little more than a slight curve in the very corner of his mouth. Elizabeth LeStrange rolled her eyes, weary of the dispute that was inevitably to follow. Thom and Harry looked one another in the eye, neither faltering. A flush of bravado pulsed through Thom's body, the thrill of doing something his father disapproved of energising him. "Yes, father, I am. Is there something you'd prefer me to do?" "Well, it doesn't exactly fill one with pride to see one's son internationally broadcast brawling and scuffling like a pleb. If you're going to debase yourself and do something controversial, at least be original." "Well what do you suggest then, father? Humour me..." Harry LeStrange turned, and retreated through the sitting room and into his study. From an old, leather-bound scrapbook which he kept on a bookshelf, he extracted a yellowed newspaper clipping, and, handling it delicately so as not to damage it, returned to the hallway. "This is an article which my grandfather gave to my father. It is entitled "Crimes of the Gentry". I shall read you a few of the examples, and you shall see what depths I would prefer you stoop to before this base profession you have entered yourself into. The first option it offers is that of the Gentleman Burglar. It suggests one must never steal anything so nouveau-rich as cash, but rather concern oneself with ornaments, first editions of Montesquieu and silver candlesticks. A head for heights is necessary, as the self-respecting gentleman burglar will spend many hours clambering across the roofs and guttering of hotels on the Cote d'Azure. It states that a gentleman's calling card is a must, as is leaving a few cunning clues for the police chaps, such as a few empty sachets of after-dinner mints at the scene of the crime. It suggests that one become close friends with both the victim and the detective who remains two steps behind. One must drop a few clues to the bloodhounds, but then to say "Why, how ridiculous, I could almost be describing myself." Thom rolled his eyes disparagingly, and let out an audible sigh. Harry singled out another choice. "Alright then, boy, what about this one, the Getaway Driver. The article seems to warn against driving anything so vulgar as a Mercedes Benz with tinted windows. It suggests instead a 1930s supercharged Bentley in British racing green. If it was good enough for the 1937 Le Mans 24-hour race, it says, it's good enough for a clean getaway. It says that the more plebian getaway driver will wear a stripy jersey and ladies' stockings over his head, but that the gentleman will sport a tweed overcoat, motoring goggles, and string-backed gloves, and that a fine handlebar moustache will ensure you sail through even the most stringent of police road blocks." Thom opened his mouth to decline again, but, sensing his apprehension, Harry searched for one last option. "Ok, ok, boy, well if that doesn't take your fancy, how about this one - Political Assassin. Apparently one should be exotic with one's choice of victim, and that shooting Balkan politicans is a very respectable way of passing the winter months. It suggests you only shoot those who have been assigned to you by a man whose moniker is a single letter and who has an intriguing scar on his cheek. A hunting rifle seems to be the weapon it recommends, but that one's trusty Webley revolver can be used in emergencies such as evading Moldovian border guards." Growing tired of the nonsense her husband was spouting, Elizabeth LeStrange intervened. "Do stop being so silly, Harry. Thom has made up his mind, and I should imagine there's not the slightest thing we can do to stop him. You know what he's like, he thinks rules are below him." "Oh mother, you forget that it's also a delightful ego boost, a wonderful platform from which to puncture the hoi polloi and the perfect opportunity to legally administer drubbings to cretins." Thom chuckled, and for a moment he was sure he saw a look of contemplation flicker across his father's eyes, as if he had just been introduced to an aspect of the affair that he had previously failed to consider. He knew that his father was not particularly bothered, and, if anything, was most likely jealous. With a family fortune so large one could buy a small country, any kind of meaningful employment was rendered rather pointless, and the whole situation tended to engender a life of sitting around a large Manor house searching desperately for something to pass the time, or worse, conversing with one's spouse on a regular basis. With all his financial desires or needs catered for from the maternity ward to the morgue, the simple pleasures of life became all the more appealing, and Thom assumed it highly possible his father's quiet disapproval of his new life was thinly-veiled jealousy at the prospect of travel, notoreity and bouts of physical aggression. However the polite, civilised circles of Bedford would never permit the elder LeStrange to admit to them, instead recommending light admonition and a certain degree of shame. Opulent but arseingly hypocritical was Harry's view. "Lunch is served!" The voice of the family butler, Clarence, rang through from the dining room, wrenching the LeStranges away from their charming (if not somewhat prim) reunion and towards their lunchtime victuals: a fine Sunday roast.
