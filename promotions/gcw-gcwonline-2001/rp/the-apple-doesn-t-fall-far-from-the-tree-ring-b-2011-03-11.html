The Apple Doesn't Fall Far From The Tree (Ring B)

11 March 2011
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 03/11/11 The roads had been thinning out since Camden, becoming nothing more than dirt and gravel. He tried to keep his eyes on the road, his hand nervously pawing at the gear shift as he steadied the cigarette between his lips with his tongue. The head lights of the truck were broken at the cylinder, ripped out and their red and yellow and blue guts hanging exposed. He could hear them clap against the rusted grill with every jump and bump in the road because the side windows had been broken in. It was better that way. Made it so the cabin didn’t fill up with smoke. It was getting late, the sun’s dying amber glow petering through the clouds before tapering darkly up the horizon, bits of color splintering off and mixing together. Like a mosaic. If he could take a picture, he’d put it on a postcard. It was lovely. He trusted his eyes, but gamma grass and long-leaf pines threw shadows along the road, making it difficult to see ahead until the forest broke into a clearing. Just a little further was a bridge. Its pavement was warped from heat fractures, the steel struts supporting the structure long corroded green. He couldn’t see much of anything at the end of the it or whether the damn thing was stable enough to support the weight of the Ford, but he’d made it this far without a problem. The tires sounded as though they were sliced open as he drove over the bridge. He saw the faint licks of fog dusting goldenrods in along the dried basin. The bridge had outlived its usefulness. Halfway along, he could see what curled barricades hid: a brown Charger. The closer he got to it, the more detail he could make out. Lights were on its cabin's roof. The break from the bridge was harsh, violently jostling him forward and bashing his sternum into the steering column. He winced as he tried to steady himself, the sharp pain down his center matched only by the renewed throb in his elbow. Shields on car doors were never a good sign. And then, it ambled out of its den, steady in its tail. He guessed it was about three or four lengths behind, not that he wanted to make it look like he was staring. He’d never trusted cops, especially in backwaters because they were nothing but trouble. Just like his elbow. The camouflage rag he’d tied off around it started to skirt down his forearm. Blood had stained on through in a big, ugly splotch. He tried to keep his head up. Couldn’t give the police a reason to pull him over, and if he took his eyes off the road, that’d be all the reason the bastard needed and he couldn’t have that after what he’d seen and what he’d done and the siren stopped everything. Suddenly, he couldn’t breathe. He had to focus. The Charger was getting closer. Easing to the lip of the road, he shifted gear into park. At least he thought he did. He’d never been good with a stick. The officer took his time before stepping out of his vehicle. The hat on his head was felt, the base of its bowl wrapped around with thin leather and fastened together with a buckle. The sun might’ve been fast coming down, but he kept his sunglasses on all the same. He was dolled up cream and green, his belts holding heat and his shield flashing prominently from the clip on his chest. He ripped the rag from his arm and stuffed it underneath the bucket seat where the money had been. The officer was right there, at head level. His patch read State Trooper. "Howdy." He couldn’t tell where the man’s eyes were because of the black pools covering his eyes. "Howdy." The smile from the trooper was wide. His baton clapped the edge of truck’s trim. "Noticed the front of your truck." "Yeah. Damn kids, ya know?" He didn’t dare break eye contact. The trooper’s head bobbed as he drew more comfortable, his hands clasping around the lip of the driver’s side window. "Watch your--" "License and registration, please." "Is there a problem?" "No problem, really. Just need to give it a once over." His throat went dry. "Just been looking for the nearest town is all. Was gonna call it a night when I did." The smile withered away. "Can you give me your license and registration, please?" Pressing his elbow against the door, he swallowed a whimper. He looked toward the glove compartment. He snorted. A chill ran through his forearms and across his shoulders and pooled in his stomach. Neither of them was smiling anymore. "Don’t want no trouble, sir." The trooper’s hands were long gone from the brim of the door, his elbows locked at his ribs and his grip at his piece. He had to think. Think fast. "It’s like I said, sir; I was just lookin’ for the next town over and I was fit to pull in. Even if it was just a parking lot. A gas station. Whatever." "If you don’t want trouble, I’d suggest you give me your license and registration. Like I asked." "Sir," he said, licking his chapped lips. He could taste rust underneath his tongue. "Yeah?" He took his hand from the wheel, brushing through the mangy, greasy brown wire hanging from his jaw before reaching underneath the collar of his flannel. His movements were deliberately slow. He didn’t know the trooper’s trigger finger, and he didn’t care much to find out. When his hand reappeared, it was holding a pair of dog tags that were connected around a metal beaded necklace. His eyes filtered downward. "I'm just a soldier, sir. Don’t got much to my name except my name." "What is it?" Defeat sounded in his voice. "Fred. Fred Kingsley. Friends call me Freddie." The trooper tongued the inside of his cheek as he relaxed, his hand reaching for the tags so he could read them. "Where you from originally?" "Kentucky. By Louisville." He took off his sunglasses, putting them in his chest pocket. "Tags say Georgia." "That was where I was living at the time I went into the service." "Where were you stationed in Vietnam?" "In the South. To be honest, sir?" Kingsley bit his lip. "Don’t much...don’t much like talkin’ about it." For a moment, there was a pause. He tried to read the trooper, but the man was a rock. His fingers ran along the inscription, his eyes reluctant to waver away. "I understand." He looked up. Stared right through him. Those eyes were cold. Hardened. Hazel. "Eight miles up? You’ll be coming into Coffeeville. Now it’s not big, but there’s a few shops there. If it gets dark, you pull over." "Sure thing." "Pull over, alright? I’ll be up and down this road, so I’ll know if you don’t." "Yes sir." He finally let go of the tags. "You make it in, there’s a motel just down the way. You get there? You show those to Sam. He’ll give you a bed to sleep on." "I really appreciate it, sir." "Don’t need to call me that. We got to take care of each other out here." He reached over, grabbing Kingsley’s hand from the steering wheel and taking it into his own. The shake was firm. "From one soldier to another." "Thank you." The trooper backpedaled, his hands resting on his belt, watching and nodding his head as if some transcendent force had moved him so much in those few moments that he couldn’t help himself but admire its majesty. Or maybe the veneer of those back roads of Alabama were stripped away, that dark Hell he’d never come home from lighting up momentarily by the survivor spirit of another. There wasn’t much daylight to catch. All Kingsley could do was shake his head as he hit the open road. Blood was smeared along the white chipped paint of the door panel from where his elbow had been. Money isn’t hard. I know how to make it pretty easily all things considered. It’s about connections. When I was twelve, I was the one they’d let into the legion buildings to shine shoes because dad was still a big shot at the local chapter and people seemed to like him. Didn’t know until later that it was just for show. But they didn’t mind me none and just let me focus on shining shoes and keeping my head down as they talked about things grown-ups tend to do. When I was fourteen, I worked in the local diner. Felton’s Finest. Folks used to drive a hundred miles from Marietta just for our patty melts. I started as a bus boy, working real hard after school and it wasn’t long before they moved me to grinding meat and working weekends as a short-order cook. That was back when I figured food was my passion. Still is, kinda. I was going through high school and working my free time there, save baseball season of course. By the time I was seventeen, old Ray Vaughn Felton was depending on me to run his diner. Now McDonald’s didn’t make patty melts or anything, just burgers with onions that were minced too finely if you ask me, but their shakes were to die for. So I figured we could do just as well with soft-serve ice cream. Took some convincing to get the machine put in, and most of the folks working didn’t know how to clean it because of all the small moving parts inside, but we did well with it, paying the thing off within two weeks. Then, Mr. Felton got sick. He’d lost his only son on Omaha Beach, so I guess he looked at me as if I’d taken his place. He’d said as much without saying as much. When he passed on, I didn’t know much about numbers or anything. Didn’t spend a lot of time paying attention in school. Just knew how to fry up onions and patties and grind hamburger. So I sold it to someone for a nice sum of cash. A few thousand dollars in the bank ain’t nothing to sneeze at, especially when you’re still a kid. Suppose the other part of making money is luck. Huffington’s Refuge is going to be different. It has to be different. There isn’t much left from Felton’s and I’ll be damned if I go back to moving around again. Katherine, Abby, and Sue Marie came from Flats out Arkansas way, as did Little Jimmy. Roscoe is from Rascal’s Cottage in Maine and he was at Woodstock so he’s always good for a story or two. I’ll try and fill in the details for them as I move along. What’s important is that we learned our lessons from the other communes we’ve lived in. We must look awful silly to all them regular folk, all crammed into the Kombi like we are. What they don’t realize is that this shack on wheels is the closest thing these kids have to a home. That doesn’t stop Little Jimmy for fiddling with his harmonica from time to time, or Katherine from sewing little figures out of fabric we pick up at the flea markets along the way. I don’t know much about needle point and I don’t much care because my hands shake too damn much, but it brings us close and she smells like peaches. We’re going to set it up in Coosa. They don’t mind coloreds there, which is good for Roscoe. There’s pharmacies and shops just down the way in Rockford. A lumber mill, too. We’re going to need a lot of lumber for the barn, and though I don’t know what grows naturally in these parts, I’m sure someone will be willing to lend a hand. That should get us started. The state trooper hadn’t lied, which, for Kingsley, was an eye opening experience. He couldn’t count a single time a cop had ever done right by him. Just because of dog tags. They were just dog tags. He supposed being done right by was in the eye of the beholder. He hadn’t even stepped into his room in Coffeeville, but he’d made sure to take a look around town. It was a meager place, so small that Main Street wasn’t fixed around a brick cloister of businesses and doctor’s offices like every other backwater in Alabama. It was just a dusting on one side of country road that’d been so poorly stripped of pines that they were cropping up around building foundations. Their parking lots weren’t even layered with chipped stone, save for the Rexall pharmacy; they had asphalt. It sizzled underneath his boots. The Rexall family dotted downward all the way from Virginia, and their shops always seemed to be in shanty towns like this one. At least that’s what he figured. The blood had stopped flowing down his left arm, but there wasn’t reason to draw attention, so he rolled the sleeve of his flannel down and tightly buttoned the wrist. The hours on the glass door said nine for close, and even though some of the fluorescents in the back were shut off, the door wasn’t locked. Bells rattled when he eased on the door handle, the length of twine they’d been fastened to on the other side swaying as the door hissed back onto its metal hinges. The aisles were layered lengthwise in eight, all with wide, gaping spaces at the front and back of their fixtures. The price tags on the items were hand stenciled individually. Wax was so thick on the tiles that it nearly blinded him to look forward. A voice startled him, causing him to whip around to follow it. It came from a man with a tag that said doctor next to his surname, his stomach threatened to pop the buttons of his evening coat. A paper hat rested on top of his head. Kingsley put his hands up, easing his heels back toward the door. "Sorry, sir. The door was unlocked so I figured you was still open. I’ll be letting myself out now." "Don’t worry ‘bout that." The pharmacist spoke in the softest, most unassuming drawl he’d ever heard. He waved him in, patting him on the shoulder before standing next to him. His breath stank of mustard. "You’ll just be the last customer for the day." Kingsley eased his thumbs along the belt loops of his jeans, pursing his lips and nodding his head. "Appreciate it." The pharmacist broke away, rubbing away the sheen of sweat along his brow as he moseyed toward the register at the front. The drawer popped open, his head tilting down so he could make the final count for the evening. "I ain’t seen you before," he shouted a bit too excitedly, his voice bouncing off the fiddleback trim along the walls. "What brings you through Coffeeville?" "Aww nothin’," Kingsley started, already halfway down the center aisle, his eyes straining over the shelves due to the lack of light. "Just need someplace to dust my spurs off before hitting the road again." "Where you from, stranger?" The four aisles from the left had little assortments of jarred and bagged food all thinly spread out along their deep green shelves, and while he was hungry, it wasn’t what he was there for. The fifth row was the start of medical supplies and cleaning products. Turpentine and wart cream and bandages. Aisle six carried small metal implements for self-surgery, along with disinfectants. "You wouldn’t believe me even if I told you." "Hopefully the weather is nice over there." The pharmacist snorted, his hands busy rolling pennies and dimes into rolls. "I, for one, can’t stand it here. Been prayin’ for rain for weeks and it ain’t yet come." Kingsley didn’t respond, instead grabbing a bottle of peroxide and another of bactine. He held a box of tulle gras underneath his right armpit. The hobby knife and tweezers came packaged together in the same plastic wrap. Combing the outer edge of the last aisle to the right, he stared through the darkened partition that blocked off the medical guts of the pharmacy. The pharmacist was still fiddling with his money and given how much he was sweating, Kingsley figured it wouldn’t be hard to-- He thought better of it. No need to press his luck. He walked to the register. "Might need to put that scraggly thing around your chin down." The pharmacist’s jowls bounced as he laughed. He just stared back. "The beard. I’m talking about the beard. It’s fit to have a mind of its own." Still no response. He pawed at his paper hat, brushing thinning strands of silver off to the side. "Didn’t mean anything by it." "Don’t worry." He patted his flannel, reaching into his shirt pocket for a cigarette only to find that he had none. "Wouldn’t happen to have Winstons, would you?" "Plum out of them, I’m afraid." "Anything else you got that’s cheap?" "Got some Strikes. They ain’t got no filter, though." "That’s alright. I’ll take ‘em." He reached into his jean pocket and slapped down a twenty. "I bite the filter off anyway." Sue Marie is pale as a ghost, so a little sun does her good. She’s been volunteering to do everything, be it helping cut lumber or going into town to get food. She just needs to hold off on the thyme. Whenever I talked to her about my dream for Huffington, her eyes always filled up and her smile would beam. She’s always been willing. Thinks the idea of it is romantic. And even though she looks frail, she’s lived on a cattle ranch, so she knows what from what. What I didn’t expect, though, was just how much she knew about geometry. She was the one who figured out how to keep the barn level, and since it’s my money, I’m quite appreciative that someone stepped up before we got too far along with the foundation. I don’t mean to make it sound like nobody else is pulling their weight. Abby’s been doing her part, but she’s only got one arm that works right, and it frustrates her so damn much that she can’t do more. I’ve caught her sobbing when everyone else is asleep. They’re barely audible over the crickets. We’re in this for the long haul. I have to keep reminding her that she’s part of the family, and that she can do things that the others can’t even if she doesn’t think so. We don’t leave family behind. I keep telling everyone that we’re equals. That we don’t take advantage of family. That we’re all brothers and sisters. They’re still just kids. I’m the one who makes the decisions. They respect me. After we cook dinner, we always make sure to unwind for the evening. The mind needs a rest, after all. The creek that runs along the property line is nice, the ripple sounding over the rocks and helping me sleep at night. We drink beers as walk along the creek from time to time just to see if it feeds out to a larger body of water. There’s a shoal just down the way, and we found a few old lawn chairs just like grandma used to have, all caked with sand and their bases rusted something fierce. We took them for us. Not like it’s hurting anybody. Little Jimmy is playing his harmonica and I can hear the crackle of the fire growing nicely. Roscoe’s been asking Sue Marie to undo them buns on her head for some time now, but he doesn’t know her like I do. She’s been so damn impressive as of late. When she undoes them, it’s like she has a cape of black. I put her on my shoulders and her hair stretches all the way down to my back. She’s not as soft as I’d like, but in the moonlight, I don’t mind her all that much. He could taste stale lilac in the air. The walls of the motel room were varnished a harsh copper, uneven strokes leaving behind small ravines streaking downward and crossways without rhyme or reason. There was no television. No phone. He didn’t need those things. The sheets on the bed were white and laced with starch. He found a leather-bound bible when he opened the drawer to the nightstand, its letters stenciled in gold. It was the cleanest place he’d slept in years. There wasn’t a kitchen, but there was a dining room table, one chair for each side of its square base. He took the Bible and put it down on the table along with the paper bag he’d been nursing against his chest. Unbuttoning his flannel shirt, he tossed it into the corner as he turned on the nearby lamp. The skin around his ribs was stretched taut along the bone. Blotches of soot smeared across his chest, mixing in with bushels of brown hair that went from his stomach all the way across his shoulders. He needed a rope to keep his jeans from falling down his hips. He’d not been in the company of man for a long time. Hunching over in his seat, he spilled the paper bag out across the table before reaching into his pocket for his zippo. Lucky Strikes and needle-nosed tweezers and a knife and he bit his lip as he flipped past Corinthians. He needed nicotine. Needed something to take his mind off what he was about to do. He took a drag from his cigarette, unpacking the tweezers and running its tip along the flame from his zippo. He patiently waited until they went black, singed vapor rising up to the popcorn ceiling. He flexed his arm. Elevated the elbow. Held his breath. He stabbed the tweezers into an open cut along his elbow. He rooted around inside. Pushed deeper. He groaned. Bit the inside of his lip. He swallowed chewed skin. Gingerly, he pulled back. A small ribbon of glass came out, held fast between the prongs of the tweezers. It was just one of eight cuts that he’d counted that were deeper than superficial, though he wasn’t sure if he’d have to go back inside to clean them out in full. Wincing, he applied pressure to the wound by pushing it into the pages of the Bible. Smoke slithered from his nostrils as he waited to get enough courage to continue on. It was the first time the good book had been useful to him in his life. There was a kid in my platoon. We called him Wingbat, but his real name was Gunther Peavy, and he came from Oregon or someplace in the Pacific Northwest because he always talked about how it rained out there. He had a soldier’s chin, and if you didn’t pay attention to his lazy eye, you’d think he came straight off one of them posters we’d all seen when we got our physicals at the Salvation Army. We called him Wingbat because he wouldn’t shut up, always saying that he shouldn’t be here, that he’d never dropped the math class his school said he did. Nobody cared for him, but that stopped around the time we realized that he knew more about how to treat wounds and jungle rot than the pricks in the MASH unit. Then we just called him Gunny, even though he was a private like the rest of us. It’s not like your name matters once you’re in a grave, anyway. He said that when the war was over, he was going to start a vet and take care of animals because they deserved to be cared for just like the rest of us. The sergeants were savage toward him, beating him up, shouting about how pathetic he was. That was just their way. They’d had the humanity beaten out of them too, so they supposed it must’ve been fine to keep the cycle going. And even though he wasn’t any good with a gun, they always put him at the front of the line because "it would build up his American character." And when we went through My Lai 4 and none of them put up a fight and we were told to take no prisoners and I'm not going to write about it anymore. The farm is up and running. We’re growing corn even though it might be a little past season. There aren’t seasons here, though, so we should be fine. Once we get ears, we will have something to eat and to barter with. It isn’t much, but it’s a start. I came up with the rights for everyone in Huffington. We’re all equals, but it goes deeper than that now that we are growing food. We all share the load, and as such, we all share some of the profit. This spawned conversation about other things, like how we’d rotate leaders and how we’d take care of the sick. They want to build a community so badly. Their energy is like food. I just wish everyone was carrying the weight. But Little Jimmy has been sleeping around the barn most of the day and Abigail, poor Abigail, she still hasn’t been useful for much. She keeps staring at me, telling me she knows. I think it’s the isolation. That’s what Sue Marie says. Roscoe says there are snakes near the creek, and those could make for good meat. I’ve made him swear he won’t tell anyone else. They won’t eat it if they knew where it came from. Katherine is still coming up with ways to surprise me with those dainty little hands. She’s the wallflower, the demure little thing that keeps to herself and doesn’t understand what she’s doing just by smelling so good. With burlap from the potato sacks, she’s making knickknacks she thinks we can take to town and sell in the local grocery. "For kids," she says. It’s a wonderful idea. We’re open to all, but nobody has come. Until today. He calls himself Dougherty. Raw Dougherty. He’s the biggest man I’ve ever seen. Sue says he’s rugged. I don’t think a few scars on the cheek makes a man rugged, but that’s just me. He speaks with such clarity. He’s as old as me, says he’s been drifting from place to place because he hadn’t found a home since coming back from the A Shau Valley. I’m sure his long blonde hair moving in the breeze is the reason why the eyes of my the girls are glued on him. The stress of maintaining Huffington and burning through our money to keep everyone fed makes me welcome him. Maybe his is a level enough head that he’s willing to put in the work and listen unlike so many I’ve come across. The service station was right by the motel, and even though grass shot out of the cracks of its concrete drive, the Pennzoil bell inside its window still hummed with red fluorescence. There wasn’t much to it, simply a pump and a large metal garage bolted onto the side of brick and mortar. He read the hours, knew he could get in before the crack of dawn, which was what he wanted because the cash was burning a hole in his pocket. He’d never been all that good with money. What he was good with was engines. Car parts. Everything to do with automotive. His father thought it was more important to learn a skill than sit around on idle hands, so every Saturday morning he’d be in the garage with an oil pan and a crescent wrench, lacquering up the overalls that’d been handed down from his brother and the brother before him. That’d been lifetimes ago. He walked back to the motel, looking up to the lone lot lamp and watching scores of moths flitting and crashing into one another for a taste of light. The F-100 was littered with problems that went beyond chipped paint and bleeding rust and broken head lights. The cab was riding low on its suspension, as was the bed. That meant the coil springs had softened. But what bothered him most, what he could feel through the steering wheel no matter how he gripped it, was the engine. The idle was choking in intermittent intervals and the hood rattled. Opening the driver’s-side door, he messed with the tangle of wires he’d ripped out to hotwire it in the first place, striking frayed copper until he got a pulse. He popped the hood so he could take a look inside. The V8 looked fine for how beat up it was, but fuel was gushing from the top of the carburetor. He cut the engine quick, getting inside before pounding on the locket of the glove box so he could get one of the wrenches he’d stowed in there. He’d stowed the Kaiser blade in there too. He took the blade, fitting it into his pocket, and milled around for a socket wrench hidden underneath the book he'd found on driver's seat and dried-up pine needles. Lord only knew where they came from. If it was only a carburetor problem, he could replace that. Ford had made most of their parts interchangeable at a certain point a decade before, so it was just a matter of getting the right part. He’d be back on the road in no time. I was wrong. The temperature is dropping rapidly and frost has stunted the corn crop, forcing me and Little Jimmy and Roscoe and everyone to cut down what we could salvage. It’s not looking good. It’s all either underdeveloped or rotted out. Green kernels don’t sell, and I... Raw came in some months ago, right at the tail end of spring, and I figured with his size and strength and know-how, he’d be an asset. But he hasn’t been at all, instead always shoving off down the creek without a shirt. That’s what bothers me most. My crop is dead. It’s my money on the line here. But there he is, standing in front of everyone, telling me not to worry because local towns don’t barter with folk like they used to. Said we have no in with the supermarket, and even if we did, we wouldn't get a fair price anyway. Says there’s no money in it. And maybe he’s right, but I’m expected to let him say his piece without firing back? He keeps saying he’s not trying to usurp my authority but what he’s doing isn’t right. He doesn’t get that we’re supposed to be equals, but there he is, making it all about himself with these scenes and these fancy words. But then, he took me back and showed me the weed crop he’d been running off to tend to all this time. Weed. There’s good money in that, so I suppose that counts for something. Ain’t like we have lawmen coming back here to check on us. Raw doesn’t know the people I do at the bar, though. He thinks he’s so high and mighty, that everyone is looking up to him, but the Angels say that they can give me a good deal as long as its good stuff. They only want good stuff since their main business is this stuff called crank, and while I don’t know what it is, when I got a taste, it made me feel like those morale pills the sergeant gave us before he’d order us to charge up the hills. Abby is canning raccoon meat and some of the tomatoes we’d gotten in bulk, and it’s pretty pathetic watching her jab that stump of a wrist to try to do things. We don’t speak at all, and it’s better that way because I tried building her up but it didn’t work out like it did with Sue Marie. Hate to say it, but Katherine is dumber than the two of them, and they weren’t exactly the smartest of the lot out in Flats. She says she’s saving her dignity for the right man. We aren’t shoving off, so I figured the Kombi had overstayed its usefulness. Lots of memories in her, but Katherine likes some of the Fords on the lot, and the 63 is cheap so I might well impress her with it. Besides, it makes it easier to haul things back and forth from town. His head was foggy, his eyes feeling as dry as sand. He couldn’t get much sleep, the constant feeling of being sucked into his mattress furthering dreams of weightless freefall. If that hadn’t done it, it would’ve been the groan of the bedsprings whenever he tossed or turned. The ground was safe, and it never played tricks like that. He’d grown accustomed to its rigor. Perhaps it was for the best; he’d beaten the sun to rise, and he watched it bleed through the blinds with half a pack of Strikes and a glass of water whenever his throat got too dry. He’d forgotten just how amazing indoor plumbing was. It was easier to trust a faucet than a puddle on the side of the road. Personal pride didn’t matter much to him when dry saliva chalked his mouth. While he didn’t have much left in the way of cash, he desperately needed a change of clothes. He’d stitched the wound holes along the left rib of his flannel shirt, but the grime rubbing against his shoulders made his skin crawl. The sun didn’t last, overcast skies taking precedent. He made his way over to the service station, the gnats buzzing around his ears the whole way. The air was thick and salty and fresh. It stung his eyes. The garage door was open, the man inside wiping grease off his hands with a towel. It didn’t seem like he was doing a good job because the black dashed along his hairy forearms and up over the rolled up arms of his navy coveralls. He was Indian, from what Kingsley could tell. A long weaved black ponytail fell to the small of his back. They couldn’t even afford to stitch a proper name along his chest. "Howdy." The mechanic kept his chin tucked against his burly chest. "Hello." "Don’t mean to trouble you, sir, but I need a hand." He tongued the inside of his cheek for a moment before looking up, his lips pursing as his eyes narrowed. His cheeks were pockmarked. "Well I got two cars out back, one with suspension problems and another with a blown gasket that I told the woman wasn’t worth fixing, but she doesn’t listen. They’ve been here since Tuesday, so that puts you three days behind them in line." "It’s not like--" His voice was hostile. "Did I stutter?" Kingsley nodded his head slowly. It was alright. He could deal with this. But the more he thought about it, the more his nods picked up speed. Suddenly, they stopped altogether. His browed furled. "It ain’t like that. I can do the work. But I ain’t got the parts." "Same rules apply." "Your business is your business, and I ain’t askin’ you to change on my account, but you have the books--" "Parts cost money." "Well no shit!" He rolled his tongue over his bottom lip momentarily before biting down into a toothy sneer. "I didn’t say I was gonna pay you beads and fuckin’ firewater!" "Filvmmi!" "Yeah..." He dug his hands into his jean pockets. The Kaiser blade ran against the heel of his hand. It would be so easy. Maybe the parts he needed were stored away inside that damn kindling box. He snorted, spitting a rope of snot at the redskin’s feet as he withdrew his hands from his pockets and backed away. It wasn’t worth it. "Fuck you." Katherine is pregnant and I don’t know for how long it’s been, but from the look of her stomach, it’s been a few months. Everyone is congratulating Raw, like he’d won a prize or something. He’s the one saying the lines about Huffington I’d been saying. He says things clearer than me. I can’t keep from stuttering anymore. When I look at my reflection in the creek, I swear to Christ my hair is starting to fall out. I know the sores around my mouth are getting worse because I can taste them and it hurts. I don’t know what’s going on anymore. Maybe finding out about my Katherine has done a toll on me, but I need to keep away for a spell. I scalped about half the crop Raw was growing and I’m getting a handshake of trust from the Angels and it’s just as well. As long as he didn’t drive at night, the headlights weren’t a liability, even if they were broken. The issue was how they’d been ripped out. Not every police officer was bound to have a shaky constitution like the one he’d come across. Masking tape was cheap, and it would hold the headlights in place with just a few strips on both sides. Wasn’t like it was abnormal; he’d seen plenty of automobiles use the same tactic, though they probably did it for different reasons. The real issue was under the hood. The V8 still ran at a decent clip, but with fuel flooding over the top of the carburetor and the spastic chug in the idle, he didn’t know how long that clip would last. There were bound to be other service stations in other counties, and if there wasn’t, well, he was fast approaching Mississippi. There had to be a stopgap solution, though. He propped the hood up with the strut next to the engine block before screwing the carb from the intake manifold. The good thing about Ford Motor Company was that they didn’t layer parts one on top of the other like General Motors, meaning that he didn’t have to unhook belts or shift batteries to get to where he needed. The gaskets inside the carburetor were worn down, as was the accelerator pump. There wasn’t even a float pin inside. All he could do, for now, was hook the wire retainer back to the needle valve seat housing and hope for the best. So much day was left, and even though he was tired, he knew he could get a healthy ways closer to the beaches of the Gulf. Why was he heading out that way? To be truthful, he didn’t much know why himself. Maybe it was because one could outrun their sins on those white sugary sands. He could hear the sound of rolling thunder in the distance. The movement is dead. It was dead after Altamont and I knew it the whole time but I’d earned the right! These kids don’t understand. They still have their lives, and this is how they chose to live them, calling us murderers like we had a choice in the matter. I’d seen the pieces of folks shipped back in boxes and plastic bags. Lord only knows if the parts matched. Made me want a proper burial rather than being left above to rot. Everyone is with everyone, and they all seem so happy. Little Jimmy keeps staring at me. Huffington isn’t mine anymore. Raw’s got them asking for their money. Says it’s only right after stealing half the crop. He doesn’t get it. When they’d sold everything before the trip, I’d told them that it was going to go to the health of the refuge, but it’s like they expect me to pull it out of my ass. They gave it to me. It’s mine now. Puddles of hot water trailed along the laminate from his footfalls, the towel in his hands sponging away at his chest. It’d been months since he’d bathed, and even then, sloshing about in a brook wasn’t exactly comparable. Combing the towel over his wild mane of hair, he paused, staring at himself in the bathroom mirror. How long had it been since he’d seen his own reflection? The years bled together during the drift. He couldn’t recognize himself anymore, the mangy beard and hair deep past his shoulders having overtaken his face. This was the image the folks of Coffeeville needed to remember him by. No need for them to see his real face. His body had morphed from meaty, thick muscle to chewed-up, gangly sinews. His fingernails were caked with filth. He wasn’t sure if he was ready to see the exacting toll for what he’d become. The Kaiser blade rested in the basin of the sink. His brow strained as fire trampled his tired nerves. Taking the blade by the screws, he pressed it against his right cheek. He pulled down. Tufts of hair crinkled underneath the pressure of the edge. Only small bits of hair were carried away. But he wasn’t deterred, the small pale blemish that showed from where he’d shaved making him wonder what the rest of him looked like underneath. Soon, he was becoming more forceful. He looked through the mirror, looked past it, his mind more focused on the task than the image before him. Clumps of hair fell in the basin, powdered the lips of the sink, littered along his toes. When there wasn’t beard to be shaved, he grabbed his hair in balled up clusters and sheared them away. It wasn’t enough. He didn’t notice anything was left until his tears burned down his naked cheeks. Sullen blue eyes irritated red. Gaunt cheekbones threatening to cut clear through his skin. Sunken cheeks that tapered downward like the sides of a triangle to the point of his chin. His breath caught in his throat. He was skeletal. Before he could think more of it, he gathered his clothes, putting them on before hitting the open road. The rain came down in spurts. The pharmacist had finally gotten his wish. When I started writing this, the idea was to have everything about our time here on paper so it could be permanent. Huffington wasn’t ever going to be a beacon. Most of the people here don’t realize why I was moving from place to place. It had nothing to do with the open road because, to be honest, I hated it. Those fucking hippies want to sit around campfires and smoke peace pipes and bang on drums and can’t deal with someone who dares call them out on their shit! There were no connections. Nobody was coming. So I created my own home and let the leeches come along for the ride. I kept most of the cash in the glove box because I knew it’d be safe there. But I had this odd feeling. So I went to the truck just to check, and when I opened it, there was nothing but a bunch of damn pine needles inside. There’s still some in the coffee can underneath the seat, but Christ, they took everything! So, I know Katherine was pregnant, but she never understood. I’ve put up with her and the empty thoughts that fell out of her lips for two years now, and never once did she spread her legs. She threw rocks at soldiers who were just getting off the plane in Texarkana and Little Rock because they were all child killers in her mind, and I did too because it made her smile. But that wasn’t enough. She always wanted more. Never offered to give. Always was the first one to take. The little angel from a rough life who I never could make happy, no matter how much of my soul I swallowed trying to do it. I blame Dougherty for what happened, and I know he’s going to come after me when he sees her all split open near the creek. It’s not like I feel a stitch of remorse. I suppose that means my American character is strong. The blade is still in my pocket. Let him come. I never came back home anyway. The creek separated one side of the forest from the other, and he’d walked along its rounded edge for miles. There was no bridge. It was just one long cut. The roots from trees along the bed were exposed by years of tide erosion. He couldn’t help but think of his feet. His soles were raw, and he could feel the skin ripping along the contours of his arches. Blisters were the norm, but he’d never gotten used to them. The shoal was a lucky break, the gulls resting over its sandy top fluttering eastward as he made his way across. He took the knapsack over his right shoulder and tossed it over the ridge. The wrenches and saws inside it banged together when it hit the ground. Groping the grass and pine needles at the edge of the basin, he pulled himself up, slowly pulling the frayed rope of the sack to draw it back to him before slinging it over his shoulder. Daybreak was near, meaning it was bound to get sweltering again. Maybe he could cool off in the creek when the sun got too oppressive, as it’d been the last few days. It hadn’t been a mile before the trees gave way to a clean-cut space, tendrils of grass giving way to groove cuts of dirt with withering corn stalks standing high above. Some of their ears stuck out of their sheaths, layered with white and black moss across their exposed kernels. Maybe some of them were still healthy. The rumbling pit in his stomach sure could use something to fill the hole. The field wasn’t the only thing on the plot; there was a barn, the peak of its shingleless roof having buckled inward. Its walls stood over a story tall. There wasn’t a house, wasn’t any sort of road in or out. To the left was a Ford truck with chipped white paint and a warped body. Its windshield was clouded. His feet, raw as they were, could stand to use a bit of a break. He was sure whoever owned the truck wouldn’t mind if he took it. After all, it looked like it’d been sitting there for years. He tried the door, jostling the handle, but it wouldn’t budge. Using the sleeve of his camouflage jacket, he wiped down the passenger’s side window. The lock pin of the door was pushed down. He looked left. Looked right. He clasped his hands and formed fists. His elbow thrust against the window. Nothing. He thrust again. The glass buckled slightly. He could feel it tickle up his forearm. He did it again and again and again and again and The window had given way, but only in a convex hole a size and a half larger than his fist. It’d chewed through the fabric of his jacket, stabbing glass into his left elbow. He grabbed the wound with his other hand, panting and whining as he sneered and reached into his knapsack and grabbed a crescent wrench and smashed the passenger’s-side window in before stomping around and doing the same to the driver’s side. It didn’t stop the gush of blood that was cascading down his arm. Gritting his teeth, he stalked his way into the barn, looking for a place to sit down so he could dress his wound. He tripped as he nestled the wall to the left, falling down on his face. Felt like the roots of a tree. He rolled over, growling as he kicked back in frustration until his heel went through and the pervading dust caught in his throat and made him gag. The pain in his elbow numbed as he brushed away the flatsedge until he saw the murky, fleshless grin underneath. The flannel shirt and he were one now, as was the Kaiser blade that lanced out of his ribs. A ribbon of sun reflected silver down along its neck. He plugged his nose and reached down toward it. The drifter figured a name didn’t matter once you were gone. It wasn’t like Fred Joseph Kingsley could raise a fuss if someone else decided to take his as their own.
