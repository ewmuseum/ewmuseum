Pain Pills for Breakfast and Cigarettes for Lunch: Rinse and Repeat

4 March 2011
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 03/04/11 Cemetery They said the Graceland Cemetery was famous, but it looked like the other cemeteries Jay had visited. The gate was modern, replaced maybe, and far less menacing than those he’d seen in New Orleans and Georgia. The road seemed newly paved and was surely newly plowed considering the snow covering everything else. Seven new inches of powder had fallen the night prior hiding the only thing left that had the potential to be famous: the grass. Nonetheless, his driver navigated along the road slowly upon request. Jay’s eyes scanned the headstones and mausoleums curiously as he wondered if he was more fascinated with death or the architecture. He settled on the combination while passing an obelisk he estimated to be twenty feet tall. The windows were foggy and he couldn’t read the name properly, but he imagined he was a count or a mayor. Whoever he was, Jay took a mental note for his own burial instructions. “You said near the front?” asked his driver. “Should be close. Yeah,” Jay responded. He wiped the window with his sleeve and continued to watch the graveyard reveal itself. The property was immense, some one hundred and twenty acres he’d been told, and expanded elegantly. Picnic tables had been placed strategically within grave clusters and offered a visual representation of the snow. He saw one which had been wiped clean, but no one occupied it. His driver carefully traversed a winding hill and Jay looked through the windshield. They told him that the man he was visiting was buried beside a famous boxer who had died in 1917. The boxer, whose name escaped memory, had been noted as the first man to capture world titles in three divisions. Jay smirked at the pairing of the graves. “Should we look for a headstone?” asked the driver. “They said it was a tall darker one. It would be newer,” Jay added. “I’m not sure how we’ll find it with all of these stones,” the driver said. “Maybe it’s best to search by foot then,” Jay replied. “Pull over where you can.” There was an open runway of pavement on the left-hand side and the driver pulled the Cadillac into the space. Before he could help Jay out, Terror opened his door and stepped out into the winter climate. He wore a coat from New York with fur around the hood. It didn’t protect his face nor head as he imagined it would. Nevertheless, he started to read the stones he could. There were a lot of names on a lot of stones. Some were more recent, but most seemed to be from the twenties and earlier. “If only it hadn’t snowed,” said the driver. Jay didn’t reply. He continued to read. He was looking for gloss. They said the headstone was glossy and black and tall. With the snow, everything looked the same height and nothing was black. Making matters worse was the sun. It was hiding behind clouds making anything shiny near impossible to see. He started down the road reading inscriptions in passing. “Maybe we can ask for help?” asked the driver. Jay still didn’t reply. His eyes were focused on foot prints. Most had been covered with the snow, but fresh tracks a few headstones away were visible. Jay dropped his boots into the snow and walked across the graves. He read names along the way and silently apologized for walking overhead. Around the seventh stone away from the road, the tracks stopped and Jay used his coat sleeve to wipe the head of it. It was shiny and black. A bouquet of roses were planted into the snow and Jay moved them aside to read the name on the front. It said Rich Rollins. “You’re still causing havoc three years later,” Jay said. He followed with a laugh and then went silent. He looked at the stone and wasn’t sure what to say or how to say it. Seven hundred miles and he still hadn’t finalized a speech. He thought of telling him what had changed and what hadn’t. He thought of telling him that people were still talking about him. Jay didn’t think Rich would care too much about either though. Instead, he found his box of cigarettes and lit one over the grave. He inhaled for the fallen champion and offered a shrug. “I don’t even remember him smoking,” said a voice behind Jay. It belonged to a woman. “Me either,” Jay replied. He turned around and looked at the woman. She was bundled in a pea coat with a gray scarf around her neck. Her hair was gathered in a hat but a strand hung over her face. She was a brunette. “I didn’t see you there,” Jay said. “I was looking around. It’s beautiful here,” she said. “It has its’ features,” Jay responded. “I’m Katherine.” “I’m Jay.” “How’d you know Rich?” Katherine questioned. “Well,” Jay started. He stopped to take another drag from his cigarette. A cough followed and the smoke escaped his lips. Jay coughed again, this time into his palm, and bent over. His cough got worse with each iteration and blood painted his palms on the fifth one. “Jay,” Katherine said. “Are you OK?” Terror remained bent over and continued to cough blood. It ran from his hand and tainted the snow over Rich’s grave. Katherine moved forward to help him, but Jay lost balance before she could reach him. He fell backwards without warning due to the most violent of his coughs, and felt the tough edge of a glossy black headstone dent his skull. Terror slumped into the snow, slowly sliding, and stopped coughing on impact. His sight was fuzzy, his fingers tingling, and his brain was beginning to shut off. The last thing he remembered was a gloved hand checking for a pulse. Hospital His pillow was propped up like a headstone and his head pounded. His vision was slowly returning, but he didn’t need it to realize where he was. The IV in his arm was a good indication. “You in there, Jamison?” asked a voice. It didn’t have a face, but it sounded like an older gentleman. Jay’s eyes adjusted slowly to the hospital lighting. He started to see a round nose and glasses with a thick bridge. He saw chapped lips and old contours winding across leathery cheeks. He saw grey eyebrows, grey eyes, and grey hair. The man was a doctor and Jay watched his jaw unhinge to talk again. “Don’t strain yourself. You’re in good care here.” Jay blinked and ground his teeth. He wiggled his fingers and then his toes and blinked again. “My name’s doctor Reynolds, Jamison,” the old man continued. “If you’re up for it now, I’d like to talk with you about a few things having to do with your health. I understand if you’d like to rest though.” Terror lifted his arm with the IV in it and touched his forehead. He felt tightly wound bandages. “I’m sorry, but do you know what happened?” doctor Reynolds asked. Jay shut his eyes and let seconds pass. He opened them again and focused on a stain on the ceiling. It was oddly shaped and red and Jay imagined a geyser shooting from his own head. He believed it would explain the throbbing. Terror shut his eyes again. “We’ll talk later, Jamison. Get some more rest and we’ll talk later.” “No,” Jay said. His voice was hushed but still audible. “It’s just that my head won’t stop pounding. I can’t think with my eyes open.” Doctor Reynolds nodded and looked over his patient. “You hit your head pretty hard. It’s no wonder that it hurts. Do you remember anything that happened?” “I don’t even remember where I was,” Jay admitted. “A cemetery,” he reminded him. “You were at a cemetery visiting a friend when you slipped and hit your head on his tombstone. What luck you had too considering the young woman who witnessed it. Her and your driver got you here in almost no time. A cut like that needs that kind of rush.” Terror tried to think back to the fall but could hardly remember the cemetery. He remained silent. “The woman you were with said that it was coughing that did you in,” doctor Reynolds continued. “She said you lit a cigarette and you couldn’t stop coughing after your second inhale. I’ve pulled your record, Jamison. You’ve been to doctors in Philadelphia, New York, and Dallas and each one told you that you had lung cancer. Did you not believe them or did you simply not care whether it killed you?” Jay could feel the fluctuations in his tone and understood the course the conversation was going to take. He’d dealt with it in all three other cities and had promised them all that he’d quit smoking. He promised everyone that cared that he would stop smoking. Something in him still wasn’t ready. Jay remained quiet. “I realize that it’s a decision that you have to make, but I feel like it’s my job to guide you to a better life. This is small cell lung cancer we’re talking about, the kind that starts from smoking. I’m sure they told you how rapid it can spread and how tough it is to stop without cooperation. Luckily for you, it’s still in stage one and we can fix this for you, Jamison. We can work to remove this and help you stay alive. If you keep smoking though - well, let’s just say that you can forget the friends you’ve made and the family you have left.” Terror still didn’t say anything and instead tried to remember whose grave he had visited. He imagined it was his father’s and soon believed it, considering he couldn’t remember the city he was in. “If you’re willing to give this a shot, then we can help you here. We can start you on a chemo treatment and help nullify the symptoms. You won’t have to worry about the blood in your cough or the cough at all. Jamison, I really hope you’re listening to me,” doctor Reynolds said. Jay continued to keep his eyes closed and wondered again about the stain on the ceiling. He wondered how they would clean it and what other patients would think if they saw it. “I’m going to let you rest now, Jamison. Please do think about the treatment. It’s your life, but I’m sure you’re not the only one who cares whether you live or die.” Terror could hear his footsteps on the tiled floor and they dissipated once he reached the hallway. The click of high heels entered his peripherals and Jay tried to pretend he was sleeping. The side of his bed depressed with added weight and he felt soft familiar hands along his stubbly face. “I know you’re awake,” said a female. Jay matched the voice to the hands and took a deep breath. “Hey Mags.” Maggie McVay continued to caress his cheek. “I thought you said you were getting better. I thought the doctors told you good things.” “Good things are for people who deserve them,” Jay protested. Maggie recoiled her hand and Jay could feel her eyes looking deep into his soul. “You’re the most stubborn person I know. Even when you have everything it’s not good enough. You know you’re worth a damn. You know that there’s a good person in you. Why don’t you man up and just accept it for once.” “What do I have? A title? A career? My dad’s dead. My mom went crazy after my dad died. You’ll marry that stock trader. Face it, I’m just a ring name that will likely be retired in five years anyways.” “That title is what you’ve fought so hard for. It’s your life. It’s the one thing that’s kept you going through all the tumbles.” Jay snorted, “well it turns out that belts don’t cure cancer.” Maggie’s hand returned to his face and he could feel her embracing him again. Her touch was genuine and forgiving. “I’m sorry Jay. I didn’t mean to snap. It’s just that you have a choice. You can get treatment and get out of this mess.” Jay opened his eyes finally for a second to look at Maggie. Her hair was tied in a bun and her make-up was messy. “Treatment won’t help,” he insisted. “It’ll stop the coughing. It’ll stop the internal bleeding. You’ll be back to normal in no time.” “I’ll be dead and bald in four years still,” he said. Jay shut his eyes again as he felt his head starting to pound. “And what if you aren’t? What if you live another ten? What if you have a full life?” “I heard the prognosis. Facts are facts.” She paused and let reality soak in. “I’m sorry, Jay. I wish I could have helped you like you helped me,” she said. He could feel her hand on his and he grabbed her fingers. “So it goes, Mags.” She held his hand for another ten minutes and comforted him in silence. Jay was aware of her presence, but his head continued to ache making sleep a better option. He wanted a cigarette or something to take away the pain in his brain, but understood that he wouldn’t get one. When his grip released, Maggie stood from the bed and went to the hall again. Dreaming He had been prescribed Lortab by a doctor on staff after a show one evening. He said that most of the other guys on the roster were using it in some way. The doctor also said that it’d take the pain cycle from three days to one. Naturally, Rich Rollins wasn’t opposed. It was a fact of the sport that bodies would break down if enough stress was forced. In ten years of travelling, performing, and sacrificing, it was a matter of time before he had to turn to outside help. Lortab was an easy escape. He could take one pill and fix the most stubborn migraines. Even chair shots were fixed within twelve hours. For a long time it was a miracle drug on the roster. Rich found it considerably easy to ask a friend if he had a pill lying around in his bag. More often than not, he could get his fix without calling in a favor to the pharmacy. Sitting on a hotel bed, Jay looked through the eyes of the renegade. He could feel his fingers running across the grooves of a cap. Rich opened the bottle and poured two pills into his palm. He opened his mouth and Jay felt him swallow both with a swig of water. Jordan White had botched a high flying knee drop and nearly broke his nose in the middle of the ring. Jay could feel the bruise forming and the blood pumping. Nonetheless, Rollins laid back on his hotel bed and shut his eyes. He dreamed of title shots and reliving past glories. After defeating Jordan White, he had finally staked his claim at another world title run. Hospital Maggie had cried some more just outside the door. She had wiped her make-up well enough, but she couldn’t hide the bags that had filled under her eyes. They were thick and caught mascara in the creases. She tried to stop when she heard footsteps, but found it near impossible. Heavier footsteps came her way and Maggie noticed doctor Reynolds who had a clipboard in his hands. “Is he sleeping?” he asked kindly. “For a few minutes,” Maggie replied. “Are you a sister?” She laughed awkwardly, “No. Just a good friend.” Doctor Reynolds nodded solemnly. “Then I’m sorry for what you must be going through with him.” “He’s always been like this. Always has to have things his way and always has to be right. Sometimes I wish he’d let people like me make some decisions for him.” “It’s human nature not to. No one wants to lose control. For some people, it’s all they have. Have you helped with the cigarettes?” “Helped him smoke them?” “Helped him stop them,” the doctor corrected. Maggie shook her head slowly. “They didn’t seem to be hurting him until recently. He said that he’d had a good diagnosis too. I don’t know. I guess I got caught up in good news.” “That’s not your fault,” doctor Reynolds reassured. “The fact is that he has a tough string of this disease. Some of my colleagues would likely say that it was bound to happen considering his family’s history, but his habits didn’t help.” “He said he only had four years left,” she mentioned. The doctor shook his head in an undecided way. “Could be four or it could be six. You never know sometimes. All we can tell right now is that he has small cell lung cancer. His grandfather had the same. With small cell you find more rapid development and a tough slope to climb. The symptoms are harsh and if you don’t catch it in time then it will spread to the rest of his body like a bush fire. Luckily for him, it’s still contained in his lungs. It’s not exactly curable, but it’s treatable and will give him months or even years.” “He’ll never take treatment if it can’t cure it,” Maggie replied. “That’s where we have to help him. If we can make him understand that there’s hope and there are people that love him then maybe he’ll change his mind.” Maggie leaned against the wall feeling an added weight on her shoulders. She rolled her neck. “I’ve never known him to change his mind for someone else.” Doctor Reynolds tightened his old features and stood up straighter. “Then it’s time he starts. I know we can’t force him to do anything, but you can be around him, do things for him, and pray for him. He needs to know that people care. He needs to realize that there are people worth living for.” “How rigorous is the treatment?” “It’s chemotherapy so it won’t be easy. I said it hasn’t spread outside the lungs, but the tumor isn’t small enough for operation. He’ll become tired, sick, and he’ll lose his hair. Without the chemo though, it’s hard to say how fast the disease will spread.” “So it’s chemo or death?” Maggie asked. Doctor Reynolds nodded. “Are you up for the challenge?” Maggie turned and looked through the window at Jay. He was sleeping still. “Anything for Jay,” she replied. Dreaming A knock on the door woke Rich Rollins giving Jay Terror a glimpse at the ceiling again. His vision was fuzzy and his mind was still groggy, but another knock came. The renegade sat from the bed and moved toward the door with dizzy steps. His nose was already feeling better. Out of habit, he looked through the gold chipped peep hole before unchaining the door. Jay noticed the man as Steven Caldera. Rollins opened his room to the president of the company and stepped aside. “Wasn’t expecting you,” Rollins said. He coughed soon after. Steven Caldera nodded and looked around. Rich had tossed his bag on the floor when he returned and his tights and boots had spilled out. “I wanted to congratulate you. A win at NC-17 is big, but beating Jordan White is even better. You looked good out there,” Caldera admitted. “Just did what I had to do,” Rollins replied. “And that’s why I wanted you for The Establishment. You’re not like other wrestlers in GCW. You’re smart in the ring and you do what you have to in order to get the job done. It’s those kind of traits that you’ve always had in you. Even from the first time we started working together, I knew that you’d make yourself into one of the best wrestlers in the world.” Caldera paused and pointed. “Look at you now.” Rich accepted the compliment but looked uneasy doing so. “Where are you going with this?” Steven Caldera understood his mindset. “I wanted to talk to you about the title.” Rollins’ eyebrows lifted at the mention. “In my mind, you’ve earned your shot. Beating Jordan White was the capstone to a resurgence. Not only that but you are GCW. The things you’ve done for me in the past, the way you’ve helped GCW grow, and your past accolades alone warrant you a chance at the belt.” Jay could feel a burning sensation in Rich’s stomach before he spoke. “But...” Steven Caldera grinned. “But you’re not the only one. There’s Lia Ambrosi, LeStatt Knight, Jay Terror, and even Jordan White who have legitimate claims.” “I beat Jordan tonight. I ended his claim,” spit the renegade. “And that’s why you’ll be able to beat him again,” started Caldera. “GCW is an entertainment business and the people want to see controversy. They want to see people struggling for the greatest prize in the sport. As great as a match between you and Valentine would be, a tournament to decide the next contender would be even better. Imagine the ratings and the excitement we’d generate.” Rich firmed his brows creating an angry crease on the bridge of his nose. “I didn’t sign on with you to be thrown into tournaments. You said I’d go places. You said that I’d win the god damn title again, Steven.” Caldera placed his hand on Rich’s shoulder. Jay recognized the feeling. Rollins threw him off with a shrug. “You’ll win, Rich. You’ll walk straight through this thing and be even better than everyone already believed. You have to trust me on this.” “I’ll trust it when I see it,” Rich proclaimed. Steven Caldera was smart enough to realize the tension. He smiled at his old friend and nodded as he made his way back to the door. Before walking into the hall, he looked at Rich again. “You’ve done enough for the hall already, but imagine what another title run would do for you. I know it just got out of your reach, but if you want it enough you’ll fight to grab it before it disappears for good. There’s something special about being a world champion, and I feel like that’s worth fighting for no matter what the sacrifices may be.” Caldera walked down the hall and Rich Rollins shut the door. He returned to his bed and Jay could feel the fire still burning his insides. His head was pounding with frustration and he reached the ground for his bottle of pills again. He unscrewed the cap as he had before and poured another one into his palm. For a long while, Rich Rollins stared at the pain reliever. It was so small and insignificant, but did so much for him. His brain continued to swell and beat the sides of his skull, but Rich cupped the pill between his fingers and shut his eyes again. Jay Terror’s world disintegrated into darkness with the fleeting realization that Steven Caldera was right. Hotel Jay was discharged with the same parting promise he’d made to other doctors. Although doctor Reynolds was clever, he wasn’t convincing. The struggle that was meant to be lasted for only five minutes. After the paperwork was filled out and the lines were signed, Jay was placed in a wheelchair and pushed through the front doors of the Thorek Memorial Hospital. His driver had pulled into the circle and Maggie pushed Jay to the back door. He stepped out of his chair and Maggie disposed of it near one of the front pillars. “Feeling better?” asked his driver. “Well enough to leave I suppose,” Jay answered. He watched Maggie make her way to the car. She walked slow with heavy shoulders and watched the ground for cracks. She took the front passenger seat and shut the door behind her. “You know,” started the driver. “In a three mile range, there’s a hotel, hospital, and cemetery. I think I’d fancy dying in Chicago.” Neither Maggie nor Jay replied. Both digested the word death and kept to themselves. The hotel was within walking distance, but there was no sense in forcing tired legs across a highway median. They maneuvered an illegal right turn at Jay’s discretion and pulled into the Sheffield House Hotel. The driver slid into another circle and both Jay and Maggie unloaded from their respective doors. “You getting along OK?” Maggie asked. “It’s just my head you know,” Jay said. “I’ve been grounded by headaches,” she replied. The doors were automatic. Maggie had gone through the trouble of booking a suite and led Jay to an elevator on the opposite wall from the desk. They had room 301 and found it upstrairs in front of the elevator. Inside smelled like freshly placed pillow mints. “You should sleep,” Maggie insisted. “I’ve slept for the past two days,” Jay replied. “Besides, I need some fresh air.” “Do you have cigarettes on you?” she asked cautiously. “Always do. Might not be my luckiest box considering the fall, but they’re smokes. You want one?” “No,” she replied. “You shouldn’t either.” Jay formulated a half smile. “It’s been two days, Mags.” “A day and a half,” she corrected. “And,” she paused, unsure of what to say. “And you know what the consequences are.” Jay could see the puffy bags under her eyes shake slightly. They were full and used, but ready to consume more tears. Terror shrugged. “I’ll stop after this pack. There’s like,” he pulled open the pack, “like five left.” “Just five?” “Just five.” Maggie reached into her pocket feeling the vibrations of her phone. She slid it open and placed it to her ear flashing Jay a finger to wait. Terror had his own plans however. He shoved the sliding door’s worn golden handle open and stepped onto the balcony. There was snow on the railing and he pushed a patch aside to take a seat on the edge. There was no view. There were cars and there was the road. Nonetheless, Terror sat and stared. He could see the hospital and with it he could see doctor Reynolds. The man was frail, but he was good at what he did. It was clear that he’d helped hundreds of patients much like Jay, and it was even clearer that he hadn’t seen the same resistance Jay showed. His face was white and somber while Jay was being wheeled away as if he’d lost the fish pulling it into the boat. It reminded Jay of Earl Franks, an old man he’d met at a bar in Nashville. Even his touching battle with cancer wasn’t enough to deter Jay from lighting another cigarette. It had been four months since Jay had met him, a month later than his projected death. If all went to plan, Earl was buried and mourned by a family that appreciated their final days with him. Terror pulled a cigarette loose from the box and passed it between his fingers. The drop to the concrete below was long and Jay was careful not to waste one of his last sticks. For the longest time, they had helped him climb out of the deepest pits. With every fall, the Marlboro man had a hand ready to help. Titles had been won with the help of tobacco. Rich Rollins, Jason O’Neil, and Octavian, each hall of famers, had been beaten on nicotine buzzes. Battles with Tessa Windsor, LeStatt Knight, and Andy Murray had all been pressed into overtime thanks to his 27s and he wasn’t ready to let it go before another battle for his belt. He turned away from the hospital and looked back inside. Maggie was on the phone. She had started crying and she looked like she was apologizing. It was hard to tell. Terror turned back to the cigarette in his hand and looked at the filter. He’d always hated the filter and blamed it for taking up space. Jay looked back inside and Maggie was lying on the bed. She looked like Rich Rollins trying to force the pain out through sleep. Looking back at the cigarette, he saw a pain pill. It was a tiny Lortab dancing in his palm. It called his name, claimed it needed him. Jay listened for a second wondering what hurt. He then wondered if he needed to hurt. He wondered if Andy Murray had pain pills in his bag. He wondered if anyone still carried pain pills in their bags. He stopped wondering as the sliding glass door opened and Maggie stepped outside. “Could I get a cigarette?” she asked in a defeated manner. Jay looked back at the pill and it was a cigarette again. It sat in his palm for a second before Terror closed his fingers. Steven Caldera crossed his mind and he remembered that sometimes it was better to give up to go places. The paper ripped and its insides spilled. He opened his hand over the side of the balcony and watched the tobacco catch in the wind. It whipped up and tried to find Jay’s fingers, but couldn’t lift itself high enough. The pieces of the Marlboro 27 hit the concrete below and remained there lifeless. “Nah. You don’t need it,” he replied. Maggie smiled and placed her hand on his shoulder. They looked out at the hospital and at the road and at the cars. “What now?” she asked. “We find a plane to New York,” he answered. “And after New York?” “After New York I stop running.” They continued to stare until the sun started to set. Gold was the color of the sky and they didn’t go inside until the world went black.
