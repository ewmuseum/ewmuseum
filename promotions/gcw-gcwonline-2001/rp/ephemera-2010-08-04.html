Ephemera

4 August 2010

Date: 08/04/10 Originally posted at http://prime.e-wrestling.org - 25 May 2010 He looked her up and down, taking his sweet time. Her hair was tied loosely behind her head. She wore grey joggers, and a navy hoodie, half unzipped. The logo on her t-shirt read, "Ceci n’est pas un logo." Her breasts looked smaller. "Ya look better on TV," he informed her, with the disdain of someone whose CV boasts a raft of computer programming qualifications. Roxie Sykes was too tired to gape. "Come on, let’s gettuh done," popular blogger Tristan McWinkle sighed, inclining his head towards the open doorway. His voice was high, jumpy, nasal; the sound of a rabbit too fat for the door of its hutch. "Uh. This is the place?" The hot wind off the Pacific Coast sand blew persistently around the feet of the exterior staircase on whose platform she perched. The side of the building was claimed by spray-can spaghetti of all shapes and sizes. "I was totally expecting a... well. A studio." "Listen, missy," McWinkle retorted, taking offense at her roving eyes, inattentive to his hypocrisy. "This is the twenty-first century. The revolution will not be televised, you know? It’ll be blogged." His beady eyes lit up, two bloodshot bulges in his featureless marshmallow face. "Totally," Roxie nodded in affirmation, and squeezed through the doorframe, McWinkle’s body odour only transitional as the scent of inside rocked her nasal passages. She closed her eyes to protect them. The door slammed loudly behind her. "Fire door," he spat at her as he bustled past, as if to excuse the noise. It wasn’t the noise that was bothering her. "This way." "Um," she began as she followed. McWinkle’s hacking laugh halted her. "I know. I know," he acknowledged, shifting his bulk to face her even as he continued traversing the darkened corridor, so that he was now walking backwards. "We don’t gedda lotta fresh air in here, you know?" "I know," Roxie mumbled miserably, wondering what she had done to deserve this. * * * "Get many viewers?" Roxie asked conversationally as they waited for someone, who may or may not have been called Snooze, to get the webcam from Studio B, which may or may not have been the linen closet. McWinkle shot her a suspicious glare over the top of his monitor. "Downloads? We average a couple thou on the day it goes up. Hunnuhd or two the day after. After that it tails off. This is the innuhnet, missy. Lotta competition, you know? Ephemeral, they call it. E-phe-me-ral." Roxie said nothing. She had grown somewhat accustomed to the smell, which both placated and worried her. The room was tiny, and all four walls were broken up by cheaply cut shelving, hammered haphazardly into the plaster, stacked with dust-encrusted videos and unlabelled ring binders. "Fight the power!", proclaimed a sticker on the fuse box. She liked that. "Yeah, set it down on toppa the cabinet," McWinkle ordered. A reedy, almost ghost-like boy had floated into the room, webcam grasped between two bony hands. "Then get me a coffee." Roxie cleared her throat. McWinkle turned to her, maybe contemptuously. Through his pudgy features, she couldn’t really tell. "You wanna coffee too? Snooze, get her a coffee." "A water, actually," she addressed the thin boy apologetically. He made no response, disappearing as quietly as he had come. Roxie turned back to her host. "Why Snooze?" That hacking laugh again. "You don’t wanna know." Roxie considered this. "No, really," she countered. "Why Snooze?" McWinkle shrugged his huge shoulders, straining his highest buttonhole. "Alright, missy, don’t sayuh didn’t warn ya. You know porn?" The question was so abrupt that Roxie was taken aback. Seeing what he took for shock on her face, McWinkle tutted and wiped his sausage-like fingers across his forehead. Roxie was no prude, and though she had nothing to prove to this man, she still didn’t want him to think her one. She endeavoured to show him what she was really about. "Yeah, course I know porn. Multibillion dollar industry founded on the exploitation and objectification of women. Who the fuck doesn’t know it?" Now Tristan McWinkle was the one taken aback. "I guess that’s one way to put it," he nodded studiously, and jotted something down with the stub of an aged pencil. Roxie could see the sweaty sheen he’d imprinted on the paper reflecting in the overhead light. "Ya still wanna know about Snooze?" Roxie sighed. "Sure." "Watches these naked chicks sleeping. Seriously." "They just sleep?" "Yeah. They just sleep. Well, here ‘n there they get another chick to put things on ‘em – or, uh, in ‘em. Like baby oil on ‘em. Bananas, vegetables in ‘em. Or, uh, you know. While they’re sleeping. I mean, that’s what I’ve heard." "And that’s why you call him Snooze?" "That’s why we call him Snooze." She nodded, looking away. She felt more relaxed, now. This was LA. Every guy was as weird as the next guy. Once you knew someone’s story, you were safe with them. Roxie leant back in her chair. It was a fold-out chair with an aluminum frame and a cloth back. The aluminum frame was bent at the sides from guests heavier than her. "Guess I can’t smoke?" McWinkle laughed once more, this time breaking into a genuine smile. "Fuck it, you know? Can’t make the place smell any worse." Roxie grinned, slipping a pack from her pocket. The last few Camels rattled around inside. "You?" She held out the pack, knocking the top open with her thumb. The blogger shook his head ambivalently, as if to say I’d-love-to-but. Or maybe no one ever offered before. With the white stick between her lips, Roxie realised that in this overstuffed, underventilated room she was actually quite content. Luxury living had never been her forte. The clinical hallways and immaculate buffet tables of the PRIME shows weren’t her thing. Hotel rooms with marble floors kept gleaming by back-broken immigrants, she could live without. She turned to see the jauntily angled "Fight the power!" sticker once more, and smiled. "So," McWinkle broke the silence, leafing through his appointments book, "you’re Vivica Valentine, right?" Oh, fuck this noise. * * * Maybe two of his pages had stuck together, or maybe he was just fucking with her. Roxie couldn’t be sure, because her aborted interview with the "The McWinkle Mind – What, Wrestling Fans With Brains!" fansite had told her exactly what she didn’t need to know: that nothing made her happy anymore, and when it did, it didn’t last. So she left. She flagged down a cab, flung herself into the backseat. Asked the driver about somewhere quiet for breakfast. "I know the place," he told her. He was dark-skinned, lithe. He looked too young, too agile, too well-groomed to be driving a cab. He shouldn’t be ferrying people around, she thought. He should be fucking them or fighting them. Or if he was white, he’d be selling them insurance. He dropped her at the corner of Spring and Elmyra. The place was Nick’s. It was quiet and unassuming. Nick, though, was loud, and he probably assumed a few things too. He took one look at her and got that vibe of wanting to be left alone. But he assumed, or maybe he knew, that really she could do with some company. "Take some hash browns," Roxie heard, and lowered the plastic-backed menu to reveal the beaming round face of a man who liked his food and his people, probably in that order. "Today’s are real fluffy. You like hash browns?" "I, uh." Roxie hadn’t eaten hash browns in maybe two, three years. "Sure. Hash browns." "And some eggs? Poached? Sunny-side? Boiled?" He was clicking his fingers adamantly, unaware that he’d long since attracted the attention of his longest-serving employee, a dour-faced Puerto Rican, who was not amused. "Two eggs, poached, sure," Roxie assented, smiling at the weathered waitress. "And hash browns," Nick turned to her, confirming the other part of the order. "Pick out the best, she’s had a rough morning." She click-clacked to the back without a word. The café owner turned his genial face back to Roxie’s. Although he was bald, easily in his fifties, with a head like a basketball and a face like a baseball glove, Roxie found him easy to look at. His smile was contagious, and she barely realised she was mirroring it. "You better not be hitting on me," she chided jovially, "it’s nine in the goddamn morning." He guffawed with laughter, taking the glint in her eyes as permission. "I should hope not! You’re young enough to be my daughter!" "Ah well, can’t blame a girl for trying," Roxie licked her lips devilishly, causing the old man to crease up again. "Gotta say, lady, you’re worth eight of my hash browns. Pity you’re only getting four." "Four of the best, though, right?" "I sure as hell hope so, lady. I sure as hell hope so." * * * "Guy came in here a few weeks back," Nick told her as she finished up her eggs. "Said he’d messed up his whole life. Said he wanted to travel back and make it all right." "Bet you get a lot of those," Roxie replied, swallowing her last delicious mouthful. She wondered whether a fifty-percent gratuity would be weird. "Maybe that’s true," the restaurateur said philosophically. "A product of my radiant warmth and charm." "Don’t push your luck," her eyes twinkled, "it’s still only nine-thirty." He laughed, but pushed her pleasantries aside. "No, but I’m serious, Roxie," he pressed on, having long since learnt her name (and she his). "He was young, see. Blond. Tan. Like a bunch of other guys who think they’re having those kinds of problems." "Students. Hippies." "Assholes." She snorted, fighting not to spit out her orange juice, freshly squeezed, on the house. It was the first profanity she’d heard him utter, in half an hour of machine-gun chatter. "But this guy, I saw something in him, see. Like he’s a good kid at heart. And like it really has gone wrong for him. And once in a while your heart goes out to that guy." "Sure, but it isn’t your job to sort out other folks’ problems." Nick spread his arms wide and leant back, looking to both sides in turn. "I run this place, Roxie. Everything is my job." * * * The door swung open. This wasn’t comment-worthy in itself; Nick’s was a busy place, and plenty of soccer moms and procrastinating students had passed in and out in the time that Roxie had sat over hash browns and eggs, hearing tales of lives she’d never live. What was comment-worthy was the person who passed through it. Roxie dropped her fork. It had to be him. As Nick’s voice became a thick syrup of indistinct words, she watched the new arrival, his eyes searching the room for a table. The tousled hair. The fitted polo. It was him. "Nick," she hissed, interrupting his flow for the first time since they had met, "what did you say his name was?" "Who?" He paused, momentarily confused. "Clown-looking fella on Sunset? Why do I get the feeling you weren’t listening too close to that part?" "The one who came in. The student, hippy, asshole – whatever. What was his name?" "Name?" He scrunched up his face, features bunching. "With names, see, I remember them all, but it can take me a moment... sometimes I don’t remember unless I see the face. I need to see the face sometimes." "Turn around, then," she tapped urgently at his elbow. He did. His response was immediate. "Name was Kyle Raymer." * * * They couldn’t even look at each other, it had been so long. Once it had become clear that there were unsaid things that needed saying, Nick had left them to it. But neither Roxie nor Kyle was really certain of what they were being left to. It wasn’t yet ten in the morning, but Kyle found himself calling for beer. If God wouldn’t give him grace, the bottle would. "Great shoes," Roxie mumbled. Kyle looked down. White winkle-pickers. He sighed. He took them off. He didn’t know who he was anymore. "Ro..." "No," she said sharply. "You know you don’t call me that." "Roxie, then." "Go on." "...What is there to go on about?" She flicked listlessly at her glass, counting the speckles of pulp on its inside. Thirteen. Fourteen. "I was at the hill. August fifteenth, every year." "I was there last year, Roxie." "I’d stopped going. I’d stopped caring." He reached his hand out, brushed it down her cheek, as carefully as he could. But his fingers were trembling, and he was worried he would poke her in the eye, or something, so he pulled it back. "It was difficult." "Everything is difficult." Maria, the Puerto Rican waitress, came with his beer. Normally, they didn’t serve it with breakfast. Her disapproval sat heavy in the air, like an unwanted pregnancy. "Thanks." Roxie turned to address her directly. "Would you ask Nick to come back? Please?" The waitress gave her a withering glare. "Mr. Morato is busy." "He’ll come. Just tell him. Please." * * * "Boy," Nick began, relishing the moment, "I want you to tell her everything that you told me. Everything." Kyle was already shaking his head before the café owner could finish. "I can’t. I just... can’t." "You can tell it to a total stranger, you can tell it to the love of your life." For Roxie, time stopped. Kyle looked glumly at the clock. "Can I get another beer?" * * * In the car, they didn’t say a word until they turned off 101. "You sobering up yet?" she asked indifferently. He shrugged. It was barely midday. This felt weird. "Having second thoughts?" she prodded, as if she could read his mind. "Maybe. What’s it to you." Roxie shook her head, emotion suddenly flooding to her. "It’s everything to me, Kyle. It’s everything." He parked the car. * * * They lay in each other’s arms. On the hill. Their hill. "Nothing could ever come between us, right," he mumbled. "An earthquake could never. War. An alien invasion. The goddamn economy." The slightest movement she made was amplified in his very soul. As she shrugged, he felt his heart splinter. "It’s been a long time, Kyle. A long time." "Fuck time, Roxie. Fuck time." "No. Time happens, Kyle. Time happens, and you can never take it back."
