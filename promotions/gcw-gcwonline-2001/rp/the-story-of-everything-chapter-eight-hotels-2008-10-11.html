The Story Of Everything, Chapter Eight: Hotels

11 October 2008
Content noticeA period document, served as recovered; withheld details are marked. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 10-11-08 It hadn't occurred to me, until that point, that we had been living out of Wayne's car for longer than I had been aware. We had been moving from city to city, crossing a few borders we might not have been supposed to and crossing back with seemingly no direction whatsoever. I couldn't tell. The streets everywhere had similar names, some identical, and streets didn't generally look too different from each other; maybe to other streets they did, but not to me. On top of that, I could just never figure out grid systems. I thought they were always shifting around on me, or I on them. It was like a fencing match. A grid system would attack with wobbling foil and I would parry free down an alleyway or a sufficiently foliaged side street. I had made an uncommon habit of getting banned from hotels and forgetting about it. What was it -- two or three now? There was really no telling. There was no point in telling, either. We were in Doctor Wayne's car. He had picked me up the night before, bearing thirty gallons of pure Undead Sauce. I was able to fit all the cans of boutique Jenkem in his trunk, but we had to remove an economy-sized bag of baking soda to fit it all. Thus, I had the bag in my lap, or more accurately, sandwiched between my ribs and the dashboard. The five-gallon jugs of Undead Sauce squeezed into the backseat left little room for us to stretch out in the front seats. Wayne himself was steering the car with his chin because he didn't have enough room to move his arms forward. It was quite a feat, I had to admit, that Wayne was so ably effective at space management. Granted, I found out all too late that the bag of baking soda was open, and the combined, colliding pressures of my body and the dashboard forced out baking soda in bursts over every bump and crack so that the cabin of Wayne's vehicle was clouded in powder, and white dust coated and caked his upholstery. Nevertheless, that was the least of our worries. "You're driving on the wrong side of the road," I observed. "I'm doing the best I can," said Wayne. "And I can't really talk and steer." I took this as a cue to be quiet, but he kept on chattering. "Besides, there's barely anyone out here. If I see anyone coming towards us, I'll swerve out of the way." He was haggard, ragged and ravaged by the throes of Undead Sauce and Red Zombie. His normally pristine suit was wrinkled and the shirt beneath it diagonally buttoned in places. "You got any kind of plan?" I doubted inquisitively. "Where are we headed?" "We're due south now," replied a frenzied Doctor Wayne. "And of course I have a plan." I waited for him to proceed, but it seemed like he was content to stop there. "What is it?" "I've got various contacts in the medical field that would buy the shit out of this stuff. I'm going to set up a few meetings with a few people, sample the Undead Sauce and then switch it out with Red Zombie." "Are you off your rocker?" I seriously wondered. "We can't be giving away Undead Sauce for free. I'm supposed to get this sauce for Mark David. He's probably wondering why I haven't contacted him in a while." "The dead guy? Forget about him. We're going to be living the life, buddy -- fast cars, big mansions and, my God, think of all the women." I didn't say anything. I thought about Eryn. She was dead and determinately apathetic, and I couldn't ask for anything more out of a woman. "Hey, if it makes you feel any better about it, you can try to sell your cans of shit," Wayne disingenuously comforted. I kicked at the briefcase at my feet, nervously. I hadn't yet told my friend, lawyer and doctor about the deal I had made in that building on Beech Road. "I'd feel a lot better about it if I talked to Mark David first." "Honestly, what's a dead man going to do if we dip into his stash of abandoned Undead Sauce?" I don't know if he expected me to answer or not, because I was too busy scanning the landscape for those black sedans. I figured they'd leave me alone if I kept on the job, but if they ever found out I had no idea what I was doing, they'd bury me so fast my toes would still be sticking out. "Cow." "What?" "You're going to hit a cow." Wayne looked ahead and saw the errant bovine standing in the middle of the road. The car screeched to a halt, jarring our bones against the dashboard and steering column. A burst of baking soda exploded from the open bag and powdered us both pretty good. We stopped a safe distance short of the cow, and I could've sworn that the cow gave us a dirty look before moving to graze on the highest sprigs of grass on the side of the road. "Maybe you should let me drive," I suggested as Wayne tried to readjust himself in his seat. "Do you even know how to drive?" "I'll get the hang of it." "You're not driving my car," mandated Doctor Wayne. "Then let's at least stop and stretch our limbs a little bit," I reasoned. "What do you say?" "I guess I am starting to cramp up a little bit, like, all over my body." Wayne contorted off the ignition, and we both forcibly shouldered through our doors. When we were finally emancipated from the crowded contraption, Wayne began doing these lanky stretches, and I didn't feel comfortable watching him, so I looked at the cow, and the cow was looking at us. The cow turned more towards me and started staring into my eyes. Initially, I sprang into a defensive stance, anticipating a charge from the cow, but the cow just stood there, chewing and staring into my soul. I stared back, trying to see if I could see what this cow was all about. I had never seen a cow at such a close distance. I had heard that certain cultures considered them to be sacred, and others considered them to be delicious. I had even seen in a picture once that there was a cow with a hole in its side, and you could reach in and feel a stomach or two. It was the most fascinating thing I'd ever seen. "Wayne, you're a man of science," I called out, unsure if he was still stretching or not. "Have you ever seen those cows with the holes on their sides that you could just reach in 'em?" "Nope," he grunted out in flexion. "Why?" "No reason," I dismissed. "You done stretching?" "Nope," he grunted again. "I've still got to do my shimmies. I think my lats are asleep." I groaned and started staring off down the dead road. "What's the first stop?" "I know this guy at a hospital about a couple hours or so south of here. He's an old colleague of mine," Wayne said during the final stretch of his final stretch. "All limber." "I got a map," I said, reaching into my pocket to unfold the road map I had taken from Matthais' farm. "You mind playing navigator?" "Not at all." "Ready to get back on the road then?" I nodded, and we crammed ourselves back into the car. I now had a completely unfolded map to contend with, and Wayne had a hell of a time getting the keys back into the ignition. "I think we might have to jettison something." "We were fine before." "I don't know how I managed that." "Well, there's not much in this car that I'd be willing to get rid of." Wayne and I looked around, but we both knew that neither one of us was willing to part with the Undead Sauce. "What if we cleared out the trunk?" Wayne tested these unprotected waters cautiously. "No." I responded with rivaling caution. "You don't really need that stuff, do you? Every time you use it, you come back and get us into more trouble. Save us both some pain." I wondered whether to go for the pistol holstered in my waistband, the shotgun propped up my pants leg or the briefcase clumsily at my feet. Unfortunately, all three were out of reach thanks to my entangled predicament. I'd have to resort to words. "You see this briefcase at my feet?" I kicked it around for emphasis. "I've got sixty grand in this briefcase for less than half as much Jenkem as we've got in the trunk, and there's even more than you know at the old man's farm." "Shit," Wayne responded. "Your cans can stay, but I'm serious. It's causing me problems every time you use it." "I wish I didn't have to, but I'm in too deep on both sides," I replied. "I can't stop using it now. That's not an option." "What the hell are you talking about?" Wayne couldn't understand because he hadn't used Jenkem. There was no point in trying to lay it all out for him unless he was willing to huff some with me, and I got the feeling he wasn't. "I've just got some people there I have to meet about some people here I have to meet." "Dead people?" "Yeah, don't worry about it." I said, prompting Wayne to shake his head the very acute distance it could be shaken. "Don't try and stop me, either." "Then don't do it around me. It's disgusting, man. I don't what kind of shit you've been getting yourself into, but I don't want to get dragged into anything else. I've got enough business to conduct as it is." "You're already in deep enough. Don't forget who all that Undead Sauce belongs to." "Let it go, man. As long as you stay off the Jenkem, nobody's going to touch us." "We're keeping it, right?" I didn't want him to press the issue any further with my firearms out of reach. "Until we can sell it off, but that still doesn't help us make any room," reminded Wayne. I stifled more argument, but managed to stay quiet. I managed to recognize my entrapped position, and I looked for a solution. "How attached are you to the passenger seat?" We made quick work of disassembling the passenger seat and removing it from the car. We left it on the side of the road, next to the cow. After a quick shift of the jugs of Undead Sauce, Wayne was able to push his seat back more comfortably, and I was able to find more comfort and discreteness from the car's flooring. Doctor Wayne started the car, and we were off. "We're going to have to unload this stuff at a hotel, and we're going to have to keep this room for a while," Wayne looked down at me. "Let's try not to get kicked out of this one, huh?" "Sure thing," I said, though I really couldn't recall any of my supposed prior evictions and consequential banishments. "And now that you've acquired all this newfound wealth, it's about time you started paying off the tab you've racked up," Wayne added. "You can start by covering the hotel." "How about we discuss this later?" "How about I leave you with the cow and put my passenger seat back in the car?" Wayne countered. I hated when he answered questions with questions. "You're buying me a replacement by the way." "Nonsense," I frugally rejected. "We'll come back and get it. You got a pen? I'll mark it on the map." Wayne pulled a pen out of his shirt pocket and gave it to me, looking down at me with unhidden suspicion. I marked our approximate location on the map. "That reminds me . . ." I pulled the business card out of my pocket and wrote the name Curtis Westonbrook on the back. "Curtis Westonbrook?" Wayne questioned. "He's just, um, one of my clients," I invented. "He's the head judge of my circuit." It hit me like a brick. My new boss had a name beyond his handle. Matthais knew that name. Maybe he knew more. "You know him then?" I asked, with trepidation. "I've never met him. I just know the name." "Small world," I shrugged and pocketed the card. "Hey, let me know if you see any black sedans up there." *** Now it has to be understood that I had no better friend in the world, at the time, than Doctor Wayne Gunn, attorney-at-law, and that might make it a little more confusing as to why I was aiming a gun at his chest inside our hotel room, but if I had to explain myself to myself or to anyone, our histories were at play. Wayne Gunn was neither a doctor nor a lawyer. He was an expert in forgery, and we had been running schemes ever since we'd got to talking during a random meeting on a cigarette break. He had a perfect scheme about laundry or something, and I had the wherewithal and lack of judgment to pull it off, and it escalated from there. The funny thing is, I still don't know how laundry was involved. Pretty soon, though, we were setting up games all over town. All these doctors, lawyers and other licensed professionals that Wayne had dealings with weren't really doctors, lawyers and other licensed professionals at all. They were scumbags, lowlifes, drug fiends and debt dodgers like me. Hell, I had several jobs and titles I was never qualified to perform thanks to Doctor Wayne. I ran by many names, too, to the point where I don't remember what I was supposed to be called originally. I was on the fence about shooting him all along because his forgery skills always came in handy for me one way or another. Still, I had no idea how far into which circles his services extended, but I was getting the impression that it was farther than I had ever expected. Looking back, my paranoia was foolish, and I should've probably questioned him civilly, but I heard him talking to someone on the phone, giving possible conspirators our address and room number. I knew it had to be about the name I wrote on that card. Even if he was simply arranging a meeting with one of his colleagues, I didn't want him shopping around the Undead Sauce until I had talked to Mark David. I hadn't talked to him in a while, since first finding the Undead Sauce, and I'd almost completely forgotten about my vendetta against and duty to my homunculus, John Adams. I guess I never was cut out to be a campaign manager, even if, on paper, I had seamlessly doctored credentials. "Who was that on the phone?" I poked the gun at his sternum. "Christ, Johnny! Watch where you're pointing that thing." He didn't seem to be taking me as seriously as I would have hoped. "Answer the question." "It was my colleague. He didn't want me to have to bring the stuff all the way to the hospital." "You're lying," I coldly said. I must've seemed mean when I was sober if I was, in fact, sober at all. "I need names." "His name is Doctor White," Wayne answered. "Doctor Jordan White." The name sounded familiar, but I'm sure it was purely coincidental or even so much as confabulated. It was a common name, anyway, and besides, I thought I remembered a lot of things that never actually happened. It might have sounded so familiar because it would've been the first name I would have made up if I needed to make up a name. "Does the name Andre Smiley mean anything to you?" "No," sweated Wayne. "It sounds vaguely familiar, but I can't place it." "What about Graham Nabors?" "I know I've never heard that one before," swore Doctor Wayne. "Now, Johnny, put the gun down. I'm afraid your finger's going to slip." I raised the gun and circled Wayne, making sure the aim of the barrel was somewhere around where I thought his head might be. "Tell me everything you know about Curtis Westonbrook." Wayne had his hands in the air, and he was beginning to crack and panic. The longer I played this out, the more Wayne would doubt it was all a ruse, all just another scheme of my own volition, unless, of course, my scheme paled in comparison to a much larger scheme manufactured at his hands against me. Maybe Curtis Westonbrook wasn't the head of the snake. Maybe Doctor Wayne Gunn, attorney-at-law, was the mastermind behind it all. "I told you already. He's the head judge of my circuit, and that's it. I've never met the guy. I've never even seen the guy in person. You know I try to take as few cases as I possibly can." If he was lying, he was good, and I knew he wasn't that good at lying. It was absurd to think Wayne Gunn, a benevolent doctor and distinguished attorney would orchestrate such a grand scheme against me anyway. Then there came a knock at the door. I hushed Wayne and pointed towards the door as I pressed against the wall to stay out of sight. I'd get a look at Wayne's colleague, and if something struck me funny about the guy, I'd put a hole in him. If Wayne was stacking the cards against me, I'd have had half the mind to put a hole in him, too. Wayne opened the door, and in walked a man I did not recognize. He was confused, but not completely startled to have a gun pointed at him. "That any way to greet a guest?" "I'm sorry, Doctor White." Wayne turned towards me. "Johnny, can you put the goddamn gun away?" I was confused by the sudden reversal of response, and I lowered the gun out of situational misconception. "Doctor White, this is my friend Johnny Dough, and Johnny --" "It's actually Johnny Don't, now," I corrected. "Alright, Johnny Don't. Johnny, this is Doctor White." "Doctor White's too formal. Just call me Jordan," corrected Doctor White as he extended a hand towards me. "That goes for both of you." I just stared down at his hand and put my gun back in my waistband. I kept my hand wrapped around the handle, though, in case need arose. "You're not really a doctor, are you?" I questioned as marked. "That's subjective. What is real anyway?" Doctor White pulled out a cigarette and looked around the room. "You guys mind?" He had that grinding tendency to answer questions with more questions, but I was pretty sure I knew the score. "Not at all," said Wayne, even going so far as to fumble for a lighter to set Jordan's cigarette ablaze. From there, we all had to smoke. It was the code. No smoker could let another smoker smoke alone. It was an irresistible fact of addiction. I even let go of my handgun to follow protocol, but once my cigarette was lit, I returned my hand to its post on the gun handle and spoke around the cigarette. "What field do you specialize in?" "Pulmonology," he replied straight and took a drag. He chuckled as he let it out. I didn't know what that meant or why he was laughing. I pulled the gun out of my waistband and held it at my side. "He's a great Pulmologist," Wayne defensively mispronounced. "Top doctor in the region." "You know any judges?" I shouted. "What's with all these random questions?" Jordan turned to Wayne. "I thought I came here about this new medicine you've been going on about." "Right," assured Wayne, clapping his hands together. "No more questions, Johnny. Help me fetch a jug from the car." Wayne started for the door, and I lifted the gun towards him as he put his hand on the knob. "I told you not to set anything up until I had spoken to Mark David." "Take it easy, Johnny. One meeting isn't going to matter." BANG! Wayne dropped to the ground, but I hadn't fired the gun. My head slowly turned around, and it took me a while to process this, but there was a smoking bullet hole in the wall right above my left shoulder. Wayne slowly stood himself up and dusted himself off, and I looked at Doctor Jordan White. He had already tucked the pistol back away, and he sat their grinning and smoking. I knew I should've opted for the shotgun. Never hire a boy to do a man's job. Now the sudden gusto from Wayne made sense. I looked Doctor White in the eyes. I was outmatched. "I'll just go and get the sauce myself," said Wayne as he slipped out the door. I wasn't really listening. I kept my stare at Jordan White as I secured the gun in my waistband. He tapped his fingers on the table, and even without time so inconveniently slowing down, I knew it would take Wayne longer than it should to fetch that sauce. "I've heard good things about this sauce," Jordan calmly conversed. "You tried it?" "This would all be a lot less messy if you came back another time." "I'm here now. You're here now. It'd ruin the convenience of the whole thing if I had to come back later." "I'm going to level with you. I don't know who you are, but I know you're good. I reckon I wouldn't be able to handle you if it came down to fists or firearms. I'm just in some shit right now, and it'd be better for me if you came back later." "I can't," he stated. "I got a tight schedule for a couple weeks." "Then let's do this in a couple weeks. I'll have talked to my guy by then for sure." "You gotta talk to a guy?" he asked rhetorically. "Talk to your guy. Call him up." "It's not that simple." BANG! A perfectly placed shot blast a smoldering hole in the wall behind me on the other side of my head, and again, I had missed the gun -- I had just seen the side of his unzipped jacket flutter a little bit. "Call him up." I picked up my phone and dialed [detail withheld]. Wayne answered. "You on your way back up yet?" I nodded, but the answer was irrelevant. "Get me a can of Jenkem from the trunk." Resistance. "Just do it." I hung up the phone and looked back at Jordan. He just smiled. "I don't know who you think I am, but I'm just here to buy drugs. I never came to start any static." "Then why'd you come armed?" "Shit, probably the same reason you're packin'. You never know when you're going to need to protect yourself." I nodded. He was giving me vague answers, but at least they weren't questions. "You never know who's after you these days," he finished. "And what if you did?" "Then they'd be the ones needing protection," he laughed. "I'd hunt 'em all down." "You ever heard of a guy named Graham Nabors?" "What'd your guy say?" he reverted mercurially to those bothersome questions. "I haven't talked to him.." "Who were you just on the phone with?" "Wayne. I'll call him when Wayne gets back up here." "What do you need Wayne for? Just call him." "Let's just say I left his number in Wayne's car." "Fair enough." "Alright, now what about a guy named Andre Smiley?" "What about him?" he did it again. "Have you heard of him?" I countered this time. "Wasn't he that boxer who admitted to throwing all those fights?" he turned it back on me. "How should I know?" I answered his question to the question I had answered in response to his question, which he had answered in response to my question, with a question. "You're the one that's asking all the questions." I had gotten one over on him, and I gave him a knowing grin. He grimaced, but my smirk faded when the question he had asked in response to the question I had asked in response to the question he had asked in response to the question I had asked actually sunk into my brain. "Was he that boxer who admitted to throwing all those fights?" "I think so," he shrugged. "Don't hold me to it." I thought about it. What would a fairly obscure boxer have to do with a head judge? Why was I the intermediary? None of it added up. "I think that's you," Jordan pointed out. My phone was ringing, but I didn't recognize the number. I let it ring a couple of times. "You going to answer it?" "It's not important." Another ring. "Might be your guy." "It's not his number." And another ring. "I thought you didn't know his number." "I don't know what his number is, but I know what it isn't." It rang again, and Jordan flapped the side of his unzipped jacket at me, triggering a well-learned Pavlovian suggestibility out of fear for my life. "Go on and answer it anyway." I picked up the phone and swallowed hard. I didn't respond, I just waited to hear what was on the other end. "You there, Johnny?" "Who is this?" "I'm a friend of Your Honor. He asked me to check up on Andre Smiley." "The boxer?" "I didn't know you followed boxing, Johnny, but that's not what Your Honor meant when he asked me to check up on Andre Smiley." "I'm not sure I'm following." "You have until midnight tomorrow. If you haven't gotten it tied up by then, we'll be sending someone to take care of you." I gulped and looked at Jordan Snow, who was just enjoying another freshly lit cigarette. "How do I know you haven't already sent someone?" "If we had, you wouldn't be able to be talking to me right now. Midnight tomorrow." The line went dead, and I looked at Jordan, who finally turned back to me after having done his best to try not to overhear anything. "That your guy?" "No." "Drag," Jordan released, and he trailed away watching patterns in the smoke. "We'll, I guess I'll just wait, then, until Wayne gets back. Where is he, anyway?" He should've been up by now. He should've already been up a long time ago. Visiting Mark David wasn't exactly a high priority. I needed to see Matthais. There was no way I was getting out of this one without his information. Then I heard muffled sirens penetrate the walls. "Shit," said Jordan, snuffing his cigarette. The door burst open, and Wayne was hurriedly dragging the sauce. "We've got company!" yelled Wayne. "You think they're after us?" "I didn't hear gunshots in any of the other rooms," Wayne smarmily aggressed. "This is no time for sarcasm," I reprimanded. "Did you bring the Jenkem?" Wayne held up a can and tossed it to me. Ocean Flush. "We've got to split," Jordan said, opening up the window and climbing out onto the fire escape. "Bring the sauce." Wayne grabbed the sauce, and I grabbed my briefcase. We were out of there with perfect timing. As soon as our feet hit the pavement, I could hear the cops talking in the room, but the only thing that hung around was the smoke. *** We were miles away by now, and we managed to fit all three of us in the car without losing any merchandise because I volunteered to ride in the trunk. I was swimming in cans and darkness, and the car was veering all over the road. I could hear them talking in the cabin, but I couldn't make out a word of it. I just knew I needed to get out of the trunk with as much Jenkem as I could carry with me. I reached around the trunk, pushing cans aside and felt for the trunk release as the car sped forward. It wasn't too hard to find, but once I had it open, I didn't know quite what to do. It just popped up, and the doctors had to have noticed. I had to act fast, so I scooped a bunch of cans and tossed them out onto the road, which moved beneath the car at a frightening pace. I stood up, holding my briefcase by the handle while clutching as many cans to my chest as I could, and I jumped. I landed hard on the asphalt, and cans shot out every which way, but I knew I needed to dust myself off, gather the cans and get as far away from the road as possible. It was always the damned roads, from the beginning. I knew that if Wayne didn't turn around, the cops wouldn't be too far behind. They probably had an ID on the vehicle and could have easily been hot on our trail. I got up and stuffed my pockets with cans. I tucked my shirt in and started stuffing them in there, and what I couldn't fit, I balanced on the briefcase. I ran perpendicular to the road into a field, hands holding the sides of the briefcase steady as I sandwiched the makeshift pyramid of cans down with my chin. I left no can behind. As soon as I was sure that I was at a safe distance from the road, I collapsed, letting the cans scatter around me. I lit a cigarette on my back in the grass and grabbed at a can that was within an arm's reach. "Huh," I eyed the label on the bottom. "Another Ocean Flush. What better place for it than in the middle of a field?" I felt sound with my backwards reasoning and smashed in the lid of the can with the butt of my handgun. I had made up my mind that if I only had a day or two before Westonbrook's security came after me, I'd have to look for Matthais. He was my only hope. But as I breathed in the Jenkem, I realized that maybe the only hope was no hope at all. "Shit, Johnny, you look like you just slammed your face into the ground," she said. "Maybe I did." "You got an extra cigarette for me?" I pulled another one out of the pack and handed it to Eryn. She lit the cigarette and lied down in the grass next to me. "What's it like?" I asked. "What's what like?" "Being dead, I guess." "I don't want to talk about it," Eryn said, and I felt the concern in her voice. "I'm in some shit, Eryn. I'm in some really deep shit. I was thinking I could maybe we could finally be a family. It'd just be the three of us: you, me and Lennon." Eryn turned towards me and put her hand on my cheek to keep me from turning away. It made me uncomfortable to look into her eyes, to see myself looking the way that I looked in the reflections. "You don't want to do that, Johnny." "I still haven't seen Lennon, you know?" "I'm just saying," she just said. "There's always a better way." "I don't know, Eryn. You have no idea what I've gotten myself into. I don't even know what I've gotten myself into. How can I get out if I don't know what's in and what's not?" I shook my head free and looked up at the sky for a while. I couldn't see any stars. "It's all black," Eryn broke. "I know," I agreed. "I figured we'd be able to see some stars all the way out here." "No," Eryn was frowning. "Death. It's all nothing." "I don't understand," I disbelievingly let out something akin to a laugh. "You're here now. I'm here now." I sat up and folded my arms on the points of my bent knees. "I wish it didn't have to be like this, Johnny, but I have to tell you because you need to know now." I didn't like the way I looked in the look in your eyes at all. "None of this is real. As real as it seems to you, it's nothing to me. There is no me. There is no Lennon. It's just you, Johnny." "You made up Lennon?" I stood up, fumes burning, feeling betrayed. "You made up Lennon!" she fought back. I paced around, and Eryn stood up and circled backwards in front of me. "Do you want to know why you've never seen Lennon? You don't know what he looks like! You're imagining me, Johnny. I'm just some dead girl you remember from some party that you're imagining because you've been huffing shit!" "You're just laying this on me now?" "You're laying it on yourself! I'm just here to tell you what you need to hear when you need to hear it because you can't do it yourself when you're sober." "I don't have a son?" I was still trying to wrap my head around this. I couldn't say that part of me didn't feel relieved, but the rest of me was confused and, in turn, angry/ "You never had a son, and going on about it isn't going to help you any. You've got to either turn and face what's been chasing you or run like hell, and talking to imaginary dead people isn't going to help." "Are you breaking up with me?" "I'm just telling you what you needed to hear from someone else. Otherwise, you wouldn't believe it." "I still don't believe it." I sat back down and looked off in the distance. I could still see headlights miles down, dotting across the horizon. "It can't be proven either way." "I know. That's why you have to just believe me." "You said you're telling me all this because I need to know right now," I narrated. "I won't always need to know this, right? I can forget it when I don't need to know it anymore. I'm good at forgetting." "If you look at it like that, then you don't really believe me." I really had no response, and I was content with that. "It's all black, Johnny. It's all nothing." Eryn kissed me on the cheek, and I think I knew how Jesus felt. Then she got up and started to walk away. "Wait! Where are you going?" "I'm leaving," she turned. "Goodbye, Johnny." She walked off, and something inside me told me not to go after her. Maybe I believed her or myself or whoever.
