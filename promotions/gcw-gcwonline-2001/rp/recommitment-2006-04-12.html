Recommitment

12 April 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 4-12-06 Trudging slowly through the rain-soaked streets of downtown Columbus, Ohio, Kimbusa let out a sigh of boredom. Pulling the brim of his fedora further over his face, the 500-pound sumo pushed through the stiff wind and turned a corner. Caldera hadn’t given him much to do since his entry into GCW, and today was no different. “Sit tight, I’ve got plans for us this Thursday,” he’d say dismissively, urging Kimbusa to explore Columbus a bit while he waited for Caldera to formulate whatever diabolical master plan he’d come up with that week. In truth, Kimbusa didn’t mind the hours spent wandering aimlessly through the rain. He felt altogether deflated after falling short in yet another GTT tournament, coming painfully close to that big victory but failing once again. It seemed no matter how determined he was, he could never quite break through and get those last few victories. Caldera weakly suggested that it had to do with his stamina, that he was at a significant disadvantage in such a massive tournament-style event against smaller, more durable opponents. After all, Kimbusa had lost to a man one-eighth his size in Seymour Almasy, a man who would normally stand no chance against the massive former Yakuza. But Kimbusa knew better; despite his exterior, Kimbusa was in phenomenal condition, and he had finally decided that it was not a physical shortcoming that had lead to his defeat. No, Kimbusa was emotionally drained. Despite his extraordinary physical gifts, Kimbusa had always been a lost soul, uninspired and lonely in every facet of his life. Sumo had given him respect, but the people he was surrounded with treated him like a commodity. The Yakuza was worse, and despite his ever-increasing fame and power on the streets, he was regarded with fear everywhere he went, and it devastated him. And in professional wrestling, he’d been manipulated and used more times than he could count, to the point that he was uncertain he could trust anyone in the business. In truth, Kimbusa had been happiest in exile in the Japanese countryside, but even then he found himself longing for something more. Three years ago he had disappeared from the United States and gone into hiding in the hills of Japan, retiring to a small fishing village a safe distance from anyone who might recognize him. But before long he felt empty again; after so many years of greatness, hollow though they may have been, he could not be satisfied letting his tremendous abilities be wasted in isolation. He still longed for someone, something, to inspire him, to challenge him and to drive him to something greater than he’d been. But still wrestling was failing him. Despite a pedigree that would terrify the casual onlooker, and physical abilities that were unparalleled in American professional wrestling, Kimbusa was floundering. He’d been relegated to guard duty for Caldera, though he hadn’t minded at first. But now, as a warm, wet blast of wind smacked him in the face, Kimbusa felt the overwhelming desire to do something more. To realize his potential, regardless of what he’d have to do to get there. To show the world that he was capable of unprecedented feats, that he was once the most feared man in wrestling for a reason. And it would all start this week with Ehren Moore. Why Caldera had given him such a match had bewildered him, and at first Kimbusa had actually contemplated seeking other employment opportunities. But then he realized that Caldera had read Kimbusa’s own frustration before he’d realized it himself. Moore would be a stepping stone to finding his mean streak once again, the catalyst for what would ultimately be Kimbusa’s greatest (and final) act. For retirement, isolation, is what he desired most of all. Beyond all else, Kimbusa desired to be left alone. He didn’t care for the lights, for the fans, for the fame and the notoriety. But these were mere annoyances in the way of his ultimate goal; to leave his mark, to do something absolutely extraordinary, something that would be written about and talked about and sought after for years to come, so that he might be able to slip away into the countryside secure in the knowledge that he would always be remembered. Kimbusa would be remembered; remembered as the most vile, repugnant sumo in the history of Japan; remembered as the most evil and sadistic enforcer the Yakuza had ever seen; remembered as the man who had terrorized the most violent mobsters in the world and yet could not out-wrestle a 180-pound child. It would take a dramatic, sweeping act of dominance to erase these terrible memories and replace them with something worthwhile. Kimbusa wanted nothing more than to be remembered as a professional wrestler, and one of the best to ever come to America. But it would take a new commitment to himself and his craft to make it happen. And it would all start with Ehren Moore.
