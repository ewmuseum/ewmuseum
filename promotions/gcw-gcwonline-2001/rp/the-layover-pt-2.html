Date: Several weeks ago. ---- Several weeks ago. Bedford, England. Late afternoon. ---- It was a perfect early-spring afternoon. Although there somewhat of a chill in the air, (one which necessitated an overcoat), a more mild and tranquil day could not be hoped for: bright, clear, and not a trace of wind in the air. The frail early-spring sun would occasionally enjoy a moment of vigour, and carress the back of one's neck in soothing fashion, the warmth feeling all the more wonderful given the crispness of the weather. The first brave leaves of the season had dared to begin to sprout from the branches of the trees, the first signs of life after the barren winter months. Thom and Harry LeStrange stood on the frosty grass of the lawn of Bedford School. Before them stood the impressive north facade of the main building; behind them the first-team cricket pitch, rugby pitch, and the Pavillion. The main building was a beautiful work of architecture, an impressive and grandiose edifice. Despite the fact that it had been gutted by an arson attack in 1979, the masons in charge of re-constructing the iconic building had succeeded in retaining its old-fashioned appearance. The school had not changed since Thom's schoolboy days. He regarded the main building with fondness. It was immediately distinctive as the icon of the school; built in red brick in a subtle and refined neo-gothic style, with great stone arched windows and a spire which became the school's symbol, it was an impressive vista for visitors. Ivy clambered up the walls of the ground floor, mirroring the verdancy of the school estate with its numerous lawns, flower beds and playing fields, and producing a juxtaposition of colour with the red-brick. Thom remembered fondly his final year at Bedford School, during which he would often stride about the school estate, resplendant in the brown shoes, brass buttons and waistcoat he was permitted to wear as Head Boy, taking in the beauty of the School's architecture with a sense of nostalgia to come, that it would be these tranquil and inspiring surroundings that he would miss after he left. Behind Thom and Harry stood the school's 1st team rugby pitch, expertly kept. Both men had experienced great success on this self-same pitch; Thom at inside centre, where he had been a fast and strong runner, making blazing runs and leaving would-be tacklers strewn about the place, and had also been a fierce tackler; Harry at second row, where he had been a stalwart jumper in the lineout and dogged in the ruck. Both still had their sports colours blazers in their wardrobes. Off to their left stood the Chapel, its modest, conservative Victorian exterior belying its eye-catching interior; an exquisite organ loft, beautifully carved wooden choir stalls and an elegant painted ceiling. As a younger boy, Thom had been a chorister in a school choir that had been very well regarded throughout England. During his time there, the choir had sung mass at Durham Cathedral, Westminster Abbey, toured various churches in Norway, and had sung evensong in Notre Dame, Paris. However, a combination of time constraints, his voice breaking, and the usual adolescent lapse into agnosticism, Thom had left the choir. He often found himself regretting this decision and missing being able to participate in the performance of wonderful choral works. Thom and Harry had been standing silently, considering each of the buildings around them and nostalgising, for almost twenty minutes. Thom sighed and turned to face his father, who shook is head slightly as if coming to from a reverie. They smiled briefly at each other and moved off, an almost telepathic agreement that they were both finished that needed no words. They had no particular business at the school; they had arranged no meeting and had not intended to visit any old acquaintance, but merely wished to see if the old place had changed much, since they hadn't visited in years. A brief trip down memory lane, and nothing more. *** Thom drained the last dregs of his pint of Guinness and placed the pint glass back on the table before him. Opposite him, his father did the same thing, smacking his lips and letting out a refreshed "Ahh". They were seated in The Banker's Draft, a large pub on Bedford's High Street. The ground floor was a large, bright room, with a glass door at the rear that allowed access to the beer garden. During his final year of school Thom and his friends would often sneak to the Banker's Draft at lunchtime, sit in the secluded, peaceful beer garden and enjoy a cheeky pint of beer or a cigarette before returning for afternoon lessons. On a wall bracket behind them, a large plasma television played one of the day's football matches, the London derby between Arsenal and Tottenham Hotspur. The commentary provided a murmuring backdrop for their own conversation. "So, uh, son... How goes this wrestling lark?" Harry's voice sounded almost strangled, and the words came out slowly and awkwardly, desperately attempting to bridge the gap between the two of them. His son was older now, he wasn't a child any more. The formality was easier to maintain then. Now they weren't really father and son, but acquaintances. It was now necessary to speak man-to-man, to redress the balance. Thom smiled, appreciating his father's attempt at honest conversation. "It's fun, father. I keep active, I pummel cretins, I get to travel, I've little responsibility, and I can get away with bloody murder, and the followers lap it up. And, what's more, I get handsomely rewarded for doing so. Essentially, I'm the aristocracy, but popular with the masses." Harry nodded slowly, a slight wrinkle in his brow. Thom could almost see him digesting the information, his eyes narrowing ever-so-slightly as he scrutinised the facts his son had laid before him. He had asked his son the same questions a thousand times over, each time worded slightly differently, or with a new slant. He couldn't quite understand why he felt the impulse to keep questioning Thom's career choice. Were he to attempt to, it would most likely boil down to a simple conflict between his head, his upbringing and his high-society acquaintances, who demanded him to be repulsed by such common, base circumstances into which his son had become embroiled, and his heart, which rather agreed (and further, envied) his son. "You know, people whisper behind our backs now, Thomas. Whenever your mother and I attend a gala dinner or a function, we're greeted in exactly the same way as usual, but the eyes sparkle a little more irreverantly, the smile is false, and the handshake perfunctory. And whenever we leave a room we can practically hear the hissing of chitter-chatter and idle gossip." "And so what? Let them gossip. Does it affect you in any way? You depend on these people for nothing except diversion. You don't need their acquaintance for business, it in no way affects your stature, and since when have you been so coy and self-conscious?!" "Careful, son. I hope this whole affair hasn't filled you with too brash a sense of bravado. Whoever you might be in the New World, I'm your father." "I'm sorry. But consider it this way; I'm a professional sportsman. It's no different than had I become a rugby player. It's merely a different sport. Let's leave it at that." There was a long pause. In the silence, Harry nodded, accepting this new perspective on the whole situation. At the end of the day, he mused, what did it matter? Any child born into the LeStrange family was destined simply to move from the cot to the crematorium in one smooth, drawn-out, comfortable ride. With a family fortune that would sustain an African country for a decade, any effort in life was ultimately pointless for a LeStrange: all they could want or require was already in place. At least the boy had found a way to amuse himself, to seek out new experiences. He even happy. The dastard. The pair rose to their feet and exited the pub in order to walk down to the river embankment, where they had arranged for the family driver to collect them and take them back to Biddenham Manor. As they strolled, they conversed further. "So, son, how does it feel being back in Bedford after such a while?" "Rather ambivalent, if I'm honest father. I must admit, there's nothing else comparable to the feeling of returning to the place I grew up in, the place I call home. There's a certain... comfort to it, it eases the mind. But, the more time I spend here, the more I begin to realise again the more... unsavoury aspects of the town." "I know what you mean. Every time I set foot in town I'm disgusted by those around me. What's become of society, eh? I walk surrounded by legions of gormless looking folk. An army of layabouts and sponges, wearing cheap cotton tracksuits, all sharing the same glazed, vacant expression. The sort of people daytime television is made for, who don't even bother attempting to find meaningful employment, but just sit in their two-bedroom flats waiting for their social security cheque to come in and wallowing in their own mediocrity. And it makes ME feel like an oddity, ME!" "I'll tell you what really irks me, father. No, not just irks, even scares me. Young girls of less than twelve years of age wearing short skirts and make-up and huge hoop earrings. They've not even hit puberty, and they're already whoring themselves out. I don't know if that's what angers me or the parents that allow it. The papers moan themselves blue in the face about England having the highest rate of teeange pregnancies, and I'm not surprised, they way they traipse around looking like cheap tarts. There's something incredibly disturbing about it..." "Yes, yes, and the young men I see swaggering about the place, with their trousers around their knees, with ridiculous patterns shaved into their hair and lines shaved into their eyebrows, spitting on the pavement as if they were in a saloon. And the way if you make eye contact with them for more than a split-second they turn it into some kind of confrontation. Christ, I'd like to knock them to the ground with a blow the jaw and then ask them if they can remember the names of all their illegitimate bastard children..." "Absolutely right, father, and what about the repulsive excuses for feminity that they associate with? You can't walk ten yards in this town without seeing a pasty-faced wench with sallow skin, and hair scraped back so tightly it makes her look permanently startled, smoking a cigarette and blowing the smoke into the face of the poor child she's clutching with the other hand." "And yet, this has been our family home for hundreds of years. The town has a wonderful history, it's mentioned in the Domesday Book, and it's been the home town of salt-of-the-earth families such as ours for hundreds of years. And it's all instantly effaced by the riffraff and the plebs." "It's so disappointing to feel such mixed emotions towards one's home, father. To loathe every aspect of a place you love so dearly..." There was a silence, as the two met each other's gaze. Realising that they had finally spanned a conversational bridge and discovered identical sentiments towards Bedford and towards society, they smiled at the first non-formal interaction they had shared since Thom's return. "It's been good having you home, Thomas, even so fleetingly."
