The Misanthropist

Date: Present Day ---- Thom LeStrange's hotel room. Oklahoma City, OK. Evening. ---- On the balcony of his exquisite hotel room, Thom LeStrange stood, the cityscape spread before before him. Elbows on the rail, hunched over, with his strong jaw resting on one of his fists, he looked very much like an upright version of Auguste Rodin's statue "The Thinker". Most, in this position, would consider the view before them, marvelling in its modern beauty and appreciating not only the tranquility but the modest sense of detachment from the activity of city life. Some might even venture as far as calling it a sanctuary. But of course, Thomas James LeStrange is not "most people", or at least, that was the message repeated to him ad infinitum throughout his upbringing. He liked to believe it was true. Fortune and heritage aside, he felt like an outsider in the world. No one seemed to have any common decency any more. The cities of the world were getting larger and larger; the world was rapidly becoming overpopulated - indeed, he had heard it said that if the population of China walked past you in a single file line, it would never end due to the rate of reproduction. And yet somehow the human being seemed more isolated, more cut-off than ever. As much as Thom held vulgarity, courseness and ignorance in contempt, at least they were the product of class, and couldn't be avoided. What he couldn't stand was the ego-centricity, the lack of respect, the falseness, the pretentiousness of those who should know better. What ever had happened to honesty, to conviviality? And as for this godforsaken view... How on earth could concrete, steel, glass and neon be considered aesthetic beauty? Anyone who claimed that it was a modernist sort of beauty, a synthesis of industriality and architecture which represented the modern age was a nincompoop. The cities of the world these days were an abomination, and every time Thom thought of the Mausoleum of Halicarnassus, or the Temple of Zeus at Olympia, reduced to ruins and rubble, he wanted to weep. How could so many tourists flock to such an eyesore as the Eiffel tower, that ugly, iron phallus, when the beauty of the gardens of the Alhambra in Granada remained untouched? For Christ's sake, Guy de Maupassant ate at the Eiffel tower's restaurant every day because he claimed it was the only place in Paris where you couldn't see the tower itself. People were fools, Thom concluded, but much more than that, they were ignorant, and this was the great shame. If only they knew. He felt hundreds of years out of his time. The modern world was so alien to him. He felt like the survivor of some great Golden Age of civilisation forced to see it decline and crumble. But he didn't even have the benefit of nostalgia, for the Golden Age was not his own either. He was an outcast from both past and present. People always asked him sarcastically whether it was lonely up in his ivory tower. He would reply in the affirmative, but always with a touch of truth hidden in the reply. In a world of numbing mundanity, it was difficult to be such a well-rounded, unequaled Renaissance Man. But he couldn't deny that he didn't enjoy feeling superior, however. He could criticise and carp as much as he liked; he could lament his abject despair at the state of civilisation until he was blue in the face, but truth was, he needed the status quo. Quite apart from feeding his narcissistic streak, keeping the ego monster sated until next it became hungry, he found a perverse pleasure in vitriol. It was almost cathartic. And as much as he'd always loved (and excelled at) athletic endeavours, the truth was an intellectual victory was far more invigorating than a physical one. And in all the times he'd been in his own environment, as close to his own people, he'd never been truly happy. Life was mundane, and nothing separated him. Sure, not everyone strolled into Cambridge, played Blues rugby and sauntered out with the trifling bonus of a first-class degree in Modern Languages... But people were a lot closer to his own level of excellence than he cared for. The fact was, he was in his element when he was out of it. As much as he hated bourgeous society, as much as he loathed insipid mediocrity, it was here he was most himself, where he was most sure of his own identity. And thus we come to the crux of what it is to be a misanthropist. His great hypocrisy is dependence on the very thing he abhors. Moliere knew his stuff, you know.
