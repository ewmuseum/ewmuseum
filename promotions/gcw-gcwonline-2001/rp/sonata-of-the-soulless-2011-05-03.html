Sonata of the Soulless

Date: 05/03/11 Gravel and dirt popped and crunched under the tires of the powder blue '62 Buick LeSabre as it pulled up to park along the curb. Wafts of dust kicked up behind the car and stirred in the air, creating a haze reminiscent of sepia tone and muddying the late afternoon sunlight. The day’s dry heat had receded to a comfortable warmth, the midsummer dusk refracting into a perfect, nurturing sensation, covering the mind’s eye in a sticky sweetness that seemed to ensure it would preserve itself in amber memory like a prehistoric insect. All to be dug up and examined when the time was right. He took care with his movements, making sure every action was deliberate. The gear shift went to park, then the foot came off the brake, then the ignition was cut. Riley Fitzwillis had made a habit of being mindful of every little action he took, and he testified that it was why he rarely forgot anything in his old age. With most of his friends beyond senility or death, the world had gotten itself into such a hurry that the majority of his living acquaintances forgot what they had done as soon as they did it. Riley firmly believed that was the cause of the modern world’s recklessness. Somewhere in the last twenty years humanity had lost much of its soul, and they’d forgotten who they had sold it to, all for the sake of speed and the deification of youth. He did not intend to fall in step with that mentality. After sitting for a moment, listening to the engine tick tick tick as it cooled, Riley slowly smiled and enjoyed a deep breath before looking across the bench seat to his grandson. Just upon the crest of the age of reason, young Johnathan appeared to be using all of his concentration to look like he was not thinking about anything. Staring out the window, surveying the small town street with untrained, un-filtering eyes, Johnny seemed to take the world in without a moment’s hesitation. If Riley hadn’t known better, he would believe his grandson’s mind to be a blank, carefree slate. Of course, he made it his life’s business to know better. "Come on," Riley carefully parted the silence by opening his door, "Let’s get some ice cream, shall we?" Young Johnny nodded and ventured into the world outside the little metal bubble. Exiting the vehicle took Riley considerably more effort than it used to, his old joints worn down from a life he was very proud of. Two wars, thirty years in a steel plant, and the constant battle of labor unionizing had turned a once strong body into a sore, tired shell. What made it worth the while was that Riley Fitzwillis had built more than a few things that would outlast him, that he was leaving the world more than a headstone, and he would not trade any of that for all the comfort he could receive. Looking for his grandson, Riley took notice of Johnny staring at something across the street. Squinting his aged eyes, he could make out a Jack Russell terrier rolling in the grass, pausing every couple of seconds to survey the world, then returning to its joy. At first Riley assumed that Johnny wanted to go play with the dog, or maybe that he wanted a puppy of his own, until he noticed the incredibly small, almost invisible curl in Johnny’s lip. The smile would have been nonexistent were it not coupled with the boy’s relaxed stance and unfocused eyes. Riley knew Johnny did not want to have a dog, but rather he envied it. "Not a care in the world, eh?" Johnny jerked back to reality as his grandfather’s hand touched his shoulder, scared for a split second because he forgot where he was. Riley pretended not to notice Johnny’s hesitation to answer as he continued his observation, "As long as he has his space, that little bit of lawn right there, he doesn’t care much about anything that happens anywhere else." "Yeah," Johnny finally turned to look back at the mangy pup, "I don’t think he gets worried about the same things I get worried about." A small snort mimicked a derisive laugh as Riley nodded his head, "That may be true," he spoke with reassurance and careful, steady support, "But you have to remember that he will never achieve anything. He will never build character from hard work, or strive to be any greater than he is." "That’s because he is only a dog, Grandpa," little Johnny never took his eyes off the pooch, who was concentrating all his energy on rubbing his back against the dirt, itching a sweet spot in the warm summer twilight. "Exactly," very gently, Riley used his hand to guide Johnny into turning around and looking him in the eye, "He is a dog. A great many people spend their days wishing to be like dogs, or cats, or other creatures that do not have thoughts of greatness, dedication, or hard work. A lot of people want only to distance themselves from their worries, want to pretend their regrets don’t exist, and try very hard to avoid pain and discomfort at all costs. You can’t let yourself become that." A rough hand that had seen a lifetime of brutality carefully smoothed the boy’s hair, then eased Johnny into walking toward the ice cream stand, turning away from the grass-rolling terrier as though it were temptation itself. A few people passing by solemnly greeted Riley and his grandson, walking on eggshells upon seeing the two of them. In a town of only a thousand people, every resident knew everyone else by first name, and the pain of loss was shared by all. Johnny’s eyes did not leave the ground in front of him as his grandfather did his best to say his hello’s with a thankful, strong reception. A nest of old iron bells rang with a melancholy sound as the door to the ice cream stand opened. Patrons and employees alike seemed to halt their conversations and eyed the Fitzwillis’ carefully with warm, sympathetic smiles on their faces. Riley approached the counter without missing a beat. "A fine evenin’, Joe." "Evening, Mister Fitz," the shopkeep stopped cleaning a glass and threw the towel onto his shoulder, "And good evening to you, Sport." Most of the town knew young Johnathan Fitzwillis to be an eager participant in any conversation or greeting, and it was rather disquieting that he remained so timid. A meek smile and a small nod of the head was all the shopkeep could muster from him, and Riley made a point to cut short the awkward silence. "I’m going to take one scoop of vanilla, please," Riley began to fish around in the pocket of his slacks, pulling out a wallet that was thick with receipts and worn with years of use. "Alrighty, the usual for you. And how about you, big guy?" he leaned on the counter and looked at Johnny with soft eyes. It took the child a moment to find his voice, feeling like every person in the establishment was holding their breath, waiting to hear what he had to say. After a moment’s hesitation, he finally croaked, "Chocolate, please." Holding a crisp dollar bill to Joe, Riley was met with a smile and the shaking of a head. Joe did not want to get in to the reasons why he would refuse payment, but Riley Fitzwillis had never been comfortable with gifts. Not even in times of loss. He left the dollar on the counter, then moved slowly outside to join his grandson at an ancient picnic table. The slightest warm breeze somehow gave him encouragement to discuss a grave matter with the boy. "John," it took a moment for Johnny to register that his grandfather was speaking to him, as almost no one referred to him with one syllable, "I hope you know that it isn’t your fault she died." The child stared at the setting sun, slowly nodded his head, then licked his chocolate ice cream, but said nothing. "You know it’s not your father’s fault either. Or mine." "It was the other driver’s fault," the words hung in the air without emotion. As far as the boy was concerned, it was a fact as plain as the grass was green. "No. It wasn’t," Riley’s own ice cream was melting, dripping a bit onto the ground. At his age, and with the current situation, he did not even notice and would not have cared if he did. He waited for a second, to see if Johnny would refute him, before continuing, "The police report said it was a problem with the truck’s steering system. There was nothing the driver could do." "Then it was the fault of the man that made the truck." Sarah Fitzwillis had been dead a week and buried the day before, and through all the pain of loss Riley had been a stable anchor for his family when they needed it most. Now for the first time Riley showed his hand. He was shaken, afraid for his grandchild when it seemed no one else was. Johnny’s father insisted that the boy was fine, that he was just stoic, and that he would deal with losing his mother on his own. While Riley was confident in his son’s abilities as a man, he had not served in the military. He had not build part of this country with his bare hands. He had not lost loved ones in such a terrible way until now, and Riley was certain that his son was numb to the point of being blind to Johnny’s plight. The boy pretended to be alright, pretended his mind was blank, when all indications pointed to the contrary. And now instead of mourning, Johnny was placing blame. "It is as impossible to find out whose fault it was as it is to go back in time and stop it from happening," the rock solid voice of reassurance held firm, something for young Johnny to grab on to so that he would not drown in anger, "The only thing you can do is pick yourself up, move on with your life, and remember her with happiness. That is what she would have wanted." "She doesn’t want anything. She’s dead," a cold, small voice put it plainly. A pit opened up deep in Riley’s stomach, a nephritis crept up behind his eyelids. His grandson’s statement was so final, so concrete, that he realized it was impossible to distract the child with tales of Heaven and life everlasting, of how his mother was always going to be with him, of how she died peacefully and without any pain. Johnny had made up his mind. Even if he was still able to be swayed by the story, he would most likely be able to tell that the storyteller didn’t believe it themselves. Riley Fitzwillis had never been the most articulate person when it came to his emotions. Every problem in his life had been answered with hard work and determination, so it only made sense that his grandson should do the same. With the right guidance at this point, the youngest member of the Fitzwillis clan could still grow to be a strong, confident, healthy, hard-working man whose only concern was building something greater than himself. If Riley could succeed in saving Johnny from becoming a cynic, or from turning into a boy that just wants to roll in the grass for the rest of his life, it would be his greatest achievement. Defeating Hitler and Kim Il-Sung, creating hundreds of tons of steel, and bringing together thousands of blue collar laborers meant nothing if he failed his grandson now. "Sarah was a good woman," the cliche words floated off Riley’s tongue and into the rich summer air, "And she loved you very much. And it’d be a damn shame if you quit on everything you could be because she was taken from you." Johnny digested his words for a moment before looking toward the terrier again. Panting in the heat, its back covered in dirt, the puppy didn’t hurt or hate or feel that it needed to right any wrong. Its only concern was the here and now, its only burden was the immeasurable joy of the pleasures of rolling in the grass, not caring about the world around it. Its only curse was bliss. Nodding his head to appease his grandfather, Johnny Fitzwillis remembered all the times people spoke of his potential, the times they praised him for being smart enough that he could be whatever he wanted, the myriad faceless forms that excitedly proclaimed phrases like "so much potential" and "take advantage of it" and "when you grow up." Riley shook his head, ignoring the vanilla ice cream dripping down his hand. He could handle anything that life threw at him, but he had no idea how to make someone else stronger. His grandson, meanwhile, concentrated fervently on his dessert, lapping up the chocolate ice cream as it melted in the heavy summer heat. He did not seem to Riley to care about nor think about his mother’s recent demise, only the trivial task of eating his ice cream, the visualized, physical goal in front of his face. Just the grass to roll in. "Son," Riley stiffened a bit, readying himself to bear his soul to his grandson, to help make the boy into a man and leave a legacy to this world, "It’s time someone explained what it means to be a man."
