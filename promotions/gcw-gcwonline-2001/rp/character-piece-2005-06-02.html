Date: June 2nd, 2005 Trey limped off the plane in Detroit, sandwiched between heavy canvas bags that hung to the ground. His face was recognizable all across the universe; weary and listless and hanging almost as low as the bags. He couldn't support himself anymore and let the thick bandages that wrapped from his ribs to his thighs bear the burden. Shadows oozed across his face and he used them to obscure the stitches, cuts, and bruises that jigsawed over his cheeks and brow; the sun tight to his back, he might as well have been a shadow himself. He lingered too long and moved too slowly, ignoring the pain that pumped through his veins every time he had to push through a crowd as he went to grab his bags from the carousel and leave. There hadn't been a limo or a taxi waiting for him, but there wouldn't have been if he let anyone know that he was coming home either. Jordan's advice ran through his head then, 'If you're ever fucked up to go home, then maybe you didn't know what home meant in the first place,' and he tried to shake it away to no avail. He hailed a taxi and rubbed his hand over the soft pack of cigarettes that Jordan had thrown him on the way to his flight: 'If it comes up.' The taxi that stopped was a beat up station wagon, with light blue paint that came off in chunks that hung over the cracked wood-panelling. Trey tossed his bags in the bag and crawled in carefully, face grimaced at the aching sore of his internal organs. 'Where to?' 'Can you get me to Oak Park?' 'S'a bus that runs to Oak Park, 'less you've got some money to pony up.' 'Whatever. Just get me there.' 'Damn.' 'What?' Trey looked at the driver in the rear-view mirror, a black man with a graying goatee and no other hair to speak of. 'You look like a million bucks, kid. Boxer?' 'Wrestler.' 'Thought that shit was fake.' Elbow rested on the window ledge, Trey looked out at the airport as they drove past. 'I thought it was, too.' 'Haven't watched that shit in years though, guys today ain't got nothing on the old stars. Ricky Steamboat, that was a helluvan athlete, not like now--people throwing each other through tables, throwing chairs around like they weren't nothing. You one of them types?' 'Whatever happens.' 'Ah, the hell with that. S'why I always liked boxing, other than how fixed it got--Frazier never had what it took to take Clay down, but hell, at least they fought like real men. It's some old bullshit these days, athletes all on steroids and spending more time rapping than playing, nothing like the good ol' days. Sandy Koufax couldn't sing, but I'll be damned if he couldn't send a fastball right under anyone's nose.' 'Never got into baseball.' 'Whaddya mean you never got into baseball? Every father gets their son into baseball, it's the American way.' 'My dad was busy.' 'Ain't no excuse. What's your name, anyway?' 'Trey, Trey Tremendous.' 'Hell of a name--get that from your dad too?' 'Just the 'Trey.' Was named after him.' 'You grow up in Oak Park?' 'Yeah.' 'Trey Miller?' Trey broke his eyes from the industrial landscape and towards the driver, looking back at him with a sort of awe. 'Yeah, that was my dad. Why?' 'Haha, that son of a bitch. I knew you looked familiar, under all them scars. Spitting image of your pops.' 'You knew him?' 'Knew him? I bought him his prom dae. We were tighter than brothers--back then. I haven't seen him in decades, what the hell's he doing now?' 'Rotting.' 'Ain't no way to talk about your father, boy.' 'No, seriously. He died two years back.' 'Damn. Sorry to hear that. I say him a few years ago, after he started up his own law offices. Damn. What got him?' 'Lung cancer.' 'He always loved cigars, even when he had to steal 'em.' 'Huh?' 'Unlike you, your dad wasn't born well-off. Where do you think he learned law?' 'U of M?' 'Where he got his degree, but he didn't learn anything there. Nah, he studied up in the pen.' 'My dad went to jail?' 'Oh yeah, he was a real bad-ass motherfucker from way back. Real hip bastard, got busted for trafficking in some dope from Canada an' learned the ropes in his cell. Didn't he ever tell you nothing?' 'He was busy.' 'Damn. Better friend than a father, I guess. Hell, might as well hear it from someone that still knows about him. Cops never had a problem with him when he was just pushing, but then he went and got militant. It was the 70s, shit was crazy and they didn't want no black power drug dealing negroes--nothing against the ones that were just dealing. Even the pigs that were supplying turned on him when he started throwing up the fist--just a matter of time, I told him, before they came, but he was always too damned headstrong to listen to anyone but Trey Miller. Spent a few years in state, writing letters every week 'bout how much he was learning in the library. Most people go to jail, learn to play ball or get ripped, but this crazy bastard was working on his education. Your daddy always was smart as a whip, but damn if that weren't a shocker. Lost touch with him a little while after he came out, then he went and got hitched to your mom. Damn, it's a small world.' Jordan White would've argued, then, if he had hadn't been across the country. Nothing was bigger than the world from the plateau he lingered on. Sand whipped past him and gunked in his teeth, grains that he tried to crush between his teeth. Every drag he took of his cigarette invited more sand, but it was nowhere near a flurry. It was just the wind--howling bitch--on its normal rounds. It's hard to sit in the middle of a desert and not be introspective. The sun had been up for long, desperate hours that burnt themselves onto Jordan's brow. His stomach tumbled over itself until it was tied in expert knots that could only be undone by eating his own weight in greasy meat and butter-soaked pancakes; something healthy, of course. Almost around to trusting his vision again, Jordan cranked the Impala and let it idle for a few minutes as he flipped through dead air on the radio. There was nothing left for him there--it was time to move on. The Flash-red Lamborghini he left behind him was another necessary sin thrown on the pile. It was Lexicon's own, no threat to Anarch's stock if it got left in the desert. Where things go to die, thought Jordan. But that was a different thing entirely. 'I didn't get your name, man.' 'Joe Miller.' 'You wanna talk to my mom?' 'She never liked me much, part of your dad's old life. Got hitched and shit changed. That's life.' 'You sure? I mean-' 'Don't even worry about it, boy. I liked your pops, not his woman.' Trey pulled his bags over the back seat and thanked Joe again. With a wave, the old man pulled off and left Trey to face up with himself on the cracked sidewalk that lay under the dying tree he'd grown up with. Kids behind him stared as they rode past on low-slung bicycles, bumping along the potholed street. The driveway was full of cars that he didn't recognize, for the most part. His mom's was there, and so was his sister's. The screen door cracked open and a thin black girl walked onto the porch with her hand held over her eyes to shade the sun. 'Trey? Is that you?' A chill ran down his spine; he couldn't place it. He knew her face--not why he knew it, or how he knew it. 'Yeah, who wants to know?' Her arms were around him before she answered in a tiny voice. 'Don't you recognize your own sister?' 'Shit, Andrea. It's been awhile, you know. I wanted to see you last time I was in town, but mom...' His voice trailed off. 'She just worries about you, Trey. Do you blame her? Look what happened to you--got the whole neighborhood asking her if you lost your mind. After long enough-' 'It gets easy to act like you ain't got a son?' 'It's not like that.' 'Well, what is it?' 'You can talk to her.' 'She really want that?' 'You are her son.' He nodded and followed her to the house. 'Who all's cars are these?' 'Calvin's friends.' 'He still live at home?' 'He's only 19.' A voice came from the kitchen, melodic and soft: 'Andrea, who is it?' 'It's Trey.' And then, nothing. Less than nothing. She came around the corner just to stop and stare, starting to spill the tears she'd saved up for too long. Trey was stuck knee-deep in guilt as she walked over, wrapping her arms around him, sobbing out, 'my boy.' 'Mom...' She rose her head to look at him with eyes wide and pleading. 'I'm alright.' She kept crying, latched on until Andrea pulled her apart. 'I didn't raise you to dye your hair red, and never call your mama, and...' She trailed off again as Calvin came down the stairs, defensive to the sound of his mother crying. 'What's it--oh. Trey. Ain't think you was comin' back.' He stared Calvin down, fighting at the disgust rising in hs stomch. Calvin stood firm and raised his chin enough to share his hositlity. 'Don't wear hats in the house, Calvin.' 'Heh, tryna act all respectable. Why you even here?' 'Calvin,' snapped Andrea. 'Don't talk to your brother like that, and take that hat off. Mama doesn't need you trying to start something, not now.' He took his hat off and nodded at Trey before he walked back up the stairs. Voices traveled down, words that Trey let slip past. 'Mom.' 'What?' 'Why didn't you ever tell me about Dad?' 'He didn't call after he left, either. Wasn't worth carin' about.' 'Mother!' 'What d'you expect, Andrea? Don't gotta stand up for Trey, he's just like his father.' There was a lot that Trey wanted to say--mostly centered around 'fuck off,' and 'I know why he left'--but he held back and let Andrea calm his mom down. He pulled the pack from his pocket while he waited, sitting on the back porch and staring out over his childhood. Just a Newport, he lit it and savored the endearing, okay, okay. He hacked up a goddamned lung and tossed it to the ground to stomp it out.as Andrea stepped through the door. 'Gonna tell me I should leave again?' 'No. Mom's ready to talk.' 'Oh.' Everything was uncomfortable, from the walk inside to the way the air hung in the air to the air conditioner rattling in the kitchen window. His mom sat on the couch and he took a seat in one of the chairs opposite. 'What do you wanna know?' 'Who my dad really was.' Jordan paused in the doorway of the diner and let the smells intoxicate him. Popping grease called out to him, 'eat, eat,' but he knew grease couldn't talk so he stayed quiet as he sat down, pointing out things on the menu to his waitress and responding to questions with harsh gesturing. 'Wear Your Love Like Heaven' wailed the stereo in the dim back corner--happy fucking Brits. We'll move on, yeah--but where?
