We Will Bring Your CDs Tomorrow

2 February 2008
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: February 2nd "We're going to bring you your CD's tomorrow, okay?" I remember it like it happened yesterday. I stood there in the Van Dyke Hospice with the worst feeling I've ever felt up to this point in my life. The Van Dyke Hospice was part of the Community Medical Center of Toms River, New Jersey. Up until this point, I never really knew what a hospice was all about. I knew it was where people went when they were about to pass but I didn't realize the complete lack of effort that was given to keep them alive. I didn't realize that when you where sent to a hospice - the only question left was 'when?' "Is that okay? We'll bring you your CD's tomorrow so you can listen to them instead of the radio?" I looked deep into my father's eyes that night. It was April 26th and before that, I had all intentions of going to get a drink with my friends. We were going to head over to a local bar -- Used To Be's. We wanted to get really hammered and try to pick up a couple of really slutty chicks to take home. We wanted our balls licked. But I got the call from my mother, telling me that they were transporting my father from Fox Chase Cancer Center in Philadelphia to the Community Medical Hospice. I didn't think much of it. Maybe it was denial. Maybe it was just the lack of understanding. My mother asked that I come right over when they arrived. But I just saw my father a day prior. I waited a number of hours in the lounge while he had surgery to get all the cancerous tumors out of his colon. The four hour surgery took only two and the surgeon assured us he had a number of months left to live. He said everything went fantastic -- better then he'd ever have expected. But I guess it wasn't the surgery that pulled the trigger. I guess it was something more then that. Maybe the morphine? I hesitated to go when my mother called me, frantic. I knew my mother was one to over-exaggerate. She was very emotional. Rightfully so, too. My uncle - her brother - had just passed away two years earlier unexpectantly. For her husband to be in a similar situation was something she had nightmares of literally every single night for the last year, when he was diagnosed. But it was my father. It couldn't happen to me. The doctor said he had at least six months. Six months is a lot longer then a day. A whole lot longer. My father loved his oldies music. As a matter of fact, he spent the last two years downloading it and creating a number of compilations on CDs. I remember teaching him how to do it, to be honest. It was the era when illegal internet downloading became a household pasttime. He was a former enforcer of the law -- a cop -- but still thought it was a great idea. Afterall, he WAS retired. Was it really THAT morally wrong if he was retired? I remember it used to aggrivate me sometimes when he downloaded because he'd download so much. So much so that it would basically consume all of our internet connection. We're talking the days that digital satellite link and cable connections were just released. But we were just glad that more than one person was allowed on the internet at a time. It was very much unlike the days of dialup when we all faught to see who'd get home first to get first dibs at the 'net. I remember, the night of the twenty sixth, contemplating telling my mother that she was overreacting. I wanted to tell her that I was really shot and about to head to bed, just so I could drink with a few friends. I was so emotionally drained at that point, mostly from the anticipation of the results of the surgery. But the ironic thing was, as much as I worried he wouldn't make it through the surgery... I knew he would. I remember my sister hysteric in tears. But I knew he'd make it. The last thing he said to her was that everything would be okay. Why? Because he loved her. Who would've thought the word love to one person could be so powerful as to affect all of us. Each and every one of us waiting and praying. Wishing and hoping. We definitely had our fingers crossed. "Do you want us to bring the 50s CDs first? Or do you want the 60s instead?" I stood there at my father's bedside in the Van Dyke hospice trying to figure out what he'd feel more comfortable with. He'd made a ton of CDs by this point and used to make duplicates to distribute to all the family to enjoy. He only had one brother so he constantly gave gifts to my mother's siblings. Especially her brother...my uncle. I still remember my aunt thanking him for the CDs. She said it was the music that kept him motivated to survive for so long. Its funny how music can heal a person -- even in the worst of pain. Funny. Standing at his beside, I had the same idea. I figured the music would keep him going. Keep that smile on his face. A smile that I was so eager to see, especially on a night where I had planned to be out getting wasted with my friends. I wanted to believe everything was alright. I wanted to believe. He smiled at our request to bring his CDs. His smile was his acknowledgement. He hadn't really uttered a word since being transferred to the hospice. The last words I actually had heard him speak where in the hospital, after the surgery. The doctors said it was just the morphine speaking. They said he'd be delirious because of how drugged up he was. I still remember him saying, "I have so much to do." I remember him saying, "We have to finish it." I remember him saying, "The ceiling. The floor." He wasn't delirious. He did have a lot to do. He had a lot to teach me. He had accounts to tell me about. He had taxes to show me how to file. He had a career to help me create. We did have a lot to finish. It was our summer home. We were planning on a number of upgrades and just hadn't gotten around to it because of his illness. He was too strong-hearted to just go out and hire someone else to do it. Instead, he tried teaching my brother and I slowly. The thing was - he tried teaching us without actually alluding to the fact that he was too weak to do it himself. He tried hiding it. The ceiling. The floor. It was their house. We were about to finally finish the last room of our basement - specifically, the computer office. We just had to put down the new flooring and throw up a sheetrock ceiling and we'd be halfway there. But he was delirious because thats what the doctor told us. He wasn't making sense because we couldn't make sense of the situation. Who really wants to think of those things when you're sitting in a hospital room. Especially when its extensive care. His smile at the mention of his CDs, though. That was something. It was one of the most heart-warmintg experiences that I've ever felt. I didn't know it at the time, but I definitely know it now. He definitely loved his music. I think it brought him back to his childhood -- or at least his teen years. He used to play in a band with his brother and a few friends. While his brother got a job at PSE&G and he went into law enforcement, his friends pursued their goals of being in entertainment. Hell, I remember him making fun of Joe for playing such an idiot (not to mention shrimp) burglar in the Home Alone series. We sat together on our basement couch laughing. I had never met Joe at that point -- but I knew him from my father. My father definitely loved his music, though. He loved it. I remember clearly looking into my brother's eyes. He was an emotional guy, too. He took after my mother. He was ready to cry in an instant. But he didn't, mostly because I was there. They looked at me as the strong one because of my apathetic features. I knew how to control my emotion. I still do. It's definitely an unhealthy trait. I wish I could fix it. Everyone needs a good cry. But my brother held strong. The strongest I'd seen him in quite some time. I was so proud of him. It was at that same moment when it finally hit me. I finally realized what a hospice was all around. I remember staring at my father's bed and starting to take a look at all the random tubes connected to him. There were far less then there was at Fox Chase. I followed them ... with my eyes, of course. There wasn't any protein. There wasn't any source of food. It was all just pain medication. They were trying to numb him. I couldn't imagine how they'd try to keep him alive without any source of nutrients. I walked outside of the room, still in denial. I looked for my mother and eventually found her in a lounge room. She was sitting there with a woman who I'd venture to call her best friend. It was a woman who, despite their differences in the past, still came to my mother's side in a time of need. To this day, I still see that woman with the highest regard. She came to my family's side when they really needed it. She cared. She showed it. And she really helped out. She was an angel. And she sat with my mother and looked me in the eyes as I walked into the doorway. I felt, in that moment, that she was telepathic. I felt like she sent me a message and gave me all the answers. I looked at my mother, who sat in tears - as I knew she would. I said to her... "He's not hooked up to any nutrients." She looked up. But she didn't have to. I already knew the answer. We were in a hospice. A hospice is where you send the dying when there's no hope left. A hospice is like sending them home ... and just making them comfortable. A hospice lets them die in peace. In comfort. I took a deep breathe -- nearly breaking my apathetic stigma. I was ready to bawl...but for some reason, I didn't. I left the lounge and just walked in circles around the Van Dyke Hospice. I didn't want to be there anymore. I didn't want to remotely imagine that this was the end -- this could have very well been the last I'd see of my father. A man who I'd looked up to so much. A man who'd taught me so many things. A man who I praised. A man who I'd loved. As I neared the room again, the resident nurse approached me and informed me that the visiting hours were very close to ending. But I knew she was lying. I looked at the sign when I walked through the doors. She came to me out of kindness. But she definitely lied. The visiting hours were over two hours ago. But since the security shit was about to change, they had to try and sneak us out before the staff got us in trouble. Maybe that was my first sign. Why let us stay so much later than we were allowed? I should've known. "Okay, thank you. We'll be out of here in a minute." I acknowledged her. She had already talked with my mother and arranged for my mother and her friend, the angel, to stay the night. It seemed to be common practice in such a place. I remember quickly informing my siblings. I was the last to walk in the room that night. My father had kept his eyes closed most of the time we were there. He was resting. He was just trying to take it in. The moment that really rips through my heart is when I walked in for the last time that night and said to him. "Hey Daddy .. we have to get going tonight." His eyes opened wide. It was almost as if he was kidding around the whole time and just trying to get some sympathy out of us. He would do that too! He was always a jokester! "Do you want us to bring in your CDs?" He smiled and gave us a nod. He liked that idea. I liked that idea. It was the best response I'd gotten in all my stay. It was reassuring. "So then I'll see you tomorrow morning, okay?" And I got no response. He stared at me. Just stared. "I'll see you tomorrow, okay?" And just still no response. "I'll bring your CDs, okay?" Another smile. Another nod. "Okay, great! I'll be in bright and early and I'll see you in the morning." But again no response. At the time, I thought it was the medication. Maybe he was just delayed in his responses. Maybe he was zoning in and out of consciousness and just wasn't picking up on what I was saying. Nevertheless, I kissed him on the forehead and squeezed his hand, giving it a slight massage as I slowly let it go. I smiled at him and he smiled back. It was an image that has stuck in my mind forever. I went home that night and drank a bottle of Merlot, just watching anything that popped up on TV. I think I may have conquered the entire Fresh Prince series in one shot. Nick at Night had a marathon or something. There was no way I could sleep otherwise... I was just too emotionally charged. The day soon passed. It was April twenty seventh. I woke up the next morning to banging on my door. I ignored it and tried to sleep, thinking it was a friend. More banging. More banging. God help me, even more banging. I was able to take a glance at the clock and noticed the time. 9:37....AM I finally pulled myself up ... but I didn't want to. I opened my door and there stood a former boss of mine and a long-time friend of the family. I didn't have to even open the door to know what he was there for. "Your mother called. She wants you to call her right away." I didn't speak a word to him. I nodded and he returned the nod. I didn't need to call...I knew. He walked away and I pushed the door shut. I walked back over to the couch, where I had fallen asleep the night before. I laid back down...face in the pillow. I felt the tears start streaming. I felt my heart start beating faster...and faster. Could it be? I gasped for a breathe. And again. But I stopped. My apathetic side preveiled again. I lifted my cell phone from the table only a few inches away and dialed my mother's cell number. Her hello said it all. My father had passed only a few hours after I'd left last night. They didn't wake my mother immediately because they thought she'd need the rest. Just like that, the most influencial man in my life left this earth. I've never told a single person this since the very day my father passed. I now realize that it wasn't that he couldn't hear me when I told him I'd see him tomorrow. Rather, it was his way of saying goodbye. He'd seen his family one last time and he was ready to go. He knew he wouldn't be there when we woke up. He knew he was ready to go. He was just holding on for one last look. One last smile. He knew it was his time. He just wanted to wait for us. He wanted us to be able to say goodbye, too. And so I didn't drive to the hospice that morning, but instead laid back down. They'd already taken him away to a funeral home. But I finally broke my apathetic spell. I cried. I felt miserable. And I cried. And then I drove to my parent's home -- only to see my whole family -- only to take up my apathetic role once again. I was the strength of the family. I was the one who held everyone together. I was the shoulder to cry on. And now... I was the man of the house. ----- PERSPECTIVE SHIFT. ---- "It must have been a hard time for you, Chris." Words uttered by an otherwise speecheless psychiatrist. It was very apparent in her face that she was well-prepared to start crying. But she knew she couldn't. It would have been too unprofessional. Afterall, she was the one trying to help him. She peered at Chris, awaiting an emotional breakdown. But Chris remained strong. His breathing had increased a great deal since the start of the story, but his composure was well maintained. He opened his mouth, as if to speak, but refrained. Noticing, she wasted no time to ask, "were you going to say something, Chris?" He threw his right hand over his face and held it for a moment. A swift movement of his fingers simulated the motion of whiping sweat away ... only he wasn't sweating. "No," he replied. "I mean... yes, " he repeated, confused. "I don't know," he followed. She knew very well that he had a lot on his mind and so she tried to encourage him. "Go ahead, Chris. What were you going to say." He took a deep breathe before responding. "I don't know, Erin. I've really never told this side of the story to anyone. I've been so apathetic my entire life and I've held every bit of emotion that I've ever felt inside my head. People have always told me how much they've looked up to me because of how emotionally stable I've been but the fact of the matter is ... I'm not as stable as they seem to think. I've always had a way to channel my emotion and to channel my aggression..." "And what happened to that, Chris?" "I wish I could tell you, Erin. I guess I decided somewhere along the line that I no longer needed to channel that aggression anymore and it ultimately spelt my self-destruction." "Well, why not try reconstructing that channel, Chris?" "Thats why I'm here, Erin. I've tried. I've really put forth the effort but it doesn't seem to be working. I feel like there's far more pressure on me now then there ever was. I feel like I've become more crude...more ruthless...more sadistic." Chris drops his hand away from his face and finally glares Erin, the psychiatrist, in the eyes. His tone drops a number of chords and he continues. "...more unstable." Erin, herself, takes a deep breathe. She's at a loss of words for a moment, trying to think of the best thing to say to really help him out. But she knows but one place to start... "Well Chris... I think its time to bring you back to stability." ----- To Be Continued....
