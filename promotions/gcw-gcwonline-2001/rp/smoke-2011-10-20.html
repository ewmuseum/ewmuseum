Smoke

20 October 2011
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 10/20/11 His only hope was that they would enjoy the experience. A bankrolled project. A sitcom on a network that he wasn’t sure would ever be relevant nor viewed. His pal, the monkey, was adamant. No words, only squeals and squeaks. But the kid understood. Understood every noise. Every sound. Every animated gesture. They were friends. How could he not? The kid never batted an eye. Signed checks. Hired talent, both in front and behind the magic. It was the least he could do. And tonight, they would revel in their accomplishment. Together, enjoying the fruits of grueling days and laborious nights. The kid did what he could. The monkey, visibly distressed over a proud life’s dream of work turned into a sham. Words fell flat upon a scorching desert night. Sentiment blocked. Empathy ignored. The kid, annoyed. Perturbed. A reach into a cluttered-filled pocket. Determined hand, maneuvering through denim, along bone and tendons. A journey past smoothed leather and clanging steel. A grasp of silicon and synthetics. The fingertips pressed upon the plastic screen, leaving behind sweat-filled prints. The very genetic essence of the kid. The thumb shifted through the contacts stored. A name. A touch. Phone touched ear. A grinding of teeth. “Make it happen.” Breaths, taken solely through the nose. The red lights of the cameras shifted off. An egress of the crew, en masse, onto the next location. Onto the next event. The phone call was not finished. The suit continued his inquiry. “You sure you’re ready?” No response. A pause. A soft growl. “This isn’t like before,” the suit warned. “Scrutiny follows.” “I can handle it,” the kid reassured. “You don’t need this anymore, you know. It’s different now.” No response. Eyes closed. Head tilted backward. A deep breath inhaled. “You have responsibilities now. You have to understand that.” No exhale. His body shivered. “Things have changed. You know I know that. But you need to realize--“ The belabored sigh burrowed out. “I got it.” The eyes opened. “Alright?” A pause on the other end. “Make sure you’re in Utah,” the suit offered. “There’s someone interested in talking to you.” An eyebrow piqued. “Who?” “You know him. Though not in the way you’d enjoy.” A chuckle from the suit, one enough to shake loose an unnerving feeling. The kid’s stomach began to turn. “But…” “Don’t worry,” the suit reassured. “You want to talk to him this time. See you there.” The phone call clicked off. His hand lowered from his ear. A glance. A quick blink. A quick breath. Who, was all the kid thought. Even if he was already sure he knew the answer. ****** They both tried to ignore it. They tried, only to fail. The princess, bedridden. Not that it was required. Merely recommended. Only a few months along, she could still remain active. The bump made its presence felt ever-so-slightly through cream silk-threaded nightwear, protruding from the midriff. The belly of the petite, encasing a living, breathing now-nothing, soon-to-be-something. The kid paced about the bedroom, phone in hand, thumbs at the ready. A blast of noise every so often, and the fingers jumped to work. Clicks. Clacks. A bell to ensure the safety of travel to the device on the other end. Moments later, the cycle repeated. She watched nervously. Intently. She had yet to say anything to him, for fear of his response. One look into his bloodshot heavy eyes told her all she ever need to know. He was gone six days out of the week. On that seventh day, if he found time for that seventh day, it was mostly him and a phone. Or him and a conference call. Or him answering e-mails. He might as well not have even bothered to show up. She knew he was involved. Too involved, maybe. And sure, they had found time to chat at least once a day regardless through some method of communication. And sure, she was proud of him. And yes, she knew they were still very much in love. But, still, she wondered. How far was he really willing to go with all of this? She asked of his day-to-day. How were things at the company? How was everything? How was life? She was still interested, even with the lack of involvement. Moreso now, for what he had accomplished. He found himself trying to elude the conversation. But there were to be no escape from this encounter. “How’s life at the office been?” A hint of sarcasm. A smirk to reinforce. A half-smile. “You’ve met those mouthbreathers.” A rub of the forehead. “Just try and get to know them. It’s not a pretty sight.” She sat up, back propped up, pressed upon a mahogany headboard. “It can’t be that bad.” He scoffed. “You ever have a conversation with Chris Bagwell? Like, actual words exchanged?” His eyes closed. A head, turned side-to-side in rapid motion. An agitated voice. “Jesus Christ.” “He is from Jersey,” she offered. A snicker. His guard dropped. A beep. He gazed at his phone, and then at his princess. His body hit the bed. He slithered up the mattress. Her body. He stopped, to kiss her stomach. Another one. A tickle. She howled. The phone might as well have been irrelevant. He crawled to her breast. His ear pressed against her heart. She draped her hand over his shoulder. A twitch of the muscle. A clutch around his neck. “I’m sorry about all this,” he whispered into her chest, and she shushed him quiet. “I was the one that said to do this,” she confessed. “I gotta live with it, too.” “Yeah, but…” She would not let him finish. “You don’t gotta explain.” Her hand caressed his cheek. Up and down. He started to relax. He shut his eyes. The phone rang out. He resisted the urge. She assured him everything would be fine. He didn’t have to worry. The phone rang out again. A quick glance. A tilt of the head upward, meeting her gaze. An understanding look. It rang out once more, and he sprung to his feet. He began to pace once more. Thumbs attacking full throttle. A belabored look upon his face. It pained her to see him like this. ****** Wretched disharmony, the kid’s mother called it. Complete and utter lack of control. The uncle only came around when matters slipped through his grasp. He wore his pressed and vested suit proudly. A badge of honor for the elitists only. Expensive cologne soaked membranes, caked upon the skin as to stifle senses. When the uncle talked, the mother groaned. The conversation did not go his way. He changed tactics. He’d flirt with the mother. A touch of the arm. Caressing. Contact. The mother was no longer interested. When they were younger, when she was vulnerable, there was that night. A mistake. A moment of weakness. She’d never let herself fall into that trap again. The mother had had her fill. There was no need to talk anymore. She no longer held interest. Just do what you think is best, was all she could offer. The uncle knew his own way out. But one last stop. To greet the kid. To bond. Teeth showed. The grin of the bastard bearing a gift. Shimmering parchment, fastidiously protected by a red satin ribbon. Though the uncle had tried, the kid never took to him. He was the stern uncle. Not the fun one. Those few days he could not get out of his head to this day. Some nights, he’d find himself unable to sleep. Others, his slumber jarred by the memories. Sheets drenched in sweat. A worried wife. The hell that would never go away. Those few days. The hospital. The funeral. Shedding his past in order to move on. But part of him held on, unwilling to disappear. Unable to let him forget. It appeared as memories, randomly brought about. Sometimes it came in the form of nightmares. He wanted to let go. To move forward. But they simply would not let go. ****** It only rained in the wintertime. The pitter-patter of condensation bouncing off of the Escalade’s sunroof. Tired splashed through puddles, wipers streaked across glass. The kid checked his phone one last time and ignored all the new messages, before putting it away. His sunk in his seat, his temple pressed against the window. He watched the umbrella-wielding masses parade up and down Western, and found himself longing to be as anonymous as they were. Just another face in a crowd. His mind drifted away. Ambled endlessly. All of his thoughts danced about, offering no regard towards his continued sanity. His girl. The job. That bastard and his pet monster. That bitch, and her wretched hangers-on. The whole lot of it. It drove him mad. Was it really worth it anymore, he wondered. What was there left to prove? She hadn’t fascinated him like Murray had. But Murray was different. He was pious to a fault. Following some sort of honor code that, try as he might to understand, was simply something he could not fathom. That bitch, however, he understood. She was no different than him, no matter what face she tried to put on. Conflicted, misunderstood. Wanting to appease everyone, yet ultimately seeing the futility in the attempt. Trying to fit into a world she never really belonged to in the first place. They were the same, no matter how many times she denied it to be the truth. That she ignored her rightful place in the world. That, no matter what she said or what she pretended to be, she was exactly like he was. The driver announced they had arrived at the Standard, and asked him if he needed any help with his bags. He told him he’d manage. Light drizzle turned into a torrential deluge. He grabbed his bag from the back, and when the SUV peeled off, he allowed himself to be soaked before entering the hotel. It was only rain, after all. He checked in, took his key, and asked the attendant to take his bag to the room. He needed a drink. The attendant offered to show him the way, but the kid said he already knew. On account of the inclement weather, the poolside area was closed. Not a person in sight. It was exactly how he had wanted it. He sipped from a bottle of Sierra Nevada in silence on a deck chair facing the dark blue water. He watched the droplets crash into the surface of the water. Tiny ripples, endlessly smashing into one another. His attention wandered. This might be his last shot at the title for quite some time. It never meant anything to him, less now that he was what he was. But in the back of head was that nagging voice. Murray was one thing, and she was another. But she was old, like Murray. Accomplished all the same. What if afterward, she left like he had? Would he feel the same? If he couldn’t beat her, would he feel the same type or regret? That same longing? Maybe worse, what if he won? What did he have left to prove? She wasn’t Murray, but the rest of these so-called elite waiting in the wings after her…they weren’t even close to her. The supposed next-in-lines. Sure, maybe the movie star. Maybe even the tough guy. But the rest? He couldn’t have given less of a damn. Everyone else worth their weight was already gone. What was even the point of having something of prestige that would immediately be worthless in the hands of the rest? He pulled out a cigarette and the first drag bore its way to the very bottom of his lungs. He laid back on the chair, eyes open, staring at nothing but dreary overcast skies. Irises withstood the assault of the downpour. And then, just like that, it stopped. His phone rang. He glanced at the screen and answered it. His shoulders slunk back onto the seat. “Hey.” “Hey,” the princess said. “You make it there alright?” “Yeah.” “How’re things?” He didn’t say anything. He brought the cigarette to his lips and took a drag. “Alright,” he said, the smoke escaping his lips. It was then he realized what he was doing. But by then, it was too late. “You’re smoking.” A pause. “Again.” He tried to think of something, anything, to cover this up. But there was nothing. Emptiness. He grimaced. “Yeah.” “I thought you quit.” He took a drag and let loose a stream of smoke. “I did.” She didn’t say anything for a moment. A second to get back on track. “You know I wish I could be there.” “No,” he chuckled. “You don’t.” “I do,” she insisted. He took another drag. “You don’t,” he replied with an exhale. “You can always just come home, you know.” “Yeah.” He shut his eyes. His body tensed. A face, now turned uncomfortable. “But…” “I know, I know…” Neither of them spoke. Nothing needed to be said. In his hectic life wrought with constant scrutiny with all fingers pointed his direction, he enjoyed it for as long as he possibly could. “Hey.” He didn’t respond. He hadn’t wanted to. “Good luck.” A moment of silence. “Okay?” “Yeah.” His voice trailed off and the call clicked off. The rain started up again. ****** His mother had warned him of his father’s past. His transgressions and misdeeds. It was in his bloodline. Funny. The thought hadn’t occurred to him in years. Yet at that moment, it was the only thing he could think. No bright lights in the hallway. No cameras. Just the two of them, alone. Mired in silence. The kid pulled out a cigarette. He told everyone he’d quit. He no longer cared who knew anymore. A turning of the wheel. Sparks, but no flame. Another lighter appeared, not of his hand. This one did not fail. “You’re scared,” the interloper asked. He inhaled the flame. The ember glowed. “Not of her.” A moment of reflection. “No.” The smoke fumed outward, a poisonous grey cloud that hung in the air. The interloper snorted his laugh. The kid was miles away. Eyes off in the distant. No attention paid to the world. Reality returned. Words became labored. Harried. “What’d you say?” “It’s not important,” the interloper replied nonchalantly. “What is important...” He placed his hand upon the kid’s shoulder. A look, like a father might give his wayward son. “Is that you’re not alone here.” Their eyes met. The kid’s stomach turned. He knocked the hand away, and the interloper backed off, hands raised, offering nothing but peace. A deep drag from the cigarette. Smoke surged from flaring nostrils. His words slowed. Lips quivered. The hand trembled, forcing ashes to plummet to the ground. “Why are you here?” “I already told you.” The sanctimony. The familiar elitist ensemble. The folded arms. The cold, dead stare. The lies. “I’m here to help.” Awkward silence. A blank stare. “Why are you really here?” The interloper flashed a knowing smile. The kid’s cell phone rang. He ignored it. “Your phone,” he queried. It rang two more times. But the kid’s eyes never wavered. When the phone stopped, his stare would not. The interloper rubbed the back of his neck. Head tilted towards ground. And when he looked up once more, he smiled. “See you around,” the interloper said as he began to turn away. “Kiddo.” The kid would not run after him this time. He would not ask him once more. For reasons known only to him, as the phone started up its siren song once more, he simply chose to wait. ****** He wandered around the Staples Center central floor, flanked by men of power. Announcers, road agents, security liaisons, writers, and producers of various capacities. He had been in town since last week, covering every single base possible. It started out with just those that needed to be there. The cogs that made the machine go. Every day, more and more gears. Now, just a handful of days before showtime, the real road test. The one that counted, at least. The men lobbed out questions and concerns, oftentimes talking over one another. Where to set up the A camera for maximum coverage. Will the cheap seats be able to hear every syllable uttered. Did such-and-such celebrity get their front-row tickets, and don’t forget to make sure someone gets their faces on camera. Make sure to write that down, please. Who’s in the luxury suites. How can we optimally handle the time between matches. Does security have enough able bodies to cope. Concerns about certain performers and their desires for obnoxiously excessive pyrotechnic displays. Always with the pyrotechnics, he bemoaned, and no one disagreed. They continued on and on and, save for the occasional pithy comment, offered nary a word. Instead, he let these so-called titans of industry do the heavy lifting for him. This wasn’t even supposed to be his business. If it weren’t for the vacuum in power, he’d be lounging in a boardroom, across from crusty old white men who hadn’t even viewed a single moment of product. If the rebel hadn’t left… At one point, an argument started up. It’s over a competitor that’s become problematic. They’re uncontrollable, they say. Destructive. Untenable. Malicious words are thrown out haphazardly. Arrogant. Odious. A cancer. Counter-productive to proper progress. The competitor has certain folks in their camp, folks that want this person to succeed. But the detractors made their voice known. They brought up incidents. One mentioned these occurrences are becoming more frequent. The loyalists argued that the same could be said for any number of the egos in the locker room. One believer even brazenly pointed at the kid amidst his argument. Like this, it went back and forth. Every goddamned meeting like this, the kid thought to himself. Every goddamn meeting. It is there when he decided to slip out. He traveled through the corridors, no real idea of his ultimate destination, thinking only that these folks would handle it for him. He would, in fact, rather have a drink than deal with such nonsense. He pressed through the double-doors of the bowels of the arena, out into the unknown of the cold, dark night. Rain poured copiously. He didn’t bother finding shelter, instead preferring to once again let it soak every thread of his clothing. Every fiber of his being. Through the maze of one-way streets and empty sidewalks of downtown, the kid found a bar tucked away in the underground. He descended the limestone stairs, passing by young businessmen that had partaken in imbibing. The bartender didn’t so much as give him another look when he ordered his whiskey neat. And he cradled it, taking sips as he discovered an empty booth and nestled himself in his own silent corner. That night, he simply watched. He let the waitress refill his drink instead of ambling up to the bar. The iPhone shook his pants’ pocket seemingly continuously, not a moment of attention paid to its beck and call. Anyone that tried to strike up conversation was rebuffed. He only wanted to be left alone. In his solitude, his mind remained blank. No thoughts of his future heir. No worries over the World Championship that stood ever-so-close from his grasp. Not even a concern how those bickering old codgers were fairing in their attempt to power-struggle. He just wanted to enjoy a quick moment of silence. Alone. Just the way he liked it.
