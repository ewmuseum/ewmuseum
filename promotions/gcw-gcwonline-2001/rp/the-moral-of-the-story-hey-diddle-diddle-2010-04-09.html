The Moral of the Story Hey Diddle Diddle... * * * * * * * * * * * * * * * ...the Cat & the Fiddle The more I tried to forget the more I remembered. Life was funny how it threw these little curve balls at you. The human psyche intrigued me greatly. Like why I would take my picture perfect existence and empty it's content into a blender, turn the dial to Pulp and watch it all mash up from the comfort of some whore's pussy? Who does that? What kind of man does that make me? The more I tried to push my carnal feast aside and out of my mind the more it came to the forefront. Like during Fight Club, those snippets of male genitalia flickering for a split frame in the duration of a children's movie, my memories did the same. Interrupting me trying to find a happy place in my past. A place I could curl up into a little boy and bask in the warmth of friendly, family memories. Like my wife walking down the aisle, beaming proudly with tears streaming through the foundation and blush. Yet every time her father led her down, another woman linked through his other arm. A buxom brunette with bosoms 'til Tuesday, dressed in her birthday suit, her free hand buried inside her groin and plucking out a wedding ring, putting it on her own finger as if to tease. It was my wedding ring. She'd corrupted my memories. She'd corrupted my dreams. She flickered to mind whenever I let it relax and even more-so the more I fought her. Virginia (that was her name) was the bane of my existence and the reason I was wallowing in this studio apartment. An apartment which seemed more like a prison cell. A prison cell by way of me not being able to see my boys. I longed for my boys. For seven years, Jane and my relationship withstood the test of money woes and pregnancy and her psychotic family, yet one night in a bar in England seemed to be more tumultuous to our relationship than all of that combined. A tattered heart was all that remained of my hungry cock's rampage and I was left trying to find the pieces that were left of my life. Just hoping that someday, somehow, my Jane would find it in her heart to forgive the man so dumb as to shit on the table he ate at. Let the violins play. I was a sad story. A sad, sad SAD story. * * * * * * * * * * * * * * * ...the Cow Jumped Over the Moon After I'd defeated my three contenders the world seemed to swirl into a mass of faces, microphones and camera flashes in an excited cyclone. I was Independent wrestling's next big thing, having defeated the last, the Back Alley Brawler. Jody Monroe swiftly ushered me down corridors, past people trying to congratulate me, through doors and into the lobby of the Bell Auditorium where I stared out over a sea of journalists, all waiting to probe me for my thoughts on winning. There were people from magazines and websites, smarks and the like, all at the ready with the questions poignant and to the point. Some ferocious. Some looking for that feel good story. Some of them just covering the bases so as they could get along to the next Indy show. It was a touch overwhelming. I never, in my life, expected to be the centre of attention. Particularly in my current field of employment. Yet here I was, the man everybody on the Indy scene wanted to speak to. The man they all wanted a piece of. Normal John Johnson. Jody Monroe beamed like a mother at a swimming carnival, who'd cheered little Timmy into second place ahead of poor Ricky with the lisp and the gammy leg. Her eyes twinkled as she proudly displayed the first of Just Wrestling's home-grown stars. Never before had they built someone from the ground up to become the apple of the Indy world's eye. The first feel good story Just Wrestling had ever had with its champion. Some had tried to pawn the belt. Others fell into issue with local law enforcement as they held this belt. Jody was hoping the public relations nightmares of former champions were in Just Wrestling's past and with the help of their first home-grown talent... perhaps she could take Just Wrestling to a new level. There were questions firing at me as if I were landing on Omaha beach, like machine gun fire rapidly attempting to knock me from my pedestal. How did I feel? Where to for Normal John Johnson now? How long was my contract with GCW? Were there six zeroes tacked onto the contract or is it by appearance only? Probing question yet all of them seemed to miss my personal life, which relieved me considerably. Yet of them all, the question which seemed to fire at me all the time, from journalists, passersby in the street, colleagues... the works. A little man, from the E-Wrestling Torch, rose to his feet and pulled the pencil out from behind his ear and licked the lead at its tip. He raised his pencil clutching hand, like the pencil might garner my attention further. Pointing him out, he smiled pleasantly before introducing himself as Morton Murphy. Cleared his throat into his pencil-wielding hand and stood on the tips of his toes. “John, Morton Murphy from E-Wrestling Torch. I've got one question for you...” Realising he'd had the floor, the rest of the journalists hushed, paying attention to the EWTorch reporter, ready with the notepads of their own or pushing their Dictaphone's a little closer to my direction. “...what makes you so normal?” Rose to my feet, looking out over the sea of faces, almost disappointed as I rose. Picked my shining Just Wrestling Championship belt up off the table and gave my mask a tug, just to make sure it was in place. Leaning down into the microphone I spoke, clearly yet concisely. “I got a question for you...” The silence grew. “What makes me so special in the first place?” * * * * * * * * * * * * * * * ...the Little Dog Laughed to see Such Fun The automatic doors parted ways, inviting me into the 7-Eleven. I only needed a few items so the price didn't bother me for three or four things. You know, the important stuff. Necessities like cigarettes and deodorant. Maybe some corn chips. My new bachelor diet had a lot to be desired for but that was something I'd address later on as I settled into my unwanted freedom. This hole in my gut, the one left by my family's absence in my life, needed to be filled. And only food that tasted great and was way off the track of my normally balanced diet seemed to fill that void. That... and maybe a quart of bourbon. I head down the junk food aisle and snatched up the first bag of Doritos I spied, tucking them under my arm and heading to the refrigerators. One six-pack of Welch’s grape soda later and a trip by the medicinal aisle, I had all my items aside from the cigarettes. Nearing the counter, its Indian attendant was engrossed in his physics text that lay across the counter, never paying me the dues proper customer service would offer. Placed the items down on the counter, the soda heavily enough to get his attention. “Sorry, sir.” He read the last few words of his page before looking up. His eyes widened. Lower lip quivered. “HOLY FUCKING SHIT!” Fumbling beneath the counter, the cashier (whose name tag read Karl) eventually rose holding a Dirty Harry styled cannon aimed directly at my face. The face which was hidden beneath my mask. My wrestling mask. I'd made the choice to live out my public outings within the mask, like that of El Santo who'd taken his mask to the grave, but never did I ever think of the difference in the cultures between that of a pro-luchadore Mexican community opposed to the always doubting citizens of New York. Yet the second the tip of the gun's barrel shimmered in the fluorescent lights I understood that difference. His thumb pulling back its hammer, the clicking of the bullet falling into place in the chamber, reiterated that fact that perhaps I hadn't thought this through that entire well. Never mind the fact I'd brought a handful of items to his counter and had my wallet out ready to pay. Those were minor details in the doubters mind. More poignantly, this particular doubter had a gun to trouble himself with. A gun which was aimed at me. Karl shouted some words in Sikh or Hindi or Nepalese, I didn't know the words and they didn't matter. The tone and context withstood the language barrier and I knew that I should fear for my life. His nerves shattered, the barrel wavered in a circular motion, as he tried desperately to aim for my face. Shocked, I stepped back from close range in a stunned stagger, hands up like I'd seen in the movies. His dialect was intensifying with what I could only identify as hate. Jerking the gun in short jolting movements, threatening my life, I realised that I was holding my breath. The trigger beneath his index finger began to squeeze against the steel. My teeth ground against each other as my life flashed before my eyes. I saw my boys. Saw them growing up. Playing football. Baseball. Getting girlfriends. Me living their lives through mailed photographs, Facebook friendships and telephone conversations. Okay, so it wasn't really my life as it stood, but perhaps a flash forward in what could have been. What could become of my life. A figment of my imagination so strong that it resonated in my mind's eye. And everything I saw? Seeing my sons' lives through correspondence? Through the looking glass? I didn't like it one bit. I hated it. My stomach rolled round with a vile rage and desperation to survive. I wanted to be a part of my sons' lives. Breathe it. Eat it. Live it. I would not stand idly by and miss out on each and every minute of their lives. Couldn't stand for that. As the cashier squeezed the trigger to its full potential I through my feet out from underneath me in anticipation, dumping myself on my ass. The bullet burst through a Cornflakes display, spewing out from the box down over the top of me. Where the bullet went after that I didn't know. I wasn't sticking around to investigate. Like a dog on a linoleum floor, I skittered to my feet amidst the slippery Cornflake'd floor, running as best I could against the lack of friction the cereal provided me. Those inviting doors opened up once more and I found myself out in the street, doubled over gasping for air, my wallet still in my hand. I fell to those knees. The chill of the air kissed my face while I fell further to the palms of my hands, on all fours on a New York sidewalk. Sucking breath in greedily, overwhelmed by sense of family, a hearty laugh bellowed from nearby. Within the confines of a doorway sat a bum, complete with sign asking for money for food. He clutched his sides, laughing at the masked man on all fours before him, humoured by the fact a shot from whatever calibre handgun had rang out so close by yet so unperturbed he felt he could laugh at another man's misfortune. “'the fuck're you laughing at?” The bum shrugs. “You should see the look on yer face!” And he roared harder with laughter. Confused, I pressed a hand to my face, as I rose to kneel. The mask was still there. “But... I'm wearing a mask.” “EXACTLY! BWAHAHAHAHAHAHAHA!” roared the bum, kicking the jambs of the door in his uncontrollable fit of laughter. “I dunno what the Hell you're on but it must be good.” Rose to my feet, dusted the New York grit from my knees and tucked my wallet in my back pocket. The bum drew a long breath, calming his laughing fit. “Say... don't take things so seriously.” “Oh yeah?” I scoffed. “You don't know how bad things are.” The bum splayed his hands out, as if showing off his own situation. “How much worse could they get?” Beneath my scarlet mask, a smile crossed my face. I looked over my shoulder, spying the Indian cashier who seemed just as shocked and confused as I, leaning down on his elbows with his face buried in his hands. “Every action has an equal and opposite reaction.” The bum smiled at me, his yellow teeth almost illuminated amongst the grime upon his face. Almost. “See, feller... it's all just a matter of perspective.” “That's easy for you to say,” I joke, stuffing a hand into my pocket. “You don't have a worry in the world.” “Aye.” He puts on a bad Irish accent. “But the world has plenty to worry about with me.” And he winked. I tossed a tenner at him. He let it fall against the pavement, peering down at it a moment before smiling a defeated smile and collecting it, stuffing it deep into the inner pocket of one of his many coat layers. “Thank-you. God bless.” “You telling him or asking him.” The defeat dropped from his smile. Where defeat extinguished joy ignited. “Normally, I ask. But for you... for you I'm telling him.” * * * * * * * * * * * * * * * ...& the Dish Ran Away with the Spoon “Hey, Bandit.” Meekly he replied. “Hi, Daddy.” “I miss you. How're you doin'?” “Daddy?” He completely ignored my inquisition. “Today, I watched Buzz and Woody.” “Buzz and Woody, huh? That sounds great.” His innocence warmed me. There was almost a state's worth of distance between us, me in New York and him and Massachusetts, but we never really left each other. “Your Mom doing okay?” “She cries. At night time. Sometimes it wakes me and I go to her and say 'Mommy? Why are you crying?' and she says that it's because daddy has goned away.” He paused, like he were thinking deeply. I could almost hear the tiny cogs of his innocent mind whirring together as thoughts intertwined and laced together, the hub of a tiny but efficient machine. “I telled her if she's so sad why doesn't she tell you to come back. Because Mommy is really good at telling people to do things.” Chuckle spills over the lump growing in the back of my throat. Bandit's innocent to the whole situation is nothing but heart-warming in a cold and heartless situation. Ever since my adulterous occupation of the Stoke-on-Trent hotel room, near on a month and change ago, my life has never been the same. A lot of firsts have occurred since then in my life. Firsts I never knew I'd ever endure. Lying. Cheating. All stuff I never thought I'd ever do and now I vow to never do again. I was a new man. For the little my vows actually mean. My mask was proof of that. ”Daddy?” “Yeah, buddy?” He pauses awkwardly. “When're you comin' home?” My turn for the awkward pause. How do you explain to a four year-old boy that you've thrown everything you loved so dearly out the window for a woman who wasn't his Mommy? I umm'ed and arr'ed, realising I was stalling for time. Stalling for time to explain something to my son. Someone I wouldn't lie. I was just trying to find a way to sugar coat the truth to resemble something like it but never showing him the white's of the truth's eyes. “See, Bandit... it's just... well... when Daddy was in England... Daddy did something that Daddy's shouldn't do.” “Didn't you make your bed?” “My bed?” “I heard Mommy tell Grandma that Daddy made his bed and now he gotta lie in it.” Sighed, stifled the chuckle his innocence called on. This was no laughing matter. My infidelity meant there was two-hundred and fifty off miles between me and my boys. It might as well have been a one hundred foot high wall. “Somethin' like that, Bandit. Somethin' like that.” “D'you wanna talk to Mommy? She's right here. She's listening.” I heard the hushing tones of someone trying to foil their reconnaissance. Failed foiling. I heard hands jumbling the phone's handset back and forth. Muted words of a palm trying to cover the microphone. Then the exhausted sigh of somebody losing the battle. “Jane?” “John.” The ice which dripped from the way she said my name sent shivers down my spine. “How are you? How you doing?” It felt like the lamest thing I could say, but the first which came to mind. “How d'you THINK... I'm doin', John? I'm looking after three boys under five because I can't stand the sight of their adulterous, scum-bag father. THAT'S... how I'm doin', John.” “I'm sorry, Janey. Sorry.” “Yeah? Well sorry's about as useful to me as tits on a fucking bull, John. Where does your sorry get us?” “It's a start. How can we work this thing out with all this distance between us, Jane?” Threw my arm up in frustration at the phone. “You're assuming I WANT to work this out, John. You know what they say about assuming.” Her truth struck my heart like a four-by-four to the sternum. The breath rushed out of my lungs and I sat with the weight of her words. “It makes an ass out of you and me.” Meekly, I spelt it out. “No, John. Just you. I don't think it's a good idea for you to speak to the boys at the moment, John. At least... not until we've worked it out.” “What? No! I need to speak to my boys.” “Yeah? Well you should have thought about that before you cheated on me, John. That's your problem. You never think. You just do things. And now... now you don't like the ramifications for your actions and you're making me feel guilty.” “Guilty for stopping me talking to my sons? It's your choice, Jane.” The bitter after-taste of spite lingered on my tongue. “This... this whole arguing between you and me? It's not productive. We need to get other people involved.” My mouth gaped flagrantly like a fish out of water. “Lawyers? You want to get lawyers involved.” “Divorce's don't work themselves out, John.” Her tone was matter-of-fact, like a hammer coming down upon a coffin nail. “Divorce?” “I don't love you right now, John. Every time I think of our marriage I think of your wedding ring inside of that woman. I think of you making your face while you...” “She didn't mean a thing,” I tried to excuse myself, as pathetic as it sounded. She sighed the exhausted sigh of a tired woman. “Do you think that matters?” “Our marriage matters.” To nobody, I pointed to myself, almost viciously into the ribs which protected my shattering heart. “Not anymore.” She paused, as if thinking strategically about her next words. If not strategically, than solemnly. “Not to me.” “How can you say that?” “Every night I shut my eyes and I shiver. Cold for missing your warmth, John. Your touch. You.” I imagined her in the hall-way of our home, phone pressed to her pale cheeks. Eyes reddened and puffy from crying unmercifully, dashing stray strands of her behind her ear as she poured her soul into the mouth piece. “And then I imagine your touch. I imagine what it would be like to have you touch me one more time. Do you know how it makes me feel?” Better judgement enables me to ignore the will to answer. Figure it's a trick question. “Sick, John. Sick. Disgusted. Angry. Loathsome. Loath... some. I think I hate you, John. I think I hate you. I just feel like...” She never finished her sentence. It rounded out in a guttural grunt of dissatisfaction laced with disgust. My wife hated me. “Let's leave it to the lawyers, John.” “I need my boys, Jane.” “You should have thought about that, John. You should start thinking. Think, John. Think!” “I need my boys,” I repeated, trying to maintain a sense of calm. “Goodbye, John.” The matter-of-fact'ness returned to her voice. “I NEED... MY BOYS, JANE!” I was shouting at the boop of disconnection tone. Ironically, that's exactly how I felt. Disconnected.
