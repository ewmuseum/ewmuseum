Sweet Home Chicago

17 February 2008
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2-17-08 Sunsets are most beautiful when seen from thirty thousand feet. Cities begin popping alive along the barren winter landscape, lighting up like brilliant blinking snowflakes spiraling in the geometrical patterns of human settlement. The blackened expanse of open land recedes, slowly consumed by the illuminated squares of suburban squall until all you can do is shield yourself from its overpowering glow; the aura of civilization, budding bright in the darkness of the night. Some called it Chicago. Rich “The Renegade” Rollins called it home. Something was different on this not-so-special night. The air felt heavy from the moment Rich Rollins put boot to tarmac and waked across the snow-covered concrete in front of the private hanger at O’Hare International Airport. It was frigid fucking cold; Rollins could see his breath even after he walked into the hanger and through the interconnected hallways that fed him back into mass population. Just underneath the chill, lurking like a tumor on his brain, was a strange sense of urgency he hadn’t felt for a long time. He walked down a treadmill-assisted hallway, his eyes flittering across the massive mural that sprawled in bright greens and blues and reds. The scene changed in the mural as he rode further along; lions raced through tropical jungles that ran into medieval castles, crossing rivers that led to the pyramids of Egypt and beyond. A solo saxophone player sat at the other end with his hat in one hand and his instrument in the other, bellowing a jazzy rendition of “Meet the Flintstones” to a group of children who wandered by. Students from a local wrestling school owned by Caldera Enterprises were waiting for Rollins with a car by baggage claim upstairs. They could wait for hell to freeze over--and from the looks of things outside it had--for all Rollins cared. They always waited for him whenever he came home, hoping to pay their dues while getting the chance to brush elbows with a legend. He hated their meandering glances and the motor-mouth mentality they adopted when he was around; their inexperience was one thing, but for fuck’s sake they acted like amateurs. Which they were. Still, it was frustrating, and Rollins decided to take a cab anyway. The lights of the city were brilliant tonight, a local beauty that Rich Rollins hadn’t paid attention to in a long time. Even in the cab it was cold, and the makeshift wooly skull cap the Renegade wore felt awkward taking the place where his bandanna should be. He could have bought a new one, of course. When Jay Terror took his trademark head scarf he figured he could just brush it off, because after all, it was just a snot rag he liked using as a fashion statement. There were other snot rags, right? Well, wrong. He tried to buy another one in some nameless airport last week, but he felt like he was cheating. It was like his old bandanna were looking over his shoulder, shaking its threads in disapproval. They’d been through a lot together, Rollins and that goddamn rag. They’d been to death row and back, literally, and he had never realized how much the silly scarf meant to him until it was gone. In the meantime he wore a generic gray winter cap and had to live with it, at least until he beat Terror senseless enough to get his property back. Yet another subject that seemed to weight on Rollins? mind lately: The Outlaw. The New Renegade. Jay Terror. What was it about the guy that bothered him so much? In all honesty, he had to respect the man’s fashion sense; they seemed to be cut from the same cloth, no pun intended. Terror was an upstart that had started growing on Rollins? nerves, but in his gut he knew exactly why. The kid reminded him of himself. His attitude, his swagger, his goddamn bandanna. The bandanna was his niche, his gimmick, not some greenhorn rookie with a chip on his shoulder. He liked the guy, and ultimately that’s why he had to hate him. You didn’t reach the kind of plateau that Rollins? ruled in this business by making friends and playing nice. “Stop here,” Rollins said. The taxi cab was starting to smother him in a wave of pine tree-shaped air freshener, and it was making his head spin. “Just fuckin” stop, man. Now.” He paid the driver and got out. He was nowhere near his home on the south side, the warehouse that had served as his base of operations for over a decade. This was northern Chicago; little rows of pointed roofs ran down either side of the street, their stone porches swept clean of snow. Little plastic lawn chairs lined the street like symmetrically placed statues, marking the territory of the hard working men and women who had dug their parking spot out of the street that morning. It was a Chicago thing. Rollins walked up to a random lawn chair and dusted away the white layer of powder covering the seat. He sat quietly, adjusting himself in the tiny chair, finding no comfort and not caring in the slightest. He wondered who owned this chair, and from the chrome-rimmed Cadillac sitting in front of him he guessed it was an Italian family. This was their part of town; stromboli shops were scattered all up and down this part of Harlem, their tables usually empty save for the one in the back housing a cadre of gangsters with their polished shoes and pressed white shirts covered with a bib emblazoned with a big red lobster. Maybe it was their lawn he sat it; again, he didn’t care. It just felt good to sit. It felt good to be home. ~<>~<>~<>~ The bar was called O’Malley’s. Middle-aged thirty-somethings threw darts at a digital board, the plastic kind that wouldn’t be useful in a fight unless you tried stabbing it through a man’s eye. Rich Rollins watched them play, tending to a slender glass of Jack and coke and trying not to draw too much attention to himself. About a dozen people were doing the same, happy to leave the quiet man to his thoughts. Rollins liked it that way. The bartender was a ragged fellow, tall and scraggly from an apparent standoff with his razor. His scruffy brown hair hung from underneath a baseball cap, and when he laughed he reminded Rollins of the Dude, the Jeff Bridges hippie with the fuck-it-all stoner attitude. He almost wanted to call him the Duderino because he definitely wasn’t feeling the whole brevity thing. Instead Rollins smiled into his glass and took another sip. “John McCain is a war hero, you know.” A large man at the bar was talking politics with a younger guy Rollins? thought looked vaguely like Chris O’Donnell. He loved eavesdropping. “He was shot down in Vietnam, you know.” “No shit,” the younger man replied. “Was he captured?” “He sure was. Both his father and grandfather were admirals in the Navy, and when his captors found out who he was they offered to let him go. But McCain said, “No. You leave in the order you arrived” and refused special treatment. So they beat him for another two years.” “I knew he was a war hero, but I didn’t know that. Almost makes me sympathetic to the Republican cause. Almost.” “You’re a liberal, huh?” “Yeah. I’m a big Barrack Obama supporter.” The younger man took a drink and smiled. “He’s pretty popular around here, I’d think.” “Yeah.” The larger man wore a cap that had McCain’s name in big bold letters across the front, yet seemed oddly congenial despite the party line between them. “He’s fairly new to the political scene, though. He’s only been in the Senate for two years. Definitely has the city’s support, though.” Rollins snorted. The day he voted a black man into the Oval Office would be the day he started fucking farm animals. Then again, he didn’t vote. “I’ve got to take a leak,” said the young man. He disappeared into the bathroom while his friend struck up a conversation with the Dude. Rollins finished his drink. It was time to go. ~<>~<>~<>~ An hour later Rich Rollins crawled through his front door, swatting at the lights with drunken accuracy--or lack thereof--until he finally gave up. Walking through the shadow maze that was his empty warehouse home, he made his way into his room and closed the door behind him. Empty pill bottles littered his night stand but he cleared them off. His back hadn’t felt his own sheets in over a week, and the moment his skin touched the sweaty mass of dirty covering his body switched off. His ears were still on, though. The sounds of Chicago were comforting to a man used to lots of noise. When he stayed in small towns the endless silence of the night drove him mad. At least here he could enjoy the wail of car horns and clattering elevated trains until it finally put him under. Anywhere else and he was a complete basket case. As Rich Rollins finally drifted to sleep with thoughts of stolen bandannas and little plastic lawn chairs swirling in his mind, the heaviness of the evening lifted. It was like walking out of the pressurized cabin of his plane and into the safe haven of his dreams; his ears wanted to pop with relief. He felt at peace. He knew it wouldn’t last.
