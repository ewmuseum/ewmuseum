The Chronicles of Life and Death (Part I): I'll Keep Your Memory Vague

1 June 2007
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 6-1-07 A life is a precious thing, and the soul of a child is something that should never be subjected to endless tragedy. However, not everyone would agree with that, especially parents who don’t know exactly what to do with their child. You think you know my life? You think that just because I’m well off now that my entire life was lead by a silver spoon? You think you know, but you have no idea. There is little that I can remember about my childhood; but certain defining moments stick out to me like a sore thumb. One such instance was a time when I was ten years old. It was the day that my parents decided that they couldn’t handle me anymore, and when the psychologist suggested boot camp, that seemed to be the best thing they had ever heard. Now, think for a moment and put yourself into my shoes. You weren’t the model child, but it really wasn’t your fault. You had to grow up watching your parents fight constantly. Your father was a habitual drinker and your mother was hooked on so many drugs it wasn’t funny. Now, you’re young, you don’t understand these things in the least. The bottle that your father holds every moment of the waking day, it doesn’t quite make sense to you. And the short white lines that your mother cuts and then snorts with a straw that has been cut down small; what exactly are all of these things? The violence within the household only escalates more and more with each passing day. When you’re subjected to all of these things, how can you blame the child for his violent behavior at such a young age? June 3rd, 1986 Prestera Psychiatric Building Hamlin, WV The scene opens in a small office. A young boy is sitting in a big cushioned chair with his legs dangling off the edge, swaying them back and forth. His hands are pressed firmly against the chair with his shoulders pushed up into the air. The door of the office is barely cracked, but from the other side you can tell that the psychiatrist is speaking with the boy’s parents. And every word leaving their mouths is evident to anyone within a five to ten foot radius. “Mr. and Mrs. Ramey it seems as though your child doesn’t quite have any of the symptoms to be categorized as having attention deficit.” The psychiatrist gave Jesse’s parents the good news. “Well, I’m not going to put up with him any longer.” Jesse’s father professed, “That kid has been nothing but trouble his entire life. All he does is destroy everything he gets and everything that we own. He bitches and cries; throws temper tantrums when we’re at the story until he gets whatever he wants. And her,” Jesse’s father pointed toward his mother, “she just goes right along with it and gives him whatever the hell he wants.” “Don’t bring me into this,” Jesse mother interrupted, “maybe if you were home every now and again to show him how he should act he’d be different. When you get home from work all you do is go out and get drunk somewhere and I have to sit home with that demon!” “If you had to deal with the stress I do you’d be drinking too,” Jesse’s father continued, “and you’d be gone all the time too. All I do is work my ass off just to come home to a family that doesn’t appreciate me.” The psychiatrist cut the two off, “To be quite honest, there is another option that you can both go with.” Jesse’s parents turned their attention back to the psychiatrist, instead of fighting, “We’ve suggested this quite a few times for clients with unruly children. But it will require Jesse being sent away for quite some time.” Jesse’s parents look at each other, and in unison, “Okay.” “You don’t have a problem with him being away?” The psychiatrist questioned, only for both to shake their heads, “Okay, well, there is a juvenile boot camp in Virginia that Jesse can be sent to. So, I’ll go have the receptionist gather the paperwork and call to make arrangements. They normally have openings at all times of the year, so getting him in over the summer shouldn’t be a problem.” The scene cut back into the office area, where Jesse continued to sit in his chair. A close-up of Jesse’s face was shown and a tear rolled down his face.
