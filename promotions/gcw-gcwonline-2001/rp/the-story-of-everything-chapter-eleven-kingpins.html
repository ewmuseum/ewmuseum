The Story Of Everything, Chapter Eleven: Kingpins

undated
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: November 24-26, 2008 I couldn't help myself, but here I was again, back in the reclusive illusion, faster than I'd ever fallen and harder than I'd ever hit when I hit the bottom before. It was the same except for those pacifying structures that anchored me to certain mortality. Those were gone. "Johnny." I sank deeper into this immaterial void and tried to ignore it. I had hoped it would be wordless. I was weary of words. There were too many of them, and most of them made no sense. There were words, waiting for me, piling up on the other side. I prayed there would be no words here, but there have always been words here, and prayers were always meant to go unanswered. "Johnny, I know you're there." Just because I refused to look didn't mean I could not be seen. I didn't recognize the voice and only vaguely recognized it as a voice at all. It could have been an echo of a voice in the eye of a hole that's airy content whispered around and held itself aloft over time. This is where prayers, expulsions of words, drained into and drove down. "Johnny, I'm only here to help you." "Christ!" I exploded. "What do you want?" "Relax. I'm just here to help you get your head straight." "Who the fuck are you?" "I think you've already answered that question." "Oh yeah?" I challenged. "Are you trying to be clever?" "It just looked like you could have used a little advice. That's all." "I know what this is," I warned. "You're just some dead guy I'm imagining. You're not real. You're a ghost. You're less than that." "Right on all accounts," he caught me off guard. "Do you have a problem with that?" "If it's all the same to you, and it should be, you being me, I'd just rather lie this one out and let it pass." "Didn't it work last time, though?" "Last time?" I thought back. "You got by, didn't you?" "I suppose so," I conceded. "But that was different." "How so?" "I don't know," I lied. "It just was." I was lost, and I felt like my bones were going to crumble if I had them. I couldn't remember what it felt like to have bones if it had felt like anything at all. "Is it all just nonsense?" I thought, perhaps too loudly. I found myself unable to distinguish the sounds of voices with the voices of thought. I was thinking about it too hard, and the harder I thought, the more I couldn't tell which thoughts were mine and which weren't. It was a mirror that could be heard but not seen. "Huh?" "Nothing," I defended. "I was just finishing a thought out loud." "How'd it begin?" "I'm not too sure; probably with a bunch of vaguely familiar names, words you don't understand and too much going on to know what's what," I pondered, perplexed on both edges of the blade. I thought of the whole situation with Judge Westonbrook and Emery and my position dwindling somehow between them. "More or less, how it always does." "And from there, where did it all stop making sense?" "When did it start?" I slyly retorted. "What the hell are we even talking about?" "Anything, really. How about how you ended up getting here." "Getting where." "To the present." "I think it started at an airport." I came to my senses. "You're not real. Why am I talking to you?" "It keeps the words from building up unchecked. Otherwise, they'll collect, and you'll never make it through. You've got to deal with them all or it'll all get to congested." "The more I figure it out, the more jumbled it all gets." "Then maybe you're figuring things wrong." "How so?" I echoed. "You're asking the wrong guy," he regretfully informed me. "I can only tell you things you already know. I'm just a fabrication, after all, isn't that right?" "Yeah, sure," I colorlessly agreed. I wanted to argue it, but I had no grounds. It was like practicing ping-pong against a board folded upright. There's no way to win unless you manage to smash a thin plastic ball through a piece of wood. It was only possible through some unimaginably turbulent whiplash effect. You can't reason with something so powerfully unforgiving as a machination, something that knows of forgiveness but is incapable of executing the process. I'd have to smash through; tug back and forth on the rope and pull slowly, only to release during the final counter-wind and let oblivion propel itself back into oblivion without taking me with it. "Now, you don't have to answer this," he started. "But what is it that makes everything else more real." "Nothing," I replied, switching gears. "Except for the lack of confession." "But you're willing to entertain the notion that everything on either side of your perception could theoretically be completely created by your own thoughts." "I guess I can entertain that notion." "What about you?" "What about me?" "Maybe you're imaginary," he prompted. He was trying to get a rise out of me. "Then who's doing the imagining?" "You are." "An imagination of myself?" It all seemed so ridiculous. What was I doing here? How was this helping? Imagination is reserved for the unimagined, and the imagined cannot autonomously imagine without the thumbprint of their existential conceiver. "You weren't always like this, were you?" he asked rhetorically. "It was a slow sequence of events that led you here; a ruling out of near infinite potentials that you've crafted carefully or chaotically or more likely some combination of the two." "But how did it all start? Did my consciousness just spring into being from some irreversible void?" "You could say that, but it wouldn't really help you. Asking where it began is like asking where it's going to end. Once you find out, it doesn't really matter anymore." "Well, where were you before I got here?" "You'd know better than I would. I'm just an imaginary ghost you're hallucinating." "How'd I know you?" "Maybe you didn't." "I knew all of the other ghosts I hallucinated." "There are always holes." "Who are you?" "I'm just a voice." "Whose voice?" "Anyone's who listens to it." Then he shifted course. "They're not real you know." He paused, waiting for me to say something, but I was about done with words. "Emery, Westonbrook, Doctor Wayne Gunn, everyone you've met," he continued. "You imagined them, too. Is that impossible to believe?" "No," I calculated, wondering how he knew of the figures on the other side. "But it's too dangerous to believe." "I know what you're thinking," he said, and then answered. "And I know what you're thinking." I tried to wrap my head around that one, but it only hurt sharply. Heads weren't meant to be wrapped so. "You're sick of having to escape tighter and tighter spots to survive. Each time you twist yourself free, the wrench turns that much faster. The more you slip past them, the more they'll try and catch you, and the last thing you'll want is for them to take you seriously." "So how do you suggest I stay off the radar?" "You don't." He was grave. "You put them on yours. Fire back." "That'd be suicide. Do you know how outnumbered I'd be?" I protested. "I don't even have a radar." "You still have that Undead Sauce?" "Yeah." "You know what it does?" "I've tried it." "It raises the dead." I felt the words begin to cave and crumble beneath me, and the son of a bitch, I felt him pushing me down, with his foot on my forehead, plunging me through that hole. Who had he been who had been him through me? Whoever it was, he wasn't as intriguing a conversationalist as I had imagined. Still, his claim was a bit out of the ordinary and entirely unproven. There was no way to tell whether any of what I imagined he said was true. All I knew was that there were many holes, and there was no bother in filling them when it was easier to fall through. *** Reverberations bubbled through my head at rapid pulses, and I could feel the boot on my head still. This one was more physical. I could feel its shape, pointed toe. I started to stir and could suddenly see again, blurry at first, but I felt coordinated with my body again. "Did you get all that shit out of my trunk?" "Stop kicking me, man. I'm up." I shielded my face. It was Doctor Wayne, standing over me. "You're lying down in the middle of the street, for Christ's sake!" He was exuberated. "What the hell are you doing?" "Fumes must've gotten to me," I understated. "It's all loaded away, though." "Good," he folded his arms. "We should get to work on finding Emery and cashing in." "Did you take a look at that slip of paper?" "Yeah," said Wayne. "It looked like some kind of treasure map." "What about the name?" "I couldn't make anything out of it. I tried rearranging the letters. You can spell out Emery, so I don't think it's just a name." "You got it on you?" I asked, pushing myself back up to my feet. "Let me take a look." Wayne handed me the piece of map, and I held it up, counting letters in my mind and hoping to make a breakthrough. "You can spell Eryn," I said, holding up the map and trying to make sense of the other letters. "Does that mean she's still alive?" "Forget it," rejected Wayne. "That's just a coincidence. I doubt this has anything to do with her." "There's a chance, though. Help me figure this out." "Hold on," stopped Wanye. "There's a transit map on the other side?" "Yeah." "Give that back to me for a second." I handed Wayne the slip of map, and he looked at the backside of it, holding it up towards the sun. "What are you doing?" "We just have to get to the X," Wayne brought the map back down and tore a tiny hole in the center of the marked spot. "Get in the car. Bring the old man's junk." "What about Jeremy Forton-Heath?" "Fuck him," chewed Wayne. "You can't figure out all the codes. Besides, there's no point. You can get close enough just by getting the gist of things. It'd take too long to figure everything out, and that'd be a waste of everyone's time." We were already off, and the good Doctor Wayne wasn't wasting any time, driving at his most reckless. We sped through different scenes of the same city that might have well been different cities altogether. Within minutes, I had lost track of how we had gotten to where we were, and I wasn't all too familiar with my surroundings. "Do you know where we're going?" I asked Wayne as he tried to focus on the road and the hole dotted in the map. "We're going to the juncture on the puncture," laughed Wayne. "The X!" "Do you know what's there?" "Besides our ticket to easy street, I have no idea," Wayne beamed. "What are you going to do with your money?" "I haven't really given it much thought." "Are you shitting me? We'll each be walking away with six figures. That's enough to set you up for a long time, you know." "I'm sure I'll think of plenty of ways to blow through it," I sighed. "I'm just sick of running around so much. It's too much goddamn work." "For starters, you can pitch in on a new car," suggested a serious Doctor Wayne. "This one smells like shit." "I'm not paying for you to get a new car." "You don't have to pay for the whole car," lightened Wayne. "But you should at least split it with me." "I'm not going halves," I stiffly negotiated. "I'll toss in whatever you get for a trade-in on this one." "I'm not going to make shit on this car." "I know," I said. "It smells like crap in here." Wayne grunted and drove with both hands on the wheel like drivers do when they're angry. "Ah, come on," I comforted. "It's not that bad. Just get the interior cleaned out, and nobody will be any the wiser." "I think we're getting close," said Wayne, agitated, looking around and trying to ignore me at the same time. I could tell he was having trouble not just doing one or the other. "There's nothing around here but a bunch of run down factories and some train tracks." "You see any stations?" "No man, those tracks look like they're abandoned. There's foliage and shit growing on them," I eloquently observed. "There's a church," Wayne pointed. "You think someone in there can help us?" "I don't know," I stalled and gave it some thought. "It doesn't seem like the first place I'd go to for guidance on how to deliver an old man's detachable pieces to a drug lord." We decided to go in anyway. Wayne parked the car, and I fetched the jar from the backseat. We had covered the jar with a towel because neither one of us wanted to look at its contents. Wayne said it made him squeamish. We walked up the old, stone steps and Wayne pushed the large, wooden door open. There was a man sitting at a desk, which was dimly lit by an antique lamp. He looked up at us when he noticed our entry, and I could tell he was collared. He rose and spoke in a thick accent. "May I help you?" The robed man started walking over to us, something eerily tropical about him as if he preached voodoo instead of Catholicism. Wayne apparently did not share my hesitation. He whipped out the map and presented it to the priest. "We're looking for this spot on the map, and I'm not sure we're at the right place." How could I have trusted such a fool with the map? Maybe Wayne had been off all along, and now he was handing this map to a complete stranger. The priest examined the map. He turned it over. "Mister Johnny Don't," he smiled. "I have been expecting you." "Actually, I'm Johnny Don't," I chimed in. "And what do you mean you've been expecting me?" "Relax, Mister Don't," he said, pointing to the X on the drawn side of the map. "You are here, and I will take you to here." He slid his finger up the straight line towards the sketch of the island atop the treasure map. "Who are you?" I demanded. "I am Father John Meyer." Doctor Wayne and I looked at each other, and he shrugged. "Mister Johnny Don't," Father John Meyer addressed, handing the map back to me. "I will assist you, but first I must know your sins. Please join me in the confessional booth." He gestured me along with his hands, and I looked at Wayne reluctantly, but Wayne only ushered me forward. I got into the booth, clutching the covered jar in my lap and pulled the door slut. A slot came down in the wood between us, and I could see the father's eyes. I felt cramped, claustrophobic. I didn't know how to begin, and his eyes burned bright through the window between the dark boxes like upright coffins. "I have to admit, Father," I started. "I've never done this before. Do I start it out or do you?" "I trust we both know what is in the jar?" his thick accent lingered like smoke. "What do you think is in the jar?" "Please remove the cover and hold the jar up to the divisor," he commanded, and I felt compelled to do so. "Very good, Mister Don't." "Do you know what this is all about?" "Do not worry," the father assured me. "I am an old associate of Emery's. Now do you know how to read the map?" "More or less." "The train tracks that run alongside this church will lead you to the airport." "I thought those tracks looked pretty out of service." "Have faith, Mister Don't. There will be a train," Father Meyer chuckled. "Once you arrive at the airport, you will look for a locker. Both the locker number and combination are decipherable from that map. The key is that you must count the letters rather than read them." "What the hell do you mean by that?" "Please, Mister Don't. We are in a house of worship." "Sorry," I flushed. "What do you mean by that?" "That is all I was told about the map. There will be a painting facing the correct bank of lockers." "There are tons of paintings and lockers in the airport." "This is a very particular painting, Mister Don't. You will know it by the eyes." "Huh?" I was utterly confused. "There is a cemetery behind this church, Mister Don't. In the back of this cemetery, the land slopes up, and you can climb onto the tracks from there. A train will be on its way to receive you." "Alright," I said, nodding and still trying to deal with the absurd measures of this retrieval. "Be warned, though. There is an empty grave that was recently dug in the cemetery. Please try not to fall inside." "I'm pretty sure I can manage." "You never know whose eyes will be watching you, Mister Don't. The money is in the locker. If you take the money without leaving the jar, that grave will no longer be empty. It is big enough for you and your friend." I swallowed hard. There was something even more discomforting than death about the prospect of having my earthly remains rot for eternity with someone else's bones. I used to do alright in tight spaces, but since the walls started closing in, I didn't think I'd be able to do so well for very long. *** We had boarded the train as promised, and things weren't getting any less for surreal. Strangest of all, there was only a single other passenger facing away from us as we boarded, and I examined him for a while, and he didn't seem to move. I made sure that Wayne and I found the seat furthest from him. "Check out that guy," I whispered. "What about him?" Wayne chimed, in his normally abrasive speaking voice. "Keep it down," I whispered still. "He'll hear us." "Is this better?" Wayne made his best attempt. "It'll do." "So what happened in the booth?" "He said something about counting the letters on the note or something. He said it'd lead us to some locker and tell us how to unlock it." "Then we drop off the money in exchange for the jar?" Wayne's voice started to rise in both volume and intensity. "Yeah, but keep it down," I reminded. "Plus I have no idea what he meant." "Let me see the map," Wayne requested, his voice dipping back down to an acceptable level. I handed the map over and Wayne started to reexamine it. "He also said something about some painting with eyes." "Well, there are 17 letters in this note, 18 if you count the X." "And if we fuck it up, they're going to kill us and bury us in that open grave in the back of the church." "And there are 11 or 12 unique letters depending on how you look at it." Wayne paused. "Wait, what?" He nearly shouted that last part. "Quiet!" I shushed. "We'll be fine if we just play it safe and don't try anything funny." "Alright," Wayne readjusted. "You sure it's that simple though? I get the feeling that there's something you're not telling me." "Nah, it's cool," I said. "It's just that making the switch isn't going to be the end of it. Once Westonbrook realizes he got the wrong set, he's going to come after us harder." "You sound like you're getting cold feet." "Well, we're going to have to deal with it sometime, that's all," I admitted, but only halfway to completion. "And I don't want to keep running errands for Emery, man. This is ridiculous -- following maps, solving riddles -- it's too much work. I'm not cut out for this shit." "What are you saying?" "I don't know, man. I just think we're going to have to find a way out of the whole thing instead of just finding a way out of the next thing." "We're stuck in the middle, man." Wayne leveled. "There isn't much we can do but figure it out as we go along." "I think we need to rethink our strategy, man. Otherwise it'll never end, and it'll just keep getting worse." "Whatever," Wayne dismissed with increasing adherence to my persistent demand that he be mindful of his volume. "We're still going through with the switch as planned, right?" "You seriously have to keep your voice down," I admonished, eying the passenger and losing my train of thought. "Hey, look at that guy. I still don't think he's moved." "I don't know," shrugged off Wayne. "I'm really not paying that much attention. But you never answered my question." "What question?" I asked, half-attentively. "The switch is still going down as planned, right?" repeated Wayne. "Of course," I said, and then the train jarred a bit, the old tracks and the overgrowth stressing the train car as it bumped along. "What the fuck?" The passenger slumped over, tilting without resistance and thumping against the adjacent seating. The weight of his head craned his neck over the edge of the seat as his limp cranium bobbed with the rhythm of the train in the aisle. I sprang up from my seat, and Wayne followed as we steadied ourselves by swinging from backrest to backrest along the aisles of chairs like a pair of monkeys on jungle vines. "Jack Brady," I mouthed, forcefully enough for sound to come out. "He's fucking dead." Wayne poked his head over to give his fraudulently medical opinion. "Yeah, they don't get much deader than that." I examined his corpse, and it looked clean. There were no signs of damage or struggle. He was still pristinely dressed in the same suit. I noticed a card in his breast pocket. With shaky finger, I removed the card, trying not to disturb the corpse. I recognized the print, but this was no code. There was no way this could be scrambled to mean anything else. "What's it say?" asked a curious Wayne. "It's just a number." "What number?" "One million." I showed Wayne the card. "That's a dollar sign in front of it," Wayne stated, but it was almost a question. Just a few semitones up, and it would've been a question. "Yeah." I knew what it meant. I suspect Doctor Wayne, attorney-at-law, had figured it out, too. "So much for that switch going down as planned, huh?" Wayne looked around the train car. "Any way to call for a stop or turn this thing around?" "It could be a trap." "It could be a better offer," Wayne countered. "Maybe he's tired of trying to beat you. Maybe he knows he can't." "Maybe he wants me to think he thinks I can't," I argued. "Because maybe that's the only way he can." "You're not even making sense." "I don't trust it, Wayne." I disclosed. "I don't even trust Emery for a quarter million." "You're paranoid, man," Wayne batted. "Emery has no reason not to trust us." "It's not that he has no reason," I reasoned. "It's that he had no choice. Like it or not, Westonbrook's junk is all we've got. How do we know he's not just stringing us along until he doesn't need us anymore?" "It's all in good faith." I walked over to the train doors and searched around for the emergency release. I fiddled around with it a bit and the doors flew apart like a sideways, awestruck jaw. "What the hell are you doing?" "There's no such thing as good faith, Doctor Wayne." I clutched the jar close to me. "We're going to have to jump." "What?" Wayne's volume peaked, but with the landscape of the dead tracks roaring by and the only other passenger on the train already freed from his mortal coil, I supposed it didn't matter. "Start rolling as soon as you hit the ground," I instructed, based on limited experience. "There's less of a chance you'll break any bones that way." As I rolled down the decline feeling the bruises well up and the cuts multiply from sticks and stones doing a half-assed job, I thought about falling through holes again and the one hole I didn't want filled.
