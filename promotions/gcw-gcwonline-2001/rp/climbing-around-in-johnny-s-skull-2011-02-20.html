Climbing around in Johnny's Skull

20 February 2011
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 02/20/11 Climbing around in Johnny's Skull by Barry Goldstein - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - 2:01 am These weren't exactly the big dreams that Mama Goldstein had for her baby boy, Barry. At 51, with nothing to show for it, I had become the sad shell of a man, meddling around arena hallways as the shadow of a reporter, not even local news would have me. No, this is not what Mama had in mind at all. I was supposed to be in pictures. She was old, much older than me, but not quite dead yet. She shriveled up to about the size of an ostrich egg, and you couldn't make out any distinguishing features behind the folds and flaps of a raisined grape, could it make noise. The noises she made were eerier than that. It was impossible, anymore, to make words of the sound the old bat rasped out, just that they were disappointed noises. Who could blame her? She, like me, like everyone else mostly, had tried, failed. Dad left us when I was four. Mama drank her share. It was just what adults did, I always figured, and I hadn't figured nothing else on the contrary since. I stumbled out of the dive, two in the morning, just like any other, old place. My fedora kept falling off my head. I picked it up and dusted it off once more, put it back on my head. I felt like I had something important to do. Get a grip of yourself, Barry. And then I stumbled towards something to lean on and checked my pocket planner Fuck me, I said. - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - 6:35 am I reviewed talking points over coffee, wearing a shirt I'd already worn at least once that leg. The coffee's bad, by the way. I mumbled something at some point, maybe several somethings, points, talking, in time. It wasn't the interview I wanted, no. They probably gave that one to Jacobs, or even JB. They surely didn't give it to little, old Barry. The consolation prize was big enough, though, something that people would see. Perhaps it would be the redemption of the Goldstein name. Maybe, I'd go places and better late than never, I thought. And then I thought, my god, I still got twenty-five, thirty good years in me. I took the notepad with me to the commode. I figured I'd better on account of the coffee, even if I didn't have to. Every reporter worth his salt needs a good notepad. It wasn't coming easy, but I was determined. Maybe I should have brewed a second pot. I reviewed the talking points once more, reordered them so that they all eventually led to the one question I knew was important, despite the several ways I could ask it. I definitely should have brewed a second pot. Come on, Barry, I thought. Keep your eyes open. - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - 7:04 am I felt the impression of the seat still firmly indented into the backs of my thighs as I shuffled quickly down the hallway. It was humiliating, how god damned long that hallway was, and there he was, halfway out his doorframe, watching. From there I couldn't tell, but it looked like he was tapping his foot. The nerve of him, if he was. He'd been there and worse. He'd been fired over it, and now he was back and my boss, to boot. "You're four minutes late, Barry." Johnny grinned, even when he was serious, which oftentimes made it hard for people to tell. He was dressed in a white bathrobe made from towel cotton. I tried not to slump my shoulders when I reached him and did my hazy best to stammer out an apology for my slight tardiness. "Jesus, Goldy. You have a good time last night or what?" I laughed along with it to hide my embarrassment, even though he couldn't have been too upset about it. When the camera's weren't rolling, he was a different person. He wasn't far off, but the difference was there. A few years ago, it wasn't. He finally had the sense not to be the same part of himself all the time. The whiskey he drank in front of the cameras was apple juice now. Someone once asked him how he could do that, give something up for real and keep it so close within thought's reach. He told whoever it was that he really liked apple juice. I told him he could say that again, and he didn't, and that, to me, revealed the orientation of his temprament, the projected crook of his smile had his mouth been naturally level. I offered not to take up much of his time and showed him the points in my notepad as he cleared the doorway and directed me through. As I passed, Johnny snatched the notepad from me. I stood aghast as he skimmed through my notes, tearing page by page from the coiled binding. He crumpled the papers into tiny balls and practiced his accuracy tossing them towards a waste basket with varied results. "You won't be needing those," he said. "I only really got time for one question. Anything else, you're going to just have to shadow me and take notes, but I'll see to it you get yourself a story." He cracked eggs on the counter of the sink and dropped the yolks into a blender glass, then an entire tin of sardines, a garlic clove, a banana for consistency, and a splash of tobasco for flavor. The noise of the liquefy setting jackhammered through my skull. I can still hear the echoes as he brings the unplugged blender across the room to me. My thoughts weave in between. The obvious question, why now? He'd removed himself from competition almost a year ago at what many claim was the top of his game. He traded it all for a suit and a briefcase, and in that time, nobody ever asked why he left in the first place. He removed the glass from the coupling and held it towards me. I asked him. "This'll take care of that hangover." I made aloud an honest assessment of the remedy he had concocted, noting primarily that it smelled like something that had died. "It is what it is." It was the closest to an answer I got out of him. "Now, bottoms up." It tasted more wretched than it smelled. I grabbed for the waste basket, practicing my accuracy with varied results. "The maids will probably take care of that." People had underestimated his savvy, and maybe that was all part of his demeanor, but even in the throes of his nadir, he had too much charm for his own good and Napoleonic skeletons rattling their bones against his closet door. One by one he endured his rivals, absorbing the most precious spoils from their ruins. I gathered myself from retching, in me a reflection of his own makeshift baptism. The eggshells laid without order on the countertop, also for the maids. - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - 8:21 am It was an ugly hunk of olive green with corners more often than contours, and I didn't know if I was more surprised that the damned thing was still running or that he was still running it. It had to be at least twenty years old, and the music inside it was twice that. If you stomped loud enough, you could hear the thin planks well enough to make out the type of wood if you knew what you were listening for. Oak, Maple, I didn't know. I was no expert on the percussive qualities of different woods. The cabin was littered with crumpled tinfoil, remnants of his diet in transit across the country. I get the strong sense that he would've been happy driving trucks. The air was unsettling, though not to the degree that had been portrayed in the caricature Johnny had played of himself. Stale smoke grappled with the scent of a spectre fried in oil. I got strangely used to it. We'd been on the road nearly an hour, and we were finally out of the city. He wouldn't tell me where we were going, which did little to ease my mind. He'd just topped off the gas tank, and he was having a cigarette while stretching his legs. He was fascinated by living on the road, how gas stations were the glue that held the country together because no matter where you were, you could always get a pack of smokes, something to keep you up, and something to get you somewhere else. "I guess I just felt frustrated." I asked him what he was talking about. "I worked my ass off, and there were still a lot of people who didn't take me seriously. I wanted people to take me seriously." I asked him why, and he shrugged. I asked him whether he was talking about his character on or off screen. He thought about it for a minute. "You already used your question." He finished his cigarette, shook his legs out, and got in the car. He started the ignition to crack his window and unceremoniously lit another cigarette. - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - 9:34 AM The car slammed to a halt, and my sleeping face pushed hard into the window. I sat upright and examined the discolored patch that the glass left. There wasn't much for miles, big things here and there like farmhouses that were too far away to notices us. The road was just a thin stretch of gravel, and the car was parked beside it in the grass, where a boundary of trees, two or three thick, swerved parallel to a creek. He opened`` the door and walked around the car, and I opened my door but stayed seated and asked where we were again. "You already used up your question." He pulled a bandana from his back pocket. "Now, I'm going to need you to put this on over your eyes and follow my instructions." I was obviously reluctant, even though I had a hard time articulating my reservations. It surprised me how taken aback he was that I didn't trust him enough to blindfold myself in the middle of nowhere. I was afraid that I'd end up buried, and nobody would ever find me. I doubted anyone would even look. "Geez, Barry. It's only me!" He laughed to himself. "What do you think I'm going to do to you?" He had a strange way of setting people at ease, a neverending optimism under tremendous gravity. I felt foolish for thinking the worst, but I still couldn't see why I had to wear a bandana as a blindfold. "Just humor me, alright?" He grinned and tossed the bandana to me. "There's something I want you to see. It's a surprise." I looked around at where I was and what I was doing. Might as well, Barry, I thought. What else you got going for you? I tied the bandana over my eyes. It was rank with old sweat and stale smoke. He led me by rope, and I could only think about how this could be explained if someone were to spot us. The creek bubbled beside us, and the air was fresh and pure. "Do you remember what I was like a few years ago, back when I first joined the company?" I chuckled, realizing that I should probably choose my words carefully. Fortunately I didn't have to choose any. "I sure don't." He paused, and the creek noise seemed distant. "I just knew how to cheat a piss test." People seemed to take to him, and since he sold, the company was willing to let him run with it unchecked. He could have blamed them for their part in it, but truth be told, he was more than a willing co-conspirator. As his act caught fire, he cleaned himself up, cleaner, nothing near clean. He was clean enough, though, to see that he was over so well as a sideshow, it'd be risky to move him anytime soon. I think he might have been bored. He took more work on the side. Johnny couldn't function without distraction. He still couldn't. I asked him if remembered what he was like then. "Shit. I'd take a plane to a show and a plane back from it." It was the worst I'd seen him. "The stewardesses knew me by name, and most of 'em knew my brand." It didn't answer any of my questions, though. I still wasn't sure where he was leading me. I asked if we were at least getting close. "We're getting closer." I could no longer hear the creek. - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - 10:17 AM We had entered a building, and I heard conversing quietly with some others before he took me aside and sat me down on a hard, flat surface and passed me a large mug that was warm to the touch. I asked what it was. "It's like a tea." I suppose relatively it was more like a tea than it was, say, a sofa or a golf course. "Just drink it so I can take off your blindfold." It was several times worse than the hangover remedy he had me drink earlier. I couldn't recall having ever drank anything more awful. He undid my blindfold, and I cursed him and asked what the hell was in that drink. "You really want the truth?" The room was unapologetically wooden with no windows. Thin spaces between boards let in dim light. Then, as if someone had flipped a switch. - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - 10:19 AM I watched photonegative light play off the walls and immediately determined myself to be inside of Johnny's skull. His thoughts danced around in the forms of men, natives with foreign expressions. Wind cackled whooshing through the minute structural openings until I, too, succumbed to spells of uncontrollable laughter. Fear gripped me, and yet I laughed, the laws of physics malfunctioning fast around me. I laughed to mimic the wind because it was still real to me. Space wrapped tightly around me. It all came together, and I could no longer move independently through it. I tried to move, but I couldn't move all that space with me. The weight of everything saddled me, and it was all too much to carry. I laughed so hard tears poured. As it was happening, I was under the impression that I was describing all of the unbelievable new things I was discovering. What a foolish endeavor, I thought. Looking back at it all, and even during. I did not know the words to describe what I was experiencing. There were patterns of sound, and it wasn't music, but music was the closest thing I knew to it. There was something as bright as fire. It moved in a similar way, more similar to it than anything else. I did not know what it was. I could see myself there, suspended somehow outside of it, looking between what I thought were absolute edges. I felt how small I was, a speck of dust in the grand scheme of things, screaming in the long, fast avalanche that had been burped out of an infinite explosion. I was in a ship sailing in a sea of mirrors, reflections bent between two agitating forces equal in every way, but opposite. Waves burst and collided in on themselves, cracking mocking thunders at me. I sailed through my wavering reflection, and there, where it was, its guts were in blotches of rampant color, spectres emanating through, the likes of which I'd never seen before. The ship rocked, capsized momentarily, and righted itself, then once again back the other way and again in a sequence of undulating tribladed layers, bending back from one direction to the other with each exploding rung of the spiral. I felt a euphoria, the same kind of feeling you hear comes along with near death experiences. There was a white tunnel, and at the end of it, warmth, my most cherished memories, perhaps more laughter. I parted ways with myself. So long, Barry, I said aloud. It's been nice being you. And there I went. - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - 10:25 AM A bunch of them dragged me through the pitch dark, and the only sounds I could hear were the crunches of nothing beneath their feet and the dragging silence of my corpse against the void. I could imagine neither the box they build around me nor the nails driven in to bind together the planks. I neither screamed in dread nor scratched in panic at the thumps of dirt, shovelful by shovelful, against the door. I wasn't aware that any of it was happening. Otherwise, I would've surely said something. - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - 10:42 AM In the absence of everything, all measures of time were equal and equally meaningless. I did not know how long I had been gone from all existence. It must have been some time. His betrodden horse stank of premature rot, and the tattered rags he wore paper thin slowly frayed off into the vast emptiness just beyond ourselves. He had a familiar face. It was the face I reckoned I'd see when my time came, even when I reckoned such an idea quite frankly implausible. "You've made it," he said, handing me a canteen. I hastily unscrewed the cork and drank until I coughed up water. "What is all this?" I asked. "Hell if I'd even know how to begin answering that one." He chuckled to himself and helped me on his tired horse. He decided on a course, and walking beside the animal, led it forward, steering it by the bridle. There was nowhere in particular to go. "Am I dead?" "Depends on who you ask." "What's that supposed to mean?" He struck a match to light a cigarette and shrugged. "In your opinion, am I dead?" "You sure do ask a lot of questions. You know that?" "It's kind of my job." A staircase appeared in front of us. It was the shape of a square coiled spring, I imagined. I had not seen it in the dark distance. I felt like there was a spotlight on as I swam on corpseback, but I could not see the staircase even as it was in front of me. "This is as far as I can take you." He slid me out the saddle, and I floated there. I was unable to tell which direction was up. "What am I supposed to do now?" "Take the staircase." He assured me it was there. "Take it where?" "Beats me." He chuckled. "I've never been up there myself, and nobody's ever come back the other way." I mumbled something and started off somewhere, but he put a hand on my shoulder. "Oh, and Barry, I know this is none of my business." His eyes pierce the blind horizon. "To hell with your mother. You're doing just fine." "Huh?" I felt stung into a confused stupor. "Every man has his set of stairs to climb, and plenty of good men have had plenty more steps to climb back up than you got." The absurdity had all but worn off on me. I had come to accept that this might never end as I waited for it to all be over and thought, this was what it had been like all along. - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - 10:47 AM The climb seemed like it took forever. When I reached the top, Johnny was there, sitting with his legs dangling over the edge where the last step stopped short of a long fall. He flicked the used end of a cigarette plummeting down, the dim bright only light shrinking quickly out of view. From it, perhaps, somewhere unseen, a universe exploded into being, but that was neither here nor there. "You ever wish you could go back in time?" asked Johnny, alerting that he had noticed me there. I take a seat next to him on the edge of the stairs, too cautious to lean and look down below. I asked him what he meant by that. He scratched his head and didn't respond immediately, so I asked him what he'd do differently. "I don't remember damn near enough of it to know what I did in the first place," he said. "I'd write it all down." It looked like he was going to continue, but he didn't, and curiosity had me peeking over the last step. It made me feel dizzy, and I immediately straightened back and reached for the step behind me. "Careful there." I asked him how long he'd been sitting up there. "Shit." He made recollecting calculations. "Probably a year, maybe more." I asked him what he planned to do. He had always claimed to be a man who avoided plans at all costs. "I guess I got two choices. I could keep sitting here, or I could do something else, and the longer I sit here, the more I get to thinking about doing something else." He stood up, nervously shaking his way upright. "I've been thinking about doing something else." He held his arms out forward, one of his palms inwardly downward facing the other, downwardly inward. He carefully dipped, bending at the knees, all the poise of an ungraceful diver. - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - 11:33 AM Johnny and I were back in the car, and thankfully, he allowed me to make the return walk without the blindfold so long as I vowed never to reveal anything about the location where he had taken me. The sun shone high in the midday. Everything felt bright and vivid again, and I didn't know if I was still feeling some of the effects from earlier or just appreciating more what was there all along. I felt twenty years younger, even if it was only temporary. The years had all come back by noon so barely later on that day. I asked him what was next. "I figured we'd just head back." He started the ignition and lit another cigarette. He looked so unaffected. I couldn't believe it. A procession of ghosts followed behind the car as it rolled along the gravel back towards the highway. It may not have been what I was expecting, and the ride back turned solemnly quiet, but it was in this quiet solemnity that all my questions were answered. He rarely looked back, and when he did, it was only for a quick glance in the rearview. In those momentary glimpses, his eyes changed. A mischievous spark flashed through them, and he'd hear the ghosts chasing him as his grip tightened against the wheel, hands firmly at ten and two. He resisted the urge to let them catch him. At one point I asked him what the hell was in that drink. I told him it tasted like shit. "Close enough." He nodded and drummed his finger against the wheel. "Close enough."
