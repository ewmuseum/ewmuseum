ORIGINS: The Lord of Fever and Plagues (Part I of V)

Date: 04/25/10 "Pazuzu, Lord of Fevers and Plagues, Dark Angel of the Four Winds with rotting genitals from which he howls through sharpened teeth over stricken cities…." -William S. Burroughs, Cities of the Red Night The Manila Folder The professor called on the student in the back. Shifting in his seat, the long-haired boy cocked his head to the side and grinned. "Wait. So for your job you, like, go around the world and just stay at peoples’ houses, like, for free and stuff? And you just sit around writing stuff. And do their drugs?" Professor Younan smiled. It was his eighth year as a professor of anthropology at the university and the students never failed to amuse him. "Not exactly," he explained. "But yes. That’s a good, although crude, approximation of what us field anthropologists do." "Rad," the student replied, blowing his long hair out of his face. "I wish I could just bounce from town to town, smokin’ everyone’s shit and sleeping on their couches." "Mr. Anderson, you don’t need to be anthropologist to be a hippie," retorted professor Younan. The students laughed. "Furthermore, huffing paint doesn’t count as an indigenous ritual, except maybe in Detroit." Professor Younan noticed that the students’ face had turned a little pink as he shifted uneasily in his chair. Ease off a bit, , the professor thought to himself. To be sure, it was great to have tenure. But it would best to show more restraint with his choice of words in the future, he thinks. He'd better not take it too far with these freshmen students, especially in only the second week of the semester. "Besides, Mr. Anderson," professor said, his tone more light now, "I doubt that you would keep well-organized field notes. But I digress." The student nodded his head in agreement and sat back in his seat. Professor Younan pulled out a laser pointer and directed the students’ inattentive gaze back to the front of the room where the carving of an odd figure was projected onto the screen. It was monstrous face carved in black clay and had bulging eyes, elongated curling horns, and big flaring nostrils. "Back to the question at hand, folks," the professor began, pacing back and forth. "The funny thing is, Pazuzu was known as a god of ‘fever and plague,’ and was known for bringing the Northeastern winds and pestilence. So why is it, do you think, that they held the demon in such high regard?" Professor Younan stopped and rubbed his chin for a moment, forcing a dramatic pause; a leftover trick from his days in the theater, no doubt. "Why did the Assyrians use the Pazuzu figure in amulets to protect them from the type of evil that he himself was implicitly a part of? It is just one of many-" He never had a chance to finish the sentence. The bell rang and the students exited the room quicker than the shelf life of a McDonald's dollar-menu item. Professor Younan let out a chuckle. Kids. "Professor," said a smiling young Somoan woman. She ran up to him carrying an armload of books, holding them tight to her chest. "Yes, Rachael?" the professor asked. He couldn’t help but crack a smile. Rachael was very attractive. From her dark skin to her lightly set brown eyes, she was a very beautiful woman. Maybe it was her charming demeanor, or maybe it was her brilliance as an academic. He couldn’t be sure. But what Arpad Younan did know was that Rachael was the kind of student who sometimes made him want to break certain university rules. But no. That could never be. Not merely because of the complications with university policies, either. Rather, Rachael was so brilliant that it had often occurred to professor Younan –as well as to many of his esteemed colleagues- that she was simply too smart. In fact, she was almost out of his league. And as chair of the anthropology department, that said a lot. Rachael was a graduate student now, an ethno-botanist by trade, and was one of the university’s best young minds. "Professor?" She smiled up at him, holding a small manila folder. "Oh, sorry, dear," the professor said, shaking away his thoughts. Rachael smiled. "I’ve been doing some research on Pazuzu in my spare time," she said with a beaming smile, gently placing the folder in his hand. "And I think you might want to take a look at this." " ‘Spare time?’ " professor Younan said, as he gently chuckled from behind his large horn-rimmed glasses. "You throw that term around quite loosely, hmm?" As a student athlete, full-time employee at the Anthropology department’s botany lab, as well as president of countless school clubs, Rachael had very little spare time to, well, spare. But Rachael simply shrugged away the implied compliment, beckoning for the professor to open the manila folder. "Anyway," the professor grinned after a brief silence, "let’s have a look, shall we?" Of Mice, Men, and the Miraculous Malevolence of Myopic Man-made Machines. (Or: "All Hail Technology!") Once upon a time man lived in harmony with nature. This was because once upon a time man recognized that he was part of nature. Hence for a while man lived in harmony with nature and, consequently, in harmony with himself. Whether these golden days made up the bulk of pre-history, or were short lived, is anyone’s guess. Because in that age time itself was timeless; the moons, and seasons, and ages went by without anyone needing to consult their Rolex. There was no concept of "now and then," but only the all-consuming present where you either you were or you were not. You either had food and ate food, or you starved. You either had clothes and wore clothes, or you were naked and cold. You either had a mate and fucked her silly, or you were the Neolithic social equivalent of SurReal, and hung out with other spouse-less men, getting drunk and finger-painting pictures of bison on cave walls. Yes. Those were the days. However, like month-old milk, this golden age would not stay. Long story short, the fucking world fell apart. Some say it happened because it’s in our DNA; That’s how societies were meant to evolve. Agriculture allowed people to better prepare and store food, thus giving them better control over their resources. More resources meant less work in the day, which meant more people could sit around on their asses smoking weed, thinking up crazy shit. Then these stoner dreams were related –nearly inaudibly, sometimes- to the sober craftsmen. This down-to-earth breed of men then used their steady, sober hands to invent wonderful things, thus turning stoner dreams into material reality. The wheel is a good example. The "clap-on/clap-off" light bulb, and chia pets, arguably, are not. Any undergraduate textbook could tell you the rest: These technologies led to the specialization of labor, which led to class strife, which led to warfare. You get the point. Yet, others blame the fall on Eve. Curiosity not only "killed the cat." It also tempted the first pussy. Eve ate the fruit from the "Knowledge of Good Evil," which brought about the downfall of man. But honestly, how many kids eat psilocybin mushrooms in college and manage their trips without releasing all the evils of the world? Hence the skepticism of the "Garden of Eden" theory. Still, others maintain that the fall was a punishment from the scorned gods of the golden age. The story goes that as soon as man became self-sufficient enough to have finally reached the point where he no longer needed the gods, there was a shift in paradigm. As the last icons were buried in the dry desert sands, and the former objects of power were suddenly transformed into mere archeological trinkets, the gods withered away but did not die. Triumphant, man thought he had conquered the gods through his mastery of nature. But all he really managed to do was anger them. And oh, were they pissed. Hanbi, here, was one of these gods. And he remembers those times quite well. Death of the Immortals? It was 5,000 B.C., ancient Mesopotamia. Or, as the locals called it, Pre-York. Needless to say, the architecture wasn’t much to look at in those days. Even for a regionally renowned god such as HANBI! such accommodations were the norm: a small, square hut made up of dried clay. A slightly angled straw roof with patches sealed with cow dung. Yet, for all its trite plainness, it was his home. And leaving wasn’t going to be easy. Reveling in the misery of his own dreadful situation, Hanbi passed nervously back and forth, with his long tail dragging across the cold stone floor. A cool breeze came through the "open" window that was a simple rectangle cut out of the side of the clay wall. This reminded Hanbi of the approaching night, with all it's dreadful scenarios that played out in his mind. He swung about on a giant paw and called out to his servant, "Hey, Bob, is my freakin’ chariot ready to go, or what? We don’t have all night, dude." Bob neighed in response, shaking his head. Bob was what they called a "unitaur." He was similar to a centaur –a strange being with a horse’s body and a man’s torso- but also had a light green unicorn horn sticking out of his forehead. "Almost, master. As soon as those dirty greasers get the wheels fixed." Bob flared up on his hind-legs, expressing his annoyance at the slow pace of the imported Mexican mechanics. (Or pre-Aztec, as they will be known as someday.) The point being, cheap labor was never reliable in any era. Hanbi nodded his head in implicit acknowledgment. "It’s my fault, Bob. I should have invested in one of those damn new carriages with ‘round’ wheels, or whatever the fuck they’re called. But you know how the economy is. All this damn money being poured into the war in Mesopotamia. "No blood for firewood,’ I’ve always said." "Yes, those square wheels just don’t get the job done," Bob agreed between mouthfuls of hay. Hanbi sighed. HANBI!, the immortal god of…well, the historical record really isn’t too clear on that point...was a very charismatic god. And, in many ways, a resourceful and powerful demon. For thousands of years the dozens of villages in the surrounding hills had worshiped him as their idol of choice. Sure, they gave him a little too much credit when the weather was good and the crops were plentiful. Conversely, they lay too much guilt at his feet when the seasons were ripe with plagues and famine. But just because they morons didn’t know enough to cook their pork and chicken all the way through, that didn’t make him a bad god, did it? Regardless of the weighty responsibility of maintaining the spiritual needs of an entire society, Hanbi gladly accepted these people attributing their fortunes to him. The entertainment value alone made it worthwhile. Tending humans at times was much like tending a flock of sheep. They were stubborn, needy beings who required a great deal of herding and attention. And one evening a few centuries back, after overindulging in some of Buddha’s rice wine, Hanbi even pinned down a few of "the flock" and shaved them for their "wool." Needless to say, the pubic hair garment wasn’t quite what Mrs. Hanbi was expecting for an anniversary gift. But that’s neither here nor there. The point was, Hanbi was content with his lifestyle. He’d take the good along with the bad. Hell, compared to some of the other lesser deities in the region, Hanbi considered himself pretty damn lucky. A roof over his horned head, three hot meals a day, and six sacrificial virgins a moon cycle; Who could complain? But all going to change soon. As the new tribesmen swept in from the north with their strange customs and exotic religions, the locals were beginning to mimic the lives of these invaders. They had perfected the art of agriculture to a fine degree by establishing villages with communal irrigation systems. Now people were no longer beholden to the gods for their sustenance but to this new almighty Technology. "Aqua-terrorism," Hanbi called these pompous architects of man-made rivers. Over the past few centuries, the world had begun to spin out of control. The more people turned to the razzle-dazzle of these new fads, the more they forgot about their old gods. Over the years, Hanbi felt his grip on the region slipping. In fact, because of these newcomers, with their fancy ideas on farming, mathematics, and government, Hanbi was in his current conundrum: Evidently, a well-armed brigade of these barbarians were on their way to the village with the idea of slaying Hanbi. This wasn’t the first time that Hanbi had encountered such problems. But this time was different. There was a legion of them. They had icons of their own, allegedly more powerful, gods. And they had a fancy new armor made out of some rock-like material that was a bit thicker than Hanbi’s wooden shield. "Shit," he thought out loud. "I’m really fucked." But as he paced his modest dwelling now, pondering their future as a demon race, their came a sudden knock at the door. Hanbi nodded to Bob, the unitaur, who reached out and opened the wooden door with a quick kick of his back legs. "Master," a voice called out between heavy breaths, "we must hurry. They’re almost here…" The very short hooded figure gasped for air as he leaned up against the doorway to support his exasperated frame. "Ass!" Hanbi cried out, smashing a heavy fist into the wall. "How much time do we have?" The panting man leaned forward with both hands on his knees. He wiped the sweat from his head and frowned. "A matter of minutes, m’lord. Not long." Hanbi cursed the gods –namely himself- under his breath. On cue, his wife, a hideous 5,000-pound troll named Ursa, walked out of the bedroom and into the main room. She held the small child in her arms. "We have no choice, Ursa. I’m sorry," Hanbi said, in a soft tone that held discernible disbelief while also admitting the possibility of defeat. He wiped a tear from his eye and turned to address his giant wife. "There’s freaks out there now, Ursa. Real freaks. Some of them even worship gods who ask them to cut off the tips of their penises. Does that sound like a healthy form of worship?" "No," she admitted, pulling the bundle of the little demon in tighter to her forty-pound troll breast. "Master, the mules are packed," Bob said, adding "maybe we should just hop on and leave." "Yes. Fuck the greasy little Aztecs," the hooded messenger said. "It’s time to roll." "Very well then," Hanbi muttered. He motioned for his wife to make her way to the back door. "You two be on your way. Bob, you’re to follow them. And if anything happens to them…" "Yes," Bob interrupts, quite sure of what his master’s next words will be. "They will get there safely. I give you my word." "Oh, Hanbi!" the troll cried out, grasping at his arms. "Must we give our child up to such a brute?" she pleaded with her husband as mammoth troll buggers rolled down her ugly cheeks. "We have no other choice, Ursa. You know this. Besides," Hanbi added, "he’ll be safe there. In his care." The 5,000-pound hideous troll bit her lip and shook her head. She had the look of defeat in her large brown eyes. "But," Hanbi quickly added, "let me say goodbye to my son. It could be our last time together." Ursa nodded, taking the squirming demon child away from her 40-pound breast. Hanbi accepted the wriggling bundle and gently pulled back the cloth to reveal a face that only a father could love. The child had just recently shed his baby horns and in their place were little dark buttons piercing through the skin where someday the massive horns would grow. The child had wide, glowing red eyes, gnarled diamond sharp teeth, and a patch of jet black hair on his scaly scalp that erupted into sporadic sparks of flame every few seconds. He also had a pacifier nailed into his chest. Suddenly, the demon childs’ rear erupted in a short squeal, making a sound not unlike that of a raped piglet. A dribble of rank stool began to roll down its back and into his father’s hands. "Damn, he’s so stinky!" Hanbi replied. "Damn, he’s so DARK!" Bob added. The demon child’s parents nodded their heads in agreement. Ursa reluctantly fetched the demon child and began to change his diapers on a counter top made fittingly of dried poopy poop. "What about you, my husband?" Ursa asked over her shoulder. She was afraid to hear the response and did not attempt to make eye contact with her husband. "I’m going to stay," he said, flatly "They’ll kill you," was her soft reply. "And I’ll kill many of them." "We haven’t the time for such discussions!" the small hooded figure yelled, pointing toward the doorway. From somewhere down the streets there were the sounds of shouting and heavy footsteps. "Alright, but there's one last thing," said Hanbi. He reached up and pulled off his pair of dark sunglasses. They were Oakley’s imported from the future. Specifically, they were imported from a future where sunglasses didn’t even yet exist. This was merely the prototype. So, needless to say these were very rare sunglasses. Hanbi placed the glasses over his son’s wondering eyes. The demon baby giggled in delight. He let out an acidic burp which caused a stream of heavy flames to shoot up and scorch his fathers’ face. Hanbi laughed and, beaming with pride, reached down to retrieve his sizzling, juicy eyeball which had popped out and fell into his sons’ mouth. "What a wonderful child I have." The smile lasted only the briefest of moments. Hanbi whirled back toward his wife. "You’d better be going," he informed her. "From everything I’ve heard, this Satan guy isn’t the most patient fellah." Hanbi looked down at his son for one last time as molten tears of lava formed in the dark corners of his hellish eyes, and he kissed the child on the cheek. "This devil will raise you, boy, and he will raise you well. But remember this, child: you will always be my son." With that, Hanbi unsheathed his flaming sword and ran into the streets, prepared to fend off the ungodly masses of this new world order. Escorted by Bob, the centaur, and the mysterious small hooded figure, the troll and child escaped into the quiet of the dark desert night. Building a Mystery Professor Younan frowned, peering down at the contents of the manila folder. "Pazuzu, the Darkness?, huh?" "Actually, it’s pronounced "PAZUZU!" Rachael replied, her forceful pronunciation causing the professor to suddenly take a step back. "I see," professor Younan said, trying to make sense of things. "And just how long has this guy been around for?" "About four months now," Rachael said. "And any idea of who this man is or where he came from?" Rachael was amused with the knowledge that her professor’s interest had been piqued. She laughed. "What do you think I am, professor? An FBI agent?" Professor Younan couldn’t help but chuckle to himself. "Of course, dear, of course." He turned his focus back on the manila folder. More specifically, he gazed at the picture on the front, which was the most recent photo of GCW’s PAZUZU, THE DARKNESS. It’s a somewhat off-centered photo of PAZUZU and Gnark being escorted out of GCW’s press conference room by an array of confused security guards. A charming couple, he thinks to himself. Peering into the elaborate features of the odd-looking man and his impish sidekick, something suddenly draws in the professor’s undivided attention. "Rachael," the professor said, with a start. "Do you see this?" He pointed to something in the picture. "The sunglasses?" Rachael asked, raising a brow. After a moment of confusion, a sudden illumination crept into her dark eyes. She gasped for air. "You don’t think that-" "It’s possible," professor Younan said. His limbs began to feel numb, causing him to sit back in his chair. He anxiously adjusted his glasses. "I can’t see how. But it just might be possible…" "Do you think we should-" "No!" came professor Younan’s quick, forceful reply. "Not a soul until we can confirm its existence. Until then," the professor said, drawing in a deep breath in order to carefully choose his next words, "I think it’s best we pay a visit to Iraq –just me and you, I mean- and run a few…tests." "Very well, professor. When do we leave?" "Now," he replied, reaching for his coat.
