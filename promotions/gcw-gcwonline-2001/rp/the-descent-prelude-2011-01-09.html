The Descent (Prelude)

9 January 2011
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 01/09/11 You felt your grubby, brittle fingernails fracture as they clung onto sanity; a stamp crumbling them to mustardy dust. As you fell into Rasa’s black, bubbling tar pit, there were no hands reaching to pull you out, only to buckle the straps of its prickly straightjacket. This is how you fell. The Father Lawrence Wolfenden’s baritone voice carried a natural authority which enslaved the ear. “Are we talking about the same girl?” he asked. Frustration collided with disbelief, their ferocious impact carving wrinkles deep his brow. A brown leather wallet creaked open, and from behind three vivid photos of his son, his stockbroker’s embossed card and an old train ticket, he retrieved a folded, faded photo of a plain-faced girl whose surliness dimmed the camera flash. “This is the girl we’re talking about?” Fortified within castle walls adorned with his framed qualifications, Dr. Henry Dain withstood the volley of intimidation. He’d felt parent after parent erode his academic pride, treating him as little more than a nosey child-minder when the novelty of shepherding infinitely inquisitive new life into wide world had worn off. Their signature on his cheque was akin to them signing away their responsibility. He was the interpreter through which their family conversed. “Yes, Mr. Wolfenden, that is who we’re talking about,” he replied, his voice a soothing, non-judgemental monotone. “Karina really is one of the brightest young women I’ve encountered, and if we can work together to focus her o-” Lawrence muted Henry, diverting his senses’ focus to sight. An open collar was enough to boil virulent hostility to the surface; a beaded bracelet enough for him to start playing with his food. “With all due respect, ‘Doctor’,” he stated, each bold word a belittling poke to the chest. “My wife and I have been paying for her to attend the best schools money can buy, and none of her teachers have seen even an inkling of this promise you see in her. She can barely hold a conversation without drifting off somewhere in her head. She’s lazy, she’s rude and she has no ambition.” What he could have said was, she’s my daughter by blood, only. He saw nothing of himself in her, other than eye colour, and even the green irises she’d inherited glistened with a disobedient mischief he found unfamiliar. During the rant, Henry realised that Lawrence had not once addressed his daughter by name. They were three syllables which tasted sour on his tongue. “She’s bored,” was the Doctor’s rebuttal; his diagnosis. “Bored? She’s bored, is she?” Lawrence thundered, his words erupting within Henry’s ears. “She can be bored at a public school. She can be bored without blowing the ridiculous allowance I give her. Or maybe, just maybe, she could actually give classes her attention rather than staring out the window daydreaming.” Lawrence’s fingers subconsciously tugged at his collar as a sudden flush of frustration was expelled from his core. He couldn’t remember at what age he went to tuck his daughter in and found a stranger in her bed. “Read her school notebooks,” Henry challenged, a placid core maintaining his words’ calm tone. “You’ll find that there are about half as many pages as their should be.” “I don’t follow,” said Lawrence, his words blunted. “I expect you do not,” Henry finally lectured, every word pronounced and finely tuned to resonate at the guilt frequencies. “Your daughter does the work, and she does it with demotivating ease. In fact, Karina does it so quickly that she actually gets bored in the time it takes others to catch up, so she tears the page out and does it all over again… only she’s a little less interested in it second time over, understandably. Karina does all this, and still has time to stare out the window, desperately searching for something else to occupy her mind. I assume that these teachers you‘re paying for didn‘t relay this to you?” There was a pause; the blissful silence within which Dr. Henry Dain basked. In this perilous bubble which could be burst at any second, a parent’s judgement had been overturned, and their thoughts desperately tried to rewire themselves so that the lit fuse of blame didn’t blow up in their face. He waited for the twitches above the eyes and the pursing of the lips. “If you’ve got your foot planted on the accelerator but don’t shift out of first gear, then you’re going to get a blown engine,” he pressed, reading the retreat in Lawrence’s body-language. “Karina is growing up, fast, and her mind needs to shift gears frequently. If she’s given the proper mental stimulation, then I have little doubt that she can achieve anything she sets her mind to.” “So what are you saying, exactly?” Lawrence asked, trying to squeeze Dain’s words into a bite-sized morsel he found palatable. “That my daughter is some kind of genius?” “I’m saying that she’s whatever she wants to be, Mr. Wolfenden,” Henry answered, reservation restraining the eagerness of his words. He knew from experience that the brightest of sparks, if blown stray, could trigger the most destructive of wildfire infernos. “Given the right guidance. But, as I said, she’s growing up and her brain is racing at a million miles an hour; we need to make sure it doesn’t crash. Because, with a mind like hers, if it ever does…”
