Statement Regarding Chris Benoit

26 June 2007
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
President Steven Caldera made the following statement this afternoon regarding the Chris Benoit situation. Chris Benoit was one of the most talented wrestlers I ever had the privilege of watching. It's no secret that much of my early wrestling career was based around Chris Benoit's considerable talents. Many of GCW's superstars owe their roots in wrestling, in some form or another, to Chris Benoit. After early news of his death broke, I got literally dozens of requests from members of the GCW roster asking to be allowed to pay tribute to Benoit in the ring this week, as they have modeled their styles after him for so many years. Unfortunately, new details were released that cast a shadow on the death of this talented wrestler. I'm quite sure all of you know the details by now, and if you don't I'll leave it to you to track them down on your own. They aren't hard to find. Suffice it to say, the story that emerged was one of the most tragic and horrifying that we in this industry have ever had to deal with. I felt it necessary to appear and make a statement because of the implications this event has not only for GCW, but for the entire wrestling industry. As GCW continues to strive to be a leader in this business, I feel it is important that we seek to understand why this terrible event happened, and what we can do to prevent it from ever happening again. I can imagine no greater horror than having to endure what Benoit's family did this past weekend. And I can imagine to greater sorrow than what must be experienced presently by Benoit's surviving family and friends. Of course, GCW extends its greatest condolences to all who knew and worked with Chris Benoit. On WWE's broadcast last night, his friends from the wrestling business painted a picture of a man who was beloved by all he encountered, and a man who loved his family. The man described by those who knew him, from within the wrestling business and without, was quiet but kind and quite incapable of the actions he is accused of. GCW is not here to dispute or confirm the allegations that have been made. But the tragedy that has taken place here is unfathomable. Still, it is our responsibility to investigate this thoroughly. GCW cares very deeply for each and every one of its athletes, as well as anyone who has ever stepped into the squared circle and fought for our great sport. Today our entire sport mourns, and must take a step back and honestly assess the general health of our stars. GCW has had mandatory drug testing since its inception. We have always had the strongest commitment to the welfare of our stars. We offer complete health benefits, and I personally have never hesitated to give a star time to rest and heal should they feel it is necessary. Our roster is often derided for being among the smallest in all of wrestling, but that to me has always been a point of pride. For we choose to entertain not with the size of our muscles, but with the size and fortitude of our hearts. Wrestlers deserve a great deal of respect, a respect that is hard to come by. We are accused of being fake or phony, when we are in fact anything but. This tragic event should serve to underscore just how real our business is. It takes a truly special person to be able to do what we do, but even then the human body is not meant to undergo the systematic abuse that wrestlers endure. I myself was forced into early retirement due to the massive damage I suffered. Rather than risk further, permanent injury to either my body or mind, I chose to end my career and pursue my interests in wrestling through another avenue. There are many wrestlers who choose to continue to wrestle in spite of the risks. They do so not out of stupidity, but out of a true passion for this sport. Those who know wrestling best know that it becomes your whole life, it infects your blood, and it consumes you quite completely. I say this not out of disdain but out of true gratitude, for I am still honored to be a part of this great industry. However, the addictive nature of our sport makes it that much harder to say goodbye to. I still have moments, seated behind my desk or standing in the bright lights of a GCW ring wearing a suit rather than pads and boots, and I feel a wound deeper than my damaged knees. Still, the wrestling industry has a responsibility to the health of its wrestlers, along with their friends and family. This tragic event makes it quite clear that we are collectively guilty of not being more vigilant. I am not here to pass judgment on Chris Benoit. Time and fate will judge him. We at GCW and in the greater community of which we are a part did not know Chris Benoit well enough to ever claim to know what thoughts went through his head during his final moments. And though it is quite clear that Chris Benoit will bear a great deal of guilt, I strongly believe that our task now is quite clear. We must not judge Chris Benoit, for he has already suffered his judgment. We must instead look inward to ourselves, and ask what role we, as part of the wrestling industry, might have played in this unfortunate event. We have a responsibility now to take this event quite seriously. We mustn't pass all the blame to Chris Benoit and hold ourselves as innocent. I refer, of course, to our parts in an industry that has, for too long, turned a blind eye to the systematic drug abuse that has caused the deaths of a growing number of wrestlers. While that is NOT an accusation on my part regarding Chris Benoit, it simply cannot be denied that steroids and other drugs are still prevalent in our industry, and we must charge ourselves with their destruction. Chris Benoit's friend Eddie Guerrerro died a few years ago under surprising circumstances. He had wrestled earlier that week, and died abruptly and unexpectedly. His body simply gave out. While I certainly do not wish to besmirch the great legacy Eddie left behind, his is a story that should serve as proof of what years of systematic drug abuse does to the human body. I have been asked quite often, "How is drug abuse the company's problem? How are we complicit? It is the man that chooses to take them or not." Quite right. The man must accept responsibility for his choices. But we do share in the blame, for we set for them a schedule that the human body cannot cope with alone. It must be aided and supplemented, and the result is a history of systematic drug abuse among many of its superstars. Every single GCW superstar has been tested and is clean. We keep close tabs on our stars, not out of suspicion but out of genuine concern. We truly hope that the wrestling industry as a whole begins to accept that the health of its stars is far more important than the extra revenue generated by keeping them on the road. Wrestlers are on the road an average of 250 days out of the year. GCW's global tours, like our current tour through Australia, keep many of them away from their families for extended periods of time. This is an extremely difficult business. Our wrestlers do enjoy large salaries due to the popularity of our sport, the quality of our product, and the sheer frequency of our live events. While GCW only broadcasts one live event a week, we hold two "house shows" that require further travel and toil on the part of our stars. If a star's body breaks down from this period of systematic abuse, and they turn to drugs to maintain their level of performance, how does that profit the company? We have a long-term investment in our stars. From a strictly business standpoint, they are assets that cannot be repaired or replaced. But from a moral standpoint, they are human beings with families, and we have a responsibility to them to ensure that they leave our sport with financial security, as well as a body that will allow them to enjoy a full, long life. GCW believes that the numerous and increasingly tragic deaths of these wrestlers, who gave their lives for our sport, indicates a failure in policy by all wrestling promoters. We praise our heroes for their iron wills, for wrestling endlessly without complaint. But now we must realize that the pressures we place on them are simply too great. We must be willing to sacrifice revenues, just as wrestlers must be willing to accept a more modest income, in exchange for a reduction in the time spent in the ring. GCW is hereby cutting one house show from its schedule each week, to begin upon the conclusion of our Australian tour. Foreign tours will continue to feature these extra live events, but during our time at home will we significantly reduce the schedule. After the end of the calendar year, it is our intention to eliminate all house shows and untelevised events from the schedule altogether. Clearly, this represents a major financial loss. House shows provide valuable extra revenue that helps pay for our high production costs and advertising. But a show is not worth producing or advertising if all its stars will wind up dead. We have a responsibility to do what is necessary to ensure that the next generation of wrestlers is able to retire healthy and happy. I will never hesitate to demand mandatory retirement of anyone who simply cannot physically endure wrestling any more, regardless of the implications on business. All of professional sports is dealing with this issue right now, and we must join them in being proactive to reach a solution. This incident itself might not end up being connected to this issue. Perhaps time will tell that this event was nothing more than a bizarre tragedy with no connections to the wrestling business whatsoever. I, myself, believe that this incident is intimately rooted in our business. Even if I am wrong, this incident can still serve to spawn positive changes in our industry. I won't tell you how to feel about Chris Benoit. I will not judge the man for his actions in life or death. But I will use this terrible, terrible event to enact policies that will ensure that such a thing never happens again in any shape or form. -Steven Caldera
