GCW Writing Reference

undated
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
1. Structure and the Basics -Use present tense! -No formatting is necessary when submitting for the shows. Basic HTML may be added; for italics, for bold, for centering, etc. However, no general formatting is necessary. -When submitting your text, keep in mind that what you see is more or less what you get. Take care not to have extra spaces between paragraphs, weird formatting, etc. -GCW uses a script-style format. Speech is denoted with the speaker’s last name followed by a colon. For example- Bryan: Look at that move! Yale: Wow! 2. The Announcers -The Play-by-Play announcer is James Bryan. He is a middle-aged, well educated man who is very well respected in the wrestling industry. He is well liked by the fans and by most of the wrestlers. However, he does tend to favor the babyfaces. As such, he tends to be quite hard on heels, and therefore he is popular with the crowd, but less so with heel wrestlers. He is an old-school personality, but is not so out of touch that he doesn’t appreciate modern wrestling. -The Color Commentator is David Yale. Yale is outspoken and charismatic. While he does not necessarily side with heels or faces, he tends to have a looser interpretation of the rules. As such, heel or face, he tends to favor wrestlers who do what it takes to win, regardless of what that might entail. The only person he consistently admires, however, is himself. -Despite their differences, Bryan and Yale get along very well. While they do argue points during matches due to their differing personalities, they have been commentating together for many years. As such, each respects the other and knows the other very well. They may not always agree, but they are always on the same page when it comes to commentating. -When referring to Bryan on camera, Yale calls him "JB." He does not call him "James" or "Bryan," he just calls him "JB." When referring to Yale, Bryan calls him "Dave." Not "David" or "Yale," but "Dave." For example- Bryan: Did you see that, Dave? Yale: I sure did, JB! 3. The Interviewers -The Lead Interviewer is Kevin Jacobs. He is middle aged and heavyset with glasses. Despite his somewhat nerdy appearance, Jacobs has evolved into an edgy backstage reporter. Jacobs is not afraid to ask tough questions and dig deep to get his scoop. Jacobs will not cower down, but if extreme violence is threatened, Jacobs will back off. Still, if Jacobs is forced to back off, he will try a different, sneakier approach to get what he wants to know. Because of this, the crowd generally likes Kevin Jacobs. When writing Jacobs, keep in mind that he is well spoken and quite tenacious. He should be used primarily for big in-ring interviews, or events of major significance backstage. For smaller interview roles, use one of the other interviewers. Also, due to his frequent use, do not physically assault Jacobs. -The other interviewers are Sally Ford and Barry Goldstein. Ford is young and devastatingly beautiful; a tall, curvy blonde who routinely dresses in subtly seductive attire. Despite her appearance, she is also respected for her brains. While not as tenacious or insightful as Jacobs, Ford is perfectly capable of holding her own in an interview. She will not, however, under any circumstances risk physical violence. Goldstein is in his late fifties and a veteran of the wrestling business. He is, however, a wrestling purist and quite disapproving of the current trends of sports-entertainment. As such, he has a tendency to talk down to the people he interviews, considering them to be weak or less than capable. This attitude is not distracting, however, as he manages to be subtle and hide his feelings more often than not. Because of his lack of respect for the modern industry, Goldstein is also willing to be influenced to spin stories in anyone’s favor for the right price. 4. The Officials -GCW has five referees for you to use: The only mandatory rules to remember is that Josh Briggs is the Lead Official, and therefore only officiates main event matches or other contests of great significance. Otherwise, use one of the other officials. However, each official has a unique personality, and matchwriters wishing to take an easy step to going above and beyond may wish to incorporate these traits. -Josh Briggs is a longtime veteran and does not back down from anyone. As the Lead Official he has the most authority of anyone in the ring, and is absolutely rock solid as a referee. He knows where to be and when, never gets distracted and knows exactly what’s going on in the ring at all times; it is almost impossible to get one over on Briggs. He is also quite tough and only rarely gets caught up in the action or get hurt. A match officiated by Briggs is almost always going to go down fairly and evenly. -David Fellows is a younger official whose inexperience occasionally shows through. Though all GCW officials are of the highest quality, Fellows can be manipulated by more clever wrestlers. He does have tremendous energy, however, and is an enthusiastic official. Though this permits him to keep up with the fastest paced matches, Fellows does occasionally get too close to the action resulting in some collateral damage. Need a ref bump? Use Fellows. -Hal Jenkins is GCW’s most lasseiz-faire official. Though he won’t tolerate blatant cheating, he does tend to permit a bit more than other referees, allowing for slightly more brutal matches. Obviously heels fond of cheating might fare better with Jenkins, but Jenkins will step in if needed. Jenkins doesn’t care for countouts and draws, so he tends to count very slowly in such situations. His approach is to let the wrestlers settle the bout with a minimum of interference, so use Jenkins if you want your wrestlers to be able to go at it without worrying about a referee stepping in. -Timothy Vale is a longtime veteran and is highly respected. Very old-school, Vale is quite vigilant in watching for and preventing shortcuts. Vale knows how wrestlers think and won’t easily be manipulated or intimidated. However, because of his advancing age, Vale doesn’t hold up well during long or fast-paced matches. An expert at officiating methodical, technical contests but less enthusiastic about hardcore or high-flying wrestlers. Vale will rarely become a storyline himself, but typically ensures a slower pace in the ring. -Cameron Wrigley is a heelish referee. While outwardly impartial, Wrigley secretly respects those wrestlers who are willing to do what it takes to secure a victory (and, as such, is David Yale’s favorite referee). Wrigley won’t necessarily favor heels over faces, but heels tend to fare better in matches officiated by Wrigley. Wrigley also has no desire to get caught up in the action, and as such tends to keep his distance. He does, however, like the spotlight and is known to grandstand, so if there is no danger of violence Wrigley might step in and insert himself into the scene just to get his face on camera. -None of these officials should ever really be used to define the story of a match; they are simply there to add depth to the overall content of the show and a sense of identity to some of our most important characters. For example, do not use Cameron Wrigley to blatantly cheat a face out of a win; he’d be fired for such a thing. Instead, if a face has a heel on the ropes, Wrigley might step in and cut him off for a moment. Consider such small details and incorporate them into your matches to make your product that much better. 5. Writing Matches -Writing weekly matches doesn’t have to be a chore. Use the following tips and guidelines to speed the whole process up. -Weekly matches do not need to be long. For lower card matches, two to four pages is sufficient. For upper card matches, four to six pages is preferable. Anything beyond that, of course, is welcome but not necessary. WorldWide matches do not need a lot of filler; the purpose of weekly matches is to convey a purpose in an interesting way. As such, opening stages with a lot of basic grappling can be omitted. Think of WorldWide matches as extended summaries; you simply need to tell the story. Don’t get bogged down with writing a complicated wrestling match. Be sure to balance the match fairly and allow each competitor time to do their thing. But don’t worry about churning out extensive weekly matches. Supercards and Pay-Per-View matches, obviously, are more important and therefore need to be more extensive. Supercards tend to be focused more on storytelling, so extensive match writing still isn’t necessary. Pay-Per-Views, however, occur only four times a year, and therefore long, well-thought-out and well-developed matches are a necessity. -Start the match with a bit of commentary. Don’t just jump right into the entrances; each match could use a bit of exposition to set things up. Doing this helps the reader understand what they’re about to read and why. This doesn’t need to be long; just some quick (but unrushed) banter between Bryan and Yale about the significance of the match. -Copy and paste the character’s entrances into the match. Be sure to add more commentary during the entrances; a few lines about the character’s current feud, recent matches, or other important information helps keep the match interesting and relevant. -If you’re writing a WorldWide match, you’re already at a page or more. Now you just need another few pages of text. Making liberal use of commentary helps make a match look longer; the frequent line breaks extend the match down the page. Don’t cut into the action too much, though. Be sure to give each wrestler time to display his or her skills. Unless it’s specifically a squash match, each wrestler should be given a chance to get over. After some back and forth, a finish may be added. Be sure to follow the planned finish if one is provided. -After the finish, be sure to add more commentary. Simply ending the match with the finish leaves it hanging; it needs some closure. Be sure to let Bryan and Yale sum things up and relay the significance of the match to the reader. -While it may seem tedious to add this extra commentary, adding the extra commentary makes the match longer and cuts down on the amount of filler action needed. All combined, using these tips allows you to create a sufficiently long match that gets its point across without unnecessary fuss. 6. Writing Segments -Keep segments relevant. Pure character-development segments may be allowed, but are discouraged; roleplays are the best domain for this. Remember, your job when writing for a television show is to entertain your audience as well as write your own story. So while your segment should progress a given storyline, it should also occur in a context that is both interesting and easy to understand. -Try to avoid segments featuring two characters standing around having a conversation. While there are certainly times where these are necessary, try to be creative with your setups. Don’t simply have two characters standing idly by talking to one another when you could have some action taking place. Try to include good descriptions of the scene and the characters. -Shows are written in third-person limited. As such, first-person segments are not allowed (narrated video packages are a different story; again, be creative!). Keep in mind that what the reader sees in a show is equivalent to what they would see on a TV screen. Therefore, descriptions that include inner dialogue or thoughts, or explanations of prior events are not permitted. If you need to explain something, use the commentators! Example: WRONG: Wrestler X was walking backstage, and his head was killing him. He couldn’t believe Wrestler Y got the drop on him like that; he was determined to get revenge. RIGHT: The scene opens and Wrestler X is walking backstage. He holds his head and winces, clearly in pain and furious. Bryan: Wow, looks like Wrestler X is a bit worse for the wear after that attack by Wrestler Y! Yale: Yeah, but if I know Wrestler X, he’s planning his revenge right now! -Again, open and close with some commentary. The announcers are an invaluable tool to help explain and relay information that your characters might not be able to offer. The opening commentary can be brief and helps to provide a quick impression of the scene and set things up for the reader. The closing banter can really help sum things up and hammer your point home, just in case someone missed it. While this additional commentary is not required, it is a big part of any really great seg, because it helps make things clear and easy to follow. Further, it creates a sense of continuity; remember, everything happening in your seg can be seen on the MegaTron, and is voiced over by the announcers. By including the announcers before, after, and even during your segment, you make it feel like it’s part of a whole show. -Don’t forget crowd reactions, too! While your characters might not necessarily react to them backstage, the crowd reaction comes through on the TV broadcast. They can see and hear everything on the MegaTron, too, and they’ll react to what they’re seeing. Crowd reactions can be a nice addition to a seg and helps it feel more alive. 7. Other Guidelines and Tips -Remember: Script format, Past Tense, Third-Person Limited Perspective! -The structural rules are mandatory; the other hints on how to write are just guidelines. While none of those are required, they will definitely help you write more efficiently. Also, people who make a constant effort to meet these guidelines will be producing content that looks and feels more consistent, and will therefore meet with greater success. -Don’t hesitate to use shortcuts, but be very aware of what your submission looks and reads like. When writing matches, lots of commentary can extend its length, but be cognizant of the dialogue. Don’t just write abbreviated, stunted sentences for the commentators in an effort to extend the match. Read the lines out loud if necessary, and ask if the dialogue sounds realistic. The commentators are both intelligent and witty, so great submissions will feature commentary that is both insightful and entertaining, not just functional. -Your lines of action should be well-written as well. Don’t introduce a segment with a quick one-liner; you should set up the scene with care. Detailed descriptions aren’t necessary; and, in fact, really long intros can get tedious and boring. Find a balance; get your point across with sufficient style and detail, but don’t bore your readers. -Once again, keep in mind that you’re writing for an audience. Shows are a group effort written for a wide audience; you’re not just writing for yourself, but for everyone in GCW and everyone who might read a GCW show. Therefore, be sure to consider how your submission will read to someone who has no previous knowledge of your character. Writing with a limited perspective limits your submission’s effectiveness; taking the time to set up the scene and lay things out, as well as writing content that is entertaining and compelling, is the best route to success. Naturally, you don’t need to re-establish your character every time, and you can draw on previous weeks? events. But be sure to be considerate of all readers when you write. -All support characters may be used without permission. There are two other characters that can be used: Toad and Steven Caldera. Toad is Head of GCW Security. He is usually supported by a team of security guards, so he cannot be physically assaulted. If you choose to use Toad in a segment, remember that he is a sarcastic, quick-witted smartass who is also a Hardcore legend; therefore, he’ll never, ever back down. Don’t use him if you’re writing a fight, unless you need to have it broken up. Overall, Toad doesn’t need to be used often, but he’s there if you need him. Caldera is the Owner and President of GCW. He can make matches, add or change stipulations, and basically holds all the power. While there will rarely be a situation where you won’t be allowed to use him, all ideas in which Caldera will be involved need to be approved beforehand.
