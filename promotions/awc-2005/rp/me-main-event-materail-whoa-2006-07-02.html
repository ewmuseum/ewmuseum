Date: 7-2-06 Book I The Eradication of Bad Business Zsasz wasn’t in the greatest of moods. He’d received a phone call from Kabuto Kurei, his business partner, about some bad business ventures, embezzlements and other things that went horribly awry. When Zsasz heard something like this, it only meant one thing: someone was going to die, and Zsasz was the one to execute the murder. There were two things that Zsasz loved in the world: himself and money. If it meant to kill someone to continue a correct flow of business, then so be it. Besides, Zsasz was good at what he did, leaving no trace of evidence behind, just the gory aftermath of what Zsasz did to his victims. He arrived at Tokyo, Japan at eight-thirty on the morning on June 17, one day after his client’s marriage to Stacy McFarland. How he could’ve stayed to watch Mikey and Stacy tie the knot rather than correcting problems that could’ve been corrected without him. It didn’t take a rocket scientist to find the problem and eradicate it with ease. No, they had to call Zsasz. As much as he enjoyed making two hundred and fifty million dollars a year, the eradication of bad business was on the borderline of monotony. Kabuto Kurei sent his chauffeur, Noriyuki Hajime, to pick up Zsasz. Noriyuki had been Kabuto’s chauffeur for the better part of a decade. He was perhaps fifty, small, with thick black hair, brown eyes and dressed in black. He greeted Zsasz with a smile, opened the back door with a white gloved hand and shut the door when Zsasz placed his large, muscular bulk in the backseat. Noriyuki went to driver’s seat, started up the engine and headed towards Kurei’s Manor, on the outskirts of Tokyo. Kabuto Kurei’s Manor was located on the outskirts of Tokyo for one reason: solitude. He liked his privacy and enjoyed peace and quiet more. He knew if he lived inside the large city of Tokyo, he would be literally driven insane by the insipid city folk that inhabit there. He demanded to have a manor built in the countryside of Tokyo so he wouldn’t have to put up with the city of Japan. Also, only a few miles east, he’d be a neighbor to Kenji Iwakura, the most powerful man in Japan. About forty-five minutes later, Zsasz arrived at Kabuto Kurei’s gothic-style mansion. This castle-like mansion had an ominous feel to it, as if it was haunted or felt like it was haunted. Noriyuki Hajime stopped in front of the mansion let Zsasz out and took the limo around to the other side of the mansion to park the limo. Zsasz was reluctant to enter the building; it’d been years since he’d actually been in Kabuto’s mansion. Zsasz feared nothing, but this structure always sent shivers down his spine. Zsasz made his way up the steps and let himself into Kabuto’s vast mansion. He was in the lobby, his eyes examining everything inside: a large chandelier hanging in the center of the lobby, a C-shaped staircase that lead up to the second floor, expensive Van Gogh paintings hanging on both sides of the lobby, and six guest room, three on each side. He tried to search his mind to where Kabuto kept his office. Again, it’s been years since Zsasz been in this mansion. He knew it was in a secret area of the house, but the question was where? There wasn’t just one secret compartment in this mansion; there were several. Before he could make his quest in search of Kabuto Kurei’s office, Noriyuki Hajime emerged from the garage that was located behind the staircase on the East Side. When he saw Zsasz standing in the middle of the lobby with a dumbfounded look on this face, he smiled, knowing that Zsasz had forgotten Kabuto’s secret office. Noriyuki walked toward the large, blonde man, shaking his head. Instead of antagonizing the large man, he pointed to the West Side of the staircase, not saying a word. Zsasz looked at Noriyuki quizzically for a moment, then realized his motive. Zsasz quickly bowed to the chauffeur and walked over to the West Side of the staircase, pushed the wall forward then to the right, revealing a dark passage. He took a step forward, feeling a step, then another then another, descending into the darkness. A few seconds later, he was in Kabuto’s semi lit office. To Zsasz, it looked more like a torture chamber to bring enemies in for interrogation. Sitting in the middle of the room behind a desk was Kabuto Kurei. He was in his mid forties, had long gray hair pulled back into a ponytail, a gray goatee and green eyes. He was dressed in an expensive suit with silver vertical stripes. His demeanor was obviously sour. After the news Zsasz heard, he didn’t expect his mood to change any. Standing to Kabuto’s right was a hulking man, his bodyguard named Shinichiro Tanaka. Standing six feet, six seven inches tall and probably weighed over three hundred pounds. He was bald with multiple scars all over his head, his left eye was a glass one with a target decal on the front and his right eyes was a pale gray. He too was dressed in black with a black, leather trench coat to complete the black ensemble. Kabuto Kurei stood up to greet Zsasz. Kabuto was reasonably tall, around six feet. He extended his right hand to his business partner, they shook hands, bowed and sat down. Zsasz sat in a chair in front of Kabuto’s desk and Kabuto parked himself in his chair behind his desk. Shinichiro Tanaka stood ramrod straight in his position, looking like a black clad statue. “I’m not going to beat around the bush,” Kabuto finally said. “Things in our business aren’t running smoothly, as I already told you. I should’ve listened to you and never hired Dragon Mask in the first place.” Kenshin Mamochi, otherwise known as Dragon Mask, was someone Kabuto thought he could trust. Kenshin seemed like the kind of guy who would be a good worker, a typical “yes” man, but in actuality he’s the biggest embezzling king in Japan, stealing large sums of money from large conglomerate businesses and bringing them down to their knees. The whole Dragon Mask nickname was that he was a former professional wrestler, wearing a mask similar to that of Ultimo Dragon’s, except it was a face mask, not a full head mask. “I told you that Dragon Mask was no good,” Zsasz said, putting on a banter that wasn’t necessary. All it did was made Kabuto’s mood even worse. But what was he going to do? Sic his bodyguard on Zsasz because he’s giving him a hard time about Kabuto’s poor selection of business workers? Kabuto had half a mind to do so, and Zsasz saw it in Kabuto’s green eyes. “Spare me with the false bravadoes, I’m not in the mood,” Kabuto said darkly. “Mamochi is crippling our business as we speak, taking millions from it on a monthly base. It was only two months ago where I almost had to close my empire. Something needs to be done about this, and it needs to be done posthaste.” “Of course, you need me to eradicate the problem,” Zsasz said, letting out a sigh. “Can’t you send your bodyguard to remedy this problem?” Kabuto gave Zsasz a vexed look. “You’re good at what you do, Zsasz, not leaving a trace of evidence, and you have ways of disposing bodies to not be found. Just take care of Dragon Mask, will you? And I know I’ll owe you greatly. All I want is Dragon Mask’s head on my desk, and I’ll reward you.” Zsasz sighed. “Let me guess, another one of your exotic beauties.” Kabuto nodded, smiling at his business partner. Zsasz wasn’t exactly amused, but Kabuto did have a harem of woman at his disposal, so Zsasz supposed it was better than nothing. He killed the last four women Kabuto threw at him; this one won’t be any different. “All right, fine. I’ll go and bring you his head. Since I didn’t come prepared for this, do you have a tool I could use?” Kabuto snapped his fingers and Shinichiro Tanaka produced his weapon: a Golden Tiger Katana, his trusty weapon from his Yakuza days. Fortunately for Shin, he’d gotten out of the Yakuza before he’d gotten himself deep into the underbelly of sex, violence and money. But his leaving the Yakuza almost cost him his life. Being Kabuto Kurei’s personal bodyguard was a hell of a lot better than waging war against other Yakuza clans. Shin handed his Katana to Kabuto, where Kabuto presented the weapon to Zsasz. “That should be to your liking.” he said with the utmost confidence. Zsasz nodded and took the Katana. “I’ll have Kenshin’s head on your desk shortly.” He got to his feet and made his way out of Kabuto’s office. Kabuto looked over at his bodyguard. There was something on his mind. “Do you think I should’ve told him that Dragon Mask is expecting to meet him for a fight to the death?” Shinichiro said, without looking at his employer, “Knowing you, it would have taken the fun out of everything.” Humph. “Indeed it would. Let’s see how ‘shortly’ it’ll take Zsasz to kill an assassin. I hate that Slavic bastard. Never hated anyone as much as I hate him. He’s a bad businessman, a bad partner, and when Kenshin’s done with him, I can rest easy.” Or so he hoped. Noriyuki Hajime drove Zsasz into the city of Japan. Zsasz expected that his fight with Dragon Mask would be a difficult one. Aside from being a former professional wrestler, Kenshin “Dragon Mask” Mamochi was a master in multiple disciplines of martial arts. He’d once heard rumors that he’d brutally beaten and killed a well-known mixed martial arts fighter in a King of the Cage venue a little over a year ago. It was said that after he’d brutally beaten his opponent into a bloody pulp, he broke his neck with a rear naked choke. The snap was loud enough to be heard throughout the arena. After hearing something like that, you’re in for the fight of your life, and that’s how Zsasz liked it; he never wanted to kill someone who was going to be too easy. Noriyuki stopped the limo a few hundred yards from Kenshin Mamochi’s house. “We’re here, Mr. Zsasz,” Noriyuki said. “I’ll wait here.” Zsasz said nothing. Instead, he nodded to Noriyuki and got out of the limo and made his journey to Kenshin “Dragon Mask” Mamochi’s house. It was a regular home in a regular Tokyo neighborhood. It seemed rather odd to Zsasz that someone whose been stealing money from his co-owned business with Kabuto Kurei would live in such a neighborhood. If he were Kenshin, he’d find a large house to live in and set himself up for--- Something whizzed past Zsasz’s perfect face. He leapt to one side, drawing the Golden Tiger Katana and began examining the perimeter. When he saw there was nothing amiss, he looked back to see a red shuriken stuck into the grass. This indicated that Kenshin Mamochi was ready to play. He’d always threw a red shuriken to signify that he’s ready for battle, ready to put his life on the line. But there was one problem: where was Kenshin and how did he know that Zsasz was coming to kill him? There was only one reasonable answer: Kabuto Kurei told him. Perhaps this whole eradicating bad business was a mere ploy to kill Zsasz. But why? Why would Kabuto have Zsasz killed? Unless he found out about Zsasz killing Kabuto’s collection of women, it wasn’t a reason to kill him. Kabuto killing Zsasz only meant one thing: he wanted him gone due being untrustworthy. Zsasz knew a day like this would come, but he didn’t expect it to happen now. Something whizzed by Zsasz shoulder, this time it left a mark. A second shuriken scraped Zsasz left shoulder, lacerating his shoulder and tearing a hole in his Italian tailor made suit. That got him aggravated; no one ruin his extravagant suits, NO ONE! Another shuriken soared at him, this time lacerating his left cheek on his face. He felt blood trickling down his face. With his left hand, he reached up and brushed against his laceration, coding his fingertips with blood, his temper flaring. His face... His beautiful, perfect face had been flawed. Zsasz let out a roar of rage. For the first time in a long time, someone had the audacity to alter his face with a laceration. Kenshin Mamochi had stepped over the line when it came to tampering with Zsasz’s physical perfections. In his mind’s eye, he could see Kenshin’s death be a gruesome, gory bloodbath, where he’d gut his adversary like some helpless animal and remove his entrails. Oh, it would be grand killing Kenshin Mamochi--- Zsasz out a roar and dropped to one knee. He discovered that there was a kunai throwing knife stuck in his right knee. He yanked the throwing knife out of his knee and threw it aside, looking frantically for Kenshin Mamochi. He had gotten into Zsasz’s head, something people had seldom done. Usually Zsasz was the master of human chess, now the tables had been turned on the master of the mind games. “Come on out, Mamochi!” Zsasz roared, his eyes rapidly scanning the perimeter of the neighborhood. “Come on out and face me like a man! Enough with this insipid games!” “My pleasure.” The voice came from in front of Zsasz. He turned to face the voice, expecting Kenshin Mamochi staring at him and, sure enough, Kenshin “Dragon Mask” Mamochi was right in his face, wearing his signature red dragon mask. He was wearing a pair of black, baggy pants, black combat boots and was bare on top, exposing his fully tattooed upper body of an intricate dragon design. Kenshin’s physique was completely ripped and large in muscle. He looked huge for being five feet, nine inches tall, and weighing one hundred and eighty-five pounds. Zsasz tried to straighten himself up, but Kenshin was too fast for his larger opponent. Mamochi spun to his left, raising his right leg into the air and caught Zsasz in the back of his head with a reverse spinning roundhouse kick. Zsasz saw colors flash before his eyes as he fell forward, plunging face first onto the grass. Kenshin smiled at his fallen adversary, chuckling to himself. “I thought you’d be more of a challenge than this, Zsasz,” Kenshin said. He bent down to pick up Zsasz’s weapon: Shinichiro’s Golden Tiger Katana. He examined the sword fondly, thinking of all the possibilities of killing Zsasz with this very sword. “How ironic would it be to be killed by this very sword. Kabuto handed you this sword only to die by---” Kenshin let out a roar of anguish after he heard his right leg snap in two. Zsasz managed to drive his shoulder into Kenshin’s knee, throwing all of his weight into it, bending and snapping it backward. The velocity of the break sent multiple fractures up and down his leg. Kenshin fell onto his back, clutching at his broken right leg, screaming in pain. Zsasz gingerly got to a vertical base, still feeling the throbbing pain in his right knee. “You’ve always had the problem of talking too much, Kenshin,” Zsasz said, picking up the Katana. Zsasz stood over Kenshin, pointing the blade of the Katana at Kenshin’s chest and raised the sword into the air. “I shall cease your monotonous tongue!” Zsasz drove the Katana into Kenshin’s heart. Everything was quiet. Zsasz yanked the sword out of Kenshin Mamochi’s chest, removed Kenshin’s head and sheathed the bloody sword in its scabbard. He heaved Kenshin’s headless body over one shoulder, carried his head with one free hand and marched towards Noriyuki Hajime’s limousine. Noriyuki popped the limo truck so Zsasz can store the body and went to the backseat, cradling Kenshin’s masked head in his lap as Noriyuki drove back to Kabuto Kurei’s manor. “Here’s your head,” Zsasz said, plopping Kenshin Mamochi’s head onto Kabuto Kurei’s head. “I’ll take your exotic beauty and take my leave. Kabuto Kurei looked at the head of Kenshin Mamochi’s head, coagulated blood was smeared on the top of his desk. That didn’t please Kabuto. “Where’s his mask?” “I’m keeping his mask,” Zsasz replied, holding the mask in his large right hand. “It’s sort of a...souvenir for my victory over Kenshin. Now, I’d like to collect my reward and see myself out.” “Okay,” Kabuto said, looking at his large bodyguard, who hadn’t moved from his post since Zsasz left to kill Kenshin. “Shinichiro, give him his reward.” Shinichiro’s speed caught Zsasz by surprise as he drove a boot square into Zsasz’s face, almost laying the man out. Zsasz rolled out of the way before Shinichiro turned Zsasz’s head into a pancake. Zsasz got to his feet, and with lightning speed, he stepped forward and drove the side of his forearm into Shinichiro’s throat. The large bodyguard dropped to the floor, choking on his Adam’s apple while Kabuto watched on in horror. He’d underestimated Zsasz’s fighting abilities. Kabuto reached for one of his drawers of his desk and pulled it open. He produced a pistol from the drawer and closed the drawer. Zsasz sensed something was amiss, reached into his jacket pocket, turned and threw an object at Kabuto’s head. The object was Kenshin’s kunai throwing knife. The knife caught Kabuto right in the center of the forehead, killing him instantly. He slumped over in his chair, then fell out of it. Zsasz scoffed at his former business partner. Never try and get Zsasz killed, it’ll backfire on you. He looked over at Shinichiro. Dead as a doornail as well. He looked at his ruined suit. He should’ve dressed more appropriately when he entered combat. Oh well. Burn this suit and put a new one. With Kenshin’s dragon mask in hand, Zsasz stepped out of Kabuto’s office. Neither one of us said much to each other; I guess there wasn’t much to talk about. The mood in the car was as dark as the night sky outside. I’d caught Stacy given me glances every now and again, obviously worried about me and now Sam Hagen’s death had affected me. She’d wished that I didn’t flight out to Ireland, but this was something I had to do. I need to say goodbye a man who was like a father to me, a better father than my own biological father. Twenty minutes later, we made our way to the Boston airport. We got out, said our goodbyes, kissed and I told her my next flight would be Friday, to Richmond, Virginia, for the first FRESH! on the West Atlantic tour. Having Pearl signing me to the West Atlantic tour was a good thing on my part; I can spend more time with my now pregnant wife and my family. I wouldn’t have to fly back and forth from country to country like the superstars on the East Atlantic tour. Stacy and I said our last goodbyes, kissed once more and I disappeared into the airport. I arrived in Belfast, Ireland at around seven that evening. I’d missed the funeral, but I managed to find Samuel Hagen’s burial plot. I kneeled in front of it, placing my hand on the new tombstone and began to cry. I’d lost a great individual in Sam Hagen. He meant everything to me. He was the one to gave me a career early in my life. He’d turned me into one of the greatest Irish bare-knuckle boxers. If it wasn’t for Sam, I wouldn’t be where I am today. Being a bare-knuckle boxer was a vehicle to jumpstart my successes. I made enough money to support a family with Karen Crenshaw first, then more money came my way to support a family with Stacy McFarland. I did bare-knuckle boxing for fourteen years, and a great fourteen years it had been. It had its highs, winning eight bare-knuckle boxing titles, and it had its lows, inadvertently killing Ian McLeod and the embarrassing loss to Paddy O’Shea (which I got my revenge at Divide and Conquer, HUZZAH!). But through it all, Sam was there, patting me on the back, telling me “Ye did good, kid. Ye did good.” Now with Sam gone, he’ll never tell me that I’ve done good ever again. I let out a sigh, patting the tombstone. “Thank you for all the memories, Sam,” I said, and I started to cry even more when I said these last words to him: “Ye did good, kid. Ye did good.” I let out a good cry. I cried for a good five minutes, in remembrance of the greatest boxer of all time, Samuel Hagan. I straightened myself up after I’d regained my composure. Since I’m here in Belfast, I thought it was a good idea to pay respects to another fallen comrade... Twenty minutes later, I was standing over the grave of Ian McLeod, a grave that I hadn’t paid my respects in almost eight years. After this death, I only visited his grave once and never looked back. I was too ashamed to look back, due to the fact that I’d killed him in a bare-knuckle boxing match. I know it wasn’t my fault, but to this very day, I feel as though that it was my fault. If we did have that fight eight summers ago, he’d still be alive. I sat at his grave with a bottle of whiskey in my hand. I’d purchased it before coming to this graveyard and have a drink with my fallen friend. It was a ritual for the two of us: we’d have a drink after every boxing venue; it didn’t matter if we had a match or not. When we had our drinks, we’d talk about who would win in a boxing match if we’d ever fought. I always told him that he would win because he was the better fighter. But alas, I was the victor on that fateful day, and he was the loser...the slain loser. After that day, I wanted to retire from bare-knuckle boxing. I didn’t want to go through that again...killing someone again. My hands were lethal weapons and they should no longer be used as tools of destruction. I was retired for two years until Sam Hagen talked me out of retirement. I realized that I still had the ability to fight. Then came Paddy O’Shea. He humiliated me, beat me at my own game, forcing me into my retirement. Again, I’d gotten my revenge on O’Shea at Divide and Conquer. Anyway, I sit here at Ian’s grave, opening the bottle of whiskey, and pour about a quarter of the alcohol all over the tombstone. “Drink up buddy,” I whispered to myself. I gave myself a healthy swig of whiskey after giving Ian a drink. A few moments later, I saw moment in my peripheral vision. I turned my head to the left, seeing a tall, aging reverend coming towards me. He was Paul Donahue, a long-time reverend in Belfast for the past forty years. Now seventy-three, he hadn’t changed one bit. His blonde hair was as bright and vibrant, but giving away to an aging white shade, his green eyes shined like emeralds, but sunken into their sockets. He still walked ramrod straight, as if he was a mighty, valiant individual, but now had the support of a marble cane. I stood up, smiling at the reverend as he made his way towards me. I met him halfway, greeted him and gave him a young. “It’s been a long time, Michael,” Reverend Donahue said. “Aye, it has,” I replied, letting go from our embrace. “Wha’ are ye doin’ here? Don’t ye live in th’ United States now?” I nodded. “Yes I do. I flew out here to attend Samuel Hagen’s funeral, but I was too late. Now I’m here to pay a visit to a friend I haven’t seen in a long time. Havin’ a drink with him for old time’s sake.” He let out a noise that sounded like ehhhhhh. “I knew Samuel Hagen,” he said, clearing his throat. “’Twas a good man tha’ one was. I’m sorry fer yer loss, Michael.” He looked at the burial plot I was sitting at. “Who’s yer friend?” I smiled, looking back at the tombstone. “Ian McLeod. A former bare-knuckle boxer and a good friend of mine. I...” I trailed off, not really sure to continue on with my sentence. I didn’t want to tell Reverend Donahue I’d killed Ian in a fight. “I hadn’t seen him much after his death.” Good save, Mikey. Good save. “Why not?” he asked. Because I killed him and was too ashamed to look back. “I don’t know,” I lied. “I guess I never found the time with me being busy and all. Now was a good as time as any.” Reverend Donahue nodded, as if he believed me. Then he smile. “Do ye to talk about anything?” He let out a chuckle. Obviously it was a joke. “We don’t talk about much,” I replied. “Not much anymore anyway. “What’s there left to say when everything else has been said, you know?” “Aye. Indeed I do.” Then his brow creased into a serious manner. “How did he die?” Oh hell. I was afraid of this. “He died in the ring. Boxing match. He was killed. Shattered me to pieces.” “I bet it would,” he replied. “A friend’s death would shock anyone.” I took a swig of my whiskey, nodding my head. I shouldn’t be drinking in front of the reverend, but I couldn’t help it. I needed a drink. “When he was still alive, we’d always have a drink after boxing matches. It didn’t matter if he had a fight or not. We’d go to a pub after the boxing venue was over and talk about who would win a fight if we ever had a fight. I always told him that he would because he was the better fighter. But when that fight came...” I trailed off, my eyes began to brim with tear. “I killed him.” I took another swig of my whiskey. “Since then I couldn’t come back here. I was too ashamed to show my face around Ian’s family, Ian’s wife, even Ian’s little girl...” I started to cry. “I wish I could go back to that day and called the match off. I’d anything just to have him back. I miss him, and I have such a heavy burden on my shoulders that can’t be lifted.” Paul Donahue saw the pain in my eyes. He felt sorry for me. “Do ye know it wasn’t your fault that ye killed him?” I nodded. “Unfortunately, I don’t feel like it was my fault. I’m responsible for his death.” “But tha’ doesn’t mean ye meant t’ kill him.” I looked over at the reverend with tear-blurry vision. Reverend Donahue continued. “Ye did kill him, but it wasn’t yer fault. Sometimes things happen an’ ye can’t change ’em, an’ in this case, ye can’t change that you accidentally killed yer friend, an’ now yer sufferin’. “I don’t wanna suffer anymore.” My eyes were locked on the reverend’s. “What should I do, Reverend Donahue?” “Ask fer forgiveness,” Donahue replied. “Tell ’em yer sorry, that it wasn’t yer fault. He’ll find it in his heart t’ forgive ye. God did give him the power of forgiveness. If ye don’t tell ’em yer sorry, ye’ll still carry yer burden.” I turned to face Ian’s tombstone, kneeling down, placing my hand on the whiskey saturated tombstone. “I’m so sorry, Ian,” I slowly began, trying to find the right words. “I know you’ve been waiting for those words for eight long years. I couldn’t bring myself to tell you I’m sorry, because I was ashamed, ashamed for what I did. All I did was made my burden worse. Please, Ian, please find it in your heart to forgive me. I never meant for this to happen.” My crying became heavy. “Please, Ian...forgive me. I would do anything to bring you back. Forgive me...please...” I felt a hand on my shoulder. I looked up at Reverend Donahue, and he had a smile on his face. “Very good, Michael.” I straightened up, looking at the reverend. “Do you think he forgave me?” I asked, my eyes went back to Ian’s tombstone. Donahue’s smile became larger. “Aye, me boy,” he replied, nodding his head. “I believe he did. Do ye feel your burden lifted?” After he asked that question, I felt a lot better about myself. “As a matter of fact, it was.” I smiled at the reverend. “I feel better about myself after asking for forgiveness. I think he knew it wasn’t my fault.” Reverend Donahue fell beside me, wrapping an arm around my shoulders. “Of course he did. Come, it’s startin’ t’ get dark.” We walked side by side, leaving the cemetery. From above, I could feel Ian McLeod watching over me, smiling.
