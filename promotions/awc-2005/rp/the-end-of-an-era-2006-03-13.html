The End of an Era

13 March 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 3-13-06 Two months had passed after the death of Ian McLeod. Since that day, I took it upon myself to leave the sport of bare-knuckle boxing. That day was one of the most traumatic days I ever went through, killing my best friend with my fists wasn’t the way I wanted to go. That match was supposed to be something fun for the both of us, and to determine who was the better man. Well, after that day, you know what happened. I made a “killing” - no pun intended - in that match, a killing I wished never happened. I know it wasn’t my fault, I truly do, but there were days where I sometimes wonder if I should continue to blame myself for what had happened to him. If I did, I knew my life would enter into a downward spiral, entering a moment of self-destruction. Talking to Susan McLeod was the hardest thing I’d ever done. How could I tell the wife of Ian McLeod that I accidentally murdered her husband? Really, how? How could you bring yourself to say, “Hey, so-and-so, I killed your husband in a bare-knuckle boxing match to see whom was the better man. How do you feel about that?” Man, if I could turn back the clock and never had that match, I would, but unfortunately, I can’t. I remember driving to Susan’s house, about to confront her about the untimely death of her late husband, by my hands, no less. I was in a state of shock; my whole sense of reality was tweaked after killing Ian McLeod about a half-hour ago. It seemed like an eternity after that match when in actuality it was only thirty minutes ago. By this time, Susan would see her husband, give him a hug, asked him about how his fight went and join him in a leisurely shower. After that leisurely shower, Ian would talk to his then six-year-old daughter, telling her that Daddy’s okay, Daddy had his match and he’s still alive, still alive to come back to his family. Tonight wouldn’t be that night. Tonight, Ian McLeod wouldn’t go back to his family. I sat in my car for a very long time, staring at the house. My first impulse was to put my car in drive and simply drive away, but that wouldn’t be fair to Susan. I wouldn’t want anyone else to tell her except me. It would be best if I told her that her husband was best. It’s going to be hard, but it had to be done. There’s no two ways about it. Steeling myself, I got out of the car, and was about to walk up to the front door until I stopped myself. I looked down at my hands and realized that I hadn’t removed the bloody tape from my hands. Fuck. Susan wouldn’t want to see that. If she saw that, she would go completely ballistic. I went back into the car, popped open the glove compartment and fished for a pocketknife. Finding it, I cut off the tape from my hands, threw it in the car and put the pocketknife in my pocket. With that mess out of the way, it was time for me to tell Susan, and her daughter, Penelope, the bad news. I fought off the urges to leave and went to the front door and rang the doorbell. There wasn’t a response. Well, it was approaching eleven o’clock that evening, so it wouldn’t have surprised me if she were in bed. Perhaps I would come back tomorrow to tell her. Before I could turn around to leave the porch, the door opened, and there was Susan McLeod, dressed in a pink bathroom rubbing the sleep from her eyes. “Who th’ fok comes to me front door this late at noigh?” she asked sleepily at the person in front of her. When she squinted up at me, realizing who she was talking to “Oh, hi, Mikey.” She yawned, then smiled. “What are ye doin’ here?” I was fighting back the tears that were overwhelming me. I needed to be strong to tell Susan the bad news. I conjured up a smile, but I didn’t feel the warmth or the conviction behind it. In the back of my mind, this was a bad idea. I should just walk away and leave. But I made this far; there was no turning back now. “Hi, Sue,” I said, with a hoarse voice. I cleared my throat and started again. “Hi, Sue. Can I come in? I need to talk to you about something.” She let me in. We walked into the living room, where she offered me a seat on the sofa. Susan was starting to wake up more. She saw the depressed look on my face, saw that I’d been crying. She saw that I was hurting. Hurting from what? Her first inclination was that I was hurting from the match I had against her husband, took a bad beating and was a sore sport about it. When she realized - more like felt - that it was more than that, she started to get worried. “Wha’s th’ matter, Mikey?” she calmly asked. When I didn’t respond, she continued. “Did somethin’ happen tonoigh’?” Then she had a weird feeling, a feeling that something was amiss. She felt some sort of “detachment” from her husband. “Did somethin’ happen t’ me Ian?” I let out a sigh, then turned to her. Tears were slowly streaking down my face at this point. I had no acknowledgement of them until I felt something warm rolling down my face. “Well, Sue, I---” I purposely broke myself off. I wasn’t exactly sure how to find the right words, and blurting it out would make things worse. Did somethin’ happen tonoigh’? Did somethin’ happen to me Ian? That was an understatement. I was the cause to what happened to Ian. “Well, Sue, as you know, Ian an’ I had a fight...” “Aye, I know tha’,” she said impatiently. “Get on with it. Where’s Ian? Did somethin’ happen t’ him?” I couldn’t get control of my emotions now. I started to cry. “I dunno how t’ say this,” I began, carefully choosing my words, “I...I killed yer husband with me hands.” Susan’s eyes got really wide. She opened her mouth, about to say something, but no words came out. She couldn’t believe what she’d just heard. Ian? Dead? No it couldn’t be. Ian couldn’t be dead, he couldn’t! “I dunno wha’ happened,” Susan heard me say, breaking her out of her reverie. “Everythin’ was goin’ smoothly. It was thirty minutes o’ hell, an’...an’...I-I killed ‘em... It wasn’t me intention t’ kill me best friend. I...I’m very sorry, Susan. I didn’t want anyone else t’ hafta tell ye what happened to yer husband. Would’ve been easier on me if I did.” Susan McLeod hadn’t moved nor said anything for a very long time. I thought she wasn’t going to move anywhere or say anything. She sat perfectly still, as if she was petrified into stone. I reached out and placed a hand on her shoulder. This jerked her out of the world she was in. Her head snapped towards me, looking at me with blazing, accusing eyes. “How dare ye?” she snapped. “How dare ye come in here t’ tell me ye killed me husband? What were ye fokin’ thinkin’ partcipatin’ in a fight like tha’? I fokin’ hate ye, Mikey! Get the fok outta me house, ye murderer!” She got up, pushing me off the couch. I quickly got to my feet as she was beating on me, her arms and fist flailing against my body. She’d lost all control of herself and a pent-up rage was unleashed. Something inside her snapped, and I don’t blame her. I killed her precious husband and she blamed me for it. I would’ve done the same thing if I were her. She half-threw, half-pushed me out of the house. I went tumbling off the porch and landed on the front yard. For such a small woman, she had a lot of power. “STAY OFF ME PROPERTY, MIKEY!” she shouted. “IF I EVER SEE YE AGAIN, I’LL FOKIN’ KILL YE! NEVER COME BACK HERE AGAIN!” She went inside and slammed the door behind her. I heard muffled screams emanating inside the house, followed by uncontrollable crying. I think I heard Susan’s daughter, Penelope run down the stairs to see what was happening, but I don’t exactly remember. All I remember was the shock, the anger and her accusing me of being a murderer. I got up, looked at the house for a few moments then shook my head. Susan had made up her mind about me. I was now a murderer in her eyes. I was shattered from all of this. I knew this was a bad idea, and yet, I went along and did it. Perhaps it was a better idea to let someone else tell her than have me do it. Maybe that way she would’ve been so hostile and violent. Hell, she would’ve acted like that either way, so it was a lose-lose situation for me. I got into my car, turned on the engine and left. After that night, I walked away from the world of bare-knuckle boxing... I no longer called myself Mikey O’Reilly anymore after retiring from bare-knuckle boxing. The name Mikey O’Reilly had the same equivalence of calling myself a murderer Instead, I went under my real name, Michael Farren, took down a job at a grocery store in Belfast and everything was back to normal for me. No one bothered me about my former career as a bare-knuckle boxer, no one wanted my autograph, none of that. I was finally a regular, productive member of society, not some infamous boxer who had the ability to kill people in the ring. After my retirement from that sport, people looked at me funny. I would hear snickering from some people, some people would stare at me accusingly, much like how Susan McLeod did to me, and others would just leave me alone. In fact, the people who left me alone didn’t bother me at all. It made me like I finally fit in with the regular society of people. The more people left me alone, the more it made me feel good. When I’m done working, I could go home and relax, and not worry about fighting for a living. Sure, working at a grocery store wasn’t as exciting as fighting, but I made a good living from it. I’d been divorced for two years back then and I was living alone. My first wife, Karen, and I had a falling out when she told me she was Christian. I know it sounds like an insipid excuse to divorce anyone, but I asked her point blank if she was a Christian before I started dating her and she said no. She was formally from England and moved to Ireland to get away from her tyrannical family and wanted to start all over. We met when I was twenty, dated for awhile and married. We had a son together named Liam. When she told me she was a Christian, it was worse than telling me that she was having an affair. I always had harsh feelings about Christianity, holding themselves on a high pedestal, telling everyone that they’re all sinners and Christianity is the highest form of religion. Those who weren’t Christians should convert immediately. That was the vibe I get from any Christian I’d ever met. Sure, people who may follow Catholicism were Heretics and crazy people back in the middle ages during Inquisitions, but that was a long time ago. As far as I’m concerned, Catholicism and Christianity are no different than any religions that are followed today, how people use them and use them are the icebreakers. Anyway, let’s move on, shall we? I don’t think you want to hear any more of my religious politics. I finally felt that my past was put behind me until someone from my past walked into the store. It was Samuel Hagen. I hadn’t seen him in a few years. He retired about three, four years before my fight against Ian McLeod. He had given his training school, promotion, everything to his son, Sam Jr. Samuel hadn’t changed one bit, except for some aging. At seventy-eight years old, he remained a hard-ass, even after retirement. He looked even more worn now, his skin was tanner and had more wrinkles. He literally looked like a walking piece of leather. I had my back turned to him, too busy to really talk to him. I was stocking shelves, trying to keep myself busy. Before he came to the store, I managed to stock three and a half shelves. The store wasn’t too big; it wasn’t like your average supermarket now. The store I was working in was no more than half that size. I didn’t even hear Samuel enter the aisle I was stocking. “I see ye’ve reduced yerself to a per-ductive member of society.” I smile when I recognize the voice. It wasn’t a voice I hadn’t heard in a few years. I turned around to see Samuel Hagen. My smile turned into a broad grin. “Well, fok me,” I said, my grin broadening. “I see they let ye outta the old folks’ home.” I chuckled at that remark. Samuel mocked my chuckling. “Always th’ smart-arse ye were,” he said, taking a step toward me. “Gimme a hug, ye skinny lil’ shoit!” I gave him a hug. It felt good to see and hug him again. I wondered if I would see my former mentor and boxing promoter again. “I see yer lookin’ well,” I commented, after breaking the hug. “Takin’ good care o’ yerself I hope.” Samuel nodding, smiling happily. “Aye, me boy-o, I am. Need t’ at me age. I noticed tha’ things go downhill as I get older. I see yer havin’ a happy life here. People treatin’ ye good here?” “Aye,” I replied. “People treat me pretty good here. Sometimes they question me about my time as a boxer, but other than tha’ they treat me like an equal here. It’s nice here.” “Ah,” Samuel said, understanding how I felt about the whole situation. “I’m glad that you’ve moved on, I really am.” Then his cheerfulness changed to that of stern seriousness. There was more to his visit than I imagined. I kind of figured that he would have some reason why he would come down here, and just to see me wasn’t one of them. “I wanted t’ talk t’ ye about somethin’----” I immediately cut Samuel off. I knew where this was going. “No, I’m not goin’ back,” I said in a tone to leave no room for argument or discussion. “Aw, c’mon, Mikey,” Samuel said. “Tha’ was yer passion. For twelve years ye made yerself a household name, and pretty much a legend in the sport. Ye even told me it was yer passion!” “It ain’t anymore,” I snapped. “It’s no longer a passion when ye have the ability t’ kill someone with yer bare hands. I can’t do tha’ anymore. I’m not gonna put lives in jeopardy when I enter th’ ring t’ fight.” I turned to see that my manager wanted to see me for something. I nodded and told him I would be right there. “Sorry t’ cut this short, Sam, but I need t’ go. I’m not goin’ back t’ bare-knuckle boxin’. After killin’ Ian, I vowed tha’ I’d never step into a ring again.” Much like what I did to my boxing career, I turned my back to Samuel Hagen and began to walk over to see what the manager wanted. The way I turned my back to Samuel made him upset. Throughout his life when anyone showed his back to him, he deemed it as a act of disrespect. He probably thought I disrespected him, disrespected his former profession, and disrespected everything that made up Samuel Hagen. “Ye can’t turn yer back on bare-knuckle boxin’,” he began, but he knew I wasn’t listening to him. Then he relented. “Ian McLeod wouldn’t want ye t’ turn yer back on it.” That remark definitely hit home with me. Samuel was right; Ian wouldn’t want me to turn my back on boxing. If he was still alive today, he would tell me to continue with my career, no matter what the repercussions were. But how can you continue your career after you’ve killed someone with your fists? That’d put a damper on continuing with your said career, wouldn’t you agree? I stopped halfway, turning to face Samuel Hagen. My felt that my whole demeanor went down the shitter. I felt defeated and hurt after that remark made by my former boxing promoter. My eyes went to the tiled floor, letting out a deep, long sigh. “I know tha’ Ian wouldn’t want me t’ not turn my back on everythin’, but wha’s the point continuing when ye killed someone with yer bare hands? After witnessin’ somethin’ like tha’ it’s best t’ hang th’ proverbial boots an’ retire.” I paused for a moment, waiting to see if Samuel wanted to say anything. When he remained silent, I continued. “I remember tellin’ Susan McLeod tha’ I killed ’er husband. I remember the way she looked at me: she was full of shock, full of anger and her eyes burned me, accusin’ me o’ murderer. Maybe I am a murderer, maybe I deserved t’ get beat up by Susan, an’ get threatened t’ be killed by ‘er if I was ever near ‘er again. Ye don’ know wha’ it’s like t’ have t’ tell someone ye killed their husband. I don’ wanna go through tha’ again. It hurts too much.” Samuel Hagen stepped up to me, placing his hand on my shoulder, and his old, worn eyes gazed into mine. “Actually, Mikey, I know wha’ it’s like to kill someone.” He recounted one match several years ago in a boxing match. The situation was similar between Ian McLeod and myself, but there was one small difference: a World Heavyweight title. Samuel Hagen fought against a man who was named Benjamin Farley, perhaps the greatest Irish World Heavyweight Champion in the sport of professional boxing. “He was built like a fokin’ tank,” Samuel had said, reflecting on his opponent, “and he would hit just as hard.” The match started like any of the matches Samuel ever had. First round was a weak, either man didn’t have much of a showing. By the second round, Benjamin had taken control, beating the shit out of Samuel and was on his way to retain the World Heavyweight title. But halfway into the round, Samuel had battled back, throwing everything he had at the Irish tank. Finally, without realizing it, he caught Ben hard with a massive left hook, taking him down to the mat and eventually beat him for the World Heavyweight title. When reality finally sank in for Samuel, he had inadvertently killed Ben Farley. That same night he was stripped of the title and was suspended for three months, the reason: “I didn’t want t’ be a liability, they told me,” Samuel concluded, his voice had a hint of sourness. He dropped his hand from my shoulder, his gaze remained on mine. “Ye see, after tha’ match, I wasn’t sure if I wanted t’ come back, but after my suspension, I came back an’ went back t’ wha’ I love best, an’ tha’s t’ box. Wha’ I did t’ Ben Farley was an accident, nothin’ more, nothin’ less.” He brightened with a smile. “Wha’ ye did t’ Ian McLeod was an accident, nothin’ more, nothin’ less. Sure, yer gonna blame yerself for a long time, but eventually ye’ll see tha’ ye did nothin’ wrong.” He paused for a moment to shift his weight. “I’ll tell ye wha’...I’ll give ye some time t’ think about it. Whatever answer ye come up with, I’ll always respect. If ye want t’ come back to bare-knuckle boxing, tha’s fine. If ye don’ tha’s fine, too. Whatever decision ye make, I hope it makes ye happy.” He smiled at me again, then patted me on the shoulder. “I better go. It was nice seein’ ye again. Ye better go see what yer manager wants before ye get in trouble. Take care, me boy-o.” I was so engrossed in Samuel’s story that I completely forgot about my manager. When I listened to Sam’s story, I started to think, the wheels in my head started to turn. I started to think that it might not be so back to come back into bare-knuckle. Hell, if Samuel Hagen could adjust after the death of Ben Farley, I could adjust too. Perhaps I should go back to bare-knuckle boxing and get back into the swing of things. Yeah, I should go back to bare-knuckle boxing... But right now wasn’t the time. About a year and a half had passed when I made up my decision to come back into the sport of Irish bare-knuckle boxing. It was good to be in hiding for about eighteen months, but it was time to go back to what I do best. In early 2000, I had my first match, which went could’ve went to my opponent. I had a hell of a time trying to find my groove after eighteen months of rust. Hell, the training regiments I used to go through in the beginning were taking a toll on me. I kept thinking to myself over and over again that it would’ve been better if I remained retired, I think I’m getting too old for this shit, heh-heh. But I survived the training regiments and my first match back in those days. It was hard, but I managed. I didn’t pass with flying - though I did see many pretty colors after my first match back - but I passed nonetheless. You know what I mean, right? Anyway, I had my matches and I rolled over every person they put in front of me, building me up to my ninth title shot in the sport. When I beat my last opponent to get my title shot, the match was set on January 15, 2000, three days after my twenty-seventh birthday. I smiled at that. I was going to be a great birthday present after all: my ninth bare-knuckle boxing title. Bare-knuckle boxing in Ireland had no weight classes. It only focused on one belt and it was up for grabs for anyone at any weight classes. I’ve seen people who weighed less than one hundred pounds hold the title and people who weighed over four hundred pound to hold the bare-knuckle boxing title a time or six. Even regular Joes who aren’t full-fledged athletes won that fucking title. That title had attracted an array of dysfunctional people throughout the years when Samuel Hagen opened up his bare-knuckle boxing promotion. Finally, on January, 15, 2000, I get my shot at my ninth bare-knuckle boxing title. I had no idea who I was facing, in fact, no one knew who I was facing that night. My opponent was an enigma, a complete unknown in the sport of bare-knuckle boxing. You’re indeed a complete unknown when no one really knows who you are, don’t you think? It’s hard to prepare against someone when you have no idea what that person is capable of. All I knew back then was that he was a rising bare-knuckle boxer, so he was capable of anything. I entered the ring. When I saw who I was facing, I wanted to laugh out loud. My opponent looked like a child in the ring. He looked to be in his teens, maybe twenties, stood around five feet nine inches tall, probably weighed about one hundred and fifty pounds. He reminded me of me when I first started when I was thirteen. He almost looked like me when I was thirteen years old. The only thing I couldn’t get passed was his size. He was the first opponent who was smaller than me. Throughout my career in bare-knuckle boxing, I’d faced people more than twice my twice. People who were up to a foot taller than me and ranged between one hundred to two hundred pounds heavier than me. I always humiliated those bigger than me, using my speed and agility to take out those larger than me. You know what they say: the bigger they are, the harder they fall. I was the David to their Goliath. This opponent was different. He was smaller than any of the larger opponent I’d previously faced. I was going to be interesting to face someone who was smaller than me, someone who’s faster than me and possibly could hit harder than me. I heard the bell ring and both of us were ready for combat. The small opponent came flying at me, throwing a barrage of hard fists. I’d blocked most of them, but the few that landed hurt...bad. His punches hurt more than all the opponents I’d ever faced in the fourteen years I’d boxed. I collected myself, tried a throw a few punches, but the small fucker was too fast for me, assaulting me with more punches. For the first time in my career, I felt completely overwhelmed and helpless against this small kid. I couldn’t throw any punches that would connect for the first few minutes of the match. When I regained my composure, I studied my opponent carefully, seeing if he’d open himself up to any weaknesses. I saw him come after me again and lost his footing. I smiled at this and took control, throwing hard hands into the body and head of my opponent. Just for shits and giggles, I pushed him into one of the corners, I brutally assault him with heavy lefts and rights. When he tried to get passed me, I would push him back and continued my onslaught. Feeling content with myself, I threw him across the ring, watching him land hard on his back. Now, I was ready to end this match and claim my ninth title. He slowly got to his feet, clutching his back, but I was ready for him. I was going for my One Punch, a punch that no one would be able to get up from. As I went for my One Punch, I made one tactical error, the opponent moved after I threw it. He head-butted me right in the abdomen and threw and uppercut... ...with his elbow... Everything went in slow motion for me. I slowly fell backward and everything went black. DING-DING-DING! I heard the bell ring when I came to. I had realized what had happened. I lost. I lost to a man who cheated me out of the bare-knuckle title. The referee should’ve seen the head-butt and elbow he used against me and he didn’t. You were not allowed to use anything other than your hands against your opponent. I felt like someone stabbed me in the back. I watched the small kid hold up the bare-knuckle boxing title high up into the air. He was holding up my title. After that match, I went back into retirement, but this time, it was for good. I had a good fourteen years in the business, but I wasn’t going to hang around a promotion where people cheat to win titles. When I heard the name of my opponent, I sneered. If I ever saw him again, it would be too soon. I would rip his head off and shit down his neck. If I ever saw him again, he’ll know what it’s like to be screwed... If I ever saw him again, he’ll be destroyed. His name was Paddy O’Shea...
