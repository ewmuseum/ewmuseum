The Lavelle Chronicles, Ch. 16

28 August 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 27th/28th August The Lavelle Chronicles, Ch. 16 Some say that life is like a stack of cards - you sometimes get dealt a bad hand, but a recurrence of bad hands is like an omen, a losing streak in poker, and luck doesn’t seem to shine your way and your income bill has flown through the roof and you are in debt. These are normal things for a lot of people (mostly those in Las Vegas), but then sometimes life would deal you a good hand, a line of ace’s or kings and queens and you become the high-roller, the man living the 'normal' life, with a steady income, a wife and two kids and the house in the suburbs with a picket fence, but there are those that don’t get either. They get stuck with the jokers, the 2’s and 3’s and they can’t, no matter how hard they try, get the jack-pot or the lucky charms. One man once said that times of general calamity and confusion create great minds. The purest ore is produced from the hottest furnace, and the brightest thunderbolt is elicited from the darkest storms, but if we aren’t devoted to the deepest and inner most sanctums of ourselves then we destroy what we create and what create has been seen through history. I believed this my whole way through high school as I looked back at history and the recurrences of bad hands in the stack of cards that is life; the death from our two worlds wars, the kidnappings that recur every sixty seconds in Mexico and yet Charles Caleb Colton was so right in the sense that our great minds and our pureness does get tarnished by what we create - this always left the one simple question in my mind: “why don’t we do something?”. No man had an answer, not even the most famous man alive or most intelligent, because to do something would mean to change us, which is something not many would consider. This left me with the strange thought, that everything that had gone wrong in the last couple of months was a bad stack of cards in a poker game and I was stuck in a loop, a never ending loop and nothing would ever change until I did something. Another man once said that if we look inside, we awaken and if we look outside we dream. As I sat in the hospital bed in Birmingham, in my weakened state, I realised the words of these men meant more than a good essay title and I decided I would have to put my foot down when I got back to Albany, because nothing was happening and this bad luck with life would have consequences on many of those around me. | Albany, New York | | Lincoln Avenue | | Time: 9:30 pm | The night sky had become clouded and mysterious, the winds breeze breathless and chilly. The drive home from the airport was lonesome and tiresome. I had taken four pain killers for my broken rib that I had received a week ago at the hands of the wrestling company I worked for. My life had hit a new low; I had lost what I was holding in wrestling and Lois was still missing. I felt like superman, alone and cold, without being able to tell people how you are feeling. My phone buzzed in the background and the caller ID read, Tony Rush. I refused to answer it, chucked it into the backseat and pulled out a CD, a Bob Marley CD. Of course, the last time I had listened to this, I was a teenager high on weed. Times had changed and my youth had been replaced with too much responsibility. Most of my old friends would still be in college at this time; studying for exams, drinking and going to late night parties and here I was, stuck in traffic on a freeway, involved with the mafia, cops and FBI. Things seemed to be changing dramatically in my life. It seemed almost predestined. 'Get up stand up' began to blast out of the speakers in the Ford Mustang; I turned off the freeway, onto Lincoln and down to Pino’s shooting range. I needed to practice my shooting with the Beretta, because I had yet to master hitting the target. I could see Jackie and Hailey loitering around at their usual corner. A small Toyota pulled up to the car next to them and the next thing I know, Hailey’s been shot. The car speeds off, leaving a line of fumes in its wake. Jackie is screaming at the top of her lungs for help, but most of the people wouldn’t hear her. The people on this street were either high on drugs or underground in various strip clubs and poker bars. I pushed open the door and ran across the road, ignoring the pain I was receiving in my side and legs. 'Son of a bitch. He shot her, stay with me Hail’s,” Jackie bellowed. 'Em, I'll call an ambulance,' I pulled out my mobile and dialled the operator. After two minutes I was connected to the nearest emergency phone room and gave all the details I could about the shooting and which street we were on. After closing my mobile Jackie was crying looking at all of the blood. 'It'll be here soon,' I commented. 'Soon! Hun, the nearest hospital is forty minutes away; she'll be dead by then. You have to do something,' Jackie pleaded. 'I - I - I can't ...' 'What do you mean? Please, you are the only medical student in this vicinity. You must know something about this,' Jackie continued to look at me. My palms began to get sweaty with nerves and my mouth dry; I was never prepared for this in medical school. I couldn't just help her, could I? 'I can't. I was never prepared for this,' I knelt down and looked at the bullet wound. 'Hun, most of your life you aren't prepared for. Da ya think I was made a hooker ... it was somethin' I had to do. I wasn't prepared to watch Hail's die. Now, you can't just sit there and bullshit with me, please, you must know something.' 'I, em, I can't ... I'm sorry ... I ...' 'This is your entire fault ...' Jackie yelled. 'My fault?' I watched closely as Jackie’s tears turned to rebuttal and soon to hate. 'Yeah, those were Harrington's boys. Hailey was picked up a couple o' nights ago. They questioned her and she answered everything truthfully, poor thing, never knew how to lie. Now they have come to finish her off.' Before I could respond the same Toyota came charging around the corner, guns blazing. I dove to the ground, covering Hailey and pulling Jackie with me. I waited till the crackling of bullets had ended and the coast was clear. Jackie threw me off her and gazed at the blood now on my jacket. 'Are you hit?' She was in a panic. 'No, it is Hailey's blood ...' I felt ashamed to know that I could help her, but I couldn't at the same time. My medicine and surgical practice wasn't at its peak. Not like an emergency doctor, and yet I felt suddenly responsible for Hailey who was dying on the street corner in front of me. Jackie knelt down beside Hailey. 'You gotta know somethin', Pierce. Somethin', anythin',' she pleaded again with me. 'I - I - em, I can't,' I said. She stared at me once more. My mind was saying no, because if Hailey dies, I'll lose my medicinal degree in college, but my heart felt compelled to help a girl I got shot and involved in my messed up life. 'Okay ... Okay. You need to apply pressure to the wound, try to quench the bleeding.' 'You gonna help?' Jackie asked. 'I'll try ...' I ran back over toward my Mustang. I was looking for the First Aid kit that came with every new car. It was compulsory, just for use in emergencies. My ribs were killing me now. A large stitch was forming in my side. I wrenched the First Aid kit out of the boot and ran back across the street, ignoring the many onlookers. “I know what you are riskin’ Pierce, but thanks for helping’,” she said and kept applying pressure. I nodded my head and taking a large breath of stagnant city air, I pulled out a pair of latex gloves, slipped them on and pulled out a pair of pliers. I looked around and handed the First Aid box to Jackie. “See the bandages and the white roll. You need to keep mopping up around the wound.” “This is too much …” Jackie was now panicking. “Hey, you got me here to perform something I have never done. Now, I’m willing to risk my career, but you need to help and listen to my instructions.” “You are right,” she said. Jackie looked at Hailey once more and soon turned toward me. “What da ya need me to do?” “You need to check her pulse at her wrist, if it goes dead, you need to perform contractions on her chest,” I showed her a prime example of how you hold your hands and where you place them. “Then after every five contractions you give her a breath and just keep doing that until the pulse returns or she breathes.” “Thank you, Pierce,” she said and began to count Hailey’s weakened pulse. Taking one last deep breath to hold back the vomit and nerves; I looked the propulsion of the bullet, it had gone straight and was probably stuck between the muscles in the subcutaneous layer of the skin. Pushing the pliers into the wound, and holding a torch in my mouth, I focused with all my might on the wound. After a few moments I had the bullet out and a sigh of relief came from the small crowd of drug addicts, strippers and hookers. “Ya did it,” Jackie said hugging me. I turned away, feeling like I was about to throw-up. I put the bullet into a plastic bag for the medical staff and forensic team when they arrive. I checked Hailey’s pulse, it was weak, but it wasn’t falling anymore. I applied some roll of white bandage around her waist and put her into the recovery position. “We were lucky you were here,” Jackie said. “I just want ta thank ya.” The medics arrived a few minutes later and the cops had already begun a routine questioning and Jackie wouldn’t stop thanking me. Tony Rush was soon on scene, dressed in similar agent attire - the usual suit and shirt, with the gun holster. He approached me with a smile after talking to a forensic investigator who was analysing tire marks on the tarmac. 'Is this relevant? Or just a daily routine save from you?' He was smiling. 'Harrington’s men did it,' I replied and walked back to my car. I placed my bloodied latex gloves and my jacket into the nearest dumpster. 'Why? Are these the two hookers that gave you that information on him and the places he liked to go?' I nodded my head and pulled off the bloodied shirt, opened my suitcase and pulled out a new one. 'Whoa, what they hell happened ta ya?' Rush said while staring at the bandage wrapped around my waist, along with bruises on my shoulder and body. 'Fell off a bus and went through a window,' I smiled and buttoned my new shirt and threw the old into my suitcase and opened the car door. 'Are you serious?' Rush was shocked. 'Yeah, but it’s all in a days work. Did Harrington ring?' 'No, I think he’s waiting for you to be home,' Rush stated. 'So, this wrestling thing, is that why you look like crap?' 'Yeah Rush. Listen, I would like to head home, I‘m really tired,' I replied. Rush nodded his head and went back to his own car and followed the line of cops cars and an ambulance down the street. I hoped into the Mustang, turned on the ignition, took some more pain killers, took a deep breath and pulled out of Lincoln, cancelling my shooting range trip and decided to go home instead. | Western Avenue, Summer Plaza apartments | | Time: 10:15 pm | Upon my arrival home, I stopped into Mrs. Winchester’s and knocked three times to let her know it was me. The neighbours on our floor now had a code, three knocks and one quick one. It was just a precaution for the next time a mysterious person arrives. I waited a few moments and gazed at the peep-hole on the door. Mrs. Winchester opened after a few moments wearing a night gown, dressing gown and her grey straw-like hair was tied into a bun, pulled tightly around the top of her head. Shelby came running out, wagging her tail and barking with joy. 'She’s a very good girl,' Mrs. Winchester said. 'Well, I’m glad she behaved,' I smiled. 'Thanks for minding her for me.' 'It was the nice gentleman that persuaded me,' she said. Mrs. Winchestor smiled and closed her door. I followed Shelby to our room, unlocked all of the bolts and entered. The lights flicked on and Shelby ran in and began sniffing around, a sort of common thing amongst animals, a sense of territory. I checked the mail, mostly bills and went into the kitchen for something to eat. The fridge was empty. After a few moments of unloading my clothes and locking the door again, I took a quick shower and changed into a pair of jeans and a red shirt. I left it out of my jeans; just to be sure I could hide my gun. The door bell rang and I refused to answer until there were three knocks and a quick knock. I moved to the door, checked the peep-hole and saw Tony Rush standing in casual tracksuit bottoms and a Boston Red Sox t-shirt; he was brandishing a large pizza. 'What are you doing here?' I asked letting him in. Shelby was all over the pizza box. 'Somebody seems hungry,' Rush laughed and placed the box on the counter. 'I got bored at the headquarters, so I went for a jog, got a pizza and decided to come here. They don’t have digital on the TV in my hotel room,' he said. Rush pulled out a large piece of pizza, placed it on a plate and licked his fingers. 'Is Hailey okay?' I grabbed my own slice, removed the ham and chicken and gave it to Shelby. 'She’ll be fine; you worked a miracle on her. Sort of an ER moment there,' He laughed and struggled with a long line of cheese hanging from his chin. 'Well, I’m waiting on the call,' I turned on the TV and ate my own slice of pizza as Shelby licked her own lips, requesting more ham and chicken. 'It’s a waiting game now. Did you remove the away message?' Rush said, mind deep into an episode of 24. 'I forgot. I’ll change it now. Do you think she’ll still be alive?' Tony Rush paused what he was doing and looked at me. 'Sometimes they do come back, but then sometimes, they don’t. This happens a lot in Boston, and sometimes we are too late. I hope that isn’t the case here,' he stated and looked away from me and back at 24 and Jack Bauer. I guess Tony Rush was right. I dipped my head and put away my pizza - suddenly losing my appetite. I changed the voice message on the phone and sat next to the balcony window over looking the city at night time. | Lower Hudson Avenue - near to the Hudson River. Time: 10:50pm | The waves crashed against the cobbles and sand. Marvin Harrington gazed at the river and the full moon and then began to walk back up toward what looked like an abandoned warehouse. Opening the large hatch doors he moved in, put on a light and stares at the fading body that belongs to Lois Wood. A large pool of blood surrounds her stomach. 'Tides are changing,' Harrington whispers. 'Full moon,' Harrington laughs. Lois Wood just grunts in pain, her throat dry and her clothing stinks of dead fish. 'Don’t worry; it’ll all be over soon. Either you’ll be dead or Pierce Lavelle will be,' he says, but Lois fails to see the humour and with some left over strength spits on the ground next to Harrington. 'You are quite a boisterous one, aren’t you?' Harrington asks, but Lois doesn’t reply. 'A few days ago you were all for rebuttal and talking. Now, you recline on the ground, drenched in blood and damp dirt and you can’t say a word, but you spit.' Harrington kneels down next to her face. 'Not a great army brat after all.' 'You should ...be...ashamed,' she manages to blurt out. 'Ashamed? Of what my darling?' he begins to taunt her, looking for an argument. 'I’m not your darling,' Lois says, her eyes roll into the back of her head and for the first time in a long time, Harrington actually shows some remorse. He tilts Lois’s head back and sets her into the recovery position. He looks around for a blanket and decides to use his dirty grey trench coat. After tending to Lois Wood, Harrington puts back on his trilby hat, grabs a few quarters and makes his way out of the warehouse, locking the doors and down past the crashing waves toward a stolen van. He hops in and drives off. Leaving the moon to watch over Lois Wood. | Western Avenue Time: 12:30 am | For the past two hours and fifteen minutes I had watched two episodes of 24, eaten most of the pizza and knocked back two beers and I had yet to receive any phone call from Harrington. Rush was sprawled out on the sofa, his feet dangling over the arm chair, a can of Cola lay in his hand as he continued to snore. Shelby was out cold in the bedroom and I lay on the beanie bag on the floor gazing at the static on the TV. I found it difficult to sleep with my ribs. It was bad enough I found it hard to take deep breaths, but I couldn’t even lie down in a comfortable position. I rose from the beanie bag, picked up the mess left by my two new roommates and made my way into the kitchen. The house was covered in a blue glow from the TV and amongst the noise of static lay the quiet whispers of Shelby and the horn-pipes that were Rush’s snoring. I poured myself a glass of water, took two panadol dissolvable tablets and slipped them into the glass, watching them foam as the water soon dissolved the substance of Paracetamol. I pulled out the icepack from the freezer and lay it on my side. After swallowing the disgusting taste of my medicine, I walked out to the balcony and found myself standing in the middle of a rain shower. Closing the porch doors behind me and drying my hair with a nearby towel, I decided to go and lie down on the floor in the bedroom. Within moments of this brief decision, three knocks and one quick one came from my front door. I opened the door and in front of me was Mr. Dowlas, the electrician on our floor; he had a way with broken TV sets and also black-outs. He was standing in his pyjamas with a tool box in one hand. “How can I help you, Mr. Dowlas?” I asked. “Well, there has been a power failure on our level and the source is this room,” he replied, trying to look over my shoulder. Mr. Dowlas was a small man, very petite, with broad shoulders, a monk style hair cut and a large moustache. “Em, my TV and lights work fine.” “No, no, no. You see, it doesn’t matter whether your sets are working, and from the blue glow I see all you have is static.” “Listen, Mr. Dowlas. It is one in the morning, can’t this wait till tomorrow.” “You know how we old folk like our late night TV shows,” he said with a smile. “Yes, but it is late and I doubt you’ll be up at this time to watch a late night viewing of Dad’s army,” I laughed. “You are right, Mr. Lavelle. Sorry for disturbing you, is it alright if I come back around 9:00 am?” I nodded my head, closed the door and saw Tony Rush seated on the chair, stretching and drinking from the cartoon of orange juice. “Great, now I’ll have to buy a new one,” I smiled and threw the remainder of the cartoon into the sink. “Hey, that was good orange juice,” he retorted. “Nah, it was stale …” I laughed and opened a can of Fanta. “Yuck,” Rush knocked back the rest of the can of flat Cola and looked around. “I hope that doesn’t come up in the morning,” he said whilest laughing, but stopped when the phone began to ring in the background, we both froze. “You answer it,” Rush said. “I can’t, you answer it.” “I can’t, I don’t live here, remember?” It was Tony Rush’s way of getting out of everything. “Fine,” I pulled the receiver to my ear and turned on the recorder. A lot of breathing was heard, along with some traffic, car horns and some girls asking the person if he had a lighter. “Hello Pierce,” the voice said into the phone. “Harrington,” I uttered with a short breath. “Indeed. Listen, I have no willingness to go back to prison,” he started off with saying. “I think it is time I started this negotiation so that I can get out of this stagnant city.” “Where is she, Harrington?” “Patience boy. Remember, curiosity killed the cat,” Harrington laughed manically. I looked at Rush in the background, who was holding an A4 pad, which read: keep him talking, I’ll get a trace “That is just a saying,” I couldn’t find any other words and Rush sighed with my lack of skills for interrogation. “Did you get my present earlier?” Harrington asked with a sort of temperament to his voice. “No, what present?” “One of the lovelies you seem to like,” he replied. I knew he was talking about the attempted murder of Hailey Lewis. “Listen Harrington, just tell me when and where and I’ll be there.” “Oh, but I still have 80c worth of talk time left. I’m not finished here.” I sat back into the chair, put the phone to my chest and took a deep breath. “I can hear you …” he sang into the phone. I felt the hairs on the back of my neck stand up. “Okay, fine, what do you want?” “Simple, a little father-son time.” “I’m not your son; you killed yours, if you remember.” “That was quite a night.” He laughed gleefully, with a hint of a chuckle of sarcasm. Tony Rush put the A4 pad down, showed me the tracker and it read 3rd Avenue. Harrington was on a pay-phone, which meant Lois would be a couple of minutes from wherever Harrington was in 3rd Avenue. “Here’s the deal, Pierce.” I felt my heart jump into my mouth. It was the time. All this time of waiting around and the negotiation for Lois’s life was finally here. Jackie had said earlier that we are never prepared for the different situations in life, but that we have to take each step or issue as it comes. I wasn’t prepared for this, but I knew I had to keep calm about it. “I want you to come alone. No cops and don’t tell anyone where you are going.” “Okay, fine.” “If you lie to me, Pierce, and I find you have come with the cavalry of cops; then I’ll kill Lois and then myself.” “You have a deal Harrington,” I was too eager for my own good sometimes. “Meet me at the docks, on Lower Hudson Avenue at 3:00 am. Come alone, or she’ll die.” The phone went dead and I shot up into immediate action. “I have a plan,” Rush said and followed me into the hall. | Lower Hudson Avenue - near to the Hudson River and dock | | Time: 2:45 am | The street lights flickered in the background, newspaper headings flew gently above the ground with the early morning breeze. The streets were so quiet, that the little pitter-patter of rat’s feet along the dumpsters and sewer could be heard. Every whisper and car engine echoed silently with the early morning birds. I pulled the Ford Mustang to a hault as the red light flicked on, all the while keeping track in my rear view mirror, I felt extremely uneasy about my surroundings, the perfect scene for a murder, well sort of. If you ignore the many homeless people covered in newspapers and cardboard boxes that snore softly with the rats and pigeons. The green light flicked on and I put the car into first gear and took off down the cobbled road and turned into lower Hudson Avenue. A stagnant smell of salt water, dead fish and dirty boats seeped in through the tiny dent on my window. I cranked the air conditioner onto full blast, hoping to clear the cobwebs and horrible fumes that now made my stomach turn. I pulled off the road and watched the small amount of traffic lights disappear. There were no more car lights behind me and I couldn't shake the feeling of nerves as I turned into the Hudson docks and drove silently over the bridge, my mind in full concentration on the worry I could end up in the freezing cold river and the saying 'sleeping with the fishes' would become a fitting truth. My phone buzzed along the dashboard and my heart leapt into my mouth with a large thud. The caller ID read, Tony Rush. I flicked it open and to remain suppressed, I place the ear piece into my ear and listened. 'I'm in place,' he said quietly and the phone went dead. I turned off the car lights, drove on until I reached a large hanger bay and in front of me was an outline of a large van, or jeep. I put my phone under the car seat, removed the ear piece and put my Beretta into the small of my back. As soon as my feet touched the slimy gravel, my knees turned to jelly with fear. Breathing in the lovely sea air didn't help; for a couple of moments I leant against my car, sipping as much bottled water as I could - hoping to keep my late night pizza down. The hanger bay door suddenly opened and I was blinded by the light. After a few moments Harrington stepped forward, dressed in a black tweed suit, with a trilby hat and some black leather gloves. Lois was seated on a chair, her hands tied and her mouth gagged. She had a large gash on her stomach and her upper eyelid was cut. 'Ah, Pierce, good to see you,' Harrington moved forward, leaving Lois seated on the chair in the warehouse. 'I see you came alone, you are very wise. It is a pity you didn't work for me, we could have ignored all of this and you could have saved all those poor innocent people that died.' 'Don't put their deaths in my hand; I never wanted to work for you. Now, you have me ... please ... let her go.' Harrington laughed and turned to Lois. 'Did you hear that? He's a begging man,' he said and turned back to me. 'I wonder if he'll do tricks,' Harrington chuckled once more moved forward and pointed a silver Calibre pistol at me. 'Sit, stay and play dead.' He found this all too amusing. 'Harrington, you promised you wouldn't hurt her, you gave your word that she wouldn't be touched.' 'I lied,' Harrington continued to point the gun at me and kept a calm and serious look amongst his stance and appearance. His eyes were covered by the trilby hat; all I could see was the smug grin of his lips and the dimples on either cheek. 'I thought it was a mafia tradition, that all members keep promises and remain close ...' 'You watch too many Godfather movies, kid,' Harrington said. While keeping one arm steady on me, he pulled out a cigar with the other and lit it. 'Hollywood emphasized the mafia relations a little too much. We aren't all about family and respect, if we were, we'd all be dead. Including myself.' 'You have me here, just let her go and we can continue this talk until the morning sun rises ...' 'You still haven't got it, have you?' Harrington blew a large circle of smoke into my face. I coughed with the inhalation of a dry smoke. 'Two and two is equal to four; thing is kid, I never needed this here woman. All I needed was the fishing rod, the bait and now the catch ... you.' 'You have me, so let her go.' My voice had become deep and I was becoming aggravated by the mind games of Marvin Harrington. Lois let out some muffled words under the gag, but Harrington just smacked her across the face. I leapt forward, but found the barrel end of a pistol in my third eye. I eased back, continued my gaze at Harrington, all the while each muscle and limb were shaking with uncontrollable fear, my heart pounding in my chest, ready to burst out through my rib cage. 'Alright, you want a promise, here it is; this here woman will be safe from harm, she was only a pawn in something big. I came here to finish off what my boys failed to do two months ago and that's to kill the last piece of the puzzle, you.' 'ME? Why me? Two months ago? You mean the fire?' 'Of course. I wanted both you and that other one dead. Unfortunately, my boys seemed to have missed you and ended up dead themselves, but I was always a firm believer in finishing off messy jobs by myself. Pierce, this is your last couple of minutes alive.' I swallowed deeply, drew a breath of the pungent-odour of dead fish and moved closer to Lois. 'Can I just see her?' 'You have less than a minute. The sun will be rising soon,' Harrington watched me move closer to Lois. Lois had tears in her eyes. I knelt down beside her and hugged her, but instead of hugging her, I untied the knot that was holding her hands tied, she looked at me through the corner of her eyes, fear and anticipation of the unknown lay shrouded in the mystery waters of her blue eyes. 'It'll be alright,' I whispered. Suddenly, I found myself being pulled back by my collar. Harrington had a tight grip and threw me against the side door of the van. The hollow steel let out a bang as my back connected with it. Being winded for a few moments, I failed to rise to my feet. In the distance I watched as the purples, yellows and bright oranges began to seep over the horizon, replacing the darkness that surrounded the city at night. I stared once more at Harrington, who began to remove his hat. I stood up, looked into his eyes and watched as he pulled the calibre pistol to the height of my chest. 'No .....' Lois screams after removing the gag. Three loud shots are heard and darkness soon fills in around me… To be continued …
