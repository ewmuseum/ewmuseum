Ma Famille

28 November 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 28 November, 2005 a. everybody needs a little devastation The docks were empty that day. Ghosts of wet footprints faded from sight and the water still with beauty and death. It had been in the newspaper delivered to her back step - girl, five, pretty, drowned. Missed. Foul play had been stamped across the tableau, the photograph of an empty lake, of a pair of white sandals lined up at the edge of - at the edge. The trees closed in, carnivorous and tall, cast shadows on the sky, the water, Ellis’ face. (Ellis’ father took her fishing once a year from four to sixteen. Twelve days spent with her feet skimming the algae off the lake and she didn’t recall much, maybe the viscous squirming of worms between her small hands, the resistance of their skin against the point of a hook, momentary, before the metal slid in easily. Slick and wet. The two of them didn't bother with words, not even when Ellis was older. The pine trees and creaking boats and splash of bait into the dark, clear water did it for her. It was the only time she spent with him, hunting in silence.) Now, Ellis can still feel the muscles in her hands seizing, involuntary, echo of an action. The sensation of her thumbs pressing against a soft neck, pale, windpipe thin and easy to find; the hard ridge of collarbones pressed against her forearms, soft bump of bone at the base of a neck. Ellis can see it, even now. It’s easy to picture. Skin, pale against hers. Nails pink and even, eyes wide, mouth thin. The slow sinking curve of arms in the water, white skirt wet and tangled, drifting; the violent, giddy satisfaction of surrender. ** b. home ain’t where the heart is Ellis’ mother is talking to Aunt Vivien as if she’s discussing the United Nations. “Vivien, what’s the new hair color for winter?” “Brown-blonde on the under-twenty set, black for over thirty. I just love the new winter cut, too. It’s simply adorable. It’s going to look super fabulous on Eleanor. James – you know my husband, James? Anywho, he thinks Eleanor will look marvelous in a modified Paula Jones look. I like to think of the cut as a P.J. – modern yet funtional yet political.” Vivien never fidgets. Her hands are attached to skinny arms attached to skinny shoulders. Ellis guesses she is the kind of women who stuffs her dead cats and displays them lovingly on the coffee table. “Wow,” Ellis says. “Oh, Eleanor. You look so cute, darling, really thin.” Vivien squeezes Ellis’ shoulder to accentuate her point. “Well, if it will look good on Ellie, I want her to have that haircut. I think she needs a new look. In fact, I’m buying her a whole new fall wardrobe. She’s been somewhat depressed. I think this will perk her up. Don’t you, Vivien?” “Mother, goodness. Don’t waste your time. I’ll just have Dr. Billings up my Prozac.” “Excuse me?” Mother takes a tone that sounds suspiciously authority-figure. Vivien seems a little taken aback. “What? It’s the new century. I can’t talk about psychopharmacology at Thanksgiving?” Mother’s cell phone rings and with it, she decends into the level of Hell reserved only for dermatologists who advertise on subways and socialites who talk on cell phones at Thanksgiving dinner. “I’m sorry, darling,” “What are you sorry for?” Ellis snaps. Two or three manicured nails dismiss Ellis with the ‘I’m talking on my cell phone at Thanksgiving dinner’ sign. Another defining mommy moment. “What is she saying?” Kendall asks. Kendall is Ellis’ older sister. Kendall’s bedroom ceiling is purple. Kendall watches television and does a lot of yogalates. “I don’t care,” Ellis says and picks the green out of her stuffing. Kendall gasps and swats her sister’s hand. “Don’t do that! I spent forever on the stuffing.” Ellis tosses her the shut-the-hell-up-you-know-you-can’t-cook-for-shit look reserved only for Kendall. Kendall sighs. On the other side of the table, their older brother is involved in a heated discussion with father, Jim. “I’m trying to help you, Jon. From father to son,” Jim says, pouring a pool of gravy atop his mashed potatoes, “Fruit Of The Loom doesn’t chafe your midsection as much as Hanes does.” “And I’m trying to help you, dad,” Jon replies, “Real men wear boxers.” “Oh, phooey,” Jim says, “Boxers are for them hom-o-sex-u-als.” “Au contraire, mon pere,” Ellis says, “I like my men in boxers.” There’s spinach stuck between Jim’s teeth when he responds, “Don’t all them loose girls?” “Father!” Kendall shrieks. Kendall likes guacamole and lettuce dipped in hummus. “Don’t know,” Ellis retorts, undeterred, sighs pensively, “Why don’t we just call up Mistress Jenny and ask her?” Jim seethes. Jonathan snickers. Kendall blushes. Ellis eats. Mother snaps her phone shut and expels a theatrical sigh. “Aunt Muffy’s dead. Who knew hair diffusers could be so dangerous?” -- Ellis is getting high on the balcony. Jonathan, fresh from powdering his nose in the restroom, takes the seat next to her. “Ellis. Can I talk to you about something?” “No.” “Thanks,” Jonathan sighs. “I need to tell someone.” Jonathan has Hugh Grant hair, but he’s fat. Jonathan thinks he’s a sculptor, but Mother is still waiting for him to become a podiatrist. Ellis takes a drag and coughs. Jonathan sips green tea. “I don’t know where to begin,” he says. “Don’t begin,” Ellis says. “I think I’m gay,” Jonathan says. “Tubular,” Ellis deadpans. “El,” Jonathan sighs. Ellis yawns. “Can you talk to me, at least? Can you talk to me for once?” An owl sings. “No.” Jonathan tries to growl. “Why are you like this?” Ellis supplies no answer and instead blows a ring of smoke. She is getting quite good at it. Jonathan shakes his head and angrily swigs his Lipton. “I don’t understand you, Ellis. I just don’t understand you.” “My name is Ellis,” Ellis offers, “I like to smoke pot and shoot people.” Six minutes later Kendall walks onto the balcony. Kendall drives a hybrid Toyota and does pilates, too. “What are you guys doing out here? It’s freezing. It’s snowing.” “Want some?” Ellis waves the joint and giggles. Kendall looks at Ellis. Ellis cracks up. Kendall looks at Jonathan. Jonathan is muttering and ripping the label off of his green tea. Later, Jonathan tells Kendall he’s gay. “Oh, sweetheart. Everybody and their mother knows,” Kendall shakes her and takes a drag. “Everybody and their mother knows,” Ellis repeats and hiccups. Kendall and Ellis cackle and high five each other. Jonathan sighs and watches as a snowflake falls onto his big toe. -- Eye contact is important because it makes people trust you. “I trust you,” Mother says, and Ellis tries to makes eye contact. “Okay,” Ellis says. Mother is wearing two different shoes. One is red and one is black. Mother hops onto one foot. “Black?” Mother hops onto the other. “Or red?” “I don’t know,” Ellis says, “Do it again.” Mother complies. “Black?” Hop. “Or red?” Ellis sighs, perplexed. She cannot choose. “Again.” “Black?” Hop. “Or red?” “Ehhhh.” Black? Hop. Or red? Hop. Black? Hop. Or red? Hop. Black? Hop. Or red? This goes on until Mother figures out what Ellis is up to and then proceeds to shriek and throw the other black heel at her daughter’s forehead. “Fuck, owww. OW. I think I’m dead.” Mother is suddenly concerned. “Oh, Ellie, baby. Sweetheart. Come here.” “I’m sorry.” Mother strings her fingers through Ellis’ hair. “Don’t touch me.” “Sssh.” “Stop it.” “I love you.” “Motherrrr.” “Fine.” -- “Ellis!” Jim barks, “Get over here!” Ellis stops mid-drag and walks over to her father. “Open my beer, would you?” Blink. Ellis tucks the joint against one corner of her mouth and grabs the Bud bottle with one hand, twists and pops the cap against her belt buckle with the other, and hands it back to her father. Jim snorts. “Where’d you learn that there from? A Coke commercial?” Ellis shrugs and jumps over the back of the couch, landing next to him. “What are you watching?” “The game,” Jim replies. “Them damn Seminoles don’t got no defense. They’re all over the place.” It’s more of a statement than a question. “Aren’t they always.” Jim takes a swig of his beer. Ellis purses her lips and blows. “Dad?” Jim’s eyes don’t leave the television. “What?” “Can I ask you something?” “Already did. Might as well do it again.” “What happened?” Ellis looks at him. “What happened to us?” Jim takes another swig. “How do you mean?” Ellis knows that Jim knows what she means. A minute and twenty-eight seconds later, Jim emits a low sigh and talks from his nose. “Shit, Ellis. God only knows. I mean,” Jim looks at her, “Shit happens for a reason, right?” The joint is dying out. Ellis’ fingernails play with the paper. “I guess.” “I love you, Ellis,” Jim says, “I love you more than you will ever know.” Ellis nods slowly and takes one more drag before answering, “I know, Dad. I know.” *** c. it’s time to do something good for myself In Ellis’ dreams, her daughter is alive and smiling. There’s blood on Claire’s collar and blood in Ellis’ hair. There’s a train, and Ellis is fucking petrified on the tracks. It won’t stop for Ellis alone, though, because Ellis hasn’t earned it, because Ellis isn’t worth the effort, because Ellis is nothing without the people she needs. Ellis sees Claire running away, laughing as if she’ll stay young forever. Her hair shortens and her waist thins and it’s Logan; he turns around and half smiles in that way of his and asks Ellis why she cares so much. His words bury themselves like knives in Ellis’ throat, with a crushing sort of heaviness. It goes like this: Ellis breathes in and Ellis breathes out and it doesn’t get any easier. She feels like laughing until she starts shaking, until there’s nothing left to be sucked out of her. She feels like standing on top of a building and screaming until her throat bleeds. She feels like destroying something, anything, herself, maybe, maybe. Ellis cares and she can’t stop. She doesn’t stop. It’s morning and the feeling is still the same. It’s morning. (Ellis used to tell herself, after Logan, that she’d had her heart ripped out once and it’d never happen again, not because she’d be smarter the next time but because she’d tear it out herself first and lock it away for safekeeping. Ellis lies a lot, though. She’s a fucking softie - she’s out there for the world to see and ridicule and hurt. Ellis thought it was worth it, though, she thought a lot of things, and now all she can think about is how black the water seemed, of the gem around Claire’s neck, of the look on Claire’s face before she descended beneath the surface, all of the words that Logan used to say, all of the lies he told, all of the times she’d believed him. Ellis is a fucking poor judge of honesty because she trusts the people that matter implicitly and painfully, and she still hasn’t learned. Ellis has loved all of them, once. She loved them and they used her. She loved them and they lied to her. Ellis used to think that it was worth it, though, and the worst thing is, she still might. Being who she is, she should’ve known that everybody acts, she should’ve kept believing that, she should’ve.) And this is what Ellis wants to do: Ellis wants to slide down against the wall and maybe cry a little and feel her daughter’s hand on her shoulder and not have to say a single goddamned word. Ellis wants to taste the blood in her mouth and she wants to be sixteen again. But what Ellis wants most of all is to go to sleep and never have to wake up, what Ellis wants is to live in her dreams again and never have to understand that -- that. That. There is a tear stuck in Ellis’ dimple when she steps off the plane back home. Chandler is waiting for her with open arms, and Ellis steps into them. This.
