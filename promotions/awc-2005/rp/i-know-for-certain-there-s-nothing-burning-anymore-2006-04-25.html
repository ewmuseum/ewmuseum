i know for certain (there's nothing burning anymore)

25 April 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: April 25, 2006 question: how do you live with that kind of guilt? answer: i don’t. i live around it. i live in spite of it, or i don’t live at all. i die in slow increments, one day at a time. been doin’ it for months now, sometimes seems like i’ve been doin’ it my entire life. every time i close my eyes, i see her. most of the time, she’s on fire. sometimes, though, she’s wrapped in a bath towel, or she’s painting her toenails raspberry red. and that’s when i die the most. - The only promise they made to each other was that they wouldn’t make any promises because - because their lives are already built on lies and fabrications - easier than truth, and far safer, and - and it’s a test of wills and force, between them, but - but they are so evenly matched that the greater power comes in bending. * For Kip, acting is existence. Acting is the reason the world was created and the reason it’s eventually going to end. His death scene is going to be spectacular; he is going to die surrounded by children and a plump wife or husband or he is going to be shot and killed during a robbery in a convenience store, and it is going to be seemingly meaningless or, well, anything can happen, really. He can play it any way he wants to. That’s the point of acting. It doesn’t have to be a lie. It’s the truth - any truth, all truth, every truth. Everything that happens can happen in eight million different ways if it’s just played slightly different. An inflection on a different word, the gesture of a hand; anything can change history and create a different future, but actors are the only ones who actually get to experience this: actors can stop and change the way they say any word and change their expression and change everything. The entirety of the world is mutable and it all ends when God says Cut. Kip has his own fantasies, is sometimes God in his own head. He mostly understands himself; he understands that he’s hollow until he fills himself with a character, pours someone else’s blood into his flesh and when he is no longer Troy, or Ford, or Hunter, he fills himself with a character named Kyson, Kip, Kiiiiiiiiiip, Kid, Dude, Hey you, That actor, The cute one, I know his name, I’ve seen him somewhere, Wasn’t he in that movie?, I think he’s from Texas. -- He can still remember the drive from Texas to California, dust-choked and sun-baked in the desert and so fucking full of dreams that it hurt, nitroglycerin in his veins, threatening to blow any second, potential swirling in what might become a toilet if he didn’t do something fast, if he didn’t do something now, if he spent one more day in Conroe, Texas guzzling beer in a bar and bullshitting his way through a life that he eventually decided, with fierce resolve, meant nothing to him, that he would die. “Get out of my way, Dean,” and then he threw his duffel in his car and got the hell out. The dreams kept him alive. The dreams destroyed him. He was meant for something bigger than this. If he wasn’t, he should be. The fact that he was so far from what he wanted made him want to cry defeat and collapse on the ground until the southwestern desert picked him clean to the bones. It was one year to the new millennium and Kip was surrounded on all sides by things that seemed to have no ending: the sky, the land, this road. He wanted to shake his fists at the sky and shout, “Make it happen! Whatever you have planned for me, make it happen now!” -- and the dreams kept him alive. ** If anyone had bothered to ask Kip, he would tell them that the reason he is so mysterious and vague and whatever other words reporters and his friends use in place of his preferred distracted is not because he’s an artist, because that’s a bullshit answer — to a bullshit question, yes, but nevertheless, even bullshit questions have answers. The reason is because his dreams are very straightforward. People who don’t have straightforward dreams have to be straightforward in life, but Kip has never had the problem of symbolic dreams; no oceans representing bathtubs representing childhood trauma, no horses for good luck — well, he doesn’t know; it’s all bullshit. Dreams have meaning, but it’s not meaning you can ascribe to them, and you can’t call a show cornflakes. Kip tells people his dreams are straightforward, but they don’t understand what that means. People will usually assume that what he’s saying is that he understands the symbolism in his dreams, but that’s not what he’s saying. What he’s saying is that his dreams are like stereo instructions or, even better, a paint-by-number kit. He used to make those for Lucas when he was still a baby. Sometimes he still does; he will sketch out what he’s seen that day, and label every piece with a number, and make Lucas a code of smeared grass and mud and a piece of clothing. Lucas likes it, and even though the pictures never look like Kip’s images of them, they’re still beautiful. Kip dreams he’s driving. Every single time, no fail. He can’t remember a dream in which he wasn’t driving, which is a little ironic to him since he hates cars and prefers to walk so as to damage the environment as little as possible. But it’s unavoidable most of the time, like the chemicals for processing prints, or the oil paints he uses on canvas. Sometimes it’s different cars, but usually it’s a compact. Lately it’s been a Mini Cooper with a Union Jack topper, but it could be anything. Once or twice it’s Dean’s red F150. Sometimes it’s Escher-esque cars that would never exist, but they drive well, and that’s what matters. He could be driving through the deserts or driving through the rainforest. Then there are road signs, telling him which way to go. They are the green and white of American road signs, all the time, but the language could be anything, even some he didn’t realize he knew. Most often they are in English, with some Spanish thrown in—izquierda, you fucking moron—fucking turn left! And, literally, that is what the road signs will say. People tend to think he’s joking when he tries to explain that no, his subconscious mind fights its way to the forefront and gives him directions. The best example he has of this came late in life: he fell asleep on the airplane on the way to Australia, and dreamed of driving through rolling green hills that looked almost like a pointillist painting, because nothing gelled together. On the side of the road, about halfway there, was a tall, stooped over man wearing a pointy wizard hat. He held a sign, and the sign said, “Your true life is about to begin.” When he saw this sign, in the dream, he stepped on the brake, and let the car coast down one hill and up another, and when he pushed the gas again and the car gained speed, the landscape stretched out in front of him as though he had all the time in the world to get where he was going — and he could go as fast as he wanted, but he would still get there at the right time. --- “you’s the wind benea’ mah wingsch, kippy,” slurred dean, stumbling into their bedroom with whiskey on his breath. *** Sometimes, Kip dreams about memories. He dreams/remembers the feel of the Australian ground, the cold and soft of snow in the Canada, the rough scratch of Dean’s beard, the hard line of Dad’s spine, the skin stretched taut over Alyssa’s hips, his son’s long eyelashes that looked soft and smooth but pricked Kip’s fingertip. The slick, sharp scales of fish and the slimy inner skin of banana - the spring that poked out of the mattress on the floor of the first apartment he ever lived in by himself, and the rough wood floorboards that gave him splinters; he’d sanded them down and varnished them, but not before he had a splinter in each finger from tracing the wood grain, and he’d pulled the splinters out and colored in a black and white print with the blood. It was artistic and pretentious and ridiculous, but he still had it, somewhere. Kip wears his memories on his body, some of them, for everyone to see: scars from shoots and tramps through the woods and surfboards hitting his face. His fingerprints aren’t obscured, not quite, but close enough because they’ve changed a lot over the years with each additional wound. People always want to know the story behind each scar, how did he get this one or that one? Well, he sometimes says, with a smile to gentle the words, when skin is damaged, the immune system responds immediately to prevent infection — and before he can get to the part about the dermis laying down collagen fibers, whoever asked has glazed eyes and is nodding, and Kip is spared having to painstakingly explain how each wound was inflicted. Scars are a sign of valor, a sign that the body has survived attack. Even self-inflicted attack; well, especially self-inflicted attack. That isn’t something too well understood anymore, especially in the western world, where smooth skin is a mark of beauty and scars are considered ugly. Kip loves scars, though, and wounds; anything to tell him where something has been, any roadmap of a life. If there are perfect shadows, and a shot is framed well, scars and wounds and texture all stand out in sharp relief, and if Kip closes his eyes and sits very still, he can call up the memory of anything he’s ever touched and, sometimes, things he hasn’t. Once, when he processed his film, he saw that on the film was a picture he had never taken, of a person he had never been able to stop watching, fingers that always compelled his gaze, hands that made his own ache, the scars he had never touched — but he’d wanted to. Oh, he’d wanted to. He is aware, though, that sometimes touching is too loaded, and isn’t the sort of thing that one can do casually, because sometimes it's causal. Memory has power, the creation of memory is powerful — and Kip didn’t need to touch Dean, or even be near Dean, to conjure what his scars felt like. Sometimes when Kip is doing something else, the memories he never got to make come to the forefront of his mind, and he has to bite his lip to bring himself into the present; has to taste his own blood to stop thinking of the blood that could have been; has to breathe in steam or scent to rid his tongue and throat of the tight desire; has to carefully run his hands through the musty box of dirt he brought home from a shoot in Australia, stare at the small jar of Canadian snow, long since turned to water but still powerful; has to concentrate hard, has to focus to force his mind to lose the ghosts of nonexistence. ---- they wrestle and tussle and wrangle, they struggle and swing and bite, they shove and slam, they bruise and bleed, they sleep fitted together, like a pair of blades, clean and curved, resting before the next clang and clash of battle – it is what it is, it is how it is, it is, it is often rough but never angry, it is sometimes gentle but never sweet, it tastes like coffee or like whiskey or like salt sometimes, sweat or tears or both – they yell and they snarl, they push and they fall – it is theirs. he is his. they are. **** Conroe to Los Angeles is twenty-nine hours if you take at least one nap and eat at least two burgers, because you can’t go in a straight line, because there are deserts and mountains and places in West Texas where there’s no gas for a hundred miles. Conroe to Los Angeles is twenty-one hours if you don’t stop for anything but fuel, if you keep the window down and the music up loud enough rattle your teeth. Conroe to Los Angeles is eighteen hours if you don’t stop for anything but fuel and if you do eighty and you keep adding water to the radiator and you hold on to Saint Christopher so tight that you have a halo on your palm by the time you cross the Arizona line. Conroe is darkness and Conroe is drunkenness; Conroe is nightmares of Los Angeles, of his mother, of flashing silver and steel, of blood swirling with the sewage in shadowed alleys. Conroe is neither here nor there, not the journey, not the destination, not the dream, not the waking. Conroe to Los Angeles is thirty-two hours, and the road ahead turns at the flip of a coin. Dean doesn’t have any time to waste. ----- they kill for each other. fire, water, lead, silver, steel, and sometimes there is blood, and sometimes there is nothing but the dark. sometimes when they say it’s over, we’re over, their voices shake because they know they’re lying. they know that this haunting, that monster, each of these things are like the individual flames that make up a forest fire - extinguishing them one at a time is a losing battle. they know, and they still fight. they are, they cannot stop, they cannot be what they are not. it is theirs. he is his – – they are, and – they will be again.
