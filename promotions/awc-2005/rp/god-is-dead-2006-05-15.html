God is dead

15 May 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 5/15/06 The Captain doesn't much believe in God anymore. Or Allah, but really, they're one in the same. Most people (ignorant Westerners, in Suleimon's mind) think Allah and God are two different beings, but they're not. Islam does hold Jesus Christ and Abraham, among others, as major prophets. But that's not the point. The point is, Captain Suleimon doesn't believe too much in God anymore. At one point in his life, he was a devout Muslim. Very devout. Knelt and prayed in the direction of Mecca every day. Didn't touch pork or beef and cheese in congress with each other. But something changed. The scene is Istanbul, circa 1987, bustling streets, people trying to make a living, honest or not. One of the men who was on the honest side was Farouk Aziz Salim Suleimon, who had a loving wife and two sons at home, the elder of which was a young lad named Tariq. Farouk was a very religious man, a trait he had instilled in his sons. So religious, so devoted to Islam, Farouk even quietly lent his verbal support to jihadists, terrorists. Men who used hijackings and murder to wage their holy wars on the infidels. He never took up arms or sent them a red cent. And he never really spoke about it in public, or at work, never knowing really who was listening. But in his heart, he welcomed those fighting in the name of Islam, for the Ayatollah, fighting a war against the filthy secularist pigs in Iraq. Saddam Hussein? An abomination. He admired Khadaffi and Arafat. He secretly wished to fight those wars, but he knew his duty was to his family. So he worked diligently, raising his sons to be devout Muslims like he was. And they were. Tariq especially. He wanted to be just like his father in every way. He even made hajj with him the previous year, an experience that at the time, filled him with such wonder. Then, something changed. One day, his father didn't come home from work. It was eight o'clock. His brother was in tears, his mother worried sick. Finally, the phone rang. His father was in jail. He said he was innocent, that someone was stealing money from the registers at work and they blamed him. He swore his innocence, and Tariq at least believed him. He knew in his heart that a man of Allah could not steal. The trial though... the evidence was seemingly insurmountable, even if it looked questionable to the sanest of folks in the courtroom. The truth of the matter was, the jury, judge and Farouk's defense attorney were all on the take, but no one really knew that. The men who framed Farouk really went to great lengths to make sure he was put away for a long time. The charges went from petty theft to embezzlement and even grand theft auto. But no one knew anything of that nature. The justice system in Turkey didn't rally promote justice and it could be bought easily. All they knew was that a family man, a devout Muslim was on trial for a crime they believed he didn't commit. Tariq believed he was doing his part by praying extra hard every day, asking Allah to shine the light of his wisdom upon the jury and judge and make them acquit his father. Every day, his prayers were focused on his father, and yet, when it came time for the jury to lay down its verdict. 'Guilty.' Tariq was crushed. He cried in his room, and for the first time in his life, he questioned why Allah would ignore his prayers. He didn't eat for five days. He couldn't sleep. He cried uncontrollably at times. As the sentence of life imprisonment was handed down, Tariq sat in court. He was stone-faced. As his father passed him in court on his way to prison, he turned to his son. He knew that Tariq was having a crisis of faith, he could see it in his eyes. He looked Tariq in those eyes, and he said, 'Do not fear for me. Allah tests those who are strong with hardships. Trust in God.' Tariq took those words to heart. He prayed even harder every day now, hoping that his father would not have to suffer through the hardships must longer, that the hardships would make him a stronger man. He visited Farouk every week. Every week, he and his father had the same conversation. 'Father... I prayed for you all week.' 'As have I, son. I have prayed long and hard for my deliverance.' 'So did I.' 'Good. Soon, I shall be free. Allah merciful shall send someone to free me. He shall send one of his harbingers, and he shall free me from this hell, and I shall be with you and your mother and brother once more.' Tariq believed him, but he believed him less enthusiastically each week. Each week, he waited for Allah's harbinger to come and free his father. He knew the harbingers his father spoke of. His father thought that Allah would send one of the mujihadeen. Tariq did too. Even though he saw that they were busy blowing things up in Beirut or hijacking planes, he held out hope that they would come and do Allah's work at the Istanbul prison that his father was trapped in. Three years, he waited. Finally, his mother got a phone call from the prison. As she hung up, her face looked ashen. She stumbled over her words. 'Who was it, mother?' 'It... it... was the prison.' The silence was pregnant. Tariq, choosing not to believe the look on his mother's face, felt as if the words 'your father has escaped' were the next ones to come out of her mouth. But they weren't. 'Your father... your father...' She couldn't finish. She broke down and cried, and at that instant, Tariq knew that his father hadn't escaped. The funeral was three days later. Farouk Suleimon had run afoul of a gang in prison. He got into an argument with the leader and insulted him, so late at night, having stolen the guards keys earlier that day, broke into Farouk's cell, gangraped him and brutalized him in the process, leaving him for dead. He passed away in the prison infirmary, which was less an infirmary and more a dingy closet with a few bandages in it. To most who knew him, Farouk, an innocent, devout Muslim, died that day. But to Tariq, more than that had died. His father had died. And his faith had died. When he got home, Tariq went to his room and threw his Qu'ran out of the window. He tore his prayer mat with a knife. Religion... how useless! HE spent all that time praying, and for what? His father to die? He wanted his father free... free of the prison, free of the torment... home like he always was. He was distraught. He didn't pray anymore. He had a cheeseburger, just to spite the spiteful God who took his father away from him. He went different places to go and try to forget about his pain. One such place was the library. Where he happened to chance upon a book about the Pasha dynasty of the Ottoman Empire... 'White Lord this! White Lord that!' The Great Captain was in one of his trademark huffs, this time, watching some film of the Furious Fists of God. Their reliance on religion... it bothered him... it reminded him too much of a face he once knew. But it also reassured him. Suleimon fell silent. Dogan happened to walk in on the entire tirade, and seeing his cousin fall silent, he was perplexed. His tirade, silenced. But why? Suleimon began to laugh. Now this really started to bug Dogan. He stepped forward... 'Tariq...' his voice trailed off as he cleared his throat. Suleimon, startled, turned around and raised an eyebrow to Dogan. 'What's so funny? Why are you acting so strange?' The Captain giggled again. 'He's praying for the White Lord to bail him out.' 'So, he's relig...' Before he could finish... 'Doesn't he know... the White Lord only helps those who help themselves?' Knowing what he knew about his Uncle Farouk, Dogan quieted down, nodded and crept back into the shadows.
