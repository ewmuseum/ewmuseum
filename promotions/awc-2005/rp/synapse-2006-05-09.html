Synapse

9 May 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: may 9th -- -- -- There was once a little girl who would get into accidents, a lot, falling down or getting hit or tripping, so that she would go home to her mother bruised and bleeding. Mother would fuss over her, denying her the privilege of the scraped elbows and skinned knees that were a badge to kids of that age. She kept her white and whole, and the girl resented that so much that on her ninth birthday, she rode her bike straight into a parked car. All she has left of that incident, though, is a small scar on her knee where she had split it against the sidewalk. She’d healed soon enough, much to her mother's relief. Bruises fade and scratches mend; those are the things that always heal with time. ** a. i can fly, but i want her wings A plume of smoke curls up and up between her fingers. The bitch is always smoking and if she isn’t then she’s talking, talking on and on in her infuriatingly smooth voice, spinning words into sentences and sentences into flawless gorgeous constructs of logic and then leaving them off in persuasions that never go anywhere anyway, as if she realizes halfway through that she doesn’t need consent, she doesn’t need a Yes Sir No Sir How high would that be Sir, what the hell is bullshit?; Ally can beat Ellis until she’s screaming and dead and the druggies downstairs won’t twitch an eyelash. -- (sometime later she’ll realize a way to butter up the sister - ply her with chocolates and marijuana and black-bean coffee [newyorkrecipe] before shoving her, faultlessly languid on poisonous kindness, into the preheated oven.) ... and I think, sometimes, you’re really stupid, Ellis concludes, tilting her head and wrinkling her nose at the joint, unpleasantly beautiful with those long, long bones and that thin waist, chemicals working their way to her brain, too young and too old. They’re on the balcony. The sky is a mess of black and white and yellow -- black of the night, white of the moon, yellow of the stars. (stars are the closest suns, Ally knows, but Ellis never learns.) What are you talking about? “What are you talking about?” asks Ally. Ellis’ tongue is heavy. She smacks it against the roof of her mouth, a bundle of tissues against what’s the primary cause of neighbor Don’s cleft palate, and it’s like she’s trying tread water despite the currents, big flippers a stumbling block. The tongue is supposed to manipulate food, but Ellis finds it manipulating words more often. That, Ellis begins, “you’re stupid?” Yeah. -- “ .. yeah.” The lips are tangible and tactile sensory organs utilized for the articulation of speech. The words Ellis spews from her lips are an amalgam of pot-induced haze and exhaustion, and are not articulate at all. “I.. uh - - what are we talking about?” Beneath them, Cortland is loud and sloppy. Somewhere, off to their left, there's a police siren; on the corner of William and Church, a seventeen-year old snags his first deal; in Suggett Park, one man's fist connects with the nose of another man's, and on the balcony, Ally tucks a knee under her chin, tucks her hands under her chin, and folds her fingers together. -- the eyes are organs of vision used to detect light, but when she closes her eyes all Ally sees is the blood and tissue of her eyelids, the face forever scratched into her memories. “I don’t know anymore.” That sentence means too many things to quantify. Ellis hates symbols, metaphors, allegories. She wants her life to be prose. Literary devices are for the pretentious and the dead and, anyway: If Ellis juts her head out into the wind, just a little, she can physically smell lights, or some idea or philosophy of lights that catches the air and sends it straight to her head. They smell like a phonecall from faraway, the voice a human transmits through wire, a mini-human, a Thumbelina she could put in her palm and give a big kiss to make it all feel better. (this is the pot speaking, of course.) “I can smell lights,” says Ellis. Her lips articulate a giggle, or something like one. Don’t worry; we won’t go into the anatomy of the arm, the hand, or the physiology that encompasses the muscle there; Ally simply reaches over, gathering Ellis’ left hand in hers. She holds up their palms, slowly, and the white of the moon bounces off of Ellis’ ring -- Ellis sucks in cancer, heaves it out, (diffusion, smoke/carbon dioxide swimming through the air;) and smiles at her sister. Their hands fit together like they always have. Ellis’ tongue is heavy, and when she tries to talk it’s like she’s trying to tread water, but the most important thing to remember is all you need to live is to breathe. (the next morning, ellis flips through the obituaries and takes a sip of coffee. it’s nine, the sun is bright. she thinks, this is walking through a cemetery of the freshly dead, this is saying, Hello George and Mary and Lin when I don’t even know you, this is standing at the gate of heaven and shouting goodbye, goodbye.) -- here’s the thing: the moon creates movement. they both learned this in junior high -- the moon creates the tides and it can drown or save a man. after school, ellis skipped soccer and disappeared into the library. she surrounded herself with all sorts of books with words like orbit and crater and gravitational pull, and read until she couldn’t understand anything anymore. there was a lot she didn’t understand (and wouldn’t understand until later), but she understood enough at thirteen to get the highest score on the test. ally was stranded somewhere near the bottom of her class’ list, at the time. when ellis plastered her test on the fridge one night, ally bit into an apple, grinned around the shards and said, “good job.” years later, ally understands everything, understands that ellis knew then the only thing she needs to know now: that although the moon creates movement, it still needs a star to orbit. ** b. i dream to heal your wounds, but i bleed myself Two days later, Ellis is brushing her teeth in a hotel room in Norway when Chandler calls from a phone line buzzing with static and digital echoes. She can barely tell his voice from the warping of sound waves, for which Chandler apologizes. He explains that he’s using a new software program to make the call, which involves going through a company in Manhattan that makes you stare at pop-under advertisements for lotteries in New Hampshire in exchange for free direct dialling anywhere in the world, no strings attached. Oh, Ellis says, and ignores Chandler as he goes on: broadband plus New Hampshire lotteries equals free phone calls to Europe. One million permutations times x number of cognizant computer programmers equals the Hand of God. Intuition is simply compressed logic, says Chandler. Give technology enough time and data and anything is possible. Plus, I’m at the office and Howie doesn’t want me calling long distance. He told me to use this thing. “What do you want?” “It’s about the Walter case,” says Chandler. Along with the static, there’s plastic ripping on the other end of the line; he better not be eating one of her goddamn granola bars. Ellis sighs, hucks up saliva and snot and toothpaste and spits into the sink, watching it all whirl together into one giant mess of white, swirling down the drain. Then, she coughs, and wipes the back of her mouth with her wrist. “What about it?” “I need your help.” The florescent lighting above flickers, buzzing like a fly. Ellis turns the water off. “Yeah, I guessed that. What do you need my help with?” “Well,” replies Chandler, moving the phone receiver between his neck and cheek as he shuffles through files, “I know you kind of, er, talked to Dennis, you know, that night we brought him in?” “So?” “So, Dennis’s dead, and he was our only suspect, and I was just wondering if he, like, told you anything, or what --” “We were talking about my shoes, Chandler.” “.. Oh. So, you didn’t get anything good?” “If you consider his fashion advice applicable to what you need, then sure. Yeah. I got something good. Black only works with black. How is that?” “Shit,” mutters Chandler, and Ellis can feel him digging into his temples with his thumbs, “God damnit.” “I’m sorry.” “Yeah, yeah,” says Chandler, discouraged nevertheless. “It’s just, you know, this case has been at my ass for weeks now, and. I don’t know. Everything was just a little easier when you were here, you know?” “Yeah.” Pause. “I have no idea who I’m supposed to turn to, now. As a suspect, I mean. There’s no one. Well, there’s Dennis’ girlfriend, but she’s got a pretty strong alibi, and Katherine didn’t have a lot of family, really. No one worth looking up, at least.” “You never know. Could be the grandma. You remember the Coulson case.” “Yeah,” snicker, something like a smile, maybe. “Yeah, I guess so.” Pause. A beat. Another something. Chandler sighs, “Yeah. Well, anyway, I’ve got to go. Have a good trip, do what you do. I’ll see you when you get home.” “Alright. Bye,” says Ellis. She hangs up before she can tell him that the Hand of God has never been achieved, will never be achieved and, in other words, is part of a mythology created to give people hopes and dreams as well as a feeling of significance – a mythology whose manifestation is about as likely as the resurrection of the dead. -- the Hand of God gives out scars. When she enters the bedroom, Ally is sitting on the red bedspread of the king, reading old dead poetry that tries to hide its life in the corners of the words. The lexicon is a hide and seek, and the poets manipulate it like a lawyer. Ellis collapses beside her, legs curling to her chest. It takes a couple of minutes for her breathing to steady, her lips to purse, and that’s when Ally folds the corner of the page and quietly leans over, switching the lamp off. * and, ally dreams a lot. every morning she wakes up knowing that she has just been Robinson Crusoe in the night as she tastes the saltiness of unbrushed teeth in her mouth. she can never remember, however, what has happened. after all, she knows that her life is not a textbook of words, and this is sad. when she was twelve she grew very ill, and dreamt of millions of cockroaches infesting every part of skin they could find as she drowned. she was sweating and screaming silently and grasping at her sister when she woke up, finally. when she wakes up, now, in the middle, she isn’t holding onto Ellis, and suddenly she misses being twelve and acting like the world could possibly be at the feet of a very small thing, and chasing butterflies halfway across the sky because butterflies were gods with wings and believing, hoping, wishing like some mad fool that it would snow on Christmas. ally walks over to the window, sits on the sill and looks at the stars, slight pinpricks that twinkle just enough to suggest that there’s another galaxy, another people, way out there. (scientists are never charmed as poets are, but scientists want to know the stars to make them known, to dispel myth and legend. poets, on the other hand, don't want to know them proper, simply pretend that they do.) ** c. strumming tendons of the virgin The next morning, walking to a pharmacy, Ally asks, “What’s wrong with Aimz?” A paper bag bumps noisily against Ellis’ right leg. Sometimes it’s the left, whenever she switches the paper bag from hand to hand. Through the slats of the overhead bridge steps, she sees the world; at least, she thinks it’s the world, though it might seen like a few shrubs and flowers from very far away. “What are you talking about?” “I mean,” answers Ally, “Like, you guys hate each other?” The shoulder joint is composed of three bones: the scapula, the clavicle, and the humerus. The act of shrugging was creating from the process of weighing two objects in a person’s hands. This process was instinctively used by humans to compare weight based on which object requires more normal force from one’s hand to accelerate upwards, into gravity. “I don’t know.” “Seriously.” “Seriously? I don’t know.” Ally gives her a look. “What? I’m for real, Ally. Like, she barges in with all this, I don’t know, this audacious sort of ego, starts ripping on me left and right, you know, claiming I don’t deserve to be at the place where I’m at today. I don’t know. She’s just weird. It’s frustrating.” “What’s frustrating?” Along the bridge there were crowds of lights. They pass two women, one in blue and one in red, like a sequence of veins and arteries. An enemy is an enemy is an enemy unless you have down time with him. “What’s frustrating,” begins Ellis, kicking a pebble. “What’s frustrating is that I don’t know what I did. What’s frustrating is that she goes on and on about, you know, on and on with this blah blah Ellis doesn’t deserve to be champion bullshit, she’s not as hardcore as I am, and I don’t know what the fuck I did. Maybe she just hates women.” “Maybe,” says Ally. “That’s not healthy.” They step into pools of pollution and are joined by hoards of tiny students making their way to school and their mothers (holding their hands tightly, all determined to compete at the extent of their ability to embarrass the children). “Whatever. I don’t give a shit. Whatever happens, happens. I don’t give a shit if she wins or not. I don’t live and breathe this business. She seems to. Whatever, you know, as long as she just shuts the fuck up for once.” what a lie: sure, ellis’ll forget about it days from now and months from now she won’t remember a single bit of it, not the pain, or the anger, or anything in particular, not even how bitter her sweat tasted as it crawled ever slowly down her forehead into the corner of her mouth. she won’t remember how much she wanted it all -- wanted to win and wanted to lose and almost wants to kill aimz, almost, wanted to put a goddamn fist into her stupid nose, wants this, (this whole thing) to mean more than just numbers and gold. it’ll be one of those momentarily emotional and full-blown impulsive flings. it’ll make her feel free, even through the hazy pain, even through the defeat. it’ll wound her, later, when she loses, in the beginning. “Well,” Ally side-steps a child and his mother and says, “What about Adam?” “What about him?” “I don’t know. What’s going on with you guys?” -- [ she’d enthuse about boyfriends. she’d gesture fervently about late nights out and soft blurred kisses in the rain and scattered ice rinks and paired angels in the snow. always she’d question ellis’s apparent lack of enthusiasm. she’d cock her head and pick ellis like a lock. ] Ellis shrugs again. “I don’t know. He’s fun, I guess.” “Fun,” a period in the air, pregnant in its dull glory. -- ellis dissected a heart in biology class once. she no longer has faith in the poetry of symmetrical hearts, even though she found white lace in the ventricles. also: hearts do not break, they are far too hard and squishy to do so, so when she dumps adam one of these days, she won’t feel that bad, not really. “Yeah,” says Ellis. They continue their way down the bridge. “Fun.” (if only she had some pretty pictures of the it. if only she could tie in fifty different stories.) * >> fast forward >> 1 x 3 = 1 the little girl with the scar is older now. twenty-six. the world, she thinks, is no longer out to get her, no longer there to swallow the candy-like world of her youth. the scar still burns, though. ** Stars are the closest suns, Ally knows, but Ellis never learns. Back in the hotel room, late at night with Ally dreaming she’s Robinson Cruso, Ellis opens the paper bag. TBC!
