Hold On To Me

19 March 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 03/19/2006 Ten o’clock, the moon is gettin’ high with the stars over the horizon. Ellis found Ally in the Hilton two hours before and said, I’m only going to talk to you if I’m drunk, so Ally took Ellis to this bar fifteen minutes away. They’re sitting at this table in the corner. Scene-setting: (v.) The act of setting a scene. “Back off,” says Ellis warningly, and takes a long drag. “Back off,” mimicks Ally. “Quit,” says Ellis. She sneers at Ally, who sneers back, except it’s a half-assed sneer, just a lip curl, really. “Don’t you ever miss not being the grown-up?” Ally leans over and looks in Ellis’ glass, raises her eyebrows at Ellis, then leans back down, sniffs it, sips it. Ellis lounges back in the booth and keeps her sneer on. “Maybe, but I don’t miss you on heroin.” “Heroin makes things shiny.” Ally takes another sip of Ellis’ apple juice and wrinkles her nose, winces. “I wish this was beer.” Ellis pulls out a pack of American smokes and lights up another one. Her Zippo has the Yankees symbol on it. Ally’s eyes flick to it, up to Ellis’ face, back down to the Zippo. Ellis’ wearing eyeliner; the bitch still looks like she’s in her teens. “Is that my shirt?” asks Ally, because she can’t just stare at her sister the entire night. “I stole it from you ten years ago.” “You’re such a bitch. Why do you gotta be such a bitch?” Ally inhales Ellis’ cigarette smoke again. God she wants a cigarette, she wants a fix, she wants more coffee, she wants a beer, she wants some Advil, she wants to go home, and collects spit to hurl into Ellis’ abandoned glass of juice. Right before she spits, Ally changes direction and spits toward Ellis’ face. Ellis moves faster than an old woman should be able to. The spit hits the booth’s cushioning. Ellis crushes her cigarette in the overflowing ashtray next to her leg. She looks at Ally and her pale eyes are reflecting light from the street, her tanned skin is washed out to sallowness. She doesn’t say anything, and Ally feels the exact second tick by where she’s supposed to take that back. She doesn’t. She gets up and heads towards the restroom in this dingy little bar on the outskirts of America. Ellis lights another cigarette and follows her. “I hope you die of lung cancer,” says Ally to the mirror, smudging her lipstick, “I hope you’re twenty-seven and you have lung cancer, and you collapse. Out of the blue. Just like that.” Bored, Ellis purses her lips and blows, the smoke swallowing Ally whole. “I’m twenty-six,” says Ellis. “It doesn’t matter,” Ally smacks her lips together, “You’re still going to die of lung cancer.” “Not if you kill me first,” says Ellis. She smiles, lips thin, and sticks the cigarette into the corner of her mouth. She sucks in the nicotine and it floats over her tongue and down her throat where there are words that she wants to say but won’t because Ally is smiling, too. Ally is smiling at her. “Come on,” says Ally. She’s still smiling. They leave the next day, just like that, Dennis’ Integra left behind. * * Sunday. The top forty countdown is driving Ellis crazy. They’re in sometown New York, too large for antiquated and charmingly rustic diners, too small for any semblance of elegance or efficiently modern spaces. The streets look yellow with heat and the cracked cement wavers uncertainly, pavement turning into reflective pools of darkness that disappear like the illusions that they are when the car draws closer. There are strip malls everywhere and out-dated billboards standing heavy in the sky. The lanes are wide, and even the vegetation and trees look cheap, used. What would have been beautiful somewhere else is nothing but a weed, here. It’s, Ellis thinks, a fucking ugly town. The kind trapped between the city and the suburbs, unable to settle on either side. “There isn’t even any history to the poverty here. There’s nothing.” Ellis shifts a little in the driver’s seat and slants a look at Ally. There’s almost a snarl in her voice, something sharp and annoyed and full like a poorly played set of chords. In Cortland, there is at least life and centuries behind the poorest of places, some kind of barren beauty. She hates it when people glamorize New York, hates it when they turn the state into nightclubs and lights and shopping sprees when there are people dying and children fighting over drugs and oxygen being sold in the streets. She hates it, but she can’t deny the fact that there is, at least, something individual and striking about the crowded streets, something nothing like the systematic and mass produced mediocrity they’re driving through. She’s desperate to get out. They’ve passed two Red Lobsters and five McDonald’s. Ellis has been counting. The radio swims in and out of tune as often as the girl who’s singing does. The car halts at the stop light and Ally digs into her bag, pulls out a CD with words so hastily scribbled on top that even Ellis can’t decipher them. “Put this in.” “Fuck no. You have horrible taste.” “Fuck you, my taste is better than yours,” (Ellis knows this), “and you know it.” Ellis opens her mouth to protest, but Ally is already laughing, and she can’t help but bite back her grin and look out the window. Ally isn't buying it, she can tell. “We'll see how good it is.” They drive on a highway to nowhere. The Infiniti’s meter tells them that they’re twenty-three miles over the speed limit, and the air hangs heavy like a noose above them, soft and wet even on the edge of evening. Radiohead sings. Half an hour later, “Nights like these make me think of death.” Ellis doesn’t know what she’s supposed to say to that. Ally continues, “It’s the air here. It’s filled with this kind of regret, I think. There were just an infinite number of possibilities that were never taken and lost. I don’t know. Look at how small everything and everybody is. Nothing lasts that long.” “Regrets as ghosts,” says Ellis. “Maybe. Sad ones.” “Yeah.” Ally takes a drag on the cigarette, lets her wrist hang limply over the window. They both watch as the tip flares briefly in the breeze before the ashes are blown to the ground, still glowing softly. Ellis feels a sudden chill in the air, something solid enough to choke on. They don’t talk for a while, not until Ally says, “I'm being stupid,” as they pull out of a gas station. Ellis doesn’t reply. She wonders what Ally is referring to, decides that she doesn’t have to know, not now, doesn’t want to hear it. Nowhere turns out to be Buffalo. It’s the only thing Ellis remembers an hour later, cigarette smoke and cold air in her eyes. -- Five miles outside of Syracuse, some kid tries to sell them drugs. He’s got to be younger than the both of them by a few years, and there’s a Spanish accent buried in his words. He looks like he’s new to it or maybe it’s just a one-time deal, judging by the wideness of his eyes, the beggar’s quality to his pushing. After a while of doing it, the eyes narrow, words slow into a drawl. They won’t need to find their own customers anymore, won’t need to ask. Ally almost takes out her wallet simply because of the awful starved look in the kid’s eyes, the way his hands are pressed to their window - overly eager, shaking. She almost does, but Ellis just puts the window up all the way and passes him, takes a left, and Ally can’t see him in the rearview mirror anymore. “That isn’t the way to do it.” To help anyone, to fix the problem, to make anything better. “Do you ever think you’re being a bit too fucking ambitious?” says Ellis. Ally thinks, the money would have fed him, his family, if he had one. Paid the rent, the bills. It would have made something better, because that’s what money fucking does, it gives. But Ellis says, “Do you think you’re being too fucking nice?” and never takes her eyes off the road. It could have been an act. He wasn’t going to give the cash to his goddamned mother or wife or brother. You’ll fix his problems for an hour, and then what? It’s a waste. Nothing’s going to change until this country stops ignoring its own ugliness. Ally can hear that, even though Ellis says nothing else. Neither of them is right. The thought follows her. Ally remembers growing up in Cortland, the myths that spread around school. Stories that friends would whisper to each other at the age of thirteen, words a little awed and rushed about whose brother had been caught dealing or the occasional police car they would see roaming the streets hungrily. A few years later and they had been desensitized to all of it by the filtered news reports that trickled in, the things politicians from other New York counties would say. Playground fables turned into cutting remarks over cigarettes, a sharp twist of a smile and a name of someone who had disappeared, comments on a neighbor’s sudden wealth, the new car in his driveway. She’d lived in Cortland, a nice enough town at the time, had heard about the deaths but never knew the faces attached to the names, at least not well enough for recognition, anger. It follows her, and for the rest of the drive, every face that they pass is another story that Ally’s never known, another injustice that they can do nothing about. Another person she should have, could have cared about but doesn’t. -- They’d parked the car ten minutes ago, and Ellis’s stretching her legs for the first time in hours, sand sticking to her jeans and getting between her toes. The lake here is fucking awful, though, nothing like the one that lurks in her memory. It’s eleven, so it’s goddamned cold and damp and grey; Ellis can easily count at least five cigarette butts sticking out in the sand around her feet. It’s ugly, is what it is, ugly and dirty and more a polluted stretch of sand next to a lake than a proper lake, but she knows that there will be crowds upon crowds of people here just hours later. Squinting, she can barely see Ally up near the narrowest end. The dense fog keeps rolling in and it obscures her outline, blurs and fades it into something soft and intangible, waveringly solid. Ellis thinks that Ally looks like a ghost from where she’s standing, and tucks her hands more securely into the crooks of her elbows, kicks a pebble across the sand into the water. They’ve got to get going again. -- It’s a little before one in the afternoon and the diner’s packed with men in baseball caps and muddy boots. Ellis can just make out the strains of a basketball game over the noise of the kitchen and overused voices accustomed to shouting. It’s grey and drizzling outside, the barest hints of diluted sunshine peeking in occasionally, and there’s a wet, rotten smell rising slowly from the concrete and dirt. Ellis taps her french-fry greasy fingers against the table top, itches to ask something, and thinks, why the hell not. “Why did you leave with him?” “I don’t know.” “You do.” Ally grins, puts down the half of a sandwich that was traveling towards her mouth and works her lower lip between her teeth. “It was love.” Ellis makes a face. “Don’t give me that face,” says Ally. “It was the same thing you had with Logan. I don’t know.” Ellis wipes ketchup from the corners of her mouth with a napkin and they sit for a few seconds like that, quiet and full and wondering about all the ‘what-if’s among the shuffling of heavy feet and ding of the bell at the take-out counter. “Come on. They’re running out of room in this place.” Ally puts a twenty on the table, and they head back on the road, just like that. As Ellis holds the door open for a truck driver, turning up his collar against the rain, she can’t help but want to ask Ally another question, something else. How much of yourself can you give? What is it that you’re keeping close and locked and inside of you, why can’t you just – why? -- The questions work their way out of Ellis’ mouth the next day. They’re skirting the Cortland County border when Ellis looks away from the road in front of her for the first time in an hour or so and asks, “Why do I always feel like if I let go of you, this, whatever, let it get out of sight for a minute, that I’ll lose it? Just tell me. Why is it that I keep thinking that you’ll be gone for good if I look away for just a second?” Ally stares at her then, quiet, eyes wide and not unkind. Surprised, yeah, but open and honest and clear like Ally has always been. That in itself is a relief. Ally reaches over and turns the volume down in the middle of a sentence. (i wish, i wish that something wou--). The silence is new and frightening. Ellis wants to say, you are the air in my lungs before I lose my breath, but she’s not in a movie, so it sounds stupid, like she’s trying too hard. She wants to say, you matter the most to me, or, I love you. Even, I know this car ride has been boring as fuck, but I don’t want it to end, I don’t want this to end anymore, I don’t want. I want you to just stay. Ellis wants a lot of things, wants to say a lot of things. Most of them don’t make it out of her throat. This time, though – this time she’s tried at least. “I’m not going anywhere.” And it’s stupid because Ellis thinks it’s true but she’ll never shake off the feeling that Ally is the world’s, that no one will ever really have or know her, because too many parts of her will have already been claimed by cities and mountains and small, broken down villages. This is what Ellis thinks when she looks at Ally. She almost expects her to be semi-transparent, spread thin from everywhere and everyone that she’s given herself to. It’s not. It’s not fair. And if there’s anything that this trip has taught her, it’s that nothing’s as it should be. Nothing is fair, no matter how fucking clichéd and trite that sounds, because it’s true. “It's so different now, isn't it?” “It’s better. It’s better. Do you remember the days when I used to bartend and call you every once in a while to complain about shitty tips? And when we used to live from week to week and not know where we were going, what we were heading towards? It’s not the same, but this is better, I swear.” Ally winds their hands together. “We’re here. I’m home. It’s fine.” Ellis wants to trust her, so she does. “I think you’re rubbing off too much on me.” “Yeah?” “Yeah. But it’s okay.” “Come on,” says Ally. She lets go of Ellis’ hand. “Let’s go home,” Ellis takes a breath, clears her throat. The road ahead of them stretches into the sky, long and flat and winding. Okay. Okay, but it wasn’t the same as forgetting. What she wanted was to be able to wake up in the morning and be congruent to herself as much as she could - she didn’t want layers of Ally’s past haunting her day after day. The silence snaps again. “Do you remember that time you cut my hair?” Ellis blinks away her surprise. “Yeah. I - Yeah.” “It’s not the same now, is it.” Pause. “I mean, nothing ever stays true to itself long enough. It kind of fucking sucks, that.” Ellis bites her lower lip, wants to ask, what the fuck are you talking about? purely for the sake of it, but she knows. “It kind of does. Al, what - ” “But you’re still here. I’m with you. We’re still here.” Ally is looking at her, the side of her face resting against the passenger seat, neck tilted and expression unblinking. Her hair is shorter than it had been those years ago but her eyes still the same and hands still the same and smile still the same; she just as painfully bright to look at as she had been on that lake back in Cortland. Their Cortland, and Ellis wants to shout, wants to stand on a fucking rooftop and throw words into the terrible, clear sky, wants to spill into the streets and start a goddamned revolution. “Okay,” says Ellis. And Ally meant a lot of things with thay, but what she meant the most, is: we’ll take on the entire world, or at least Cortland, side by side, and I’m not leaving this anytime soon, and you know that. -- “I’m feeling kind of useless right now.” They’re on the elevator up to Ellis’s apartment. Dings fly by, signaling the approach. “It gets easier to accept after a while,” says Ellis. “I’m serious.” “Yeah, I know. Don’t worry about it. Explain the best you can.” “Revolutions are fucking hard to pull off at this point. We’re not all Fidel, I mean.” “Look at Ukraine. Look at how much they cared.” “I, Okay. Shit. All I’m saying is, I’m nervous, kind of. I mean, is Jon even going to understand? And if he does, is he even going to forgive me?” “I did.” Ellis looks from her nails and her eyes lock with Ally’s. Ally cracks into a smile. “Yeah,” says Ally, “You did.” Ellis smiles back. The doors fly open. Walking down the hallway towards Ellis’s apartment, Ally doesn’t quite remember Mike as well as she had before. She remembers moments as clearly as anything, but the experience as a whole is a blur. Everything happened too fast. She remembers the blind desperation, she remembers lying with her face pressed in the clean green fresh grass, the weight of something on her stomach, his voice gravelly and ragged from anger and something like tears at the corner of her eyes. The air that afternoon, something she’ll never feel again, the air saturated with her blood and sweat and everything indescribable. Ally remembers all of that and more, and it’s the sort of experience that will forever stick with her, until she is dead in her grave. What she remembers clearly, though, what she remembers clearly is her sister’s hand holding hers right now, Ellis is holding her hand and the squeeze of her fingers like something that says: It’s alright, we’ll be alright, I love you, and everything is going to be alright. Everything is going to be alright. Ally squeezes Ellis’s hand back and says, “I believe you.” They’re both right, this time. and i forgive you for being away for far too long so keep breathing ‘cause i’m not leaving you anymore believe it “far away” by nickelback
