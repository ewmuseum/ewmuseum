Summer's Son #3: 'Superpioneers'

27 August 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 27th August 2005 30th July 14:06 Still chuckling to myself (though feeling somewhat bad for doing so) at the total no-sell of Kris Krimzon’s apparent near-death at the hands of Jonny Sake on my video of Fresh! this week, I pulled my light cotton jacket over my shoulders and said a silent goodbye to the small, dark room that I wouldn’t be seeing again for at least the next month. I hadn’t actually been away from Harbrook for a prolonged period since the Easter holidays, when I’d spent a week in the familiar surroundings of my parents’ apartment in the same town of Albany, so I was somewhat excited about being on the road for the summer. Placebo’s song “English Summer Rain” had made me realise just how much I missed my home country, and funnily enough I was half-hoping it would be raining in London when we arrived there a few days before AWC’s The Battle Of Britain pay-per-view event on the 19th. England just wouldn’t be the same without the sobering drizzles and threatening clouds, the grey August noons and the year’s only snow coming in April. Dragging my suitcase behind me on its wheels, I moved quickly along the corridor, not allowing myself to wonder why I was even here when the usual inhabitants of rooms 74, 72, 70, 68 were probably ‘living it up’ in Los Angeles, or partying in Miami, or more likely wasting away stacking Wal-Mart shelves nine to five every day. I stopped and swallowed as I saw the empty reception desk where the north and south complexes merged. Ghost towns never felt this eerie. It just wasn’t right, passing reception without a teasing smirk or a plastic smile from the woman who I feared and pitied in equal measures; whose name I still didn’t know. A name tag would have solved a lot of problems, but no, I was forced instead to label her with no end of nicknames that would never stick. When I tell you that “Madame Plastic” was probably my favourite, you’ll realise I was never so good with nicknames. 30th July 15:12 I should really buy a car. I nodded my hollow thanks to the driver and descended the steps of the bus, heaving my case behind me. It hit the floor with a thud, an unexpected wrench on my arm causing me to wince, and I sighed as I saw I was twenty-three minutes late. God doesn’t love public transport. The identical apartment blocks multiplied around me, suffocating at once any ideas of diversity or architectural beauty. Concrete on concrete was the dress code, “nothing ever changes” the mantra. Spearman Heights was the building in which my parents’ lives were slowly wasting away. With a deep breath, I pushed open the door – noting that traces of the canine excrement I’d first seen around the letterbox at Christmastime were still visible. Didn’t anyone care? The smell was foreign as I moved through the lobby. My entire body had to adjust itself to Spearman Heights every time I came by – admittedly, this wasn’t all too often; I rarely visited my parents more than once every three months. Excuses were easy, my mother blinded by delusions of 24/7 lectures and essays to write in the void of time remaining. I felt bad, especially as my father had been diagnosed with heart disease four months ago – no, that was wrong, my mother had seen fit to tell me about his diagnosis four months ago. It could really have come any length of time before, and with horror I realised I didn’t actually know. Every time I visited my parents, I came away with the resolve that I really should do this more often. But a week later, that desire was gone. It depressed me, seeing them. It really did depress me to see the miserable expressions on their faces (poorly shielded by false smiles) as we sat awkwardly in their small living room and they told me about the latest household necessities that they’d sold. They clung to the ideal that their only son should be left an inheritance, but it was a non-starter. I was already giving them money from my pay-packet to even allow them to maintain this poor standard of living, and whenever I insisted on giving them more I was always shot down. Even last time I’d visited, when I’d slipped two $10 notes into my mother’s purse, she’d telephoned me later that day to say that she’d found the money, couldn’t accept it and would put it aside in an envelope for me to collect next time I visited. This was that next time, and as I stepped off the stairwell onto their floor (the lift was still “OUT OF ORDAR screwd”) I knew that as soon as I opened the door I’d be greeted by my mother pushing that very envelope into my chest, and commenting on how muscular I’d become in a surprised voice, and telling me that there was lots of food. It didn’t happen. “It’s open,” came a calm voice as I tapped the door three times. I frowned: my mother would always rush to the door to greet me. Unless, of course, she was cooking. My mother was a wonderful cook, and the food invariably came first. Slowly, I pushed open the door. The dirty beige carpet was dirtier and beiger. I had a horrible feeling it was supposed to be white. Why was that the first thing I noticed? My father was sprawled on the small two-seater sofa, his legs hanging pathetically over his arm as his white face looked up at me through thick-lensed glasses. In a word, he was unhealthy. In two, he was very unhealthy. I glanced at my mother, who was sitting on the floor, her hands on her knees, rocking a little, backwards and forwards, backwards and forwards, something on her mind and I thought I knew what; it was dad, of course. But it wasn’t, as I was soon to find out. “Hi...” I tried tentatively. My father gave a weak smile, but the warmth had disappeared from his face years ago, and the effect was more stark now that the heart disease had reduced him to this. He might not be around for much longer... kept running through my head, but I banished it – not now, not now, give him time to breathe, give me time to think. My mother pursed her lips and looked at the floor. This was not normal. I’d come prepared for disapproval and unhappiness, but neither was meant to be in evidence until after I had told them I was going to Europe. Granted, I’d expected my mother to be depressed about dad’s condition, but that never usually changed the bright eyes and red cheeks that greeted my arrival every time. Every time but this. “Is something wrong?” I asked my mother, my voice barely above a whisper. It was my father that answered: “Yes, my heart’s broken. Literally!” A smile crept slowly onto his face, and I couldn’t help the same happening to mine. Somewhere along the line, he’d got his sense of humour back, and I was happy about that. Every household comedian loves the feeling of having made a good joke, and my father was no exception; if he was going to die, I wanted him to die happy. Laughter made him happy, jokes and wit and sarcasm made him happy, yet I hadn’t heard anything of the sort out of him for years. Until now. “How’ve you been, dad?” I said gently, looking down at him with the kindest expression I could muster from my position by the door. “I'm hanging in there, Tim,” he said hoarsely, and took a few short sharp breaths before he could carry on. “I’ve got –” He lifted his arm to show me a wire leading across the room to the telephone on the wall. “There’s a button – I can press –” “The doctor thinks he’s at high risk of a stroke,” Maggie Shipley interrupted curtly. I looked at my mother with concern, but she averted her gaze at once, interested (at last) in the grimy nature of the carpet on which she was seated. “Oh,” was all I could manage, and Maggie watched the floor, and Clemence watched me. “Why are you sitting on the floor, mum?” I demanded. My patience towards her disapproving glances at me was wearing thin, since I didn’t know what had brought them on. I anticipated the answer, and a couple of seconds later, my suspicions were confirmed. “We sold the armchair,” she said coolly, still not meeting my eye. I let out the loudest possible sigh. “What do you do with the money I send?” I asked, exasperated. My mother got heavily to her feet – I moved over to help her up but she tutted loudly – and waddled (yes, it sounds harsh, but she’s not too overweight, it’s just the way she walks) over to the kitchen. A moment later, she emerged with a small tin, and thrust it into my arms. “Here, have it back,” she sniffed, as I carefully opened the lid to reveal a huge wad of banknotes. I stared up at her; she stared at the floor. “Mum, you’ve got to use this money.” She said nothing. “Mum, look at me!” She turned away. I sighed and flung down the empty box. It landed on the carpet with a clang, and my mother jumped. “What have I done?” I yelled, raising my arms up either side of me, but she wheeled around and rushed over to my father, wide-eyed. “Clemence...” “Maggie, it’s fine,” he protested, but she insisted on checking his heart rate. It was normal. Now she turned to me, fire in her eyes. “Tim! You can’t shock your father like that! His heart is weak! He could have a stroke, a heart attack, he could d –” She broke off and sat once more on the floor. This time I helped her down and this time she didn’t stop me. I sat down next to her and awkwardly placed my arm on her shoulder. “It’s fine – Timothy – it’s fine,” my father wheezed. I looked up, concerned, but he nodded gently at me to let me know he was OK and so would she be. I stood up and dusted off my jeans with a sigh. “Well, since I’ve clearly done something wrong that I know nothing about, I suppose I’d better be off,” I said loudly. My parents looked at each other, and then at me. “Where to?” my mother asked, raising her eyebrows. “I could catch an earlier flight,” I mumbled, but that was more to myself. Seeing their expectant looks, I knew that it was time to say what I’d come to say. “I – I'm going to Europe, mum, dad,” I aired breezily, swinging my arms by my sides. “Why?” my mother snapped suspiciously. I’d expected her to be interested – “For school, Tim? Research?” – but it seemed today was not the day for reading my mother correctly. “It’s for my job...” “And what job’s that now?” she interjected at once. “FedEx...” I trailed off. I’d never told my parents about wrestling and was usually able to fob them off with a quick company name-drop when they asked about my job. I’d been using the FedEx line for nearly half a year now – ever since the day I’d been told about Clemence’s heart problems, in fact – and aside from her initial suspicions on day one, I hadn’t had any interrogation. But now, it seemed, was that time. And I really wasn’t in the mood. My mother raised an eyebrow, a little character trait I’d inherited. “FedEx, huh?” I nodded and swallowed. “Help me up, Tim,” my mother ordered resignedly, realising that she wouldn’t be able to get up again so soon after last time. Her knees weren’t in the best of health. I helped her up, wondering why, and once more she went off to the kitchen. I cast a baffled glance at my father, who closed his eyes. He was in on this. Maggie came back with a large brown envelope. “This came for you.” I took it from her and pulled out the contents. The envelope had already been opened, and from the sounds of things, my parents knew what was inside, and weren’t too happy about it. It was a comic book. I frowned. “Mum, it’s a comic book – I –” “Look at it,” she said quietly. I did. My blood ran cold. My palms began to sweat. SUPERPIONEERS was the title. I flicked slowly through the eight-page volume. Someone had spent hours lovingly recreating cartoon images of me and my former tag team partner Tony Aliso – in a park using martial arts skills I never knew I had on faceless grey alien types; in a gym exchanging blows with two legends of the Far East; and in a wrestling ring... against William Curr and Adam Dick. My hand shaking, I looked through that last story more slowly. My breath began to rattle in my throat. It was a perfect recreation of the match that we’d had with Dick and Curr. PRIDE 19. February 9th 2005. Except the last frame... that hadn’t happened... It was of one of my opponents, crouching down by my head, his teeth clenched over my cheek. Not again... I dropped the comic book to the floor. Its envelope followed a second later, along with the money. Without even a glance at my parents, I ran to the door, grabbing my case (not yet registering how stupid it had been to leave it in the corridor in this kind of place) and rushed down the steps as fast as my legs could carry me. Three hours later, the plane left the runway, and I was on it.
