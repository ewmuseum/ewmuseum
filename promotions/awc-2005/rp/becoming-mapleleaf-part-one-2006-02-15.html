Becoming Mapleleaf: Part One

15 February 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2/15/06 Years and years ago, right after the fall of the Berlin Wall, before the invention of the DVD and microcomputers…there was a young boy. This boy is about seven years old, has a tuft of blonde hair, and is of moderate size. He is not tall for his age, but has the frame that has a potential to fill out nicely. His bright blue eyes shine through his small, pinkish face. The boy is dressed in blue jeans and a striped red and white polo shirt, with tiny Keds on his feet. He paces around the living room of his small apartment, outside of the Toronto city limits. He doesn’t know it, but years from now, he will develop a disdain for this apartment. But those days are far in the future. As of right now, he wants to just be a child. It is hard to do with the sounds of arguing through the walls of his bedroom, parties involving heavy drinking and cocaine upstairs, and the never-ending hodge-podge of noises from outside his window. Yes, long before this company was even founded, before wrestling made it to exciting new heights, a modest boy lived in Canada. He had above average intelligence. His mother was a simple housewife, his father a floundering science fiction author who enjoyed the drink. The young boy walked through the living room, his father’s back to him as he sat, hunched over the typewriter. The boy dare not disturb his father, as he saw the half empty glass of whisky next to him. “Son? That you?” the man asks. “D-dad,” the boy stutters. “I was just going to watch some television.” “G-goddam television. Why don’t you-you-do something useful, you useless—thing? Do ya homework!” “I already did it, daddy. Mrs. Matherby says that I’ve scored the highest on the last two math tests!” the boy proclaimed, proudly. “Oh, ya? Is math gonna help ya pay my bills?” “No, but—“ “Ha!” the man stumbled out, taking a long swig of whiskey. “Ya gotta learn about life, son. All that matters is money, and you ain’t gonna be makin’ it by doin’ math problems. Now get me another glass of juice!” “Juice?” “Ye, daddy’s medicine! Come on, pour up,” the man growled. The young boy walked over to the bottles. At this point, he could hardly read, but he knew enough to know what the bottle was. It had a white label with a Canadian flag, the same kind that he himself would drink years later. But this isn’t about later. This is about then. He pours his father a small glass. He brings it to the desk. His father growls, takes the glass, and throws it against the wall. Then he backhands his son in the face. “Goddamnit, son! W-what did I always tell you?!” “Fill it to the rim.” “That’s right, and that ain’t no rim! That’s a whole half inch from the rim. Now do it again, and do it right!” “Yes, sir,” the boy whimpered. The boy walked across the rug and into the liquor cabinet. He saw the bottle looming in front of him. He was unsure of what exactly this liquid was, but he knew one thing; he didn’t like it. It made his daddy act in ways he would not normally act. It made his daddy into a monster. His eyes would turn red, his hands would shake, and his words would be stumbled. Yes, this young boy, at the tender age of 7, knew even then the horrible effects of this liquid. He unscrewed the cap and stuck his nose into the bottle. He was instantly repulsed by the liquid. Nevertheless, he began to pore into the small glass. He then had an idea. He walked over to the sink, after filling the glass halfway. He turned on the water and watered down the rest of the drink. He then, after filling it to the rim, brought it over to his father. His father took one sip and nearly gagged. The boy turned red. He knew he was in for it. “What the—shit! Shit, shit, shit! YOU!” The boy walked back a couple of steps. His father got up. From his father standing, the boy realized how tall daddy was for the first time. He was a solidly build man, standing at about 6’4”. His approximate weight was about 250 pounds. He had short, stubbly blonde hair on the sides of his head. The top was giving away to balding. This man had the boy’s cold, blue eyes, peering down at his son. Of course, the man’s eyes were now corrupted, red and swollen from years of abuse. His face twisted into an evil smile, the thin layer of stubble covering and shadowing his face. He grabbed his son by the collar and slammed him into the apartment wall. “YOU! How DARE you!” the man bellowed. “After all I do…ALL I DO…you do this to me?!” “I’m sorry, daddy,” the young boy choked out. “I just…you get so…mean.” “Mean, huh? You think your daddy is mean? Well, you ain’t seen nothing, yet!” The elder man backhands his son across the face…hard. His son’s nose starts to bleed. He then grabs his son under one arm, using his mighty strength to lift him like a bag of feathers. He opens the window at the face side of the apartment. The cold air seeps in. The apartment is located on approximately the seventh floor. Trees, modest gardens, and streets are seen from below. The man grabs his son and dangles him out the window. He begins to scream and holler. “I COULD DROP YOU RIGHT NOW!” the man screams. “If I wanted to, I could splatter your brains on the sidewalk like shit! But I’m not gonna do that.” The man lets go of his son for a split second, then grabs him again. The boy cries in a high pitched tone, tears and blood now mixing and pouring down his face. “I told you, you little shit, not to fuck with me! If you do it again, you’re going down to the sidewalk. You understand?!” “Y-yes.” “What?!” “Yes, sir!” “Good.” The man grabs his son and pulls him back in through the window. The little boy scurries off to his bedroom, closes the door, and locks it. He takes a sock that is on the floor and wipes his nose and face with it. He is too afraid to go out to the bathroom because his father could still be on the warpath. He lies down on his bed, looking through his small window that is opposite the room. He wishes. He wishes that he and his family could have a better life. He wishes that his daddy could be happy so that he did not have to drink that awful liquid. He wishes that his mother was still able to function instead of being bogged down on all those pills that he doctors gave her. She wasn’t coughing or feverish, so why did she have to take all those pills all the time. He wishes that he could just get away, be on top of the world…for just one moment…. Slowly, the boy drifts off to sleep. “Son?” The room is dark now. The digital clock next to him reads “7:30”. The boy gets up and walks to the door, opening it, half asleep. His father is standing there, a brown bag in his hand. It is known as the end of the drill. The drill has several cycles. The first is the initial threat. This usually entails his father making threats, getting angry, and using bad words. The second cycle involves the actual abuse, the hitting, threatening, and screaming. The third cycle involves his father, always carrying a brown bag, with a fake smile on his face. “Son, I just wanted to let you know…daddy’s been under a lot of pressure, and I’m sorry. Here, son, I got you a present.” The boy opens the bag. It is a brand new Gameboy, just like he wanted. “Thanks, daddy!” he said, giving his daddy a hug. There is no trace of the smell of the bad drink on him. “You’re most welcome,” his father said back. “Now, go wash up. I’m gonna make us some dinner.” “What about mommy?” “Mommy is resting, son,” he says. Mommy was always resting. Happy ending to the story, right? Not if you’re this young boy, because he knew deep down it would happen again, like a hundred times before. No one is safe from it, the boy realizes, because the drink takes power over your life. It takes precedence over your loved ones and your own wellbeing. Of course, a young boy like this has no idea about any of those things. In his simple mind, all he can comprehend is that “the drink is bad.” He knows nothing about the disease of alcoholism, or the other demons that take hold of his father. He knows nothing about his mother’s comatose condition, absorbed in pills starting with almost every letter in the alphabet, because of her inability to cope with her now-disintegrating life. All this little boy knows is that he is stuck. He is stuck in a place that offers him little to no support for emotional development (which explains the lack thereof in his later life.) A story of heart-ache, betrayal, mistrust. It all plays together like pieces of a puzzle, except somehow, the pieces of this puzzle never fit. They are misshapen, forming a pattern that is corrupt and is not congruent with the identical image on the box. This faulty image embeds itself into the mind of this young boy. These images haunt him for years. The story of Jean Pierre and Philippe LaShute does not end here. This is just a beginning, an appropriate stepping stone between truth and ignorance for those that truly do not know the boy who would become Patrick Mapleleaf. This was a boy of radiance, splendor, and class, corrupted by a man who had no hold on his own life, and a woman who was so drugged that she did not have time to care about her son’s wellbeing. In our next installment, we will see a pre-teen Jean Pierre LaShute, coping with choices that would eventually become part of his descend to hell.
