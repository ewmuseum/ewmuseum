The Need to Know (Conclusion)

21 June 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 21/6/06 Everything’s big in Texas. I had heard it before, but I had thought it was just an expression. It wasn’t. We’d pass fields that seemed to span on forever. Cows were nearly thirty feet tall. Their wrecking ball sized droppings alone splashed into the road as we drove through literal shit storms, trying the best we could to avoid the scatological blotches that splattered around us. It was, in a way, like twentieth century warfare. The Kia, too, seemed remarkably small compared to the wide lanes. It felt like we were driving a fucking Micro Machine. Trucks would drive right over us, and we’d have to stay in what we perceived to be the middle of the gigantic lane or risk being steamrolled by the massive truck tires. And everyone drove trucks; state law, or something. And they all wore those fucking ten gallon hats too, and the hats were actually large enough to fill with ten gallons of liquid. Texans would actually take off their hats when thirsty, fill them with whatever poison they picked, and guzzle it down. It was the Texas way. “Jesus Christ,” I said while ashing a cigarette out the window, “it’s going to take forever to make it through Texas in this vehicle.” “You got any other ideas?” I sat and thought for a while, puffing on my death stick diligently. The Kia was no longer efficient. A small car wouldn’t do here. What the fuck do the South Korean automotive engineers know about driving in Texas anyway? They don’t have do deal with Texas over there on the Pacific Rim. They’re all too busy running away from Godzilla anyway. Hell, Godzilla wouldn’t make it here in America. The only reason he had his way with the Japanese was because of the uniformly miniscule stature of the country’s population. In America, aside from being green and scaly and all, Godzilla would be normal sized. He’d probably have to get a desk job, drive a sedan to his suit and tie job, and handle a bunch of documents. He’d be like an accountant or something. I’d have him file my taxes. “We need to ditch the Kia,” I bluntly stated. “No way, dude,” Hasone responded quickly, “I’m still paying off the lease.” “We’ll stash it somewhere and pick it up later. We need something bigger that can cover more ground. Pull over at the next exit with a gas station.” And about fifteen minutes later, we came to the first town we saw over the Texas border. It was a town of about five thousand called New Boston, though it bore little resemblance to the original Boston, but we knew it would have a place where we could stop. We picked a place that had a diesel fueling station for semi-trucks and a shitty diner for the semi-truck drivers. We parked the car around back, not wanting to risk the discovery of a temporarily abandoned car clogging up one of the parking spaces. It could be reported and towed or stolen by virtue of two average sized Texans carrying it away, one on each end, as if they were moving a couch. We sifted through the dumpster out back and hid the car underneath an empty bag of beef jerky littered in front of said dumpster. This was all possible because we had come to know that time and space were relative things, and things like speed and size could easily be manipulated and exaggerated. Or perhaps that was a perceived cop out for the lack of continuity in my thought processes. Regardless, we walked into the truck-stop diner, a virtual bazaar of mesh-hats worn on the heads of America’s roadsmen. They came to gather at places like these, some attracting magnetically after miles and miles of isolation and others pledging their loyalty to their path of solitude. The ones that did gather compared destinations and cargo; each trying to prove his journey was a trip across the toughest patches of road on the continent or designating their cargo as the more substantial. They boasted to each other about their most memorable deliveries, and mutually agreed on the likeability or displeasure of certain towns in a verbal game to see who has passed through more places. Hasone and I grabbed a corner booth and sat discussing plans quietly when a waitress came to serve us. She couldn’t have been more than a junior or senior in high school, and hadn’t been in the workforce quite long enough to have lost the hospitable attitude of such workers in small town after school jobs. She would have been a pretty little thing too, if it wasn’t for the fact that she stood just short of eight feet tall. “Howdy! Can I get something to drink for y’all?” she asked with a shining white-toothed smile. Her eyes beamed at the cuteness of our smallness in comparison to her statespersons. “Can I get a coffee?” Jason ordered with a rise in tone towards the end. The cream and sugar were already on the table in proportionally oversized containers. “And for you, sir?” “I’ll just have a cola,” I said, with a flutter of cool in my voice while scraping the Frisbee-sized ashtray towards me. “Alright, then! I’ll be back with your drinks to take your orders soon,” she informed us as she bent down to our levels while smiling at our diminutiveness and tapping on the table with both hands, trying to convey a gesture of comfortable welcome. As she left, Hasone and I continued our plotting. “Are you suggesting that we steal one of the semis? That’s insane!” Hasone exclaimed in an excited whisper that would have been a dull murmur at any slower a pace. “I don’t see what the problem is,” I stated flatly, leaning back and folding my arms across my chest. Hasone leaned in, anchoring his weight by planting his palms on the table, “If we steal one semi, all the other semis will chase us down! It’s some kind of trucker camaraderie. Just look around, for Christ’s sake! And if they catch it, we have a dozen of methed up truckers to contend with. They’re all methed up, too. It keeps them awake.” “It’ll be fine,” I coolly assured him. I took a puff of my cigarette and continued, “We’ll just outrun the bastards.” “Outrun them? Neither one of us knows how to drive a semi!” “How hard could it be?” I shrugged him off. “Look, I’ve got a plan. Its fool proof, but we’ll have to get our hands a little dirty. Are you in any way averse to that?” “It depends,” he stated, slowly calming down. “What’s the plan?” Now Hasone and I switched positions. He leaned back and folded his arms, and I leaned forward perching my elbows on the table and locking concentrated eyes. “Alright, after we get out of here, we wait outside until we see a trucker refueling. I’ll ask for directions to Austin to distract him. The son of a bitch is going to be eager to brandish his expertise at memorizing routes. When he’s explaining directions to me, you slip into his truck, preferably from the passenger’s side before he’s done telling me which turns I should take, and you have to be quick because he’ll know the shortest way to get there. I’ll try to buy you as much time as I can, but I don’t know how much I’ll be able to help you out. When he’s done, I’ll walk around the other side of the semi and get in on the passenger’s side, which you should unlock if it isn’t unlocked already. I’ll have a lit cigarette on hand, at which point you’ll start up the truck and floor it, ripping the gas pump out while I toss my lit cigarette out the window. There’ll be gasoline spilling everywhere, the cigarette will light the place up, and everyone here will be taken care of by the explosion.” I sat back again and refolded my arms, and Hasone sat still, not even blinking an eye; pausing to process the idea in his head. And then, crisply breaking the pause, he replied. “That’s probably the worst idea I’ve ever heard.” He readjusted his posture and cleared his throat. “You’re suggesting that we blow up a collection of truck drivers? Here? In Texas? That’d be an automatic death sentence. No lawyer. No trial. They’d send us straight to the end of the line for the electric chair!” Hasone was nearly hyperventilating exasperatedly. I waited for him to catch his breath. “Relax, man. We won’t get caught. Gas stations blow up accidentally all the time. There are statistics to prove it. There won’t even be an investigation.” And then, the waitress came back to take our orders, wielding a bucket of cola and a kettle drum full of coffee. *** The sun beat down through the gigantic windshield of the semi-truck we were driving. All it took was some more convincing along with a joint in a bathroom stall to get Hasone to go along with my plan. It went off without a hitch. Unfortunately, the semi was more difficult to maneuver than I had first imagined. And because we were in Texas, the fucking truck was gargantuan. From a sitting position, Hasone could either reach the pedals or see over the steering wheel, but not both. So I sat on Hasone’s shoulders and steered the beast while he operated the gas and brakes as consistently as he could based on my instructions. We made a stop in Dallas to know, and since we needed to both be awake to drive and were making such good time anyway, we both knew again. The highs were getting shorter and weaker to the point that we still craved another electric high as soon as we woke up. But we couldn’t just stop in a bathroom a thousand miles from home and shock ourselves forever. Instead, we begrudgingly decided to hit the road again. The spasms of withdrawal made driving a chore, so we pulled over on the shoulder of the highway towards Austin and got out of the semi to clear our heads. At the time, we were so focused on our own electricity cravings to remember that everything in the state was abnormally gigantic. Driving the truck had made everything seem so proportional that when we lit our cigarettes and lay back in the grass, we were surprised to actually sink down, submerged completely in the freakish, jungle blades. I looked up and saw the rising smoke from my cigarette mix and mingle with the rising smoke with Hasone’s. I saw the blades of grass separate out of my peripheral vision and immediately turned my head to the rustling foliage. Two cockroaches, each standing on its hindest of legs and measuring near a foot and a half in stature a piece, stopped right in front of me, thoroughly examining me while they discussed their findings in heavy southern drawls. As I could hear them clear as day, and it prompted me to question whether or not I was having an acid flashback. “You reckon he’s dead?” asked one to the other. “Go on and give him a poke,” replied the other. So, the one poked me in the face with one of his long, thin limbs. The protruding hairs off his stemming arm made my face itch. I began to wonder whether or not I was still knowing. Maybe the electric trips weren’t getting shorter at all, but were instead growing much, much longer. When I was knowing, it seemed so realistic that I did not know if I was knowing or not unless I considered that when I wasn’t knowing, I was sure that I wasn’t, and I wasn’t sure now. “He’s still a might warm,” said the other to the first. “Think we should carry him back to headquarters?” “I reckon not right now, but we should mark his location and gather the others to help us drag his carcass back.” The first one then nodded in agreement, and both turned to face out from me. They hunched the bends between their thoraxes and abdomens to squeeze out chemical dumps right in front of my face. And then they scurried away, back through the grass to alert their colony. I breathed out a sigh of relief and breathed in a whiff of their chemically distinct waste markers. Soon I’d be surrounded by the insect Lilliputians. I knew that I’d be severely outnumbered, so I looked up, prepared to rise when I saw Hasone pacing back and forth and in circles around the field. He called my name, looking for me as if I was a tennis ball that had rolled into a corn field. I angled into a sitting position in the grass, and Hasone turned. “You ready to get back on the road?” he asked, the impatience in his tone more noticeable than normal. “Give me a few minutes. My head still isn’t straight.” I took out another cigarette and lit it. I held the box out towards Hasone, but he threw forward a palm of refusal. “I wonder what we’re hauling,” Hasone said, almost to himself, while looking past the back end of the trailer. “I’m going to take a look.” Hasone unlatched the door to the trailer as I took my time getting up to follow. He was quick at work, back to his sometimes overanxious self, and I felt half-asleep, though I wasn’t quite sure which half it was. By the time I got around, the trailer doors were already flung wide-open, and Hasone was inside, inspecting the print on the box. “Holy shit, dude. Get in here!” I pulled myself up into the open trailer and knelt down next to the box Hasone was reading. I looked around. They all carried U.S. Army stamps. We had a truckload of military grade weapons at our disposal and no fucking clue what to do with them. We didn’t have the tools in the truck’s cab to open the boxes. We could probably stop somewhere and buy one. All I knew was that it was going to be a fuck of a time getting across the border if we had to explain what the hell we were doing trying to go to Mexico with a truck full of military artillery, United States military artillery. *** We had rolled into San Antonio, and we pulled into the parking lot of the first place we came to just off the internet that looked like a suitable place to stop and know. It was a bar just outside of city limits that looked like it had come straight out of a cowboy movie. The double door entrance was propped open, revealing two smaller push open partial doors that were there for no more practical of a purpose than décor. We stepped out of the truck in synchronization. I was still wearing an American flag jacket to fit in despite the heat, and Hasone and I each had an assault rifle strapped around our shoulders in some kind of fitted casing. We pushed our way through the doors, and I looked around. The bar was crowded with Texans, graying men in boots and cowboy hats, drinking, shooting pool, throwing darts, remembering the Alamo, or gathered around a table in a poker game. They just called it Hold ‘Em, here, no need for the prefix. Everyone in the bar had a carefully maintained handlebar mustache, even the women. And there were antlers everywhere. They shot anything with antlers and hung the heads up on the walls. There was even a human head with antlers there…some poor deformed sap who was born with some unfortunate genetic mutation. I recalled having read somewhere that Brownsville had the highest rate of deformed births in the nation; something about the lack of air pollution standards across the border. Brownsville was probably full of these antlered mother fuckers, running around batting heads for alpha male supremacy. I felt their peripheral eyes upon me as Hasone and I shuffled our feet towards the back of the bar, looking for a bathroom, but finding none, we shuffled back to the middle of the bar and looked around. Our confusion was sending off visible signals that even we could see. We mumbled quietly to each other. “What the fuck, man? There’s no fucking bathroom in here,” I stated, only verbalizing what was obvious to the both of us. “Let’s just leave.” “No, that’s too suspicious. Let’s just unplug the jukebox,” I replied, pointing to the clunky machine in the corner that was tiredly scratching out old country songs. I was itching for some electricity and would plug into someone’s pacemaker if I had to. This was Texas, after all, and all they eat in Texas is five alarm chili and 72 ounce steak. Any other steak would be unacceptable, and chili with four alarms or less was for faggots. In Texas, chili needs exactly five alarms. It’s the law. And if it was possible to make a chili that had six alarms, the state government would amend their laws. But it wasn’t. And then, as I was shifting my weight back and forth from my left foot to my right, the bartender stepped out from behind the bar with urgent concern. “Can I help you strangers?” he asked, fumbling around with a rag. “We were just, uh, looking for a bathroom,” I answered nervously. The bartender fired back quickly, apparently trying to get rid of us. “Well, we ain’t got one here. If you can’t contain your booze, go out back and get on out of here, you hear? We don’t want your kind round here, with your bladders and your fancy terlets and whatnot, if y’ain’t gonna buy nothing.” Hasone and I turned to each other and shrugged, each waiting for the other to say or do something. Neither of us did, and I caught the bartender making eye contact with a man chalking a pool cue in the corner. The bartender noted our unfamiliarity with the surroundings, and a shrewd twinkle flashed in his eyes. “Say, uh, them pool cues you got in them bags?” I hadn’t quite caught onto his drift yet, so I meekly responded, figuring it was a believable cover for the automatic weapons we were carrying. “Yeah.” He closed one eye, twitched his mouth a bit, and pointed to the man at the pool table he had traded glances with a second ago. “That man right there,” he said, “that man is Bucky Hammerhead. He’s the best goddamned nine ball player this side of Texas. How about you see how you stack up, unless you’re not up for the challenge.” Not looking for a game of pool right now, I turned him down. “No thanks, I think we’re just going to head out.” As I made a step towards the door, the bartender grabbed me by the shoulder in restraint. “Look, I’ll make it nice and sweet for you. If you put up a grand on yourself, Bucky over there’ll put down ten grand, and I’ll match it.” I looked over towards Bucky, and he slapped a fat wad of bills down on the table’s ledge. His fingers were wrapped with bulky gold rings. He smiled a wry kind of grin, black-toothed and fork-tongued. The balls were racked and ready to go. I turned back to the bartender and shook my head, declining gracefully. And then, then the son of a bitch said the one thing that pushed me over the edge. “What’s the matter? You some kind of faggot?” And as the word faggot echoed in my brain, an iota of his spit flew off, duck-like, with the shape of the ‘T’ between his tongue and his lips. It landed squarely on my left cheek. I calmly wiped it off and looked at my palm before grabbing the bag from around my shoulder and carrying it in my hands towards the pool table. I was sick of this fucking state. Everything was too goddamned gigantic, and the bars had no fucking bathrooms. I was irritable from having not known since Dallas, and the last thing I needed was for this asshole to call me a faggot in front of a bunch of fucking yokels whose eyes were pinned on me in amusement. At the table, Bucky chuckled menacingly and towered over me by a few feet. I could taste the beer in his breath. “Your break, amigo.” He laughed some more as he tossed the chalk at me. I batted it away with my hand and then proceeded to open the bag. Hasone yelled out, “Jesus Christ, Johnny! Have you lost your fucking mind?” But it was too late. I slid the assault rifle out of the bag as everyone ducked for cover. I aimed the gun at the fifteen ball triangle across the table from me and clenched the trigger. As the balls exploded, I recoiled backwards a bit from the power of the gun and my inexperience firing. Everyone slowly peeked out from behind their chairs and tables after the sound of gunshots returned to an awkward semi-silence, only filled by the sounds of some old country song on the jukebox that everyone knew was playing, but nobody could hear. “Looks like I scratched on the break,” I mumbled, switching the gun over to my right hand while grabbing the wad of bills that somehow still sat on the table’s ledge on my hasty way out of the door. Hasone moved quickly past the craned necks and paralyzed stares of the drunken Texans, following me back into the truck. I felt it was more than likely a favorable idea to get the fuck out of here as fast as we possibly could. *** Cotulla, Texas was a town of three and a half thousand, mostly Hispanic, that was about an hour away from the United States-Mexican border. The rising Hispanic population was a surefire indicator that we were almost there. The problem was that we didn’t know how we were going to go about crossing that border without passports in a semi-truck full of U.S. Military weaponry, that is, until we met Samuel Houston McGriff. Sam was quite the fireplug, an interesting character that looked like a mix between Colonel Sanders and Yosemite Sam. We had passed by his ranch about ten minutes after we had passed through the dead, dusty streets of Cotulla, and I told Hasone to immediately brake upon seeing him in action. “PULL!” shouted McGriff, loud enough to be audible through our closed window as we passed by. A Mexican boy, who couldn’t have been more than seven years old operated a machine that sent a clay disc flying into the air. McGriff fired a shotgun at the disc, and it shattered to pieces, raining down over an open field on his ranch. With the semi-truck parked on the side of the highway, I shut off the engine and got out of the car slowly. Hasone followed behind me, as we took a few steps towards the man shooting skeet, not missing a mark. “Allí!” the kid shouted out, pointing directly at us. Sam turned towards us, leveling the gun near his face, peering over the barrel at us with one open eye as we cautiously approached each other. As he caught a glimpse of the approaching red, white, and blue jacket in his sights, he slowly lowered the gun. We stood on opposite sides of a wooden fence that Sam probably built himself. “Reckon I haven’t seen y’all round here before. The name’s Samuel Houston McGriff, and this is my boy Miguel,” he said, extending a hand over the fence. His handshake was firm to the point that I felt like I had just slammed it into a car door when he released his death grip. “What can I do you for?” I wasn’t sure how to respond to this. On the one hand, this guy looked like the kind of guy who could help us out, but how do you go about telling some stranger who you were just watching shoot clay discs with a shotgun that you have a truck full of heavy artillery that you need to unload before you illegally crossed the border the opposite way? On the other hand, this guy looked like the kind of man who could smell bullshit, even right through the thick handlebar mustache of his. Luckily, a Hispanic lady opened the door and rattled off some Spanish too quick for me to catch. Sam shouted back. “Quit your yapping, woman. I can hear you!” He turned back to us and spoke in a normal tone so the woman couldn’t hear him. “I can hear her, but I can’t understand a goddamned word of it half the time.” Luckily, the interruption had set Sam off a bit. “Excuse me, strangers, but I forgot what it was we were talking about. That lovely lady’s my wife, Mariela. She jumped the border some years back, and if I hadn’t of married her, she’d have probably been sent back by the INS by now. Hell, you two look like nice enough fellows. Ain’t from around here, are you? Well, why don’t we head on in, and I’ll have Mariela fetch us something to wet our gullets with.” Without getting a word in edgewise, we followed Sam into his house. We took seats in handcrafted rocking chairs that surrounded a bearskin rug on the carpet. Sam didn’t seem to get too many English speaking visitors, and he sounded like he needed some to tell his stories to in order to keep himself sane. He pointed at the bearskin rug with his shotgun, which he still held on to as if it were some sort of pointing apparatus. “I killed that one there with nothing but a chokehold and a huntin’ knife. Snuck up right behind the bastard and snapped his neck, I did.” Hasone and I nodded in unison. “And you like this jacket?” he asked rhetorically, holding his suit-jacket open to show us the material. “It’s one hundred percent genuine mountain lion. The thing was running after me, trying to maul the living Jesus outta me when I blew the fucker to smithereens with a stick of dynamite.” Mariela came in with three bottles of Budweiser on a tray. She handed one to each of us, and returned to the kitchen. “So where you cowboys headed?” I spoke up for the first time since I had met the guy, “Mexico.” “You hauling something down there?” “Picking something up,” I responded, trying to select my few words carefully while still trying to confirm my read on Sam. Sam took another swig of the beer before remembering a display framed on the wall and changing the topic seemingly at random will. “See all them medals over there? I used to be a Marine until the damn government decided to discharge me on account of that I’m blind as a bat.” And to think, the son of a bitch was out there, precisely firing a shotgun with unrivaled accuracy. Still, I knew there was more to it, and he knew that I knew that there was more to it. It was a mutual feeling, too, which is why neither of us pried too deeply. I excused my self, “Is there a bathroom here I can use?” Hasone shot me a questioning glance, a bit of fury in his eyes as he wanted to know as bad as I did but couldn’t beat me to the punch. “Walk down the hallway, and it should be the second door on your right.” *** I came to a few hours later, bound to a chair in the man’s basement. Hasone was tied up in a chair right next to me, and the lack of room to maneuver our ways out of the knots let us know that Sam Houston McGriff had experience with a rope. Miguel sat with his legs crossed on the floor in front of us and watched us through large child eyes. Sam Houston McGriff paced around wearing surgical gloves and holding a used syringe. He saw my eyes open. “Ah, rise and shine!” he joked, with a smile on his face. “Now don’t get excited, I ain’t gonna hurt you, I’m just going to ask you a couple of questions. Is that alright?” “What the hell is going on? What are you doing to me?” “Oh, it’s already done,” he assured me, disposing of the syringes in a plastic biohazard container. “All I did was inject you with a little truth serum. I had a feeling you were hiding something from me, and that’s not a proper way to behave towards someone kind enough to invite you into his home. I got a bit worried when you didn’t come back from the bathroom, so I sent your friend over there to check up on you. When he didn’t come back, I knew something was wrong, and lo and behold, I see the both of yous passed out right next to the commode. If you don’t mind my asking, what the hell were you doing in there?” I did, in fact, mind, but there wasn’t a damn thing I could do to stop myself from telling the truth. “I’m addicted to electricity. I needed a fix.” “You’re going to have to explain that one to me.” “By stimulating the neurons in the brain with quick bursts of electricity, the mind works at full output. It achieves something like omniscience, an ability to travel directly through thought, instantaneously, anywhere in the realm of human existence,” I summarized. Sam was a cunning man, but this was something he could not understand unless he had known himself. He stood there, rubbing his chin indecisively before letting it go and moving on. “What are you hauling to Mexico in the back of that semi?” “The semi-truck is full of boxes of United States military issued weapons. The weapons were unintentional. We stole the truck to make better time, and the truck full of weapons complicated things. As it is, we don’t have passports. I’m not sure how we’ll make it across now.” “Is that why you stopped here?” “I’m not sure why we stopped here. I thought you looked like you might be able to help us out. I thought maybe you’d at least know what we could do with the weapons. I figured that you might even be interested in buying some off of us. We really just want to get the weapons off of our hands.” “Well isn’t that an odd coincidence,” Sam said, rubbing his hands together like a dining fly, “because I thought the same thing when I saw you guys. I thought that you two looked like you might be able to help me out.” I waited for him to go on. “You see, now I know you’re going to help me out, cause if you don’t, I ain’t gonna be too happy. And when I ain’t too happy, I start shootin’ things. Do you understand me?” “Loud and clear, sir,” I managed to squeak out. “I knew you were a lot smarter than you looked. Now here’s your mission, soldier. I need you to assassinate a drug-dealing pimp for me.” “Yes, sir,” I agreed, in no position to show any sign of hesitation. “You see, Mariela got in a bit of a financial debacle a few years back. As a single mother in Mexico without any formal trade, Mariela took a desperate offer from a man who at the time had lots of money from mysterious sources. It turns out, he was running cocaine across the border. His goons would sneak across the border and shit out balloons full of blow. One of his shipments was seized by the government, though, you see, and he still had bills to pay. He somehow managed to slip under the radar, but he had to lay low, stay away from the drug trade for a while even though he still had bills to pay himself. His generosity towards Mariela started to run out. He threatened to send her and Miguel back out on the streets unless she worked the streets for him. So she did, and that’s where I met her. I fucked her twice that night, thought that was going to be the whole of it, but when we were done, she started crying, and I can’t stand to see a woman cry. It makes my heart melt right out of my chest. So with the little Spanish I had picked up and the little English she knew, I managed to piece together her story, and I took her and Miguel back over the border with me and married her in a little shotgun service at the Methodist Church in Cotulla.” Sam stopped to take his first pause in a while. He tapped his knuckle on his chin a few times in thought. “Then, a few days ago, I caught a man trespassing on my property after I had gone to bed. He was one of the pimp’s goons, sent to kidnap Miguel and hold him for ransom. But I shot him in the face while I was still in my goddamned pajamas. Can you believe that? Still in my goddamned pajamas.” I nodded my head in accordance, and he chuckled some more. “Now let’s see if that truth serum is still working, shall we?” “I’d prefer not to.” “Nice try, Bartleby. Now answer this. Do you think you’d be turned on if a lady tickled your asshole with a feather?” I tried to resist. I really did. “You bet I would!” Sam grinned wide, absolved of any shame and now assured of our fraternity. “Me too, partner. Me too.” *** Hasone finally came to while he and I were stashed in the back of the semi, with all the weaponry. The truck barreled down the highway, shaking and bumping, as Samuel, who couldn’t see worth a wink, navigated the damn thing with Miguel’s guidance as Miguel shouted out derechas and izquierdas. I couldn’t see Hasone, but I heard him rustling around, blinded by the pitch black lightlessness of the trailer’s interior as he felt around for any sign of his present state. He fell back to the floor with a thud, not able to maintain equilibrium when standing in a trailer traveling at about seventy-five miles an hour. “Hasone, is that you?” I asked. He seemed relieved to hear my voice. “Where the fuck are we?” “We’re in the back of the semi-truck,” I responded. “Sam has a passport. He’s driving us over the border.” “But Sam’s nearly blind,” Hasone countered. “He is?” “Why the hell are you letting a blind man drive our semi?” I thought about Hasone’s question for a while, trying to think of the best way to word my response. “It’ll be fine,” I said. It was the only thing I could come up with at the time, despite the fact that I too wondered if the plan was as infallible as it had first seemed. “Oh yeah,” I added, “I agreed that we’d kill a pimp for Sam if he sold off the weapons and got us the electrolaser. He keeps half the profits on the weapons.” “You agreed to what?” “Yeah, I know. I thought splitting the profits evenly was a bit unfair. I’ll try to talk him down to thirty percent.” “No, I meant the part about killing a pimp.” “I had no choice,” I defended myself. “He was going to start shootin’ things.” Hasone thought for a while before responding. The prospect of his face exploding via shotgun blast was enough of a motivating force to coax him out of his uncertainties. “We’re not assassins, dude. How the hell are we going to go about assassinating a pimp without getting caught? I don’t want to go to jail, man, but I especially don’t want to go to Mexican jail.” “We won’t go to jail. Killing pimps is legal in Mexico. People do it all the time. They eat chimichangas and off pimps in between naps. I saw it on Telemundo the other day.” “No, you didn’t. That’s completely preposterous,” Hasone called my bluff, using his half-Mexican intuition. I changed the subject, only semi-purposely as the semi came to a halt. “Quiet, dude, I think we’re at the border.” The minutes dripped by like some plasmatic ooze, dribbling through time like the universe was melting. And in the cosmic hourglass, the mixture slowly swirled through, thick, with a gelatinous consistency and a torpid constipation. We sat, paused, in a warm, dark, rectangular container. Nothing moved. Even when I tried to move, I could offer no proof that I had. For a moment, we were encased in the middle of nothing. But then we started moving again. We had made it across the border. *** Monterrey, Mexico was a city full of maquiladoras. It was a thriving steel town, guarded over by the Cerro de la Silla, a tremendous green hill that cast a godlike, triangular shadow over everything when the earth rotated to the right spot. We had parted ways with Sam McGriff and his son Miguel after a trip that constituted no stops at all. At one point, I was forced to relieve in the back of the semi, and the distinctly pungent stench of urine still hadn’t left my nostrils. Maybe it was just the city, or maybe I had accidentally sat in some that had trailed in streams all over the floor. Sam was on his way to some gun convention to try and unload the weapons, and he had left us here to roam the city on foot, looking for the pimp. We’d trade the dead body for half the money and the electrolaser. Hasone and I rented a room in a shitty motel. It was the middle of the day, so absolutely everyone was taking a nap. It was mandatory by district law, so we had to be careful not to wake anyone up. The room was a shit-hole. The walls were cracked, the carpet was stained, the television got shitty reception, and none of the outlets were grounded. This brought a more satisfying experience, though, when it came to plugging in. Hasone and I decided to electronically subscribe to the unanimous napping of Monterrey. Soon enough, we’d all wake up at once and go back to our obligations and conversations. We’d trade stories of dreams while we returned to our mundane things. This was mine. There I was, a young boy, skin melting into hooks and bulbous valleys, shedding serpentine into a flaccid mass afoot a metal skeleton. And again, there I was later enshrined in white that glossed like the Oort cloud over my golden luster. I shined like a God, head orbited with buzzing electrons, reaching my hand through the rubble remains of a slowly exploding star to press my thumbprint upon the soil. I folded into myself, tapping at hollow walls, nothing outside but space, everything contained somewhere within, as empty as it seemed.. A worn piece of aluminum traveling through different skins that died and had to be shed again, through useless threads of jargon all intertwined to create a web of thought, so tangled you couldn’t see through. Words smashed together and exploded until their sounds became indistinguishable, all colors combining to the darkest concept of black, so black you could reach right through. It was the sound of electricity, pure, without conductor or circuit. It was free to flow out in all directions, expanding like a gas to fill the infinite without dissipating into a thinness. I thought I knew love once, but it was just a girl, and she ended up breaking my heart. Now, the puzzle complete, my soul was fulfilled, connected and spreading with the segmented mesh as the borders blur and fuse back to one continuous shape, not circular and not quite spherical, but beyond either one. And all the different things that could have possibly existed and will ever exist fit together to equal into nothing. The flaming sword swallows itself and all the ground is sucked in with it. I knew now that I must become far more than an electric vessel, constantly maintained by perpetual energy. I must become that energy; that and nothing else. The reciprocal relationship between everything and nothing was the bittersweet end. I needed to know more the more I already knew, and I knew that this would only bind me to everything as it fit together to create nothing; an abstract concept that only exists in the gathering of its infinite pieces. Every action taken was inconsequential. For the brief, fleeting moments when I locked arms with omniscience, I received my commands and carried them out. Gracious servitude was the most direct path to righteousness and absolution. Absolution washes the hands clean, resets everything back to nothing. There would be no cleansing without stains. I must, then, dirty my hands until they are hands no more in order to wipe them clean. I must drain the blood so the electricity can run through freely. God spoke through two poorly wired prongs. He told me that forgiveness is not forgetfulness, but rather the absence of memory and the existence of a simultaneous presence. So you see, everything thus far and hereafter relies on this misconstrued deconstruction. But this far into my addiction to electricity, submitting to the chance that failure would lead me to one of a variety of possible, intolerable outcomes was impossible. I would have to risk everything to become electrically pure. I would have to forego all moral qualms and invest my faith in the inter-neural messages that rose through amplitudes and frequencies through the current. Unfortunately, when Hasone and I came to, we ended up unknowingly killing the wrong Mexican pimp and dragging his inconveniently misidentified corpse back with us. *** “That’s the wrong fucking pimp!” Sam snarled as Hasone and I dropped the large bag and unzipped it to show our victim’s face. “Well, fuck, Mr. McGriff, all you gave us to go by was a description, and every pimp in Monterrey could have fit that fucking description,” I said, panicked. There was no time for argument. We had a dead pimp on our hands and I had a freshly used trigger finger. I don’t know where I hit the motherfucker, but he went down like a sack of bricks. The poor fucker probably wasn’t even alive long enough to know what hit him. Maybe that made him a lucky fucker. Who knows? I wouldn’t trade places. “Shit,” cursed Sam stamping around before abruptly stopping to light up a cigarette and think. “You guys better get rid of that thing. I sold off a decent chunk of the weapons. I’ll give you twenty five percent of what I made. That should be more than enough to bribe the border guards and get you home. I’m keeping the semi, though, and the weapons I didn’t sell.” “Wait,” I fired back without a break between our words, “I thought you said we’d split it evenly?” “I did,” Sam stared into my eyes, “but that was before you went and fucked up your half of the deal.” Sam started walking around to the truck’s cab, and Hasone and I followed. Miguel was asleep in the passenger’s seat, clutching a blanket. Sam gently eased the blanket out of Miguel’s arms without waking the child. He unfolded the blanket to reveal the be all and end all of knowing, the key to our own pure immortal beings. Sam handed over the electrolaser. I took it, holding it as securely as I could while trying to barely touch it at all out of awe. “I’m gonna have to leave you boys to find your own way back now. You got your own ride?” Neither of us responded. I was too busy staring at the electrolaser, and Hasone thought the question was directed towards me. Upon seeing my fascination with the electrolaser, however, Hasone finally chimed in, “We can take the pimp’s car. I’m sure he won’t be needing it anymore.” Sam counted out a portion of bills, conveniently US dollars, and handed a wad of them to Hasone. He thumbed through the bills as Sam would have been nodding farewell to us if it hadn’t seemed so much like he was nodding to himself. He closed the door, started the engine, and took off home. I looked at Hasone. “If Sam gave us a quarter of the profit from those weapons, he’s going to be a very fucking rich man,” Hasone said, still thumbing through the cash, counting and recounting it from the first bill to the last and then back again. *** “So where are we going to dump the body?” I asked, as Hasone navigated the pimp’s white Rolls Royce down the dusty Mexican highways. The car, usually polished to perfection, was now dotted with dirt marks. “I don’t know. This stretch looks desolate enough. We can stop here and wait awhile to see if anyone passes by.” Hasone pulled the car over to the shoulder of the road, and we got out of the car. I pulled two Pall Malls out of my pack of cigarettes with one fluid, one-handed motion and handed one to Hasone. In the other hand, I clutched the electrolaser by the barrel, turning it and examining all angles of the sleek machine. “At least we got what we came for, huh?” I smiled, rubbing the gun. Every angle and curve on the piece was so precisely crafted that its beauty was borderline sexual. It felt cold, but not any colder than anyone I’d ever held. Its metal was smooth and unblemished. I could tell it had been never used. It just had this virginal quality to it. Our cigarettes got shorter as Hasone watched the road. Nobody passed by. “You want to hold her, Hasone?” I asked, finally able to relinquish my hold on the damn thing. I don’t know why I gendered it that way, though. It had the angled definition of a man with a far more potent penetration, deep through skin and bone into the tiny intertwining threads of soul matter, but just touching it made me feel full of life; reproductive. Hasone ignored my fascination with the gun, balancing his own wonder with the immediacy of our task at hand. “Let’s go about thirty yards out and start digging.” Thirty yards seemed like a reasonable number, though we had no means of measurement greater than our own footsteps. We had tossed a couple of shovels in the trunk. After we retrieved them, we walked out a ways. “It doesn’t need to be deep, just enough to get him in the ground,” I reminded Hasone, as he hastily shoveled a hole in the dirt. We were both sweating and panting. The land was barren, burnt by the open sun. It cracked rather than crumbled. My arms wobbled with wear. It was the air dehydrating me. The air was beyond dry. Our heads turned as an engine roared. The lights on the Rolls Royce flashed on and the car drove away, the trunk and back door left wide open, hanging off the car as it sharply turned around, rebalanced on the road and drove off. “FUCK!” Hasone yelled, watching the whole scene unfold. I just stood there speechless. “Fuck,” Hasone repeated. It about summed up the situation. I didn’t know where I hit the motherfucker, but he went down like a sack of bricks. And then the realization had hit me with an equal force. “I never loaded the gun,” I said out loud to myself, never intending for Hasone to hear it. “What?” Hasone asked, his eyes widening so that I could make out the whites of them in the vast darkness. “The fucker, he must have just fainted.” “So, you’re telling me that you shot the motherfucker with fucking blanks?” I tried to respond, but Hasone cut me off again, pacing a hole in the ground deeper than the one we were digging. “You fucking idiot! You FUCKING…” Hasone trailed off, holding his head while crouching. “I…” “You what?” Hasone sprung up. “You what? What the fuck are we supposed to do now, out here in the middle of fucking barren Mexico with nothing in any direction for fucking miles?” “Kilometers, Hasone,” I corrected him. “They use metric over here.” “What the fuck, man? What the fuck are we supposed to do? Tell me, because I’m fucking stumped.” Hasone was frustrated, kicking up dust clouds. I looked down at the electrolaser, dropped my shovel, and picked the laser up to caress it again. “What other option do we have?” I asked. Hasone shrugged, tiring from his own burst of commotion. He walked out about fifteen paces away before turning around to me, holding his arms outstretched and inviting the lightning blast. I steadied my aim and fired at his chest. He went down in a convulsing spiral until he was just a tiny protrusion poking out of the earth’s surface. I turned the electrolaser around, facing the nozzle towards my chest, my arms at full reach. I shot, and the wave jarred me from close range. The gun fell out of my hands in an unremarkable display of descent. I didn’t feel the ground at all as my body nearly crashed through it. There’s no telling how far I would have sunk in if the crust hadn’t been so hardened by the sun. But that was it. That was all I felt. I sat up, bending my spine past each sorer angle to sit up, propping myself at a forty-five degree angle with my weak elbows. Hasone sat in a similar position staring back at me. We tried to search for answers in each others’ distant faces. Had we been sold a faulty product? Had we grown immune? We both stood up slowly, never unlocking eyes, never exchanging a word because our trousers were full of piss and shit. *** We finally came to a maquiladora after four or five miles of walking in the bending direction we thought might be north. We were both having constant spasms and walking very squarely as if the earth was quaking beneath us. The glass windows were easy to bust through, as their cracking wooden supports weren’t very supportive at all against any force applied to them. We trudged around the place looking for a bathroom to try to clean ourselves off. There was a door with a faded label on it with a small bathroom behind it. The bathroom was big enough for the toilet, the sink, and one person. Hasone and I took off our pants and removed our adult diapers, pushing them into a small plastic bin jammed uncomfortably between the toilet and the wall, already flowing with uncompressed trash. “Those were our last ones,” Hasone said gravely. “Are you sure you want to know?” “It’s worth a shot,” I said, propping myself up on the border of the cracked mirror over the sink. But neither of our wires actually made it into the outlet in the deceptively termed far corner of the bathroom, though mine fell inches short before I passed out from exhaustion and fell onto the floor of the most neglectfully maintained bathroom we had encountered. Hasone’s head rested on arms folded over the toilet, creating a ledge where the actual toilet seat once used to be. From what I remember, it was cracked in half, leaning against the wall, underneath the sink. *** The white orb rolled underneath God’s finger back and forth through the neurons where the body and the mind connect. Persons afflicted with the addiction to electricity oftentimes cite a claim that God speaks to them through the electricity. This, is in effect, true. As you may have known already, electricity is God. It’s the tiniest clicking impulse that allows the living their awareness. It is the force that forms circuits and bonds with itself that is infused within us all, fusing us all. When a new, more powerful force of electricity overcomes the balance created by the existing electrical activity, the new electricity pushes the old out. Briefly, the mind is still a bonded creation that’s left shattering, exploring every aspect of reality while unknowingly absent from its own body. The new electricity, however, takes over physical command until it eventually repels and evenly disperses throughout the body, returning the connection between body and mind. I woke up, back inside my body in the bathroom face down on the tile floor. I got up and opened the door, unsure whether I was still in the maquiladora or in my basement. God had spoken to me. He had commanded my body while my mind was away, just as he commanded Noah to build the Ark, or as he commanded Moses to deliver the Ten Commandments. The basement was finished with the stain of my own toil left lingering in the basement. The lines were walls with glass domes, just large enough for a man to kneel underneath. Each dome was serviced with a wire fed through the clear casings. The room remained well lit despite the fact that all the bulbs had shorted out long ago. The domes were full of all sorts, kneeling in unison, feeding on the electric shock. God had commanded my body to build this with my own two hands, and as I sat debating what my mind had experienced and my body had experience, it suddenly wasn’t all that clear to me any more. I heard Hasone wake up behind me. “Hey, Johnny, why is our shower full of money?” I think I already knew, and I was set to confirm my suspicions. “Come out here, Hasone. I think we’ve been running a wire house.” “Shit,” Hasone said, walking around and examining the inhabitants of the tubes. Some were still feeding, and some were unconscious against the glass. For all I knew, some might have been long dead from overdose before we ever noticed them. And the tube dwellers, the knowing, were impossible to classify. There is no identification for the desperate. They come in all forms and types of looks; each linked by their lowest common denominator. “We need to destroy this,” I said. It was my only analysis of the situation, my heart simultaneously saddened and numbed to any feeling at all on the sight of the desperate pulling at my wires, shitting all over themselves. We spilt a trail of gasoline through the basement and tossed a match from the doorway. We watched the house burn from the driveway; and everything in it that we were once dependant on. There’s a slight difference between the definitions of need and addiction. We no longer needed electricity, and we no longer needed to fit everything together to cancel itself back to the center, zero, nothing. And to know nothing was all we needed.
