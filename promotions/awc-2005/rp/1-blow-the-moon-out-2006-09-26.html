1: blow the moon out

26 September 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: September 26, 2006 Let me tell you the story of a girl who once had a Spirit. Ellis, now, isn't quite sure when it happened, but she guesses it was pretty early because she remembers the incident in first grade when the teacher had asked everyone to be a color and she had refused to answer. After class, she dragged her feet while she walked up to the teacher's desk, and when the teacher asked why she wouldn't answer, Ellis had told her quite truthfully that the color she most likely would be was white, but white was nothing, and no one wanted to admit to being nothing. The teacher never bothered her after that. Now, Ellis thinks that that must have been Spirit talking, because she would never do something like that. Ellis would have chosen yellow or blue or green. Ellis was the kind of girl whose friends called her Ellie, who never walked in the rain, whose parents trusted that she slept when she should, who didn't have midnight snacks topped with cream of poetry, who obsessed over cute clarinet players, who had straight hair and green eyes and a cup full of masochistic pride. Ellis was the kind of girl who had never worn and never wished to wear Spirit-colored glasses. Spirit didn't show up for a while after first grade, and Ellis had forgotten all about him until a middle school dance. The cute clarinet player had asked her to dance, and to her and his great shock, she declined and told him his black suit made her uncomfortable and that she could never bear to dance with someone who wore a defective rainbow. The boy never quite looked her in the eye after that. He never wore black again either. Ellis was rather livid with Spirit after that and shut him out. It isn't until now that she realizes she misses Spirit. Spirit is the one who presses his forehead against glass and marvels at the cold. Spirit is the one who experiments with red lipstick, who obsesses with birds on telephone wires, who takes random pictures and paints meaning with a fine-tipped brush, who buys candles but not matches, who follows butterflies to space and back without missing a single falling leaf, who writes until words fly out of his fingers, who blows kisses and uses the wind as the carrier, who smiles like a secret kept in a golden box. Ellis is black and white. Spirit is the one that colors the two parts grey. Ellis decides she doesn't like Spirit. It isn't until now that she realizes she misses him. * a. it's like unfolding a paper crane: the wings come apart delicately to tiptoe around each other in the air, and - such a contrast between that pale expanse and the inky strands that curl up, towards the moon. -- There is a small ache of wind straining at the window: the rattle of a pane, the sound of a cough, of a hand against the cool black heart of the door; then her, the arched crease of a t-shirt, the rolled sleeves, the click of flip-flops against the wooden floor. Her sad face. Good morning. “Good morning,” she says. Have a seat. Your name is? “Ellis,” she says. How are you? “Fine.” She coughs. Bad throat now, Ellis? “I’m fine.” So. “So.” You say you’ve been having these dreams? “Delusions,” Ellis says. Of love. “Heart palpitations.” Of love. “I guess you could say. Of love. Of whatever, really.” Whatever? “I don’t enjoy it much. That word. Love.” Explain. “It brings back past thoughts.” Past thoughts. “Yes.” What thoughts? “Thoughts of being younger. Thoughts when I was younger. Thoughts of caring. Caring and not caring both. Of being wild, of travelling, of seeing places, of New York, of people. Heady things, emotions. Feeling like an invention, or creating itself. Making dark and light. Believing in undulation stipends some goodness in this old heart of mine, you know.” You make yourself unclear, Ellis. “I can’t not.” I can’t help you like this, Ellis. “I don’t expect you to.” But you want to be cured. “Of insomnia. But not of heartache. Never of heartache. That would be a stupid thing to be cured of.” But it can be done. “It’s possible, everything is possible, but curing is not just an achievement. It’s the opposite.” Yet you hesitate in saying so. “I do. But we all pretend not to think so, when nobody actually wants to cure themselves of memories.” * b. the veins on her hands show and the bones below move up and down, but when she blinks there is something golden about the way her lashes do not match. maybe she's just as you imagined, or maybe she's not, but you don't feel quite as important, or self-assured, or -- because without saying a single word, she makes you feel like you're in 3rd grade again, with dirt under your fingernails and markers in your pocket that smell like raspberries and mint. -- 1992 - You're thirteen, several months past, the deep set of your eyes already becoming a woman’s. I still remember the sight of you, the way the wind carried the image of you forward like a smell. Called you the upwind girl, then. Still think of you as that, sometimes. I get that same feeling when I read about the past, like it’s being carried forward to me in the present; a present story of a few dead men. We call that history. But that’s too much of a gloom, isn’t it. Look, you’re frowning now. The next time I come down, I’ll buy you a popsicle, how about. To make you a little happier. Then you can tell me another of your fancy tales, make me a little happier. Though that time you said you were most joyful in sleep, from your claim of ghostly wanderings. I don’t tend to encouch myself in disbelief, but wouldn’t you rather real life? After all, we are both here. The sun was crippled in the morning, and you longed to hold it up with hands, support the melting of time in an artwork, a Dali. You, Ellis, you were concerned with heroics, with aesthetics. It was born into you, into the sharp angles of your face, the bend of your nose your mother loved and you hated, the lines of your too-skinny body, fleshed out in the mirror, a pre-natal superwoman. When you were younger your mother cradled you in her lap and called you her hero, so much so that you were scared she would leave you some day and shipwreck your entire identity to a wide, gaping emptiness. “Oh, honey,” she said, and you, a little younger, noticed only her washed-out blue eyes crashing against the pale beach of her face. She set you down, told you to go out to play, then said to the air, “Jesus,” and she laughed, compellingly. I remembered you yesterday, of how you worked that scab off your knee, your nails going around the corners first and how the blood came out oozing. I told you to stop, and you looked at me with your red mouth smiling, saying how it smelled like the metallic of the water, like the stuff of dreams. You scared me then because you were so young and I thought you might be something else, an Ellie or an El, or one of those schoolgirl names, but you picked that scab off your knee like someone already old, and I called your name, Ellis, like that, and it pleased you. We’ve had one hundred and forty-seven memories together, did you know? Which means twenty-one weeks since I saw you dancing on a beach, which is several months. You know I cheated and had to use a calculator. But that’s all right, for someone like me, or I’d have done a degree in Math instead, you know that. How did you like that you spoke to me that day? How long did we speak for? Not long, probably. I can’t even remember your name now, but perhaps these are the best sort of memories, the vague furrows of past that bear seed. Did you know that every footprint a man makes can be tracked? I think of you walking, and all the stamps you made in the sand with your little feet. God, you could’ve been invincible. * c. she, with waterfalls flowing from her tongue and constellations in her eyes, does not know the world, and would never say that she did. but she knows little girls with dark hair and thin fingernails, know barbershops with painted blue fronts and polished windows, knows red tassels and characters threaded with gold, knows how the sun sets below the trees, knows millions of things that you could not imagine; that you could not notice; that you could not see. -- 1995 – You're sixteen. Your eyes could tell stories but you won’t let them, they’re set so deep you’d have to pluck them from the skull to see. You go to school in pea-green trousers. Damn, did you look stupid, like I must have with my trousers hitched up standing in the middle of the water. You dreamt, then. More than anything you dreamt. Sometimes you dreamt that you were just standing there, in the middle of nowhere, a flower in your hair, even though the color of the flower was different each time, like one of those series of pictures where everything was the same save the one object you needed to identify. For you, it was mostly the flower, though sometimes it wasn’t. Sometimes it was the ground you were standing on, from forest to grass, from grass to desert. It was a progression, things getting drier, and you yourself turned from a tree to a mummy, wrapped up in stone and past. Sometimes you woke in the middle of the night, sweat beading on your upper lip. Your white curtains billowed inward like the belly of a pregnant ghost. You couldn’t see the rest of her, didn’t want to see the rest of her, only wanted to return back to the dream where you could stand and let the air rush over you in waves. And though your feet couldn’t move, you could turn your head to look up at the sky, the same clouds almost gliding in contempt, and try to smell the smell you most missed, feel the salt of far away resting in your lungs, wonder if you could see if you tried to see the sea. At sixteen you found it more difficult, because age took away one’s imagination faster than anything else. You woke to sounds of normalcy, to the sound of running water, or the sound of Jon brushing his teeth in the adjacent bathroom, fat, complacent, normal sounds you couldn't dissect from the memories swimming around in your head. At sixteen you knew that normalcy would be the end of you, because normalcy made everything ordinary, and everything that once was special, even in the slightest, could be forgotten. Sixteen, and you only felt your heartstrings when they were plucked, and that was rarely. Perhaps once when that boy told you he couldn’t like you. But that was just once, for you, Ellis. The only other time was when you visited your grandmother and saw her white, happy face and heard her retreat further and further into herself; how her hair would curl by her forehead, like a shy snail into its shell. Perhaps then there was the plucking of heartstrings. Perhaps then. And even then, the only other time was at night, with the flower in your hair. * 1997 - You’re eighteen. Did you say time flies? I can’t remember if it was you or me. Times flies but so few people fly with it. The past few nights, I thought of being in the middle of a desert, digging up someone else’s past with a little spade, and deserts are a little like beaches don’t you think? There is so much sand, sand, sand everywhere, that you can make castles with your hands. The difference is that water erodes one and wind the other. But in both ways, what you make wears down back to the ground. If you think of it like that, it really is the same. 1997 split into two halves; the half where you stayed awake and the half where you slept, split raggedly between, and both ways you kept thinking of the time when you were thirteen, the only time when you thought you could be a part of yourself. You thought of it in school, with essays, at home, with the television channels. You never told people, but you often put salt in your coffee. At eighteen, you were starting to become better at being someone else. With a pencil you could make the futures vast, the present a jetty point, the past a small house with the wallpapered walls and the ships frozen in time -- though you knew, at the back of your mind, that it was really the reverse. Going towards futures were what people always did; it was the past with the loopholes of memory that were uncertain, that one could paint over in different shades, that one had to make vague and distant. The past was a place more immense than immensity. * 2006 - You’re twenty-seven, and I’m already old. What do you think I am doing now? You would never be able to believe. I have a deckchair now. Sometimes I lie in the sun on my best girlfriend’s balcony with a martini in my hand. On the TV there’s someone screaming about the Middle East, and my best girlfriend comes out, all fat-like and sexy, and she says, “Fuck, you look old,” and I get rather pissed off, because twenty-seven is hardly old, merely a relic. The last time you came and visited me, I was sleeping and you were wearing wings. What was up with that? Was I drinking too much again? Did some woman just call my love life a slug? I think she did, but then again it doesn’t matter. She never had access to our imaginations. If I would be honest I would say I’m forgetting you more and more, like how I can’t place the veneer of your smile superimposed on the shallows of my eyelids when I close my eyes. But we both like lying, so it seems stronger than ever, though intangible. The world is split like a fruit, the seeds blossom in various ways and means, different places and spaces, but still alike, from the same pod. I can’t see the way your branches grow, if they bend in the wind, only that we’re the same shape somehow, that your despair in losing me is something to fan my ego and mine yours. But the despair in two trees rooted makes them certain never to find each other again. It’s such a damned, ugly despair. You have these tapes, cassettes at first and now CDs, that line the shelves of your bedroom. Sometimes you sit in your room with the thin light coming through the white, still curtains, head on the black leather of your swivel chair, and thick muffs encasing your ears. “Limited possibilities,” you say. “That is what the world is, coming toward to me.” In your right hand, you click a lighter, on and off, the small flame wavering like a voice. “Limited possibilities,” you try again. “That is what the world is, coming toward me.” You can't do it. Outside, the rain is falling, and it's a day not to be outside. You could be out there somewhere, plunging knee-deep in storm trying to find the waves lapping onto an icy shore, beating your head like passion, like living life upon the end and very edge of the world. The starting point for journeys. Now, in the warm, enclosed comfort of your home, it feels like an end. * So you say your name is Ellis. “I thought we established that.” To check for MPD. “Oh. Joke.” As doctors tend to do. “Yeah.” So how old are you, Ellis? “Twenty-seven.” What do you do for a living? “Pretend to be someone else.” And are you happy? Ellis? “I’m thinking.” Take your time. “Do you mean that?” I want to know your instinctive reaction. “Okay.” So? “Happy?” Yes. “Happy, happy, happy. I’m not bad.” Not bad? “Not bad,” says Ellis, and for the first time, so wildly, she is smiling. * Your name is Ellis, and I am your Spirit.
