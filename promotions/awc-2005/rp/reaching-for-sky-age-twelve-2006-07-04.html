Reaching For Sky - Age Twelve

4 July 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: Juky 9th, 2006 It was like a normal day for Kris Krimzon – a morning beer, some good television, and a little bit of video gaming. As he was sitting at his computer for the first time in the day, he heard the front door open. “Tamara”, he thought. He looked at his watch. Two thirty, the usual time Tamara came home from a day’s work (Kris still has no idea what her occupation is and is too scared to ask). Moments later, Tamara yelled from the kitchen, “Kris?” Her tone was not asking if Kris was home, he was always home. Instead, it was confusion as though she was wondering what something was. Kris sighed, sat up from his chair, and followed his wife’s voice into the kitchen. In the kitchen she stood, holding a yellow-wrapped package, thick and wide in both directions. “Kris, do you know what this package is for?” Something in his mind told him that this isn’t time for joking, but it was his nature. “Yeah, that’s my cocaine.” “Don’t be stupid,” Tamara said sternly. “There’s no return address on it… Here,” she said, handing Kris the package, “go open it.” Kris took the package back to his computer where he had sat. He stared at the package in amazement. Something about the package told him that it was important. Slowly, he opened it. He pulled out its contents slowly. Inside was a hard-covered book and on top of it was a letter. The book’s cover was red and had a velvet appearance, although it was not velvet. The bindings were golden-coated metal with beautiful one-millimeter deep carvings. There was no label or title anywhere on the cover of the book. He looked at the letter which was folded in thirds. He unfolded it. The first thing he noticed was that the letter was extremely short and contained no address. “Kris, your father gave this to me many, many years ago. He told me to give it to you when you’re old enough. This is a diary of you’re father that he completed shortly before the accident. Anything you’ve ever wanted to know is in here. I’ll see you soon.” When he read those words, his legs went numb. No one has mentioned his father in many months and whoever sent this was not sugarcoating anything. He set the note on his desk and picked up the book. Something burned his eyelids as he felt them begin to feel watered and salted. He closed his eyes and inhaled deeply. He tried to calm himself down, but it didn’t work. He hasn’t felt this way in years. Slowly, he opened the book. Exhaling, he began to read the words written by a man he never got to truly meet. Skyler Krimzon’s Memory Diary Age: 12 I woke up with a startle. I don’t remember where I had fallen asleep, but it had served well. For the first time in a long time, it was a ray of sunshine served as my alarm clock. A full night’s sleep had been accumulated and I felt fresh. Looking down, though, I realized my appearance told a different story. My short black hair was messy; my face dirty. My jeans which I had worn for months now were torn even more; my shirt smelled horrid. I’ve grown accustomed to this and this is, sadly, the life I chose. It’s not the life I want, but it’s the one I chose. The way I see it, I had two choices. I could have chosen a life of conformity, living amidst the glory, engulfed by the fame of clichéd “coolness” and popularity. Even as young as I am, that is not a life I want. Instead, I chose a life of my own. I have no need for friends, I have no need for parents, and I have no need for anyone. I have all I need. Nothing is ever certain in my life, but I’ve decided two rules to live by. One: I will speak your mind and do as I wish. I live in a country where I have these freedoms and I’ve decided to exercise them. Two: fuck rules. Nothing is illegal if no one catches you. Nobody knows me, nobody can see me, and nobody can find me. I like to compare myself to a poltergeist… you can’t see me, but you will still feel and see my effects. I look at my watch, gleaming with silver, to see what time it is. Six thirty in the morning. This watch alone tells a story about my life. I walk around this town, not sure of the name, watching people. I was at a lake when I got this watch. A family was swimming joyously, not knowing of my presence. On a table next to the lake sat the watch. I snuck up, swiped the watch and walked away. I never looked back. That’s how I’ve been living. I see, I steal, and I leave. I’ve only bought what I needed, unless it was on special occasions, such as stealing more than $50. On special occasions, I’d go to a local fast-food restaurant and buy some lunch and an ice cream. I always got ice cream. I never went to the same restaurant twice, no matter how much I loved its food. People seem to remember you if they see you more than twice. That’s the last thing I need to be, a memory. Suddenly in the distance, I heard crying. It wasn’t a woman, not a man, but a little boy. At least, that’s what my assumption was. I was curious, so I followed the sound. Looking over the corner, my assumption was correct. A little boy was sitting on a porch, his face buried in his hands, sobbing. The boy couldn’t have been older than six and he had short cut blonde hair. I waited for ten minutes, waiting for his mother or father to come out. They never came. Slowly, I approached the boy. He looked up as I got within about five feet, his face was bright red and coated with tears. “Hello,” I said. He simply looked at me, not responding. “What’s wrong?” “I..,” he said, fighting his tears to speak, “don’t know where my mommy or daddy went.” “What do you mean?” I asked. “Th-they haven’t come h-home in a l-long long time,” the boy said, nearly choking on his tears. “How long?” I asked. I felt as though I was quizzing the young boy, and that was the last thing he needed right now. “I-I don’t know… L-like a week?” the little boy said. “A week?” I asked rhetorically. “Where did they go?” “I don’t know!” the little boy said, bursting into tears. “Come on,” I said, helping the little boy to his feet. “Let’s go inside.” I had to coax him... “W-why?” he asked. “I’ll make you a sandwich, buddy. I’m sure you’re hungry,” I said. Come on you little fucker, let me in! “O-okay… What’s your name?” he asked. I was startled by the question. “Um, my name is Skyler. What’s your name?” “My name is Eric,” he said, now talking more clearly. He turned and opened the door to his house. The house wasn’t really enormous or fancy, but it wasn’t run down. I followed him, shutting and locking the door as I stepped in. My plan was simple – stay here as long as I can and bail when, or really IF, his parents come back. “The ‘fridgerator,” Eric said, clearing the tears from his face, “is over there.” He pointed to the kitchen. “Thanks for makin’ me a sandwich.” “No problem, buddy,” I said as nicely as I could. I walked to where he pointed. “So,” the little boy yelled from the other room, “where are you from Skyler?” “No where really,” I said. There was a brief silence from the other room. “What?” he asked. “I’ll explain in a second, let me make you a sandwich,” I said. He was silent. I opened the refrigerator and stared in happiness. It was filled with food. I pulled out some turkey, ham, found some bread, and made him a sandwich and made myself two. I put all the stuff away and scarfed down one of my sandwiches before I went back into the room. I walked back into the room and little Eric was sitting patiently on one of the couches. I handed him his sandwich and sat next to him. Before even taking a bite he asked, “What do you mean about being from no where?” “Well,” I said. I paused for a moment. I don’t know what to say… I don’t want to reveal too much to the little shit. “I used to live in an orphanage but I ran away.” “What happened to your parents?” he asked. “I don’t know, bud. The only home I ever knew was the orphanage,” I said truthfully. “Well where are you going then?” he asked. “What do you mean?” I responded, not knowing what he really meant. ”Why don’t you buy a house or something?” he asked ignorantly. “I can’t pay for a house, Eric,” I said. “Why not?” “I don’t have the money.” “Get a job.” “I can’t.” “Why not?” “Because,” I said, inhaling for a pause. “I’m not old enough.” “Oh…” He took the first bite of his sandwich, as did I. “Well,” he said still chewing, “my daddy told me to remember that ‘part of where you’re going is knowing where you’re coming from’.” “Well, bud, I don’t have anywhere to go,” I said. Who knew a little boy could be so knowledgeable? “You can live here with me, Skyler!” he said, beaming. “Sure,” I said quickly. “I’ll make you sandwiches every day.” He smiled at me. For now, I have a place to stay. I don’t know for how long though. “So,” I said, taking another bite, “how old are you?” “I’m six,” he said. “How old are you Skyler?” “I’m twelve,” I said, “and you can call me Sky.” “Okay,” he said, eating his sandwich quickly. I sighed. Eric seems like a good kid to me. I’m used to lying and deceiving, it’s a part of my life. Except, I’ve never really… “interacted” with the person I was lying to. I’d see them once, lie, get what I need, and never return. It seems as though now if I stay here, I’d have to keep the lie going, all the while talking to him and telling him stuff about myself. God, I’m in for it this time. “Hey,” I said, breaking the silence. “Do you mind if I go and take a shower?” He laughed, but I wasn’t sure why. “You wreak dude, go ahead. The shower is upstairs, you can find it.” “Okay,” I said, finishing my sandwich as I went upstairs. I didn’t really plan on taking a shower; I just wanted to snoop around a little bit. Kris stared at these words in amazement. He was stunned. Physically stunned. Everything he thought his father was was just a dream, completely fictional, and it burned inside. For the first time in years, he wiped tears from his eye. He stuck the letter on the page he was on and shut the book. He couldn’t bear anymore. He set the book down. He sat still for minutes, unable to move. He sat still for minutes, thinking about the man he never got to meet.
