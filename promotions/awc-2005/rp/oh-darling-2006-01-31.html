Date: gay Ellis keeps in her mind two memories: one of a lush, verdant forest and the other of dying. Her world has shrunk down to a period, compressed to something the size of an atom or smaller, shriveled up like an oasis in blinding desert heat, a minute space where her thoughts wind round and round her head, slide down past her throat and squeeze her heart until she feels like she might gag or vomit blood. At times like these she retreats from the physical world, deliberately. No one bothers her. No one bothers. In her mind, Ellis climbs up a vine-invested path. A bird screeches at her from where the shadows are pooled together like water high up in trees. Rain starts to fall. She catches a drop at the ends of her lashes and momentarily she is caught by it, the tiny hanging crescent of it and her steps slow down; she wipes it off and continues along the way. It gets in her hair, sliding down and clinging stubbornly against the back of her shirt. At the end of road a dark shape rears, massive as a wall of flesh and just as impenetrable. She walks closer. Her hands are tracing along the edges of bone – old bone, brittle with age but the texture of it still hard and smooth as petrified wood, or agate. The solid block of it soars into the sky and she thinks she can see the end of it, so high up that there are bodies of dead birds littering the perimeter. They’d flown into the wall. The one nearest to her twitches. Its feathers are pointed at the ends, furs jagged with a dark clotting substance. She looks at it, looks away, jerks her head back again. She takes out her gun and shoots it. The pattern the shell makes against bleach white bone is like the shell of a tortoise: flowered but red instead of green. In her mind, Ellis walks along the edge of the wall, touching clean skeletons, kicking aside birds. Her back is turned. The air is unnaturally still and stifling like trying to breathe in sand. A bird cawls, the sound a whining, sawing shrill. Her gun is still in her hand, the stink of powder on her clothes, burnt on her fingertips. Her hair will smell awful tomorrow. She is sweating something awful. She sees a bird up in the sky and raises her arm, steadies it like she’s done hundreds of times before. Her head snaps back from the recoil. Her arm breaks off. There is no blood. She’s looking up at the sky from where she’s fallen on the ground and it’s closing down on her, the babyrobin blue is bubbling to black around the edges and the air is suddenly alive. She can’t breathe. She’s dying alone. Her hand clutches at some withered roots and a few leaves crumble to pieces in her grip and as if in a dream she lifts it above her, opens her fist so that it peppers down from the sky. She can still see the sky. It’s very blue except around the edges, which she can’t focus on because it’s all blurry and her breath catches. She feels her heart slowing. There was something to his gaze as he watched over her that reminded her of a museum curator, proud of the piece squirming on the floor like an animal. At this she stopped herself and lay as still as she could, and would bite down on her lip ‘till it developed sores if she started to move around again. She was afraid now. When she opened her eyes (she hadn’t realized she’d closed them), there was someone standing in front of her. “Who’s this?” a boy asked, and it was a boy, his voice hovering between deep and that curiously high pitch of adolescense. He bent down and she saw his large eyes, his face as boyish as his voice. Her voice wheezed. “Go away.” He sat down in front of her, crosslegged. He smelt nice, like freshly laundered clothes, he had smooth cheekbones which still held some vestiges of baby fat and his smile was bright as anything that ever lived in this city even if that wasn’t saying much at all. He said, “But you’re bleeding.” Practical, patient. It infuriated her. “Don’t you get it?” she said. “The city’s in an uproar. All the little groups of murderers and thieves know they’re going to get taken over by a better group of murderers and thieves and no one wants that.” The pain was making her dizzy. He took her hand and she could hardly lift a head to stop him. A shuddering breath eased out of her chest; she kept her mind stubbornly closed off. He peered at her even more closely and said, “What’s your name?” and his hand on hers, nails clean and hardly jagged and encrusted with dirt as the other’s had been. “Ellis,” she said, automatically, tiredly. He stayed and leaned over her. She looked up to see his face childishly excited. For a moment she wondered what that kind of face was doing in New York but then she remembered with a sort of vague defeated feeling that the city accepted everything and everyone, took them and hid them in its warrens and its soaring skyways and its niches that stored refuse of every kind, dregs like trampled weeds. “I like you,” he announced. “Do you want anything for your head?” He placed his small hand on her neck. She would’ve shrugged it off except she was trying very hard not to move it. “I can handle it.” Breath. Even. “There won’t be a later because you’ll kill me. No use.” She was resigned. It was a curiously logical and calm feeling. He looked at her in surprise. “Didn’t Chandler tell you?” Pause. “I guess he wanted to scare you, didn’t he. It makes sense – he’s like that. But don’t worry, you’ll - ” “No,” she said sharply. “You’ll like us,” he said. He sounded slightly wounded; she could’ve laughed. “I’d rather die,” she told him. Her throat hurt.
