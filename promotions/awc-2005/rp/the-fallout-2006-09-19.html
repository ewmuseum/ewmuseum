The Fallout

19 September 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 19th September, 2006 As my father stepped up assuredly onto the podium in the 5th precinct of the NYPD in New York City, I sensed an air of calm about him. He had the rare ability to be able to stare adversity right in the face and disregard it. In the wake of the suspected assassination of Dean Fisher and Lilly Baines, I hadn’t the energy in my body to confront a boisterous crowd of members of the press and explain in my own words what had happened. Fortunately, Daniel Reno was an expert in holding off the press until more concrete answers could be formulated. This press conference’s sole purpose was to ward them off the suspicion that the Democratic Party was responsible. It had only been thirty minutes since I witnessed the two bodies collapse to the hard, stone sidewalk in a heap. The crowds’ gestures left no room for doubt – these people had been shot to death. The same image crept into my troubled mind minute after minute of the maniacal Joseph Lynch turning away with his sniper rifle and grinning, almost as if he took pleasure from the killing of two innocent people. His partner, Ignacio Torredo, was across the room from me listening intently. He too had no clue what had transpired, or that Lynch had betrayed him. It wasn’t that my father’s word weren’t worth listening to, but I distanced myself naturally from the press conference announcing the news I never wanted to hear. As opposed to sitting despondently, I thought back eleven years to an event that ultimately changed my life. It was the first time I knew for certain that the political world was the world I wanted to be a part of. Eleven years ago… Within the last week, I counted four times my father had to attend a press conference of some kind in New York City. Before we made the short trip from New Jersey, his promise was a two-day stay in the Big Apple. It had turned into a week. My mother Patricia and he had frequent hushed conversations about these meetings, and each time my father would dismiss her inquiries. He was a private person, in truth, and this unsettled me. I didn’t look at him as my father. As Daniel Reno combed his hair, I approached him with care, making sure not to disturb his pre-conference routine. Ever the observer, he saw my approach in his peripheral vision and turned on the spot to surprise me. For a twelve year old, I was relatively tall. Every time my father knelt down to address me as he did now, I was demeaned. It was as if his status put me below him in the grand scheme of things. “Wes, are you and mom ready yet? This is a big, big day for me and the party,” my father announced in a loud, clear voice, putting in a little bit of vocal exercise before the conference. “Yeah, I think we’re ready. I’m not sure about mom, though.” Speak of the devil, and the devil doth appear, although the woman who stepped out from the bedroom was the antithesis of the devil. Patricia Reno had dark, brown hair which reached down to her lower back. Today it was platted to perfection. Daniel Reno and his wife of seventeen years embraced and shared a kiss. I shuddered at the sight of it – parental affection was not my cup of tea. Daniel Reno went about his business in a meticulous and well-thought out fashion. As he revised his notes, which he probably already knew like the back of his hand, I sat beside him. He didn’t address me, so I spoke to him. “Dad, what are you talking about today? The current economic conditions in the country again, or something else?” “Now’s not the time, son,” came the abrasive, rude reply. I was slightly taken aback, but I had come to expect this behavior from my father before big events. There was to be nothing but complete silence for him to concentrate. I trudged back to my bedroom in silence as my mother preened herself in the mirror. I had to face facts – in my family dynamic, I was the loner, the outsider. That wasn’t going to change anytime soon. He stepped down from the podium following his speech on the future of the Democratic Party with a swagger I had become accustomed to. Although he was my father, I felt adrift from everything he stood for. There was no closeness, no camaraderie. As I stared blankly at the figure of my father being accosted by members of the press for snippets of sound bites and promises for the future, my mother tapped me on the shoulder. “Look at how confident he is, Wesley. All those people want to speak with him. You may not realise it, but your father is a very important man.” I shrugged, observing my father wading his way through the sea of press. “Maybe he is, mom, but I just don’t feel close to him anymore. Ever since he started speaking at these – these conferences, it’s like he’s not even interested in me anymore.” My mother feigned a look of sympathy, but I knew she was preoccupied by her husband’s dazzling charisma and witty charm before the press. The cameras flashed in unison, temporarily blinding all those around him. “It doesn’t matter,” I said, speaking to myself more than anyone else. “Nobody ever listens, anyway.” Daniel Reno’s superficiality was clear as day, but that was the way things worked in his world. I was simultaneously compelled and angered by his work. It constantly kept us apart, but it had an intrigue about it that I couldn’t put my finger on. I could barely share dinner with my father right now, but as he strolled leisurely towards my mother and I, suddenly his mission in politics became obvious to me. I was instantly drawn in. My attraction to the political world didn’t stop there. It was no flash in the pan moment. The next morning I awoke with a renewed sense of optimism about my future. Up to last night, I had no direction. Now I had learned why my father had to sacrifice so much of his time for this. The reason was simple – politics was beneficial to the world if handled correctly. My father’s goal was ultimately to make the world a better place, and I admired him for that. As I stepped out of my bedroom, my father was nowhere to be seen. It wasn’t much past 10:30am, and I found it strange that he was not here practicing a speech or revising his notes. My mother hurried into the room, appearing to be hassled for some reason. “Good morning, Wesley.” “Morning, mom. Um, where’s dad? Shouldn’t he be here working on something?” “Oh, he’s gone to meet a couple of friends downstairs,” she replied as she searched frantically for her handbag. “Are they connected to politics in any way?” I enquired, my curiosity getting the best of me. My newfound interest in my father’s work had grown tenfold in the last day. I headed to my room to get changed, anticipating the answer. “Oh yes, of course. Your father’s here on business, after all. He’ll be back shortly.” “I think I’ll go and meet him. He’s downstairs, right?” Before my mother could utter a word, I had thrown on a pair of jeans and a jacket over my pajamas. As awkward as I appeared, nothing could faze me right now. I was an eager twelve year old, and I left the hotel room in a hurry to make my way downstairs. I had to tell my father that I was ready to commit to the lifestyle that had entranced me in the last twenty four hours. Maybe I had been resisting it the whole time because of the distance it would mean between my family members and I. No matter how hard I tried to suppress it, though, I couldn’t. The three men sat around the table with a purpose about them. Their briefcases lay on the floor beside them, acting almost like their personal sidekick. One of the men was tanned and of a small stature; the other was well-presented and his most notable feature was his moustache, which curled at each end. My father was definitely older than the men. As I walked in their direction, I felt no shyness. It was complete and utter confidence. The idea was absurd – a twelve year old speaking to politicians about anything was. As I opened my mouth and caught my father’s eye, the look on his face told me he had somehow been expecting me. “Wes, come on over here,” he said, waving me over with his hand. The two other men at the table greeted me with warm, amicable handshakes that nearly took my hand off. “Joseph, Ignacio, this is my son Wesley. Is there something you want to talk to me about, son?” I formulated my thoughts carefully, in the hope that I would reply with no fear or anxiety detectable in my voice. “I want to be a politician,” I announced proudly. To my utter amazement, no polite laughing greeted my unbroken, squeaky voice as usual. The men regarded me with a great degree of respect. My father’s face lit up in a smile, almost as if he knew I would eventually come around. From that moment on, things just got better and better. Later that day, my father brought me to the Democratic Party headquarters in New York City to show me around. I learned many things about the ins and outs of politics that day, as well as meeting some rather eccentric people. Throughout the visit, the theme was the same. It was all about making changes for the better. It was about improving the way people lived. “What do you think of it, Wes?” “I think… I think it’s really, really cool. I think I could get used to it,” I managed, although I spoke with nervousness. “Let me just tell you something, Wes. I know you’re very interested in this line of work, and I am fully behind you. There’s one thing you should know, though,” he said, a tone of warning evident in his voice. “What’s that, dad?” “Sometimes in politics, it may seem all rosy, but there’s more to it than meets the eye. Nothing is certain. You are forever on edge. Do you think you can handle that?” He looked at me with an expression of concern, almost as if he believed the time had come too soon to ask me these things. I nodded in acceptance. I had to handle it. “Good. How about we go get some lunch, then?” The press conference ended on a high note, with Daniel Reno rolling back the years to wield his political wand and silence the crowd’s speculation. The blame had been diverted from the Democrats, for now at least. A temporary solution to the problem was the best I could have hoped for. In the midst of the crowds and the cacophony of voices, something occurred to me. As a young boy, and indeed as a young man, the one lesson my father repeated to me like a mantra was that in politics, there’s also more to situations than meet the eye. I hadn’t identified the bodies as Dean Fisher and Lilly Baines. The dense crowds around them prevented that. Before anyone had a chance to recognise them, they were gone, shipped away in body bags and gurneys. Had they been killed at all? Were we all assuming too much? “There’s nothing more we could have done, son,” Daniel Reno reassured me, patting me on the shoulder, his firm hand sending a shiver down my spine. “Dad,” I began, “Do you remember when I was around twelve years old? That day you brought me to the Democratic Party headquarters in New York?” “Yes, I vaguely remember. My memory’s not up to scratch anymore, though.” I looked deep into the eyes of my father, my own flesh and blood, and in almost the same tone as he had used on me that day, I rhymed off his mantra. “Dad, in politics, sometimes there is more to a situation than meets the eye initially. We owe it to ourselves to investigate this further, because I am in no way convinced that Dean Fisher and Lilly Baines were murdered today.” I made sure to speak in hushed tones to avoid creating a melee in the area. My words struck a chord with Daniel Reno. His eyes darted from side to side, assessing the situation just as I had done moments ago. Then he made his decision. “You’re absolutely right, Wes. We have to find Joseph Lynch and bring him into custody. Then we’ll have justice.” The figure of Phil Allen dressed in a suit and tie combination caught my eye, distracting me as my father motioned for me to join him to go back to the car. Allen jogged towards me, his discomfort in his new attire evident. “Wes, you’ve got a match this week, kid! You can’t be out chasing the bad guys forever. Let your dad handle it.” “I can’t, Phil,” I explained, “This thing has gone a lot deeper than we expected, and now we believe Fisher and Baines are in fact still alive.” “You know, kid,” Allen said, grinning, “You’ve done a damn good job so far in your wrestling career, and this time I’m not only gonna trust your judgement, but I’m coming with you. I owe it to you.” “Phil, there’s no guarantee you’ll be – “ “That’s no problem, Wes. I’m coming with you, though. So come on, let’s get on our bike!” As we made our way out of the press conference area, I was relieved to be escaping from the scrutiny of the press for now. When I had more answers for them, they would get them. Until then, I desperately hoped that my hunch was ultimately going to be a correct and worthwhile one. If not, my career was most certainly in jeopardy. Joseph Lynch strolled back and forth in the basement of a New York apartment block, his demeanor that of a man concerned. He had his hands in his pockets, and his equipment was tossed to the side after he had successfully accomplished his mission today. He had been promised a reward, and that was all he was here for. In the darkened corridor ahead of Lynch, two silhouettes came into view. As they moved closer to him, he identified them as a man and a woman, both of them holding hands. A smile slowly appeared on Lynch’s face as he extended his hand to both of them. “Mr. Fisher, Ms. Baines, it’s a pleasure to finally meet you both.”
