Distortions of the Mundane I: Two Straight Lines

25 September 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 25/9/2006 I had been walking, at the time, for about a half mile from the train stop, and before boarding the train, I had wandered around the city blocks for some time; a much greater distance than this last half mile, I would have imagined. I had recently acquired a used messenger bag a month ago, and I was carrying it with me as I walked. It was filled with various objects; other acquisitions that were of no use to me but arranged in the bag, nonetheless, in accordance to their fit within the various zippered compartments. The bag hung level to my right hip as my right arm looped through the strap that stayed nicely on the same shoulder. It was a comfortable strap. I guess I could say I liked the bag if I had been thinking about it, but the strap was so comfortable that I didn’t pay much attention to the bag as I walked. On the other side of the crosswalk, a man carried two large, white, plastic buckets by the handles. The buckets looked obnoxious and heavy, but he carried them evenly. They were filled with painting tools. The man looked both ways and crossed the street. He looked about twice my age. The light was red, but there were no cars coming from the perpendicular direction, and besides, buckets are heavier to carry when you’re standing still. I noticed that I was standing in front of a church. The name was something in Spanish; “Nuestro Senora de las Americas.” I hadn’t noticed all of this, though, until the man had crossed the street and put both buckets down on the first cement step for a rest. He must have been some kind of Spaniard. His English was simple and accented, but it was genuine in its efforts, and he seemed like a personable enough man. “You must be tired from walking,” he offered. I wasn’t listening closely enough to know if that’s what he actually said, but that’s what I gathered once I got past the realization that he was talking to me. “Yeah, but it’s not that far. I’m just coming from the train stop,” I assured him. He savored his brief rest, loosening his arms and shoulders with the buckets at rest on the step. He looked down at my shoes. “It’s important to have comfortable shoes to walk in. If your shoes aren’t comfortable, you will hurt your back.” The way he looked at my shoes; I could tell he thought they looked uncomfortable. “They have good soles,” I replied. I was a bit insulted that this man had the audacity to be so presumptuous towards my footwear. I hadn’t really thought one way or the other on the shoes before he brought them up, but I felt obligated to defend them. I would’ve clocked the bastard right then and there if he hadn’t been so good natured about the whole ordeal. I don’t think he meant any harm by it. “Yes, but you see, you must have good arch support. You should always have a pair of running shoes or tennis shoes with good arch support.” I just stood there, slightly nodding but more so focusing on my shoes and his shoes. Perhaps the arch support in my shoes was not so good after all. Perhaps I would be soon stricken with a case of the flat foot- that damned, dastardly, dreaded flat foot. I looked up at the man, and he was now looking at my bag rather than my shoes or his own shoes. I remembered the bag, eased on my shoulder. He pointed at it. “Are you a salesman?” “No,” I replied, “but I can sell things.” “It must be heavy to walk with a bag. It is better for your back to have two straps so the weight is more balanced.” That good natured bastard! If only his observances weren’t so well intended… I thought for a while before I responded. I must have developed a noticeable lean to my walk. I felt my posture weep into a willowy sideways arch. Over time, I would end up walking at right angles with my torso jutting sideways and my upper body parallel to the ground. I remembered the buckets, the weight evenly distributed between both of this man’s sides. Sure, with age, the gravity of bearing weight had shortened his stature, but his spine was straight as an arrow. That straight backed, good natured bastard! “It’s not a heavy bag. Just some stuff in it.” I self consciously shifted the strap over my head so that it rest on my left shoulder as the bag rose a few more inches above the level of my right hip. “It is okay if you are carrying it for one block, but if you carry it for six or seven blocks, it gets heavy like a baby. That is why so many women carry their babies in sacks with two straps.” His command of the language required careful consideration on his part. “I think I can manage. I don’t have far to go.” “Yes,” he said. “You are young.” There, we exchanged pleasantries and parted ways. I walked across the street, and I assume he entered the church even though I never looked back to be positive. I was too concerned with my walk. The process of walking becomes painstakingly difficult once you overanalyze the motor skills that it takes to walk. Subconsciously, the whole process is naturally smooth, but when I consciously tried to walk, my movements became awkwardly angular. My joints grinded against each other like robotic cogs, and my upper body swooned back and forth trying to correct for leaning. At random intervals, I would fall over because I was focusing too hard on too many things at one time. To fall over in public is wholly humiliating. Most people never stop to think about how complex of a movement it is to walk, but to walk takes either unparalleled focus or no focus at all, and I was caught somewhere between the two where I was prone to falling. It was a terrible disaster, and I thought I’d never make it home. *** A few blocks down, I heard an infantile cooing. It sounded like it was coming from above. Instinctively, I looked up and saw several explanatory open windows, but upon a double take, I noticed something alive in the branches of the trees that hung decoratively along the boulevard. There was a lady at the trunk of the tree, looking up with exasperated concern on her face. Her arms pivoted on her shoulder joints, seemingly unrelated to her focus, as an oblivious terrier walked back and forth along the radial arc at the end of his leash. She pulled a cell phone out of her pocket and made a call. She cradled the phone between her shoulder and her tilted head. As I approached, I could hear her desperate conversation with the operator on the other end. “My baby is stuck in a tree!” she repeated whenever a pause in the conversation arose. Other than that, she offered some details to the operator and worry etched itself in the wrinkled lines on her face. Hope arched in the wrinkles on her forehead, though. I approached the tree and looked up, admiring the scene above me as if it were on display at some contemporary art exhibit. I knew nothing of art, but I knew what I liked. She pulled on my sleeve. The strap of the bag slid on my shoulder, and I heard the unsettling contents shift around inside of it. “Sir, you have to help me! That’s my baby up there!” I stood there for a second, still observing the infant who had somehow toddled his way up to the leafy branches above. I wondered how he had done it, the tiny rogue, although I knew that I was in no way responsible and therefore, I saw no immediacy in offering my assistance. Sure, I had misplaced a baby or two here and there on a tree branch before, but that was back when it was the fashion. “I think I’ve come down with a case of the flat foot,” I attempted to excuse myself. She pulled at my shirt as I turned, gripping onto the material at the small of my back with both hands. It was a rather foreign thing to do, I thought, for her to grab the back of my shirt like that. “Please, sir! You must do something.” She looked concerned and helpless, but my bag was starting to weigh heavily on my shoulder, and I wanted to go home. I looked around, though. There were people walking along the boulevard in all directions, in comfortable shoes nonetheless, and here we were, seemingly in the eye of this cyclone of pedestrian traffic that swirled around us. Her grip on my shirt was getting tighter, and she tugged it up and down as I tried stepping in one direction or the other. I feared she would follow me, and the sun was bearing hotter as the temperatures peaked to their afternoon highs. I also was hesitant to step out of this cyclonic eye. “Fine,” I said, “I’ll do something as soon as you let go of my shirt.” She released her white knuckled clutch, and both of us stood there pretending the whole incident had been much less awkward. On top of that, I had no real idea how to retrieve the infant from the branch. I was never much of a card at climbing trees. The bark always scratched up my arms. I was considering trying to dislodge the baby by pelting it with a rock to knock it off the branch. This, I thought, would be the most convenient way that I could help without having to climb the tree myself. I was in no mood for climbing. I found it to be disagreeable most of the time and preferred only to climb when it was absolutely necessary. I scanned the grass where it met the brick building on the corner. It was a good place for rocks, and I found several. They were smaller than I would have liked. I picked up two rocks- one in each hand- and compared their weights. “This should do the trick,” I affirmed, keeping the rock that felt denser. The woman then stood in front of me, blocking my view of the tree. She waved her hands in front of her chest, back and forth across each other. “Aren’t you going to climb the tree?” “No,” I answered, looking up at the baby. “I’m going to knock it off with a rock. You’re going to have to stand under the branch and catch it. I think a sturdy bag would do. If you don’t have one, you can use mine.” I offered her the bag on my shoulder, but she refused it. “You can’t do that! You’ll hurt my baby!” “Nonsense!” I refuted. “I’ll aim for the torso.” “No, you can’t do that! You have to climb the tree and carry him down.” She was being unreasonable now, but I was already too vested in the situation to step out now. Besides, the pedestrian swirl was settling, and passers by were gathering at a distance to watch the whole imperiled baby situation. I was starting to get impatient. I dropped the rock and walked with locked knees towards the tree trunk. I wrapped my hands around the lowest hanging branch and interlocked my fingers around the top, the bag still hanging from my shoulder.. With great effort, I pulled myself up while pushing off the side of the trunk with my feet. After a few failed attempts to swing myself onto the branch, I finally pivoted my way up, scraping against the wood and causing itchy discomfort to crawl over the scratches on my arms and on my chest and stomach underneath my shirt. Atop the branch, which seemed to slightly depress underneath my added weight, the baby sat further from the trunk, and I inched my way over to him by sliding my rear outward and guiding myself along the branch with my hands. The infant seemed to be having a grand time alright, and it didn’t react well to my attempts to collect him. This was fine with me. I never knew how you were supposed to hold a baby, and I didn’t want to do it the wrong way. “I don’t think it wants to come down,” I voiced out from the branch. I looked around. “And I don’t want to come down either!” Now that I was up here, it seemed like a fine place to stay for a while. I’d have a smoke up there and watch the traffic navigate along the boulevard from my leafy turret. The drivers were always in a rush somewhere, and here, the side streets were especially frustrating. They all were one way streets that pointed the same direction. There was also a dizzying circular drive at one end that whipped cars around like a centrifuge- cardinal directions becoming ambiguous and temporarily offset. It was a vehicular labyrinth, and it amused me to watch motorists unsuccessfully pilot their cars back and forth as they tried finding a successful way around the mazy pavement. You could see the drivers swearing through their windows. “Please! You have to do something!” the lady called back up to me. She was starting to irritate me more so, but I soon realized that she was right. The baby was being very distracting. I couldn’t stand to share the branch with it; it kept making cooing noises and slight shuffles. I’d look over and see its mouth wetted from drool, and its impoliteness angered me. If it had just let me be, I wouldn’t have minded sharing the branch so much. But the woman was still at the base of the tree, shouting at me, and her baby was making a mess of itself and didn’t know how to keep quiet. Certain behaviors are unacceptable in these types of settings, and I felt the baby was violating the appropriate, respectful conduct that should be given to fellow occupants in a tree branch. On the whole, it was unacceptable. “Alright, you little shit,” I grumbled to it. “You’ve had your time. Now go on and get down.” “Please grab him before he falls! Be very careful!” That damned woman. It was difficult to keep my head straight with all of these distractions; the shouting and the cooing. I just wanted a damned cigarette so I could watch the angry drivers. And then there were the birds and squirrels. It was all very distracting. “Jesus Christ,” I muttered to myself. Then I went through my bag and found, among other things, a length of rope. It was always good to carry rope around; it oftentimes proves quite useful. I had made sure to keep it with me when I went out. I didn’t always, though. Sometimes I would forget to bring it and would end up regretting that I didn’t have any rope with me. “Hold on tight to this end,” I instructed the woman as I tossed one end of the rope to the ground. With the other end, I ran the rope around the baby, underneath its armpits. I fastened a tight sailor’s knot in the front. “I’m going to feed the baby down to you slowly,” I informed her. She nodded, and I held the baby over the other side of the branch so that the rope made a pulley. I pulled the rope over the branch a few inches at a time. The baby slowly lowered to the ground, and the woman, now ecstatic, rushed over to her child and untied the knot. “Thank you!” she cried out to me, and then she turned her attention back to the baby, showering it with affection and carrying it away, her leashed dog following behind unaware. Upon the branch, I lit a cigarette as the gatherers went back on their ways, and I contented myself by watching the frustrated drivers trying to weave down the one way streets while they cursed to themselves, gripping their steering wheels tightly. *** I had continued back on my way home, the rope zipped back in the bag and the bag hanging back over my shoulder. My feet were getting tired again, and when my feet got tired, I could no longer walk with my toes pointed forward. Instead, the toes started to shift outward, and my gait would turn into a teetering hobble. The jutting angles that my thighs made out from my hips served a double purpose. They kept my pants from falling completely. When I came to crosswalks, I would run through the oncoming traffic, holding my belt loops with my fingers while swinging my legs around me in two outside arcs. Sometimes people would stare, and I’d try to straighten out my walk, but my pants would slip down my insufficient hips. When I walked like so, the trigonometric movements my legs made were inefficient, and they wore my feet out even more. I had to sit down and rest my feet for a while. There was a bus stop on the corner where someone had erected a bench out of some old wood. The carpentry was amateur, but it was built well enough to sit on. The boards were thin. It wasn’t very comfortable. I found myself staring at a man walking across the street. He barely avoided traffic. He would have been hit if some of the drivers hadn’t stopped for him. He wore a red hooded sweatshirt, and he was doing something with his hands when he walked. As he got closer, he hunched over more and more, and his steps became more pronounced. He appeared to be curling into a ball. I’d imagined that he was probably on junk at the time. Yeah, it looked to me like he was definitely on junk. He sat down next to me on the bench and looked at me smiling. His facial hair sprouted thinly and unevenly in patches on his face. His open mouth smile was full of holes and the teeth he had left were crooked and tinted green. Some were much larger than the others. Still, he didn’t appear to be much older than I was. I couldn’t make out everything he told me. His voice was quiet and his words didn’t seem to go together. “You know, man, I didn’t do it for myself. I did it for the children…and the ones who couldn’t make it themselves.” “Yeah, definitely,” I nodded. I figured he was talking about crossing the street against oncoming traffic, but he could have feasibly been talking about anything. I sipped on a bottle of pineapple flavored soda I had in my bag. He looked at me and said something, but I had no idea what it was. I nodded, and I looked down at my bottle of soda to avoid looking into the stranger’s grinning mouth. He said something again, almost in a whisper. “What was that?” He repeated himself, but I couldn’t make out a word of it. I assumed he had asked about my cola. “It’s okay.” I said. I began thinking about it. “It’s alright, but it’s not that good.” I had expected better. The drink was too carbonated, and I couldn’t taste enough of the pineapple flavoring. He kind of laughed, but neither of us knew what the other was talking about. “You know Lawrence?” he asked. I raised my eyebrows and shrugged. “Yeah, man. You gotta know Lawrence.” He opened his mouth again. “And you know Harlem?” He was talking about streets, I presumed. “Yeah,” I admitted. “They’re over that way.” I pointed east as I had confused one street for another. The streets were northwest quite a ways, but it didn’t matter. He shook his head. “That’s where my glasses are. Someone stole ‘em.” “What?” I asked. I wasn’t sure I had heard him correctly. His voice was too goddamned quiet, and his speech was slurred. He was on the junk. He pointed at my shoes. “How would you feel if someone stole your shoes?” “I guess I’d be pretty pissed off about it.” “Damn right, man. I’m pretty pissed off about it.” By now, his hood was down so I could see his face in the light. His mouth opened from time to time, and the color and rot of his teeth made me somewhat uneasy. He looked like someone had just socked him in the face with a staggering hook. It wasn’t cut or bruised, but it was just apparent in his expression. The bus pulled up at the stop. I was close enough to home, but I figured I’d sit on the bus for a while and ride the length of the route until it came back. I wanted to go home, but the bus provided a way for me to excuse myself gracefully from the bench. It was more of a whim than anything. The bus was stopped at a red light. It didn’t move for a while. I could still see the stranger in the red hooded sweatshirt sitting on the bench. He had put his hood back up. I thought he might be looking at me, or at least looking for me, through the bus window, but he probably was just looking off at nothing in particular. Still, I felt like I had stolen something from him that was more important than his glasses when I disappeared onto the bus. Or maybe I didn’t feel that way at all, maybe that’s just how it was and I felt something completely different. The light turned, and the bus rolled along the road. I stopped thinking about the man in the hooded sweatshirt when he was out of sight. I hadn’t thought much about the baby in the tree or the painter in front of the church. Instead, I thought of the infinite latticework of transportation and congestion on the city blocks. I thought of one way streets and three flights of stairs and too many keyholes. I gave up on getting home for a while- thought that maybe I had pissed off some kind of vengeful deity somehow. I thought about the rope in my bag. It was a good rope. I was handy with rope and knots, and I could make a good noose if I had something high enough to support it. It’d be an okay way to go, I thought; much cleaner and more respectable than other bloody, instantaneous methods to off yourself. Only a coward makes a mess of his own death. There were other things though that I could do with rope, and I didn’t want to waste such a fine rope. When the bus started to get a bit more crowded, I pulled the cord that ran along the windows. An electronic ding sounded at my stop request and the bus pulled over at the next corner. I walked three miles back home. It didn’t take that long. I can walk for a good distance when I’ve got my mind set on it. As long as I walk in a straight line without bothering to get caught up in the moving squalor of these human anthills and beehives I can cover a lot of ground. Really, to get from one place to another, all you need to do is walk two straight lines at most. It used to be one straight line, but the goddamned infrastructures did away with diagonal lines. Two straight lines aren’t so bad though. You can get mostly anywhere in two straight lines, I’d always figured, and I had gotten by alright so far with this philosophy.
