Values.

3 October 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 10-03-06 Better days. Where were they? Where had they gone? So quickly had they slipped from my grasp, and before I even had a chance to indulge. The sun was setting, and I was sitting on the side of my bed watching the presentation. It was fantastic, and the only time I’ve ever woken up early enough to see such magnificence. My parents never appreciated things like. In fact, they never appreciated anything. They were the kind of people that would wake up, every day of their lives, with a repetitive routine rolled up, like a fresh-from-the-press newspaper, and ready to go. I was never like them. They always wanted me to be like them, but I refused to walk the same line they did. I guess you could say it was too thin for my liking, as I’m used to allowing the tides of each day of each week take me along their journeys. There’d be times where I’d wake up and want to do something nice. Other times, I’d have an itching to do something really bad. All of that said, it was never my fault. It was my family’s faults for living their lives like fucking hamsters, and attempting-on several occasions-to lure me into their miserably leveled schedules. They wanted to be a normal human being, and it’s safe to say that I never wanted the same. To be normal is to never really live. Being “normal” is the quintessence of a whole family sitting together in a gigantic living room, reading newspapers and playing Gameboy. The conversation ranges from “How was your day” to “How was school” to “Dinner’s ready”. The bigger half of the Spicoli family maintained an autopilot state of mind, because it’s what they knew. Whether it’s a good thing or a bad thing, most of my family found miraculous ways to keep themselves from being innovative, or even productive. My mother, Andrea Spicoli, learned to deal with the undying stress, within the household, by smoking a pack and a half of Camel Lights every day of her life. My father, Edward Spicoli, who’s a pathetic Trekker, (Yes, they’re usually called “trekkies”, but my father always used the title “Trekker” when conversing about his love and obsession for the first three Star Trek series) is a veteran of the gore-fest that was the Vietnam War. He was never at the “front of the house”, per se, during the war. He was a communications lineman; a specialized worker who would set up and repair power lines. However, he was discharged after falling from a telephone pole. He was being shot at by Vietcong and ended up breaking his fall with… his back. His spine was pinched, and he shattered vertebrae. He was a different person when he made it back home, or so I’m told, and it took him years to find that level of sanity again. This, along with a sister, who’s completely fucked beyond all recognition, and an uncle who did everything he could to end his life, are what makes up my family. I don’t generally swim in the cycle of family values, but all of this made home life sour. I had to share the top floor, which is an attic that’s been somewhat refurbished, with Uncle Grady and it was the first time I was ever actually frightened about living in that house. Before Grady’s breakdown and my sister’s unpredictably experimental turn-around, things were “decent” in the Spicoli household. Sure, my father was a complete nut job and my mother’s in denial, but that’s what makes things interesting. As I said, to be normal is to carry out the “same shit, different day”. There’s nothing more mentally painful than realizing, as you open your eyes to the sunlight darting into the open windows of the attic, that things aren’t going to be different today. In fact, things will remain on this same track until I find the courage, and balls, to do a tuck-and-roll and find my own way out. Until then, this is my family. I love them, and I hate them. If anything, I learn lessons by watching and studying them. They are, for lack of a better description, strange and unusual. That’s in my genes, and it’s time I start accepting that. My sister, Gabriela Spicoli, is a rave head who I barely ever see anymore. She likes her ecstasy and fantasizes about meeting Daltry and Townshend of The Who. She’s said to me, on many different and awkward situations, that “Tommy” changed her life. She’s twenty-seven years old and prefers to live life as if she were still a teenager. To her, it doesn’t get any more simplistic than that. The best part about it is that my family is so oblivious to how she’s going about things. She’s been employed once, and that was at Rite-Aid. The only reason she was even hired was because she was boning the thirty-year old manager at the time. Her room, which is usually unoccupied, smells like stale sour cream and onion potato chips. Her walls are decorated with psychedelic art and “Who” memorabilia, and the stereo’s always on. Whether the room is vacant or not, the music never stops. Needless to say, I still live in this light-blue, three-story house. Bought nearly twenty years ago, the yard surrounding the house is corroded. Dolls are scattered throughout the property, destroyed by animals and the anarchistic rebel crowd that seems to be consuming this little town, and a better portion of the lawn has now turned a, diseased and fried, yellowish color. My family, including I, didn’t care much about the upkeep of what surrounded the house. It was the house that my family managed somewhat well. I say “somewhat” because a family that allows their lawn to look like Gettysburg, twenty minutes after the epic battle, have the downright inability to make anything look “perfect”. There’s brown scumrings circling, like horse tracks, around both of our bathtubs. There’s spider webs in every corner of every ceiling in the house, and no matter how many times I was told by my mother to sweep them out of Dodge, they were always there. I suppose my mother got used to it. Besides, she was too busy working for the Department of Motor Vehicle and keeping her brother, Grady, from taking his own life. My uncle, Grady McMason’s life has been interesting, twisted, and tragic all at the same time. The only reason he’s tried to electrocute himself, bleed to death, overdose on prescribed painkillers, and plummet from a 22nd floor balcony at a Hyatt hotel was because of a single Friday afternoon. A deli-manager at Edward’s Market, Grady was cruising on home after a smooth eight-hour shift at work. His fiancé, Mallory, was the absolute joy of his life. There was nothing in this world that made him happier than his strawberry-blonde “sweet cake”, and he always looked forward to that drive home from work. Turning into their driveway, Grady noticed a car he never saw before. It was a red Fiat Barchetta, and it most certainly wasn’t there’s. He exited his car, and grabbed a dozen roses, which he was going to surprise Mallory with, from the backseat. He wasn’t sure what was going on, but didn’t think anything of it. After unlocking the door, Grady entered the house and noticed a spill at the foot of the stairway. He crouched down and dabbed his finger in it. After smelling it, he realized it was wine. He then heard a splash, and then a romantic giggle from upstairs, which propelled him to run up the stairs. He stormed into the bedroom, and found nothing but a sea of blankets and pillows. Another splash, and a moan this time. Grady turns around and runs down the hall, placing his ear on the bathroom door. Before he could even possibly hesitate, Grady pushed the door open and saw the horrifying sight of some long blonde-haired guy nailing his fiancé, doggy-style no less, in their bathtub. Grady was he even splashed after a final pounce before Mallory realized he was standing at the foot of the door. “Oh my God, Grady…” was all that was said before Grady closed his eyes, turned around, and left the house. He threw the roses in one of the garbage cans in the corner of the driveway and ended up slitting his wrists in a shady motel, as far as you can before you actually leave the little town. The thing about Grady is that he’s regretful, and when he does things like this, he panics. He may of wanted to do something drastic to prove a point, but he was in an agonizing amount of pain. He was literally forced, through self-inflicted trauma, to contact his sister, Andrea. I remember when we received that call, as well. We were eating TV dinners and watching a marathon of “Family Matters” episodes when that phone rang. I could remember my mother getting up, answering the phone, and running out of the house without explaining the situation to any of us. My father hates Grady, and pretty much stays in his own little world, so it didn’t much matter to him. It was only a day later that we added another person to the household. It was only a day later that my father slammed Grady against the front door and threatened to burn him alive if he ever took it upon himself to take his “anguish” out on his kids. That was one of the only problems my father had with Grady. He knew him as being an uneasy, quiet individual. To my father, being “quiet” never meant anything positive. “People that were quiet in ‘Nam didn’t make it through a week” was what my father always used to say. “People that were quiet in ‘Nam are the people being memorialized on some giant black wall in Washington D.C. They didn’t get to kiss their family goodbye. All they saw was the fire, the explosion, and that last bit of light before death takes the wheel.” As for me? Well, they call me Michael. I guess you could say that a lot of what has gone on within those four walls of that light-blue house has made me who I am now. Who am I, you ask? I’m just like everyone else. I’m an impulsive son-of-a-bitch that's just trying to do as much as I can before the lights officially turn off. I want to do things in life that'll stain the presentation of my life when showcased in front of a herd of frat boys. I want everyone to gasp when they hear my name, and only because of my known past. I was brought here for what reason, in the first place? Is there a point to my existence? If so, I was never 'breifed' on what I supposed to do. Without raison d'être, I tunneled my way out of Angela's womb, and now I sit beside her on a couch every day, wondering what's next for me. I don't want to wonder anymore. The fun and games begin here. Wondering is for when you're a child, when literally nothing is explained to you in full detail. I know what I need to know, and that's all I need. It's time to discover. fin
