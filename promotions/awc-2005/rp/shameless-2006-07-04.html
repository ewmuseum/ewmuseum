Date: July 4th, 2006 so fuck you and your untouchable face fuck you for existing in the first place 'untouchable face' -- ani difranco ** A person lies. A person tells white lie upon white lie until they are blinded by the glare; the effect is not unlike looking at sun-reflected snow. A person might call herself Ellis. When asked, a person might say that it was this quality that made them such a remarkable cop. Turn to the left, smile. Lie. Lie again. Summer also might pass this person by in small measured drops, beautiful and poisoned like beads of mercury. The weather would be hot, portentous, and the stillness of the air like the moment before a match is blown out. A person might know too much of fire, might've tasted ashes too often to mistake the feeling. Turn to the right, smile. Lie again. A person can run daily along the riverbank in the hours before dawn. Before Adam wakes, sometimes, or an hour after. It might be the time they hate most of all: the pounding of their sneakers against the dirt, the sweat trickling down their back, and the weight of the days pressing damp against everything else. A person might call herself Ellis. Some days, a person might think they feel the weight of resentment instead. And sometimes, a person wishes they didn't lie. Turn to the left, smile. Lie. ** a. but tonight i'm gettin' ripped wide open If it takes a piece of rock ten hours to orbit, man only needs an instant to craft a complete turnaround. But unlike the number of days in a planet’s year, an instant is so quick that it can scarcely be predicted. Forty-one minutes is elation, but by the forty-second, jubilation is over. New passes and new tackles, making happiness shape into hope. But hope is long gone. After losing to Anton, Adam corners her briefly in the hallway to console her. I'm sorry, he says. Whatever, she tells him, It doesn’t matter, and grips the lining of her tanktop when she walks away. Two minutes later, she realizes her palms are sweating. Turn to the right, lie. In the showers, she stands in front of the hot spray so warm she fears it may cook the flesh beneath her skin. She rubs her face, eyes closed and breathing through her mouth, breathing steam. She feels a little like a statue beneath the sad tilt of a geyser rushing down, the water deflecting off her body like a shot. She’s standing in a warm cloud and she don’t want to move, can’t move, for ever swiftly she’s paralyzed by the water, a million pinpricks suffocating a heavy thing. She doesn’t want to move. She can only to scrub her cheeks with the flat of her palms and for a moment, feel grimier than usual. Her breath is stuck in her throat, and no speech avails her even if she wants to shout from the top of a mountain that something strange is happening, a click of events that might make a star stop burning or grind an orbit to a halt. She doesn’t want to know the truth. If it takes any amount of time to come clean, it would take her infinity. A loss is a loss, she tells herself. She can’t take it back, no matter how she might want to. People will win, there will be opportunities never taken, but there is always that next show. She loses in Canada and she loses at the abortion clinic, but she dreams otherwise and if anything, that soothes the sting. At least there is the prospect of the long flight back from Canada, with sadness compressed by distance into comfort. ** After the shower, Ellis feels like she’s a ghost in black, click of feet making her way into some brandless dimension. This is what the dead must look like, she thinks, and it feels this way. It frightens her so much that she can hardly speak. It’s good that there’s suddenly a sound; she was about to bury herself. She looks up and for a moment her eyes can’t focus. The room is off-white, like rain, and Adam appears like a marsh monster. But he’s too late; she’s stopped being frightened. Instead, she tugs between the two a thin line of silence. She wins. He speaks first. Adam sits on the far side of her locker room and then gets up, as if needing a new destination. Sits down again, nearer, just slightly. From his mouth projects some loud, harsh noise, and he could be screaming for all she knows, because all she sees is the way his mouth moves quickly as if trying to find the right words to say. She wishes he wouldn’t. After all, taking comfort from Adam Dick is a dastardly thing. This baby, he says. Yeah, she says. And it’s not to say that she feels good about it. Frankly, it’s devastating. But she’s not the kind of person to be scared for long. Yeah, she says again, and she’s suddenly too uncomfortable, as though she’s some small insect caught in his mesh netting of words that aren’t pliant against his tone or inflections, that rush out too fast to make any sense. He’s fidgeting and scratching at his knee even as he’s smiling, trying to be encouraging, moving like he’s baiting the forced stillness of her posture: the straight back and the crushed left arm. Still she sees that mouth, thin lips that sink toward each other in the middle, the small uneven teeth. We’ll - we’ll figure this out. Soon, he says, after a pause, and then with blink of the eye he’s gone, as a shadow when a room switches from light to pitch black, and his absence makes him unroll across her brain, into corners she’ll never be able to clean so that she’s left baffled and irate and pregnant all at once, wondering how lovers make themselves into strangers and friends into dust. She guesses it’s the evolution of trust, perhaps, that can only go from a hundred to zero like a plane swooning to ground, its tailbone crushed beneath the weight of a loving land. ** While Cortland supposedly has freezing winters and cool summers, the elbow of summer rains too much. She would maybe prefer it if she were outside, catching drops on her tongue when no one is around to look. But now, the night after Divide And Conquer, she’s just stuck trying to open her bedroom window with the rust on its hinges that flake when she pushes. It’s difficult to get leverage when she’s sitting and she would rather bake in the heater than stand, so all she really does now is watches the rain. This is an activity a little depressing for the summer, and because of this and everything else she can’t too quickly paste on a smile but she’ll do her best because that’s what she told herself this morning, sitting up in bed with a big wind whistling between the window panes. The same wind whistles as she sits on the grey sofa with the yellow cushions in the living room. No one is around and she blends like a chameleon, eyes buggy from watching too much Golden Girls the night before. Her heart plunges and skyrockets like a moodswing; the television is her friend. Close-ups of old women she doesn’t know make them seem like friends, too, and she notices the oddest things like Blanche’s cheekbones and Dorothy’s hair. Rose’s stare. All immaculate onscreen, meticulously placed and tweaked by skilled cameraman, the smooth dialogue flattening out the imperfections. It’s these details that keep haunting Ellis, these things on the TV. She’s a practical woman by nature and would’ve blocked them out otherwise but they slip in when she stops thinking and suddenly she’s flooded with images of babies and pink bows and gift baskets. Tangible things. Turn to the left, lie. If you have an idea of what you want, all you need to do is to believe. ** Two hours later and in the end, Ellis concludes that it hasn’t been her week. The conclusion of her match with Anton was a too-big heartstopper, and she remembers her listlessness after like a Goodyear blimp that announced her distaste for AWC and wrestling and the world. Also, she suddenly wants her baby. She’s getting tired of lying, and is convinced that the only thing holding her up from destruction is the kind lift of God’s hands. ** Half an hour after that, finding space in the static of her cell phone, the number that she punches in wrongly four times because the buttons are shaking or her fingers are shaking and fear comes like a fifty foot wave. The line cuts off twice and she tries to find better reception standing in different parts of her and Chandler and Jon and Ally’s living room, once even standing on the couch with the springs sinking her feet into sponge like a trap. The first time, she’s left shouting Hello? Hello? at the operator, who hangs up on her not knowing who she is, and thrice after that, facing the unbelievable dead buzz of a lost connection. This is not the kind of fate to be met by someone who has beaten criminals at their own game and certainly the phone ought not malfunction when this same somebody is close to spontaneous combustion from the sudden feelings building up inside. And she thinks she might be angry but above all frustrated and relieved and pleased and the static clears as she says, Hello? as she says, Hello? and the both of them are saying, Hello? Hello? together with same word but a hundred miscommunications. Eleanor, she says finally, and cannot bring herself to say her name again. Mom. ** It’s turning out to be one of those ridiculously awkward conversations, as bad as the one where the first boy she ever liked asked if she had wanted to attend his Grandmother’s funeral. At the time she had said, No, but now she’s only saying Yes, yes, yes. Yes, I’m fine, Yes, Chandler is fine; yes I did the laundry, yes I do have milk in the fridge, yes I know it’s important for the bones, all the while really wondering if, as Mom speaks, if she’s alone in their too-big living room with the clattering sounds of the Spanish maids and screams of the TV from where Dad is permanently parked in front of. Maybe she’s sitting down with a coffee. Maybe it’s a martini. Or two. She can’t ascertain, although the martinis would be more likely. Instead, she fills up the silence with babble about maybe being pregnant, and maybe wanting to keep it. What are you going to do now? Mom asks after a while. Sleep, she says. Sleep and think. Try to think about many things, or a few things. Think about – What the hell you’re doing? Mom says, unkind. Ellis stops, startled, and she doesn’t really know why because what else did she expect? Her eyes shut and open in the amount of time she takes to breathe because she lives like she breathes: an involuntary, helplessly honest kind of life. Yes, she whispers. Then she steps off the couch, and the static blurs into Mom’s voice and hers. She’s getting tired of lying, but lying is where her comfort lies. ** When she gets off the phone with Mom, Ellis feels like throwing things, making a wreck out of her own apartment. The wind is high and the leaves make their own hurricane outside, so much like hers, a mess on a crude scene of black and white. Her in her NYU sweatshirt, the room and apartment empty, fatigue drawing at each corner of each eye. All she can do for a few minutes is to look at air. Then she stands, and walks out, sees the bright corridor of the apartment building and the sunlight from the double windows at the end of the hall makes glittery flickers in her hair. Outside. The windy, fresh day comes against her with open arms. Walks back inside. The kitchen, the scrubbed counter, the glass-top table, the way she reaches into the drawers and cupboards, trying to remember how to make macaroni and cheese again. She eats facing the refrigerator. The cooler makes funny noises. It reminds her of two people waking up at the same time, shifting slowly beside each other in a state of half-consciousness, and yet pretending to be fast asleep, just simply wanting to have a longer time together. Now she has her stubbornly noisy fridge, and it has her. She eats her second mouthful of macaroni, and tells it, At least we have each other. Her voice makes strange loud echoes, and then dies. A bird outside screams. And then, like how waking up is a painful thing, she makes herself stop being angry. ** She goes to Adam’s later, after Jon comes barging into the apartment with his boyfriend. Ellis stands in Adam’s doorway, wringing the corner of her tank with sweaty fingers. When Adam opens the door, Ellis hopes he can’t read her. She says that she is stupid sometimes, and all Adam says is, Well, because how can he disagree? He wants to shake some sense into her, tell her as politely as he can, Hello stupid, I love you, but we need to stop avoiding this and figure it all out, I’m glad you’re here. I’m not scared, says Ellis, defiant. I just – don’t know where to begin. Everyone starts from somewhere, he says, looking at her directly even though he doesn’t quite want to tell her that she looks terrifying looking terrified because this woman can waver apprehension into the hearts of any opposition. He knows that even the blindest of AWC fans will one day embrace her for that, despite all of the indifference and bad decisions and taking back those bad decisions. You are a different kind of person, Ellis says at long last. From me. And there are a lot of kinds. Of people. Some people are made for babies and for kids and for families and picnics and vacations and Disney World. But. But some people are made to be different. Some people are made for alcohol and pot and getting up too late in the morning and shooting people and, and some people aren't made for families at all, but I'm willing to try, Adam. I want to try. I want to make this work. Which means this is: I want to wake up in the mornings and know where all the vectors converge to give no movement. I’ll wash my hair and brush my teeth and clean my face and mechanically note that I need an eyebrow wax or the birthmark on the right side of my neck is fading. I want a garage and a car and a kid in the backseat and I want to turn up the heater two out of five with bad music on the radio and drive to pre-school and I want cement hand-prints and art class painting on the fridge and parent-teacher conferences. I want happiness to not be uncommon. Happiness will be sitting by the river on weekends sitting by strangers and skipping stones into water, heavy things bouncing on a million molecular pinpricks. It’ll be enough evidence to know there are miracles. This is where I want to begin, this is where I want to start, with you, breaking out into a run and meeting the sharply damp air and bright green grass with my feet meeting the pound in my head, thinking of a hundred facts that will never change: you, our baby, and our family. And there is a moment where he stares at her, unblinking, does that thing with his forehead that makes her slightly anxious. Turn to the left, pause. What the hell you’re doing? says Mom. And Ellis suddenly isn’t so sure, and maybe she is the kind of person to be scared for too long. She grabs the silence by its horns, shoving them into her own gut. Her chair grinds backward as she gets up and tries to walk out quickly before she says anything else but Nevermind, I’m sorry, this was a mistake, I shouldn’t have come barging in here like, like -- Ellis. She stops, just like that with her mouth a straight line, folding whatever words she has left beneath her tongue. He only says, very quietly, I want it, too. I want it too. And when she turns around, he’s smiling. ** Girl and Boy are not two faces of the same coin, but rather two reflections mirroring two faces with two layers between them: one for him and one for her, pulling, contracting and pulling again with no definite end to no definite beginning. To love or not to love isn't the question, because nine months have never made such a big difference as they do now. i see you and i'm so perplexed what was i thinking and what will i think of next ‘untouchable face’ -- ani difranco
