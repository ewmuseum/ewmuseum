Summer's Son #5: 'Existence: Parallels Past And Present'

30 September 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 30th September 2005 30th September 1997 15:36 School was over for another day. Football in the park awaited, followed by the wonders of a TV meal and the sickening realisation that Mrs Chandler’s homework was in for tomorrow, after all, and it just wasn’t possible to cram a three-hour essay into thirty-five minutes before bedtime. The last class filed out, the chattering and gossiping rivalling undertones of mutiny among the ranks; their Maths teacher had let the set out late for the third week in a row, and surprise surprise, the balding old Walrus (so-called because of his rather exciting moustache) had set them a huge pile of homework. “At least we’ve got the week for it,” Timothy Shipley pointed out helpfully as he bounded along beside his friend. Tim almost had to run to keep up with Curtis Stratton, who was a clear half-foot taller than anyone else in his year. At secondary school, height seems to correlate with playground aggression, but with Curtis this wasn’t the case. The true nature of the gentle giant was betrayed by the unadventurous mop of fair hair adorning the top of his head, though he wasn’t as blond as Tim, whose hair, Curtis observed as he peered down from above while they crossed the road, was almost entirely devoid of colour. Tim’s hair would darken significantly over the next few years, as would that of plenty of other fair-haired boys going through puberty. Unfortunately for Curtis, his own hair would never get that chance. BAM! 6th October 1997 10:21 My throat was dry and my head was pounding but I wasn’t crying. Poor Curtis. He hadn’t stood a chance. Thundering along at 50mph, the van driver hadn’t stopped to look. Of course, we should have been more careful. Not all drivers are kind enough to halt graciously at pedestrian crossings. But we’d danced across without a care in the world, until BAM!... and that had been it. Life snatched away from him instantly. And I swore blind I’d seen his spirit rise up out of his battered body and ascend to heaven. I didn’t even believe in God. Just the sight of him lying there... covered in blood... sprawled in a position which just seemed wrong... and the van screeching to a halt... and passers-by running over... and me just standing there... helpless. Still in the middle of the road myself. But what could I do? There were a million things. But which first? How do you prioritise in a state of emergency? It’s the shock that hits you first. Not sadness or anger. You don’t realise quite what’s happened when you’re standing in the middle of the road watching your friend exit the land of the living. You want to do something, you know you should do something, but you don’t know where to start. And then they arrive, with their big white vehicles and their screeching horns and their blazing lights. They take over. And they take you away – you’re just a child! They don’t want to worry you. But you fight them! YOU WERE INVOLVED! THEY WEREN’T! You’ve got a right to know what’s going on. Or so you think. But nobody else seems to. Nobody else seems to care what you think as long as you’re out of the way. That’s until the social workers arrive, anyway, and that’s a whole new story. But the parents come first. And the parents are worse. All things bright and beautiful, All creatures great and small, All things wise and wonderful: The Lord God made them all. I didn’t know the words. They were down on the hymn sheet in front of me anyway, but I wasn’t singing. My mum made to nudge me, thinking I should sing, but my dad stopped her. I still saw it, the movement of her arm out of the corner of my eye. But I didn’t care what my mum thought. It didn’t matter. Curtis was dead. The organ was playing and the coffin was still. People were crying. I just stood there. Numb. My throat was dry and my head was pounding but I wasn’t crying. Curtis was dead. 15th December 1997 12:47 It would be Christmas soon. I sat, my head slumped against the window, watching the snowflakes fall. My peanut butter sandwiches (or what was left of them) sat untouched on my lap. It hadn’t snowed for nearly a year. Last time, I’d gone out with Curtis. We’d gone sledging down the big hill near the motorway. Of course, you weren’t meant to, but there was no way anyone with the remotest shred of intelligence could end up anywhere near the road. Besides, the cars had only crawled along. The gritters hadn’t done their job too well, as my father had liked to point out daily at dinnertime. We’d gone sledging down the big hill, and Curtis had been a bit too big for the red plastic sledge we were sharing, but it didn’t matter. We’d even tried to get in together for one run; it had been a dismal failure. We’d fallen out onto the hard hillside and Curtis’ elbow had hit me just above my right eye and I’d had a bruise the next day, but it didn’t matter. And now it was snowing. And Curtis wasn’t here to enjoy it with me. Though, I wasn’t enjoying it. Not without him. It wasn’t the same. No one was fun anymore. Not for me. “I'm still hungry,” sounded the loud, whiny voice of Dean Pembridge. His minions sniggered, though I didn’t think they’d be sniggering if they knew how pathetic they looked, desperate just to be around this abominable excuse for a human being, tall(ish) and chubby(ish) with slicked-back hair that looked like what it was: a ridiculous attempt to imitate the Italian-American mobster look. Dean Pembridge was my case study for why the government should introduce selective breeding: another few children like him and we might have World War III on our hands. His genetic line needed closure with immediate effect. Paying little attention to his latest ingenious plan to disrupt the class or make life a misery for someone different, I turned back to the window, pressing my forehead against the cold glass. Moving away, I saw the mark I’d left, and watched it wither away to nothing. Slowly... not like a car crash. Not like Curtis. If he’d gone slowly, I could have spoken to him... told him it would be alright... seen him smile one last – I whipped round, looking angrily for the thief of my sandwiches. Oh, how convenient! Dean Pembridge! My stomach growled as I turned away once more, resigning myself to a hungry afternoon. 18th December 1997 09:01 It wasn’t all that nice being Dean Pembridge’s new victim, I reflected as I picked myself up off the ground and dusted off my hands. I noticed a graze across my knuckles, and quailed as I imagined the desperate attempt I would make later today to explain away another minor injury to my nosy mother. Curtis had never liked Dean Pembridge. WHY DID I JUST BRING CURTIS UP? I cursed myself as I frantically sucked in the harsh December air, staving off the feelings of nausea that had been accompanying any thoughts about my lost friend for a good while now. I busied myself in a sea of Geography, making the connection every young boy does between the polar continental air mass you learn about in the classroom and the weather I was experiencing at that exact time. Then I was thrown back against the wall. Oh, God... Dean Pembridge was back. But then, why wasn’t he taunting me? I sneaked a peek out of my left eye, making sure to keep my head down, and no, it wasn’t Dean, it was Graham Cox, one of his cronies. A tall, lanky type, with cropped ginger hair. “Enjoying yourself... Shitley?” Graham giggled, shocked and excited at his own use of a bad word. Sighing, I resigned myself to a Dean-esque verbal tirade. “Well, say something!” he commanded, giving my leg a swift kick. I withdrew it, bringing both legs close to my chest, and said not a word. “Hey! I'm talking to you, Shitley!” he yelled with a self-satisfied smile. “I can hear that,” I said coldly, letting the words escape my mouth before I could think better of it. “Huh? You WHAT? Hey, come and listen to this, Giles, Shitley answered back.” Giles didn’t come over. Graham’s look became a little anxious, his head inclined over to Dean’s group of friends on the other side of the playground. “DEANO!” he shouted, hands cupped either side of his mouth. But it was no use. I hardly dared to believe it, but right now, Dean Pembridge wasn’t interested. I nearly grinned as I caught the sour look from Graham Cox’s face. “Right, well I’ll just have to prove it to him,” Graham muttered to himself, and reared back with a kick aimed for my face. I rolled away just in time, and he hit his foot on the brick wall, yelping with pain. “Not so good without your esteemed leader, are you, Graham?” I observed, scrambling to my feet and making sure I was well out of kicking range as I felt a strange surge of confidence shoot through me. “You watch your mouth,” he warned me, ripping off his shoe (the laces weren’t done up) and tossing it aside so he could massage his foot through his black cotton sock. “You’ll get cold, you know,” I said pointlessly (why was I apparently so concerned about the well-being of a long-time foe?). “It was two degrees when I checked the temperature this morn –” “SHUT UP!” he raged, coming towards me menacingly – or so he thought. Really, he looked more comical than anything, trying to minimise the time his exposed and injured right foot spent on the tarmac. “You’re nothing without Dean, Graham,” I said boldly, then wished I hadn’t as he unleashed his trump card – thoroughly unfairly: “Heh, but you’re nothing without that Curtis to protect you, Shitley.” Gulp. Gasp. Like a hammer to the heart. I couldn’t speak, I couldn’t move, I couldn’t breathe. Unfortunately Graham could do all those things. He could also punch. Hard. 30th September 2005 11:11 Cashing in on a situation he knew nothing about to cut me deep, to take the wind out of my sails, to snag my line on a sharp rock. The land of the dead is where man fears to tread, or so I’d always thought. But Graham Cox and The Illustrious Face-Eater had a lot in common. I spat into the sink, the phlegmy accumulation from my mouth slipping slowly down the inside of the washbasin. A small spider caught in the flow struggled, flailing its eight legs as it inadvertently inched towards the plughole and oblivion, unable to stop its descent to impending doom. Tonight was my last in AWC. I could hardly believe the time had flown so quickly. It seemed only yesterday I’d been clutching at straws in my attempt to get a PRIME contract out of Hin See, a man who hadn’t even known my name. And this was it. The culmination. Days, weeks, months of toil... a battle against confusion, a fight for clarity. I was still none the wiser as to what The Illustrious Face-Eater wanted, but now I knew what I wanted. It makes such a difference when you feel you’re in control. Before, The Face-Eater had been calling the shots – his immature pranks, and his more serious mind games, pulling in my history to help him out whenever he’d floundered. He was no better than those D-list Hollywood blondes, the ones who drop names to earn a mention at the bottom of a column or an invitation to a party. And that realisation had helped me. How could I be scared of a name-dropper? How could I fear the intentions of a man who had to resort to scraping the barrel with something of which he knew nothing? I couldn’t deny that he’d hurt me. The last weeks had been some of the toughest of my life – unable to turn a corner or open a door without anticipating a repeat of one of the horrors that had plagued me. The life-size cardboard cut-out. The photograph, with Tony’s face eaten and mine firmly circled: he’s next. The fire ants; the video tape; the Breaking Point on Pierce. Chip, chip, chipping away at the cocoon I’d built around myself. But now we were on level terms. I’d done it. I’d got there. I’d become a real wrestler. I was now that guy who could turn a booing crowd with just the first seconds of his entrance music. I was now that guy who could get on the microphone and say whatever he wanted without the worry of people not understanding. People now enjoyed watching me, and wanted me to win. What I had with the Face-Eater was more than a petty feud, but it meant so much to me that people actually cared about the outcome. Of course, there’s the added pressure. The Tim of old might not have been able to stand it. Millions of people around the world glued to their television sets, counting on you, relying on you to produce the goods, to bring home the bacon, to win the match. I had a responsibility not just to myself but to the fans. Responsibility. And now, I could handle it. Because it wasn’t just Tim Shipley against The Illustrious Face-Eater. It was Tim Shipley and the fans against The Illustrious Face-Eater. In fact, it was better than that... it was Tim Shipley and the fans and Tony Aliso against The Illustrious Face-Eater. For his memory. In his honour. I would channel the spirit of the man without whom I wouldn’t have got this far. His level head and his experience and his ability, his friendship and his partnership, his Breaking Point, his smile and his wink and his “Hey, buddy”. Tony wasn’t the first friend I’d lost prematurely, and this time I was going to stand up for him. The Illustrious Face-Eater. Stealing Tony’s memory. Stealing Tony’s name, as those of Mafioso lifestyle might claim. Like Knucks. A lurch in the stomach and a tensing of my nostrils as the name even crossed my mind. I’d made no progress on tracking them down. I just couldn’t. First was the time factor, but really that was just a shield for my absolute fear. Thinking the name brought up the memories, the thoughts, the emotions that I really wanted to hold down. For now. Knucks was a long-term goal. But that wasn’t fair, and I knew it. You never reach the long term, as economists know. A long run is just a series of short runs. Long-term objectives are idealistic, contrived... and never achieved. I owed it to Tony to just pack it all in right now and go. But I wouldn’t. And I knew it. The Face-Eater matters more, I told myself. And it was true. All my aggression, all my antipathy was towards him. Not Knucks, Tony’s killers, but the Face-Eater. The man who was using Tony as a tool. The man who wanted to get under my skin and I didn’t know why. I didn’t even know who he was! But only hours remained. Only hours until the 10-Count match. I wasn’t even scared. I wasn’t even nervous. 10-Count? The concept would have terrified me on any other day. But not now. I knew what I wanted to do and I knew what I could do. One way or another I would dismantle the Face-Eater and then... the moment of truth. The mask would come off. And I would know what the past two months had been about.
