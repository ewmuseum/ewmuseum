Date: 06/06/06 It will happen quickly and painlessly, like a surgeon’s knife slipping in and out: adrenaline from another impossible escape, her alone beneath someone she doesn’t know, Adam turning to grin and his lips right there. Like surgery under anesthesia, the sting will not manifest until later. ** a. constant quarreling, the childish fits Ellis and Ally used to play at being grown up when they were younger, in their mother’s bedroom with the lights dimmed and her old phonograph on, playing Nancy Sinatra and jazz from two eras ago. Ally’d always go for the lipstick first, swiped it on with deadly and purposeful accuracy, the color of it redder than the inside of her mouth in winter, the shine of it oily and daringly glamorous at the time. Ellis wasn’t quite as brave. Ally had said that it tasted like medicine, like the chalky little tablets Mom took to fall asleep at night. Ellis asked her how she knew and she just smiled with those fire hydrant lips before falling into loud giggles that smudged the outline of red into messiness. Ally wore white satin gloves and they’d write messy messages with Mom’s fountain pens. The ink would spread uncertainly across heavy paper, their letters curved and childish. Now, jeans tight at their waists and a ring heavy on Ellis’ finger. The records have been gone for a decade, their mother and her mysteries but her perfumes and bottles a little less. They used to play at being grown up and now they are, the sheen of lipstick and gloss and their white smiles hiding those thousands of little cracks and failures, trepidation sinking into their stomachs and love in their mouths; mouths that stay closed for fear of letting it spill out, love that’s there for the taking if only they are as mindlessly courageous as they were fifteen years ago, if -- -- if only someone would say the word. “Pregnant,” and the word is so heavy that it could drown or save a man. The lights in the kitchen buzz with age and the kitchen smells like pot. It hits Ellis like something physical for the first five minutes and then, slowly, fades into the background and Ellis wonders if this is what growing up, growing old means: getting too tired to believe. “Pregnant,” repeats Ellis, mouth filled to the brim with false bravado. She has no lipstick on. In the background, there is the sound of the news on the TV: another drive-by on Clinton, nineteen-year old, random, crime this and crime that. Ellis tears into cole slaw with a spoon. She realizes, with a little jolt of coldness in her stomach, that this is just the beginning. She can taste it in her mouth, the destruction of youth, of something once great, of something that was beautiful, before. It tastes acidic, something that burns and festers, salty. It tastes something like regret, some indefinable sadness. She’s pregnant and it’s over and resignation is a feeling that Ellis isn’t accustomed to, and she hates it. She hates a lot of things, but her convictions can’t do anything now; it’s over it’s over and it’s over for good. And that, that feeling of giving up, that weird tightness in her chest -- that hurts more than anything else ever has. Mom once told her, after her first arrest, that she puts value in the wrong things. Ellis’d proved her wrong. She’s good at surviving. She’s survived being an outcast and she’s survived being a failure. She’s survived Logan, as fundamental of a betrayal as it had seemed back then. Chandler has called her tough, weedy. Ally doesn’t say so in so many words, but nods, sharp, and doesn’t say anything about how stupid she had been before. Had said, you can’t save everyone, instead, and as stupidly true and pointless it had sounded, she was right. If Ellis thinks about it, it’s almost funny, this acknowledgment coming from somebody who could wrap her in a blanket and crush her down to nothing but calcified powder, brittle and insignificant, like the remnants of an insect’s shell snapped and broken on the floor. Ellis spoons cole slaw. The carrots and cabbage and mayo come apart in her mouth. She thinks, childhood is something you are convinced was perfect once you leave it. ** There are a lot of things that Ally knows she only has one chance to do. Standing in the kitchen doorway with Ellis’ back to her, shoulders folded a little, Ally thinks that if she’s ever going to say something or do something, this is it. Now. She walks up to her, slow, and leans against the space of table beside her. “.. Hey.” Ellis doesn’t move, chin tilted towards the table, chewing silently. She swallows and runs the tip of her tongue over the inside of her bottom teeth before saying, “I don’t like cole slaw.” Ally looks at her, long, then shifts her gaze away. Ellis looks at her, and the structure of Ally’s face disappears, blurs into perspective. There’s a joint behind her ear. Ellis remembers telling her, yesterday – Don’t try them. They’ll kill you. What, are you trying to tell me that you’re suicidal? You never listen. There’s no reason to, that’s why. “I don’t know what I’m –” “I know.” What Ally doesn’t say is that she thinks she knows, anyway. Ellis doesn’t talk this much with just anybody, isn’t pushed towards these questions with just anybody, doesn’t have to explain herself with just anybody. What Ally is for Ellis is possibility. Plain and simple. What Ellis sees when she looks at Ally is conviction, is belief, the fundamental, necessary kind. What she sees is faith. Faith, Ellis repeats to herself, faith, but life right now looks more and more like a disaster waiting to explode. She’s not that religious, has never been; what else is there to believe in if you don’t believe in the divine? Pregnancy isn’t it. It might be, for other people, but not for Ellis. Ellis’ problem has always been that she’s too ambitious. “I’m going to make dinner. Hungry?” “Sort of. I guess.” She sits at the table and watches Ally move around the stove first, then lays her palms flat on the glass surface and stares at her reflection. The table’s clean but for the carton of cole slaw and a pile of tangerine peels in one corner. Staring at her reflection, Ellis thinks she isn’t a good person anymore, not even a nice one. She likes making people feel bad. She likes flaunting what she has, feeling superior. She doesn’t really give a shit about orphans in Cambodia or world hunger. People die. Ellis doesn’t know if she cares anymore. Actually, she’s pretty sure she cares only about the things that affect her life, the people, and nothing else, nobody else matters. None of this makes her feel bad, and she tries to comfort herself with the thought that no matter what had happened, it was pretty much inevitable that this is how her life would have turned out anyway. Fucked up, basically. Twelve minutes later (two for a cooloff), Ally puts a plate in front of her, holds out a fork; the first mouthful is sharp and clean and rich and new. It is, somehow, comforting and familiar even though it shouldn’t be, even though it’s nothing that Ellis has ever tasted before. Some things are just like that. The silence lies quietly, and it’s not until she looks up after scraping the plate clean when she catches Ally’s eyes, still and inquiring and a little concerned, that she thinks about -- it. About it again. She thinks about it, and she’ll deal with it later; maybe the next morning or the one after that because all she wants right now is to eat and to rest. She wants somewhere to lie down and she wants someone to lie to her. She wants someone to tell her that things will be alright, that they’ll turn out okay. Not good, probably, but bearable. And just this once, she’ll be good at pretending. ** With a third of her dinner down her throat, Ally puts her plan into motion. She says, “You’re pregnant.” Delayed reactions have become familiar things lately. A pause on the pitch when she looks for a certain word, something reflexive like second nature and blinking; a pause in her breath before she picks up the phone; a pause in her head when Ally’s words finally register. “Pretty much.” Ally’s spoon taps against the plate’s edge. She’s watching as Ellis shovels green beans to the please discard area of her own plate. “I think you should keep it.” “Did I ever say I wasn’t going to?” “Well, no, but.” Wait, what? Ellis looks at her, expecting something more, just like she always has. “I just thought, after Claire, that –” “Don’t talk about Claire.” The plan has gone haywire, and Ally should’ve figured; bringing up someone’s missing daughter probably wasn’t the best idea out there. “I’m sorry.” “Don’t be,” says Ellis, quick and stiff, twirling a bean around with the first two throngs of her fork, “It’s not your fault. Nothing ever is.” “What is that supposed to mean?” Ellis sighs and drops her fork. It clangs, resounding, against the glass of her plate. Her lips purse and her gaze is averted to the ceiling. “El, c’mon..” “God, Ally,” groans Ellis. She looks at her sister. “I’m pregnant, okay? Yeah. I’m pregnant. And I’m going to have the baby and I’m not going to marry Adam. Okay? Is that good enough?” “Ellis,” firm, but – “No. I don’t want to talk about it. There’s nothing to talk about. I just told you all you need to know. Just drop it.” “... I know you’re upset about Claire, but–” “I said drop it, Ally,” voice low, brimming with something almost like venom. Nothing pierces the air but Ellis’ breath. She lives likes she breathes, an involuntary, helplessly honest kind of life, a kind of life that manifests itself in everything she does. Cuts heal but never disappear. Scar tissue. Ellis is a fast healer, but she has the scars on her shoulders and the scars in her mouth to prove emotionally otherwise. ** At night, on the balcony, Ellis sits. Jon is inside watching Bravo with his boyfriend; Chandler is in the kitchen doing paperwork for the Katherine/Dennis case. He brought her boxed Chinese, and Ellis politely declined. Everyone is always trying to save her, and Ellis is getting sick of it. Ally comes out six minutes later, predictably. She stands beside Ellis, smelling like tangerine peels, and lights up the joint behind her ear. The smoke is a contrast against Cortland’s black, threatening to collapse. It meshes uncomfortably. Ally takes a long drag and lets the cancer infiltrate her mouth. Then, she offers it to Ellis. “Smoking’s bad for the baby,” she says, and slumps further into her chair. One leg’s drawn to her chest; the other lies, lazily, across the top of the balcony’s banister. It takes Ally a while to smile. She places a hand on the curve between Ellis’ shoulder and neck. Her fingers are cold. Ellis reaches up and grabs Ally’s hand, squeezing it. She wonders which direction the wind blows wherever Claire is, how the nights solidify, how the light stains her room before dawn. If history walks on delicate feet through the streets, weightless, whispering stories that remind her of her mother and father. ** Ellis was twelve and thin, eggshell skin and blue veins jumping in her wrists, hair close enough to black and heavy at the base of her neck. Twelve, and her mother stands in the kitchen, wrapped up in silk and too many expenses, slices cake for the guests and says: you’ll grow up pretty. You’ll marry a rich man and spend too many days sipping champagne by his side, arm tucked into his in some semblance of comfort. You won’t be unhappy. Twelve, and she dreams in black and white film - a little glamorous, a little deadly, the passing beauty of something long gone and unattainable, now. Fourteen years later, and this: she’s beautiful and waiting to be sold, waiting to be mailed in. Thin still, hair to her shoulders, feet arched like the dancer she never was. Fourteen years and her mother is nearly dead to her but her words aren’t, fourteen years and she’s looking back, jeans and tanks and a diamond ring on her finger; she’s perfect and still waiting for the happiness they promised her with kisses and silent words. and this is all that's left: the empty bottles, spent cigarettes you were so condescending
