Memory Manifests Not Only in War

17 March 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: March 17, 2006 Everything is a drug. Every emotion felt produces a chemical alteration in the mind. Every situation, every experience even, reroutes the electrical and synaptic pathways that map the processes of thought and the shape of being. Peace. War. But of these, I know neither. I know only the dull murmur of godliness, the art of detachment and quiet observation. The present exists and dies within a wink, and the only crystallized thing I have to hold onto is memory; that aching shadow, that vaguely bordered silhouette. Far less abstractly, marijuana is a drug. I had gotten my hands on a very small quantity of what I was told was “bubblegum” strand. I never put much stock in looking for special flavors because that always meant an absurd hike in price. But this stuff smelled sweet, very pungent, though too subtle and pleasant to recognize without the visual. The night had started out innocently enough. It was no different than any other night at my house. The triangle of couches pointing at the entertainment center were half full with their normal residents, a collection of like minded gentlemen and scholars, some accompanied by their significant others. An intricately painted piece rotated within the triangle. The room stunk of smoke and ash, two very distinct scents as the latter has a grittier, earthen odor. We were immersed in it though, and noticed nothing. We became the smell, the stale, idle smell of slow corroding. It was the state of existing in stagnancy, not quite the harmony of perfect equilibrium, but the swirling yet unmoving flush of a broken toilet. Of the friends who shared the temporary dwelling we were renting, Hasone was the most responsible one. He turned to me, looking up with heavy, bloodshot eyes and asked if I wanted to split a pizza “From where?” I managed to cough out. “Second Story?” His words struck a nerve with me. I remembered that pizza place well. I had watched it from a safe distance once when I was on acid. The whole event and the scenes leading up to it was disastrous. I remember putting on Tool’s “Aenima” and taking out my guitar as the LSD was just about to kick in. Over the next hour, I remember flailing around the drywall, boxy living room of my old apartment and mashing my fingers on the becoming mangled strings. The music then became invisible to me. I sat down on the couch and closed my eyes to shield myself from the noise and waited. When I opened them up again, the living room was demolished. The furniture was overturned and there was so much ranch sauce spilled everywhere that I could feel it crawling up my legs. The apocalypse was taking shape in my very apartment. I had to leave. “Now here’s Tom with the weather.” I locked the usually open door to Apartment 309, hoping to keep the maelstrom contained. The hallway ahead of me made me feel like I had been swallowed whole by a snake. It weaved around me as I stepped a slow, straight path to the stairwell. I carefully tread each step of the stairs, as they narrowed towards the bottom. I looked down the stairs, and alarms began flashing in my head. Two other men had just entered the complex. They were wearing bar attire, blue and white striped button shirts, jeans or khakis, and gobs of hair gel. They had noticed me in a different but mutually incapacitated state. “How you doin’, man?” I mumbled something probably representative of my current condition. The other one then chimed in. “This dude looks fucked, man.” I tried to defend myself. “I’m tripping. I gotta go.” The two men then exchanged a rigorous high five, as their order oftentimes does. Then, in his infinite wisdom, the first man issued an advisory farewell. “Just think of a shit-load of different colored fish swimming backwards!” It was then that the bile climbed my throat. A hate flared inside me for him and his aura of cliché. I trembled for the want to grab him by his slick hair and grate his face on the coarse, spackled walls until his skin peeled and flaked off. But he was already on his way back up the stairs, and I needed to get out, get as far away as possible. Nonetheless, I was in desperate need of a cigarette. Luckily, I was stocked. I pulled an American Spirit out of its especially bright yellow box and lit it hastily, battling the wind for control of the flame. I walked at a brisk pace southward, which was a worse idea than I had expected, but there were bad neighborhoods to the north. I was approaching Green Street, the main street in town. Men and women in dressy casual were chattily bustling down the street, in and out of bars. Traffic lights and neon signs rained a blurry prism of light into the air around me as I stared at the light posts that shrunk down the horizon. Vehicles burst through the spectrum of color with shouting horns and whizzing tires. Brakes shrieked and sirens blared and flashed, loud in every sense. It was too late. Order made way for chaos, and I had to keep moving. I hustled until the noise quieted into the background of silence. I sat on a cemented ledge somewhere around the brink of fissuring chunk of imploding earth. Across the street there was a brick building, its austerity and its square, humble architecture were gentling. The lack of modernity made me feel as if I were sitting before a giant photograph, maybe a still from the past that had reemerged in front of me. Or had I fallen in front of it? I stared harder, and the photograph started moving, swinging to life with human breath. People were walking into the building and in front of the building. Then other people would walk out of the building with pizza. I heard their voices, big or small, I couldn’t tell because I had lost any auditory standard to compare them to in relation. “This is the best pizza, I’ve ever had!” “I am so drunk right now!” All these things, with exclamation marks abound. Maybe they were just thoughts. I could’ve sworn they were spoken though. “Give me a slice, dude!” There was something surreally offsetting about this whole picture. The lights on the second story were on, but I couldn’t see well through the windows. This random pizza place nestled beneath the core of my comforting scene was threatening. There was something sinister, something cannibalistic about the patrons. Perhaps some came out missing limbs. Or perhaps some never came out at all. There had to be fate’s work at play. There must have been some lottery to determine who eats the pizza and who is the pizza. I sat for hours, afraid to cross the threshold. It was 3:14 am. I remember this clearly, because it had seemed important to me at the time. It was 3:14 am, so I walked across the street. People had slowly stopped coming in or out, but some still passed by. I walked across the street drawn to the hope of being spared, reborn in a sense. I wanted to appreciate things I had taken for granted in life. I could never know taste and flavor or scent or what it is to be sated unless I had escaped fate and fiery ovens to do so. I walked up the stairs, the walls on either side painted with the names of others who had been there. It was some hallowed memorial to those who had to die so that others could taste life; taste what it is to be human. At the top, I turned a wall. I was coming down. The room was almost empty. There was a counter where two tired cooks were leaning, heads supported by bent-elbowed arms. They paddled idle conversation back and forth about going home and sleep and see you tomorrow. They noticed me there. “Sorry, dude, we’re closing up.” It looked just like a shitty little pizza place. No ritualistic relics, no echoes of sacrificial screaming, no shark-like scent of blood. I had stepped back into the dull pulsing and ticking of reality. It was briefly serene, and later restrainedly enraging. “Can I sign the wall?” One of the cooks handed me a marker. I found a blank spot. “The horror! The horror!” – Kurst I popped a cigarette back into my mouth, left the marker on the counter, and left. I was a long way from my apartment, it was freezing winter cold, and I had forgotten to bring a jacket. “Yeah, Second Story is fine.” I disregarded the vivid flash of memory. It was meaningless, just a photograph of the past; as all memories are, good or bad. They are no more than microscopic patches of the surface of a vast and deep entity with a core, rotting until hollow. The paper thin surface crumbles like it was burning. We can only lapse so much from the present before we return. And we return to a world that is drastically different; where one must not eat, but gorge.
