Date: 3-23-06 I had to get out. I had to get out of Ireland. After experiencing what it was like to get screwed out of something I’d work hard for, I decided it was time to for me to leave Belfast, Ireland, and start my life over elsewhere. About two hours later after the boxing match I had against Paddy O’Shea, I could no longer stand living stand living here anymore. Why should I continue to live in a country that was full of deceit and lies? Why should I continue to live in a country that only screws those who actually make a difference? The answers were simple - you don’t. You don’t continue to live in such an environment. I knew that first thing in the morning, I would get a plane ticket and leave. But where would I go? Who would except me into their environment? Where would be the best place for me to start anew? I didn’t exactly know. But, on a plus side, I had my eye on the United States. I’d never been there before and I thought it would be the greatest opportunity to start there. I started to do some research on where to live. All the states looked pretty good to live in, each had their own exceptional attributes. If it were up to me, I would live in every state, but that would be stupid. I couldn’t live in every state at the same time, nor waste my time buying a home in ever state... and ever city inhabitant in each state. Would be too tedious. Then my attention turned to Massachusetts. There was a city named Boston that caught my eye. Boston sounded interesting, even the name of the state sounded interesting. I read up on it, said something about having a high Irish population; sounded good to me. I know what you’re thinking it doesn’t make sense. Why would I go to a city whose inhabitants are mainly Irish? Just because I want to leave Ireland doesn’t necessarily mean I want to break away from the Irish community, even though there’s no such thing as an “Irish community” in Northern Ireland. With my mind made up, I decided to give Boston, Massachusetts a shot. I decided to give the United States of America a shot. “So, this is th’ United States, eh?” Only two weeks had passed since I made my move from Belfast to Boston. I wasn’t exactly sure what to expect when I entered. Before the nine-eleven attacks, airport security didn’t pat you down like they would now. I wasn’t patted down when I arrived to Boston, wasn’t treated like some foreigner or terrorist or anything like that. I was treated like an equal. Well, I guess my skin had to be darker in order for that sort of thing to happen, speak with some sort of Persian, Arabian accent, or something like that. When I arrived to the United States, I somehow felt like a new man. I couldn’t quite explain why I did; I simply did. I guess it felt like I was given a second chance in life, given the chance to start over, which was something I was looking for here in the first place. From what I’ve seen, Boston, Massachusetts was a lot different than Belfast, Ireland. Cultures were different, obviously, and I noticed that people drove on the wrong side of the damn road. I guess people here in the United States have things backward here. Driving on the wrong side of the road... that’s preposterous! Anyway, I started looking around downtown Boston. It was pretty crowded when I was there, cars driving up and down the road, pedestrians walking every which way - yeah, it was pretty busy. People made glances at me, smiling at me, commenting about me or simply ignored me. Hell, even a few eyes surveyed me accusingly, as if I did something wrong. It reminded me of the time of the time where I had to tell Susan McLeod that I’d killed her husband. Word had gotten out fast that my match was won by death. Perhaps people in Boston didn’t like strangers, and I was certainly a stranger. To get away from the congested streets of Boston, there was a bar that caught my eye. I had a hankering for a drink, so I slipped inside and looked around. It wasn’t too big and it wasn’t too busy, the kind of bar, tavern, pub - what have you - I like. There was a buzz of general conversations that filled the room, people playing pool in one corner and a jukebox playing in the other. People drinking, smoking and having a good time. Yeah, this was my kind of place. I noticed there was an empty barstool at the counter and I took it. I sat around, watching the people. Still the same environment from what I’d described, except now there was a couple dancing in front of the jukebox. I smiled at them and face the counter again. A few moments later, a large man appeared from behind the counter. He stood about six-four, looked to be about two hundred and ninety pounds. He was barrel-chested, wore a flannel shirt with the sleeves rolled up, pair of faded blue jeans and tan work boots. He had pale skin, short, slicked back red hair, with green streaks and had green eyes. The more I examined the man, the more he reminded me of Ian McLeod. He had the same brooding good looks, the same sternness in his face. He would’ve been the reincarnation of Ian; wouldn’t have been too much of a surprise to me. He looked over at me, smiled and limped over to me. I didn’t understand why he was limping then I caught of the heavy knee brace on his left knee. There was no fluent movement in his knee; his leg was ramrod stiff. “You’re a new face,” I heard him say in a baritone voice. I looked up at him. “You’re not from around here, are ya?” “No, I’m not,” I replied, shaking my head. “I moved here from Belfast a week ago.” “You’re from Belfast, huh?” he mused. “I have some family there. Nice place up there.” He extended his large right hand to me. “I’m Cyril McGlintock.” I shook his hand. My hand disappeared in his. “Mikey O’Reilly,” I replied, giving out my boxing handle. “Real name’s Michael Farren.” Cyril arched his eyebrows. “How come you have two names, Michael? What’s with the Mikey O’Reilly?” “I used t’ box fer fourteen years o’ me life,” I replied, smiling at Cyril. “Bare-knuckle boxin’. Me boxin’ promoter gave me tha’ Mikey O’Reilly handle, an’ it stuck wit’ me since. Won eight boxin’ titles an’ I recently retired from bare-knuckle boxin’.” “What happened, if you don’t mind my asking?” I shook my head. “Not at all. I was facin’ some kid named Paddy O’Shea. I had th’ match won, then the fucker screwed me outta me title by cheatin’. Referee didn’t see it, awarded Paddy my bare-knuckle boxin’ title.” I sighed. Even though I’ve only been talking to Cyril for a few minutes, I started to like the guy. “Tha’s wha’ happened. Ye do sell beer here, don’tcha?” “Hmm?” Then he snapped back to reality from whatever world he was in. “Oh yeah, I do. I’m sorry about that, where are my manners?” I chuckled to himself. “What’ll it be, Mike?” “Beer from th’ tap,” I said, shrugging my shoulder. “I don’t care what kind. Just as long as it’s cold an’ it’s refreshin’. “Comin’ right up.” He produced a glass from underneath the counter, filled it with beer and sets it in front of me on a coaster. “There you are, Mike,” he said. “Try that on for size.” I pick up the glass, examined the color of the beer - it’s a thing I do, don’t ask - and took a drink. It went down my throat smoothly and the taste was exceptional. I nodded at Cyril with satisfaction. “Tha’s good beer, mate,” I said, smiling. “Thanks.” “You’re welcome.” He looked at me, his brows furrowed into a knit. “What are ya gonna do here in Boston? Need a job and take care of yourself if you’re gonna start out new. Do you have a place to live yet?” “Aye I do. I’m rentin’ an apartment out here. Price’s cheap, an’ it’s quiet, all thin’s considered, ye know wha’ I mean?” I saw him nod, and I changed the subject. “Wha’ happened t’ yer knee?” I looked down at his left knee, and he picked up that I was looking at it. “Destroyed it in wrestling,” he replied. “I used to wrestle in the late seventies through the mid-nineties. I had a match where my opponent fucked up so bad that I couldn’t walk on it. Tore it up pretty bad, dislocated my kneecap, took a nasty fracture to it. Took three corrective surgeries to fix my knee. Would’ve kept going if I didn’t fuck up my knee.” “Sorry t’ hear tha’, Cyril.” He shook his head. “Don’t be. No one’s fault, really. It was all an accident.” Now he changed the subject. “Since you found a place to live, what are you going to do for a job?” I shrug my shoulders, taking another drink of my beer. “Dunno yet,” I said honestly. “Probably take up boxin’ again... legitimate boxin’. Somethin’ where I have a legit contract where I can make a substantial amount o’ money a year. But I dunno any boxin’ promotions out here. I’ve been lookin’ but I can’t find any.” My eyes shift to his. “Think ye can help me?” Cyril’s eyes glance over to his right, as if he saw someone. When he found his person, he pointed over in that direction. “See that guy over there?” I turn to face the man Cyril was pointing. There was a man sitting by himself, drinking a bottle of beer, smoking a cigarette and looking over some paperwork. He had slicked back platinum blonde hair and sapphire blue eyes. He wore a pair of blue jeans, a black muscle shirt and black Magnum boots. He appeared to be engrossed in his paperwork, taking a drag off his cigarette in even intervals, while taking a drink of his beer in odd intervals. “Aye, I see ‘im,” I replied, looking back at Cyril. “Who’s tha’?” “That’s Zsasz,” Cyril said. “He’s been in the wrestling business for a while. He’s very knowledgeable of things, knows what goes on in sporting venues, when they start and where they’re located. I don’t know if he knows much about boxing venues or promotions, but he’s your best bet I would think.” He smiled mischievously. “Have a talk with him.” My eyebrows arched. “Are ye takin’ a piss, Cyril? He’s busy, I don’t wan’ t’ bother ‘im. Besides, wha’ kind o’ a name’s Zsasz? Is he from a different planet er somethin’?” Cyril laughed out loud on that one. “Dunno, Mikey,” he said, giggling a bit. “Dunno what his nationality is, to be honest with ya. Can’t really pinpoint his origin, so he might be from a different planet.” He laughed again. Then he got serious. “Go over and talk to him. Really. I don’t think he’ll bite your head off.” I finished the rest of my beer and got up from the barstool. “How much do I owe ye fer the beer?” “You don’t,” he replied. “It’s on the house. Fellow Irishmen drink for free.” He finished with a wink. I chuckled a little then went over to Zsasz’s table. I stood over him for a moment, looking down at him. It appeared that he didn’t notice that I was there. “Are you Zsasz?” I asked. I stood there for a moment, waiting for a reply. He didn’t reply. Perhaps he didn’t hear me, or he ignored me. I was thinking the latter of the two. I was about to repeat my question but he cut me off with his answer. “That’s the rumor,” Zsasz replied, his eyes remained on this paperwork. “Who’s asking?” “Mikey O’Reilly.” I extended my hand to him, but it looked like he wasn’t interested in a handshake. Instead, he looked up at me, his sapphire blue eyes examining me. His eyes went back to his paperwork. “Have a seat,” he said. Obeying, I sat in a seat across from him. He tore his eyes away from his paperwork and set his gaze on me. “What can I do for you, Mr. O’Reilly?” “I was lookin’ for a job here in Boston,” I replied, keeping my voice level and my gaze on his. “I was thinkin’ about a boxin’ job, maybe find meself a boxin’ promotion wit’ a legit contract. Do ye have any recommendations? Suggestions?” He leaned back in his chair, arms folded across his chest, and his eyes continued to examine me. “Do you have any previous training?” he asked, even though he knew the answer. The man across from him did indeed look like a boxer. I nodded. “Aye, I have. I used t’ bare-knuckle box fer fourteen years. Started when I was thirteen years old. Young scrapper I was back then.” I chuckled, but Zsasz didn’t looked amused. “Anyway, do ye think there might be someone out there that could take me under their wing in boxin’?” He turned it over in his head. The more he examined me, the less chance I felt that I wasn’t going to go anywhere. Maybe it was good news. Maybe he saw something in me that no one else did. Well, scratch that, Zsasz was the first person I met, so he’s the only one thus far who might see something in me. I don’t think so. “I don’t think I’m a wrestler, Mr. Zsasz,” I replied. “I mean, I used t’ watch wrestlin’ when I was a young lad, but I never took it under consideration t’ be one.” “You’d make an exceptional wrestler, Mr. O’Reilly,” Zsasz said, his smile became broader. “With the proper training, I could turn you into a wrestling machine.” I mulled it over in my head. It sounded interesting, but I wasn’t sure if I would be good enough. Zsasz seemed to have read my mind. “If you would give me a chance, I’ll make you good enough,” I heard him say. I didn’t make a reply. I simply looked at him with doubt in my eyes. “I’ll tell you what,” he produced a card from his jeans pocket and handed it to me. “I’ll let you think it over. When you’ve come to a decision, come to this location and we can get started on your training on the fine arts of wrestling.” He gathered up his paperwork, put them in a briefcase that was sitting next to him on the floor and got up. “For now, I must take my leave,” he said, checking his watch on his left wrist. “I have some pressing matters to attend to.” Now he extended his hand to me, much like I did for him earlier. “Pleasure to meet you, Mr. O’Reilly.” I shook his hand, nodding to him. “Same here, Zsasz.” “Call me Jack.” He flashed me another smile then took his leave.
