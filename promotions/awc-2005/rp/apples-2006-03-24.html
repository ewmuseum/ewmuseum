Apples

Date: 03/24/2006 They spent an entire summer with their hands full of berries and fishing rods and freshcut grasses down by the lakeside, just the two of them so the local bullies couldn’t drag Ally kicking and biting into some isolated bathroom and slam her down against the formica tiling, once twice three times until the breath rushed from her and they could write derogatory things like weirdo and loser on her forehead while she lay stunned and limp. In later years, through callous college profs and two-timing boyfriends and soup eaten from tin cans, there was nothing that hurt more than the callous cruelty of children. Trekking home from the lake they’d wrench long sprays of golden brown flowers up by their roots - for Mom, Ally’d said. But by the time they made it to that dusty street corner (where in Ellis’s memory it was eternally late afternoon) the blooms wilted and straggled like wet hair, from Ellis and Ally using them as fencing weapons and whacking each other across the knees with the stems. Ellis tinkered away at piano while Ally fumbled with saxophone, and when they tried their music never fit, except when it did, and then it was uneasily brilliant, as if only a membrane kept it together and cohesive, like water tension; as if soon mutual repulsion would split them again and then they’d be merely good for the rest of their lives. “What color are my eyes? And don’t look! That’s cheating.” “I wasn’t going to. Anyway, I know. Green.” “Well, alright.” Ally turned over in the grass. A gnat fizzed above her head and she slapped absentmindedly at it. Her eyes were as green as Granny Smith apples. “Why?” Ellis asked. 'This boy,” she said shortly, twirling a stem between two fingers. 'At school - ” “The one who likes you?” “ - right. Curtis.” She frowned. “I asked. You know, as a test.” He’d said brown. “Maybe ’cause he’s spent most of his time staring at his shoes when he’s around you. You know how boys get when they like girls. Too scared to even look them in the eye,” Ellis said sensibly. “But you knew,” Ally said. Ellis knew a lot of things. She knew Ally’s eyes changed color depending on the lighting. In that way Ally was a chameoleon, green against the water, blue against the sky. Grey tucked into a corner of Ellis’ bed with the sheets pulled up around her shoulders. Colorless and translucent in the cool dark. “But I’m your sister,” Ellis said. 'I don’t mind looking at you.” Ally grinned. “Except in the mornings,” said Ellis. She covered her face with her arms, laughing as Ally, on cue, threw a pebble at her head. They used to play this game. They’d spend hours hiding from each other, outside in the backyard. They’d swing up trees and contort their bodies to fit the hollowed insides of logs and scramble up strange roofs and down again to hide in stranger’s porches or backwood cabins or one of the alleyways their town possessed. They’d always find each other. Sometimes they’d play inside. It was alright, until the day Ellis decided to lock Ally in her closet, where she’d been hiding, and had sat staring blankly at the opposite wall as Ally cajoled, threatened, kicked, and finally tried to plead her way out. It was just a spur-of-the-moment slip-up, verging on accidental. She’d been staring at the lock; she’d blinked, and found her fingers turning the key. There’d be boys for both of them, that summer more than others. Ally would sit swinging her legs and while Ellis cleared up the dishes she’d want to know all about Daniel, who’d left a Mickey Mouse valentine in the pages of Ellis’s Vocab book or Kevin, who’d shown up to school one day with no umbrella and so by that simple strategic maneuvar landed a walk home under Ellis’s. She’d enthuse about boyfriends. She’d gesture fervently about late nights out and soft blurred kisses in the rain and scattered ice rinks and paired angels in the snow. Always she’d question Ellis’s apparent lack of enthusiasm. She’d cock her head and pick Ellis like a lock. There was a week, near the tailend of July, when Ally took sick and the sky outside grew dark bulbous clouds and the air turned as still and moist as a held breath. Ellis tromped her way up the stars, down the hallway, ‘round the corner and through the door to where Ally lay fevered and shivering in bed. “I brought you what you wanted,” Ellis said. She pulled the record out from underneath her shirt. Ally stirred. “Don’t put it on. Just, leave it there,” she said suddenly. Her voice sounded incongruously normal; but then she coughed, and - alright, it sounded like lung or something equally vital should’ve come up with that one. Ellis winced. “Come here,” Ally said. Ellis took two steps forward and settled the record on her chair. Turning around and coming to a stop at the foot of the bed she saw Ally’d flung herself flat on her back and had thrown her hands over her eyes, though the only light in the room was the skylight from the window, and even that was dense with the gloom of dusk. Her lids fluttered. She said, “It’s cold.” “I’ll ask Mom for - ” “No,” she said sharply, then softened her voice. “No. Just. Here.” She rolled aside and attempted to lift up the blanket, then fell back again. She swallowed audibly. Her hands were limp white things and twitched like puppets with cut strings. “C’mon,” she whispered, eyes closed. Ellis didn’t move. “I'll get sick.” Ally opened her eyes. They were a feverish beauific color, and there was a look in them that’d only ever be brought about by sickness or something like it - a manic happiness, or the hysteria of loss. “You won’t get sick,” she said. “Ally - ” “I won’t let you,” she said simply. Ally was shivering violently. It reminded Ellis of the sickly trembling that’d gripped their grandfather’s body that afternoon at the nursing home as Kendall tried to spoon soup into his mouth and dropped it while it was halfway there. Ellis squeezed her eyes shut and shook her head to drive the memory away. She took a breath and blinked. “If I end up puking and moaning on the floor,” she said clearly, 'I’m going to kill you, alright? Unless this fever gets to you first.” Ally smiled. “Deal,” she said. For a while it was a confused jumble of limbs: displaced knees, misplaced elbows, Ally’s hair in her mouth, someone’s feet hanging half off the bed. Then she was flat on her back, and Ally was a skinny curve under her hand, a faint breath against her shoulder. Tucking herself under Ellis’s arm, Ally mumbled something about cold feet and closed her eyes. For what seemed like an eternity Ellis lay halfway between panic and dream, anchored only by the flex and curl of Ally’s fingers into the rumpled cotton at the neck of her shirt. Outside the rain came down, thundering like grand pianos. * * Jonathan and his transvestite boyfriend/girlfriend are fucking like mammals on the Discovery Channel. Ally makes a face as if to say, What the flying fuck is that noise? but Ellis just shrugs and asks her if she wants any coffee. The kettle is cold. Ellis pours herself a mug anyway (Ally’d said no - Coffee tastes like piss, she’d said) and sits next to Ally on the couch. Together, they watch an old episode of Seinfeld and wait until Chandler gets home. While they wait, Jonathan comes padding out of his gay little bedroom, clad in underwear that leaves almost none to imagination. He waves off Ellis and Ally as he saunters into the kitchen, and saunters back out a minute later with a can of whipped cream and a carton of strawberries. The bedroom door slams shut. “Um,” says Ally. “What?” Seinfeld on commercial, Ellis flips through the ten different ESPN channels. “I guess he didn’t notice me,” says Ally. “Maybe he didn’t recognize you,” says Ellis. “Your hair is blonde and your boobs have grown six cups.” “Fuck you. They’re C’s.” says Ally. “My point,” says Ellis, “What were they before? Like, triple negative A’s?” “More like quadruple.” Ellis settles against the couch and yawns. “Jonathan is just a dumbass,” she says, “He’s been a dumbass his entire life. He’s just using this gay angle to make him look all sophisticated and shit, with the scarves and the turtlenecks or whatever the fuck gay people wear.” “Well. Everything is an act,” says Ally. Chandler doesn’t come home that night. He calls Ellis and tells her that he’s working overtime on the Katherine case, that they got a call-in about Dennis. Ellis says, Okay, and hangs up and watches Golden Girls with Ally, and Jon comes out halfway through their fifth episode and rags on Blanche before skipping back into the bedroom with a cucumber. -- Later, Ally thinks that if nothing else, this whole experience is going to give her insomnia. Lying on her back in Ellis’s bed, she been staring for a while in the dark at the wall, considering her options which are, in order: not go to sleep, or go to sleep. The blankets around her, her pillow, all of it seems unfamiliar. Ally has a feeling she’s too old for that sort of thing now, for unfamiliarity of a bed to annoy her, but it still does. The smell is too fresh and too unused. She wonders if Ellis changes the sheets every day, if that explains it. All that fresh cotton, the clean bedding, the novel idea of this sort of room service after all this time she’s spent fending for herself. In the distance, but not a very far distance now that she’s judging it blearly with one eye open, Ally can see Ellis’s sleeping form, the faint light on the line of her back. Ellis has her face down in the pillow, sleeping on her stomach, which Ally can vaguely recall is not the most ideal sleeping situation, but that may only be for infant children? In which case she can ignore it, but she’s too tired to think about it too hard. Ellis has her blankets around her legs, strewn in and out, and Ally’s cold just looking at her. Ellis sounds like she’s asleep. Even breathing, even snoring, because Ellis is intelligent, of course - if Ally didn’t sleep now she’d be miserable in the morning. But Ally lets her head lie limp against her shoulder and, in a slightly croaking voice, whispers, “Hey. El.” Ellis makes a vague groaning sound, burying her forehead deeper into the pillow. Ally furrows her eyebrows and clears her throat a little. “Ellis. Are you sleeping?” A shuffling movement. Ellis lifts her head, puts it down heavily, and with her revealed eye, blinks slowly at Ally with an irritated expression. “I was,” she says, voice muffled by her pillow. “Sorry,” says Ally. She turns away, back towards Ellis, as if to say go back to sleep, but Ellis, in a slightly louder whisper, retorts, “I’m up now.” “I didn’t mean to wake you up.” “What time is it?” Enunciating slightly, even though it was very early, and Ally didn't feel like it would be wise to tell Ellis the exact time, which she figured to be somewhere around 2 or 2:30 in the morning. “Really early. Sorry.” “Okay, okay,” Ellis says, in what Ally figures to be a resigned tone. Ally turns over to face Ellis, who has propped her head up awkwardly with her hand and is glaring at Ally across the thick darkness. There are smudges of black circles starting under Ellis’ eyes. Ellis says, “What do you want?” “Uh,” Ally hesitates, mulling over the issue again in her head. It had been logical when she had first thought of it, the first time, but they had gotten swept up in TV and then it was time for them to go to sleep, and Ally hadn’t thought of it again until a few minutes ago. To be precise she’d been thinking about it the whole time, actually, but hadn’t gotten the nerve. She wonders if Ellis will understand; she’s putting faith into it that she will. “Do you think everyone’s really going to, you know, forgive me? And everything?” Ellis groans, an honest-to-God groan and her elbow collapses under her, as if the weight of the preposterousness of Ally’s question weakened the bones. Ellis draws the blankets up her body and says, “Do you really have to talk about this now?” “I just - yeah. Yeah, we do.” “Look,” Ellis says, rustling her blankets, “Don’t worry about it, okay?” and Ally is about to interrupt but Ellis rushes on, still in that sleep-groggy tone that makes Ally trust Ellis more than if they were in the light of day and awake. “And if you bring this up again, I’ll hit you,” which would be menancing, but Ally can already hear Ellis falling asleep by the end of her sentence. So Ally, she just nestles herself into the fresh cotton pillows and tucks an arm under her head and stares at the wall, until it’s five and she finally falls asleep, and she’s pretty sure she dreams of roses. * * Ellis doesn’t like apples very much. Something about the aftertaste makes her remember the way it feels to kill someone, although that’s stupid because she’s never killed someone, so it’s not as if she can remember it, and she doesn’t think murder has an aftertaste anyway, because it’s more of a physical act of taking, isn’t it, as opposed to an act of eating and tasting something. “Logic, logic,” Jonathan sing-songs as he sits next to her the next morning, listening, and Ellis has to resist the temptation to figure out right now if murder has an aftertaste after all, because it’d be so satisfying just to bash Jon into the ground, all the rest of him a red to match his boxers. If Ellis has to speculate, she thinks murder tastes like the iron and almost bloody lingering taste she has after she’s eaten apples or had apple juice, the same slippery irritating taste that crawls around in her throat long after she’s done, the same garishly and misleadingly sweet taste that makes her mistake apples for a simple fruit but only later after her teeth are deep into it she’s forced to take back her words. It’s a much more complex fruit than oranges or bananas, who peel themselves open. Evidently, this idea pleases Jon. He says, “Apples misled the first couple on Earth.” “Why am I talking to you about this again?' Ellis says, almost physically pulling away from the conversation, but: Jon grabs her by the shoulder closer to him and says with a pseudo-scholarly look on his face, complete with a tight, fierce little smile, “You have a feeling that I am a significant, undeniable part of your personality that you cannot seem to elude.” “I think you’re a pain in the ass,” says Ellis, snapping at him. “But a significant and undeniable pain in the ass,” says Jon, proud of himself. He leans back and claims that tomatoes are love apples. Ellis thinks that tomatoes are more like the actual act of murdering than apples, all that red and spurting and bleeding onto the cutting board or hand or whatever. She says tomatoes make her think of food or sex, she’s not sure which. And Jon, he gets up and walks over to the fridge. Couple of seconds later there’s a tomato in his hand. He’s got a knife and is slicing it in half, handing Ellis a portion of it before proceeding to mutilate his part by cutting it into smaller and smaller semicircles. It takes Ellis a while just to regard the tomato before she puts it into her mouth and watches with vague interest and disgust as Jon turns his into a mess. “Like sex, huh?” says Jon. “Well, don’t worry,” he chirps, tongue catching a tomato seed between his third and fourth finger deftly, giving Ellis the impression that if he wasn’t gay and her brother that move would have been kind of hot. “All fruits are supposed to represent sex in one way or another.” Ellis sucks tomato juice between her teeth and says, “Whatever.” “You know,” says Jon, “We don’t really look alike. And I think we should do something with this kitchen. Add a little pizazz.” Fact: Jon thinks he’s a sculptor, but Mother is still waiting for him to become a podiatrist. Ellis takes a bite of tomato. The shower turns on behind them. Jon says, “What’s that?” “My friend,” says Ellis, “From last night. Remember? I guess she’s finally up.” Smacking his lips together, Jon squints and says, “Who is she?” “You’ve met before.” “Oh,” says Jon. He tilts his head and says, “I liked your hair better when it was longer.” * * The leaves on Ellis’s basketball player’s hood remind her of Granny Smith apples. Granny Smith apples are the worst kind because in Granny Smith apples there’s nothing but the taste of apples. They’re not like Fuji apples or Red Delicious apples that have another taste to them, like they’re halfway artificial and not quite fruit. Granny Smith apples have a taste to match their color and Ellis remembers their color because they’re just barely a shade lighter than the color of Ally’s eyes and everything about Ally so overwhelmingly green that when Ellis turns away from her, there’s an afterimage of red. Ellis drives to the aiport later that afternoon with her windows half-open instead of all-the-way. She hears the sound of the wind outside, barely whistling. She’s always hated spring, too, because of how depressingly hopeful everything is around her, leaves bursting into green, everything smelling sweet and light and soft. Ally’s sitting next to her, chicken-shitted out of talking to Jon and it’s not like it mattered anyway because Jon went back to fucking his tranvestite boyfriend/girlfriend before Ally was out of the shower, and Chandler still wasn’t home, and Ellis thought she was just being a stupid little chicken shit. Either way Ally’s sitting next to her in the car, playing with the radio and asking her all these questions about wrestling to which Ellis answers, You'll see for yourself, and what kind of pot is that hanging out from the side pocket to which Ellis answers, Durban, Durban Poison. On the radio the weatherman says, There's a 68% chance of rain today. In the car, Ellis says, When we get home, you little chicken shit, I am tying you down to a motherfucking chair and making you talk. Ally shrugs and says, Alright. She says, I just need more time to figure out what I’m going to say to, you know, explain things. Ellis says, This is your last second chance, Ally. You don’t deserve any more. They’re at a red stop light. In a few seconds, it blinks green. Ally stares at it and says, Yeah.
