The Need to Know (Part 3)

6 June 2006

Date: 6-6-6 The Number of the Beast! We decided to go to Mexico to try to get our hands on an electrolaser. I had heard these things were being heavily researched by the military as a way to replace guns. I had even heard that this thing fired manmade lightning. There was a lot of scientific shit involved in the way it worked, and I couldn’t explain it if I tried. But when you’re looking for something like a fucking laser gun, it only seems right to have to get out of the country to find it. In America, that left us with only two borders to cross, and I’d be damned if they had these things in Canada. Hasone and I didn’t have passports, but who in their right mind would try to stop us from sneaking out of America? We loaded up the car, a used, black Kia with no windshield wipers and decent gas mileage. The car was South Korean, so we knew it was efficient. Asia was all about efficiency. Fuckers sleep in dresser drawers over there, all stacked on top of each other and shit, sometimes five high, and they do calisthenics at work. I saw it on the news some time ago. As for our preparations, though, we had all the supplies we anticipated using and then some, all piled openly in the back seat and over thirteen hundred miles of open road between us and the border. It’d probably take a full day of straight driving to get there, but we planned for stops to refuel. We figured we’d each need breaks to know. We’d stop anywhere with an outlet and some privacy. And then, we decided that in order to get their quicker, we’d have to take turns recharging and alternate driving every couple of hours. Hasone started up the car, and I started up a cigarette, and we took off for Interstate 57 towards Memphis. We shouted to each other over the sound of wind and traffic shearing past the open windows. The radio was on, but it was too soft to make out what was playing. “I’ve been thinking about surgery,” I yelled to Hasone, who was inches away from me. “What? Surgery?” he shot back as shit rattled around in the back seat. “Yeah. What if we got cords implanted in our brains? They could hang out the back of our heads, and we could plug them right into the fucking wall, man. We could be knowing all the time!” “Where the fuck would we find a place to get this done? We’d have to pay off a neurosurgeon. It would be pretty fucking expensive, dude, and if we find this electrolaser, that’s all the money we’ve got.” Hasone was right. We both decided to pool all of what we had of next month’s rent together for the electrolaser and the expense of bringing it safely back into our possession in the United States. “Maybe we could just get a good drill. How hard could it fucking be? Just drill until you feel it go through the bone and feed a fucking cord up there. Thread it through some of the sutures in the grey matter and let the skin heal around the cord.” “Fuck no, man. That’s a terrible idea,” Hasone immediately replied, thinking of all the dangers of autonomous surgery. “I’m not down unless we get it done by someone who actually knows what he’s doing.” His eyes stayed pointed at the road ahead, and I took a few more drags off my cigarette before tapping it on the edge of the window to ash. I stared out the window, trying to decipher a constant rhythm, matching the slightly discernable beat of whatever was on the radio to the interval of time between each telephone pole. Each measure was slightly more off tempo with each telephone pole we passed, and the discrepancy became irritating. In one quick, knee-jerk motion, I jutted my arm out to turn the radio off, pushing a knob, and when that failed turning it passed a pressurized point in a static decrescendo into silence. And then, the telephone poles passed by undisturbed. I tossed my cigarette butt out of the window and manually rolled the window shut. I had an idea. “What if we started dealing?” I asked, with my left eyebrow arched brilliantly at Hasone. “Dealing what? Weed?” “No. Electricity.” Hasone turned from the wheel for the first time to look at me, almost questioning whether or not I was serious. His unspoken inquisition was soundlessly affirmed once he saw my eyebrow and the brilliant angle at which it was arched. It was an impressive talent, I thought, to be able to arch it so! “That would never work,” Hasone countered. “Everyone already has access aplenty to electricity. Why would anyone buy it from us?” “Hold on,” I interrupted, and unintentionally skewing the direction of the conversation. “Did you just say ‘access aplenty?’” “Uh…I think so.” “That’s the gayest thing I’ve ever heard,” I chuckled hyperbolically. Hasone refocused his glare ahead of him, his thick, tree branch arms extended to grip the wheel at two and ten, rocking it back and forth like he was driving in a cartoon. I could almost see the smoke start to rise out of his ears. His tone remained calm, though. “I’m serious. Who would need an electricity dealer?” I tapped my knuckle against the upholstery, thinking of my target audiences and the best possible marketing strategies to attract them to my product. You had to think like an executive with this type of thing. Entrepreneurship runs through American blood. Coincidentally, I was draped in a spring jacket with the American flag printed on the material. But it was no muse. That was in the glove compartment. *** The fine piece of South Korean automotive engineering sat parked on the side of a gravel road just off the interstate, clam-baked against the backdrop of a fresh, sprouting corn field about twenty miles from the southernmost point of Illinois. A sign in front of the exit ramp displayed that a small town with a gas station was about five miles in along the gravel road. For now, we sat in the Kia’s foggy cabin, putting words in between coughing spells. “Hey, if the plural of one laugh is laughter, shouldn’t the plural of one cough be ‘coughter?’” The sound of ridiculous laughter, a hoarse vibration under the breastbone that creaks out during rapid, conscious inhalations, sounded. It was followed quickly by the strike of a lighter, and a small red flash alerted through the smoke. “What about kids?” “What about them?” Hasone responded, not knowing quite where I was going, probably half-questioning whether my intention was that we start a family together. “They need a place to know so daddy doesn’t find out when the electric bill comes.” “Ah, like high schoolers?” “Yeah, God bless adolescent U.S.A. That’s where most of the nation’s disposable income is in.” “What about homeless people?” Hasone added, now stoned out of his mind and excited about whatever harebrained we were cooking up. “How’s a homeless person going to afford electricity?” “I don’t know. Beg for change?” “Sure, I’ll take that. So now we’ve got high school kids and homeless people.” That was enough of a start. Hasone and I rolled our windows down and spent a few minutes trying to waft the smoke out of the car so that we could see. The Kia then bounced a few miles over a gravel path until it smoothed into a paved road just outside town limits. We were out of it almost as fast as we were in it, paying too much attention to all the mundane things were undoubtedly subtle, shitty landmarks to this particular shitty town. We could’ve stopped right there and taken over the town with nothing but the one air gun and several rounds of CO2 pellets we had between us. The town was fucking dead. Everyone was at home for supper except the single mom with the chain smoking fix who just moved here from another shitty town who needs a job to support her dumb, fuckup kids, so she pulls second shift at the gas station and the longtime locals from just outside the town limits who owned the couple of hole-in-the-wall bars, getting ready for the country trash that somehow settled into ruts in this shitty town of Tatumville to grow old and drink themselves to a sooner death after a life barely worth celebrating, let alone mentioning. We could’ve stopped right there and taken over the town, but we didn’t. Instead, Hasone just pulled up to a pump at the gas station and started filling the tank. I walked into the mart, where single mom working the second shift eyed me nervously. I was a stranger, but the good ol’ red, white, and blue was emblazoned on my jacket. I lifted my sunglasses over my eyes and rested them on the curve of my head past the hairline. “Pump three?” I asked, completely without regard for the meaning of either word in that awkward pseudo-question. Her eyes turned down towards whatever they use behind counters at gas stations to monitor fuel purchases. She waited for the total to appear and returned her eyes to me. “Twelve dollars and thirty three cents,” she fragmented. I pulled my wallet out and took out a twenty. Nobody carries exact change anymore. I handed her the bill and waited, keeping my hands in my pocket after I had put my wallet back in. “Seven dollars and sixty seven cents is your change sir. Have a nice day,” she forced out robotically. It got me to thinking about becoming a human robot. The implanted cord wouldn’t be enough, I thought. I needed to become a robot. My mind couldn’t run forever on the pumping and functions of my mortal organs. I needed to be a machine. I needed to run completely on electricity. I’d replace the organs with battery powered mechanisms, and plate myself with a metal that didn’t have the easily torn consistency of skin. I’d become immortal through electricity. I needed to know. “Do you have a restroom here?” I asked the middle aged woman, or more precisely the aura of her fear of loneliness for the rest of her life that lingered from behind the counter. I noticed her mouth squeeze into a tight frown as she pointed to a set of doors in the back of the mart. Every wrinkling year, she had started putting on more and more make up to hide herself. I thought about her as I slowly made my way through the door of the men’s room. I left the door unlocked as I searched for an outlet underneath the sink, pulling a short length of copper wire out from between folds in my wallet. *** When I was a kid, I had nightmares that I still remember now, and now I’m the type of guy who can’t even remember if he had a dream five minutes after he wakes up. Half the time, the first thing I think when I wake up is, “How the fuck did I end up here?” And sometimes it’s my own bed I’m surprised to find myself in, and other times, it’s not. And sometimes, I had a hard time figuring out which memories were actually dreams and which memories of dreams were actually real. Or, who the fuck knows? Maybe they were all one in the same. I still freak out about shit getting in my eye after I had a dream that my eye was shot out by a beam fired from an alien spacecraft made completely out of light that shone on my closet door. I could never wear contacts for that reason, and I didn’t much care for wearing glasses. They didn’t fit the shape of my face correctly, so I learned to deal with seeing everything a little bit blurry. Fucking astigmatism. I had one dream that reoccurred every few years before the last time I had it about a decade ago. I dreamt I was at the mall, with my mother. All malls had an open forum at some point in the middle where the architects felt it was proper to plant a few palm trees indoors and maybe install a fountain somewhere to somewhat naturalize the overwhelmingly human tendency of consumerism. I had read somewhere that ninety percent of all U.S. print money circulating through the hands and cash registers of America contained at least small traces of cocaine. But all of the print money circulating through the hands and cash registers of America is stained with urgent, materialistic greed. I remembered things I hadn’t initially detected in the dream, like the palpable stench of such consumerism lingering in the air. I waded through its particles, unknowingly swimming towards that forum, where drab, medical curtains hung to make a village of tiny, curtained cubicles. A nurse would bring me to one of these cubicles, a nurse with an unmemorable face or no face at all, perhaps. She would make me take off all of my clothes, and then she would take off my skin, albeit as gently as you could imagine. Once my skin was completely shed, I shone metal, a robotic gold. And then new skin would be administered to replace the old, which was promptly discarded in a sanitary container. I was old enough now to try to take my skin off myself. The gas station restroom was roughly the size of one of these cubicles, so I sat on the floor next to the toilet with my clothes in a pile in front of me, pulsating from the electricity traveling through me. I dug my fingers into my chest, and they sunk into gelatinous skin. They slid right through, and I peeled the skin back. I felt it was strange that I hadn’t noticed this before. I kept peeling at my skin, switching from my chest to the sides of my calves, sitting with my knees bent upwards and my feet flat on the ground in front of them. My head peeked around my upper leg, and I watched in amazement as I tossed the strips of skin in a pile near the bathroom door. The gold metal shone right through, but then again, I was knowing at the time. I thought that perhaps, if I could know all the time, I could travel not only through time, but also through the timeline of dreams, and maybe the dreams of others. I’d navigate freely through dreams, operate through people’s subconscious desires, and live on forever inside strangers. Soon, I was shed completely of my skin. The leftover pile on the floor was too large to flush down the toilet, so I lifted the lid to the toilet’s tank and stuffed all of my old skin inside. I put the lid back onto the top of the tank and looked in the mirror, asking myself how I was going to get past the unfortunate clerk. My appearance would be alarming. I’d stand out. Word would spread, and for no reason apparent to me then, I didn’t want that to happen. Maybe I’d take her skin. But what about her dumb, fuckup kids? “Fuck them,” I said to my inexperienced conscience. I opened the door and walked out as if everything was fine. She had barely glanced up from the Redbook magazine she was thumbing through at a rate which revealed she was only looking at the advertisements, when I realized how hideous and pockmarked her skin was anyway. I quickly averted my attention to the door and dragged myself back to the car. *** We were well into Arkansas, about seventy miles until Little Rock on Interstate 40, by the time I woke up. My head was aching, and the road seemed pretty clear except for the occasional semi-truck. Hasone heard me stirring as I bumped around in the passenger’s seat to readjust myself into a more comfortable position. My neck felt sore after my head had been weighing it down, bending it into a curve at the top of the spine, while I was out. I grumbled something as I had grown accustomed to grumbling. “How’d it go?” Hasone asked me in monotone. I mumbled something else. I was a loud mumbler. Most people mumble quietly, so you couldn’t understand them even if they were making sense. Being a loud mumbler was better than being a soft-spoken orator. Elocution was lost without volume. At least my vociferous mumbling was confident in its lack of communicability. “Sounds like it went pretty well, then,” Hasone continued, answering his own question. I kept looking out the window as I reached into my pockets to pull out my pack of Pall Malls. I lit another one up, and rolled down the window. “I’ve been doing a lot of thinking about the surgery, and I’ve been seriously considering some modifications and adjustments to transform myself into more or less a machine,” I said, with no contextual precursor. “If my body could be rewired to run on some sort of electrical circuit rather than my regulatory systems, I wouldn’t have to worry about petty discomforts like swollen prostates.” Robots, you see, show no signs of aging like humans do. Old age was one thing that troubled me. I feared one day that my midsection would shrivel away, my ass cheeks would fall off, and I’d have to belt strap my pants up right under my nipples. I’d only be able to mash soft foods with my bare gums. I feared this more than death, but I still feared that I would have to face death wearing hiked up trousers and liver spots on my hairless, hazelnut shaped head. Hasone kind of sighed in contemplation, “We’d need to find a bigger consumer base than high school kids and homeless people to pull off that kind of surgery.” “Electricity strikes me as the kind of drug that would be big in upper middle class suburban areas. Once they need a higher dosage that they can’t administer themselves from their own homes, they’ll have to come to us.” “Man, you can’t fucking shoot other people with the electrolaser.” “Why not?” “You said it was like a lightning gun. We can’t have fried mother fuckers smoldering in our basement.” “Nah, we’ll make sure they’ve built up pretty good tolerance before we start shooting them up with electrolasers. Besides, I know what I’m doing. The safety and survival of my clients is my number one priority as an electricity dealer.” Hasone seemed satisfied with my response as we pulled off an exit ramp towards the outskirts of Little Rock, Arkansas. Hasone pulled into the parking lot of a department store and turned off the car. “Let’s switch here. I’m going into the bathroom to know,” Hasone told me, already halfway out of the car. He walked at a brisk pace towards the store, and I unbuckled myself and exited the car, chasing after my swift-footed companion. “In here? It’s too risky. There are too many people here?” “I have to pick something up.” “Then why don’t you pick it up, and then we’ll drive to a more discreet location where there’s less of a chance of getting caught?” “I can’t exactly buy it or steal it. I have to use it in there. Besides, all the people here are too busy with all the tedious routines in their own mundane lives to worry about some electricity junkie passed out on the bathroom floor.” I was almost caught up to Hasone, but the son of a bitch seemed to quicken his pace as he was nearly galloping through the automatic sliding doors. I hustled in behind him, passing an elderly gentleman the department store paid to greet customers. As Hasone barreled past him, and I stepped onto the tile floor inside, I caught the old bastard pretending not to notice our entrance, looking off at an angle where he could monitor us from the corner of his eye. He was too afraid to make eye contact with us. He had heard that young hoodlums nowadays kill people just for looking at them the wrong way. He had lived too long to take such a risk. Old people walk around perennially afraid of everything. There the old coot was with his combed hair and his nametag and his blue vest, trying to look dignified or feel important. I lost track of Hasone waiting for the old shit to acknowledge my presence. It was his job to greet me, and his shying away from his duty insulted my patronage. I forgot about Hasone and walked right into the withering fossil’s line of vision. “Hello,” I spat out, the verbal equivalent of a machine printing out a receipt. “Hello, sir, is there anything I can help you with?” “No,” I stated frankly, “I just wanted you to say hello to me.” He smiled nervously, looking over my shoulder to avoid eye contact and rocking back and forth on the heels and balls of his feet with his hands clasping each other behind his back. “Hello,” he nodded awkwardly. I wanted to say something else, to berate this uncomfortable gentleman, but I didn’t. Instead, I wished I had never confronted him in the first place. Now neither of us knew what to do, and we just stood a foot apart, staring around the other man. I finally became too uncomfortable being so near the guy; felt like I was standing right in front of the grim specter of death, so I nixed any notion I had of a graceful way out of this and quickly walked away. I squinted and peered around the department store for any indication marking a nearby restroom, and when that failed, I walked in the direction where I figured a restroom may be, and two left turns around the perimeter of the store later, I found it. I walked in the men’s room. It was far better maintained than any gas station I had seen. It was too clean, though. Things this clean are only this clean for a reason, and there was something disturbing about the strong possibility that someone could see us. Only the most deviant of voyeurs would insist upon a restroom being this immaculate. I heard a rustling, though, from one of the stalls, so I walked to it and knocked. “Busy,” I distinctly recognized Hasone’s voice chime defensively from behind the door. “It’s me,” I assured him. “Open up!” I heard ripping and maneuvering inside, so I knew he wasn’t relieving himself, but he still refused to open the door. I slid underneath the space below the stall door, and stood up inside as Hasone turned his back defensively, clutching a torn plastic bag of some sort. And while we both stood inside the stall, we heard footsteps. Both Hasone and I hushed immediately, but the plastic bag he was cradling in his arms made another rustling sound as Hasone tried to slowly turn his ear to listen. The man must have heard us in there, seen the two pairs of feet in one stall, because after we heard the motion-sensor flush of the urinal, the man left with hurried steps, bypassing the sink and exiting the bathroom. Out of relief, Hasone accidentally relaxed his body, letting the plastic bag slip through his arms. I picked the bag up and looked at the label. “Adult diapers? What the fuck?” Hasone answered sheepishly, scratching the back of his head and facing the gleaming, polished wall. “Sometimes when I know, I, um, shit my self a little bit.” I had the same problem, but I didn’t want to turn this into some sentimental moment where we’re both crammed in a bathroom stall revealing that we both shit our pants a little bit sometimes after we shock ourselves. There we would be, embracing each other in relief that we both have problems containing our bowels because of our strange and wonderful addiction to electricity. “Well, shit,” I said, not intending the pun. I left Hasone to know, standing at the sink and looking at my face in the mirror, trying not to stare at the puffs and ripples under Hasone’s pants as he came out of the stall and shoved one of his spare keys into the wall socket. He passed out there, and I walked over to drag his gigantic, adult diaper wearing ass out of the bathroom. I hooked an arm over each of his thighs and began to pull, but Hasone weighted significantly more than I did, and dragging him back out into the parking lot was a chore. I needed to take a break before I got him halfway out of the bathroom, my back was starting to ache, and I figured we were making good time anyway. I walked into the stall and locked the stall door, removing my pants and looking at the dried stains inside my boxers. I was more concerned with the blood than I was with the fecal sprays, but this wasn’t the type of problem I’d want to explain to my physician. I slipped on one of the adult diapers and pulled my pants back up over them, holding my soiled undergarments at arm’s reach by my thumb and forefinger. I left the stall and threw the boxers away as I uncomfortably shuffled now that it felt like my genitals were swimming around in a cushioned plastic bag. I fell back onto Hasone, my unconscious carcass piled atop his, and figured we were making good time, but it was still important that we prepared for such delays. *** Luckily, I came out of it before Hasone did, but as circumstances would have it, my back melded onto his stomach, and there was nothing I could do to rouse him from his unconsciousness. I was stuck on the ground, unable to roll over like a turtle flipped on its shell. I tried rocking back and forth to try to maneuver my belly to the ground so I might crawl out with Hasone on my back, but I could only barely tilt him to one side or the other before he would inconsequentially roll into balance flat on his back. I had no choice but to wait there immobilized, staring up at the bright tubes of light on the ceiling. I began to think about possible ways out of this predicament once Hasone awoke. Perhaps, he could support my weight and carry me as I lay perpendicularly across his pouch. Then, however, he’d have to remember to walk parallel through doorways. I figured the best way to handle this situation would be to both tilt ourselves at crossing forty-five degree angles from the ground, stepping with our outside and inside feet in unison to avoid falling down. When Hasone started to stir, it took an unnecessarily long time for him to finally accept the situation we were in. “I know I wasn’t supposed to know before my turn to drive, but there’s nothing we can do about it now, unless you can invent some sort of time machines, which would be pretty difficult considering the fact that we’re fucking melded together. And pointing fingers isn’t going to accomplish anything.” “You’re a douche-spot,” Hasone concluded. Then, we pushed ourselves up, finding a wobbly balance with help from the walls and sinks. Walking, though, would be a chore, as it turned out, because Hasone’s strides were longer than mine, so we kept veering off in arcs around a circle of pivot points. To exacerbate the situation, I was partially blinded by large blotches from having to stare at the halogen tubes for such a long time, so as I’d swing off kilter from the unsynchronized steps, I crashed into the damned hand dryer. I hated those things. They never worked as well as paper towels. Fuck the environment. I want dry hands! “I’m having a hard time seeing. You’re going to have to guide me and match my steps,” I informed him, reaching out around me to make sure I had clearance to move. “Why do I have to match your steps?” “Because I can’t make my steps any longer.” We tried again, founding it was easiest to keep our balance at a snail’s pace, more of a jigging shimmy out the bathroom door. We slowly shuffled our way back through the automatic sliding doors, ignoring all of the customers stopping their carts to watch our conjoined movements. But as soon as we got outside, the setting sun flared up the flame colored sky, and the adjustment to the sunlight from the store lights blotted my vision even more, into an impenetrable haze. I watched the fireworks swirl around inside my eyelids as I slumped towards the ground, feeling Hasone dragging my attached body across the pavement towards the car. And then I felt everything stop moving. Car horns honked, and metallic wheels whirred and clanked by. Children made noise. Mothers shushed them. And there we were, presumably right in the middle of the fucking parking lot, and my vision had gone out, and we couldn’t fucking pull ourselves back up. *** The next part of the car ride was spent in tense silence. Hasone was still a bit irked about the whole ordeal of me knowing when I should have been driving. We should have been plowing through Texas right now, but instead we were still passing by towns in Arkansas with horribly ugly names like Malvern and Arkadelphia. Seriously, who the fuck decided Arkadelphia was a good name for a town? We stopped off an exit ramp on a desolate patch of highway and shielded our lower halves with the car doors before removing our adult diapers and disposing of them in a field. Staring at our own diapered waste was a bit disillusioning, and Hasone and I both started to question what we were doing shitting ourselves in the middle of fucking Arkansas. “I’m sorry about the whole thing in Little Rock,” I submitted. “Fuck it,” dismissed Hasone. “Can I bum a smoke?” I reached into my pocket and pulled out my pack of Pall Malls and a lighter. He lit a cigarette, and I joined him, and there we were off the side of the interstate in Arkansas smoking cigarettes. “Maybe we should just turn around and go home,” I said, not really meaning it, but not wanting to go on either. We still had a shit-load of Texas to drive through, and I didn’t take well to driving in the heat. Hasone claimed that the heat was only so bad because the car was black. I started kicking pebbles off of the road. “Nah, let’s keep going. It’ll take just as long to get back as it will to get to Mexico,” Hasone said, not really meaning it, but not wanting to drive back home either. I knew neither of us really wanted to go anywhere now, but I left it at that anyways. Hasone continued, “Maybe we should just go easy on the electricity for a while, you know?” “Yeah,” I lied. After all, the whole point of this trip was to pick up a device that would help us simulate being struck by lightning. It was somewhat ironic that we made this pretend pact to cut back on knowing in order to achieve our end goal. We flicked our cigarettes into the field, got back in the car, and kept going, fueled by false resolve. If we needed an honest reason to keep going, we’d never have left in the first place.
