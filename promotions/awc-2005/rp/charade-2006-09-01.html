Date: September 1, 2006 listen dear; this is all there is- i did not wear this dress expecting a portrait, nor did i dance in wooden ships believing to pay. now that i shall be sad for loving you and going home, it is only right you know -- * Your name is Ellis Nash. You are twenty-seven years old. You have lines in your face that you don't remember having two years ago, eyes that shouldn't look like someone else's but do, and an asshole for a boyfriend. You know people think you're a bitch. You wonder if they know you like your toast burnt with marmalade, about the last time you cried over an argument with your shrieking mother; you wonder if they know you better than you know yourself. You wonder sometimes because when you rise to greet yourself in the mirror, a stranger stands in front of you with wrinkles you don't have and dark circles you never get and shadows of ribs that never show on you because Adam always said you had the appetite of a dog, didn't he? Doesn't he? Withdrawing and disoriented in your own dark apartment number 3628, Cortland, you are surprised to find yourself in the body of a twenty-seven year old that feels as weary as that of a ninety-year old's. You are not surprised that you sigh and it tangles with the taste of ash; you are not surprised when you accidentally rub the cigarette into your bedspread. The Mirror tells you that you aren't reconciled with the reality of your age. 'I lost my life,' you snap at Him, loud enough to be angry, not loud enough to wake Jon or Ally or Chandler. 'I need time to catch up.' 'You had time,' says The Mirror, to chimes of the clock that overshadow his words. You don't listen. You are a twenty-seven year old woman who is, by all American standards, old. 'I feel old,' you tell Chandler, the next morning, over tea. 'We're all old,' says Chandler, distantly. You wish he didn't sound so much like The Mirror. You wish his eyes weren't thirty-four-year-old eyes. You wish he'd shave more. You talk about death with Ally over lunch. You both vow to die heroically, protecting something you love, as brave and reckless as you had lived. 'Out with a bang,' you declare. 'People will know.' Ally will die a hero. She will go out with a flash of green and leave a legacy that travels the world over. She will leave you a sentence to live by. You will have rather died with her, side by side. The papers would have heralded your sacrifices. The Mirror never understands, is too practical, knows how to temper his reckless bravery. When you lash out at him, he only waits patiently for your fury to subside. When you tell him you want to do something, anything, just to be out of the apartment that you don't care about death as long as you go down screaming, he fixes you tea. 'The world will know,' you vow to him. The Mirror smiles too much like an adult, too practically. Your name is Ellis Nash. You are just a twenty-seven year old woman who has never grown up, and already your life is over. -- there are flowers everywhere - bright swelling blossoms in carnival colors, defiant in the sun we treasures, we proud gems shadowed by stone and marble clouds. * This is what it's like: you think of him, for whole stretches of time, think of the reckless grin, think of the easy way he slouched and walked and slept. You think of him and suddenly the edges of your memories are fuzzy - what, exactly, was the shape of his jaw? Is that image in your mind so accurate, or is it already wavering? This is what it's like: you forget him, for whole stretches of time, and every time you remember it's like you're plunged into the past, standing frozen as he falls. You'll be reading the paper or taking a bath or dozing in the early hours of the morning and his absence won't be there anymore; it's as if he's gone into the other room or slipped out to buy more weed, as if he's due back any moment. And then something will prickle and then the flood will start: he's gone, you'll think, and then, gone? Yes, gone. And the weight of that word will knock you over and you'll lose him all over again. This is what it's like to be the one left behind. You find yourself humming songs you haven't heard for years as you eat whatever Ally cooked; you find an old razor that isn't yours or an empty box of cigarettes in a drawer in your nightstand and you think how unfair it is for his belongings to still be here. You hate him for doing the easy thing, though rationality always kicks in and tells you it wasn't his fault. But you hate him anyway. Jon thinks you're going slightly mad. Chandler finds you blanking out. Ally rolls you blunts and cooks dinner. And time is all that you have now, whole days of it, bunching up around you there's so much of it. It blends together, no day dog-eared apart from the rest. You buy groceries that rot before you eat them and fall asleep in sagging chairs with Golden Girls on and sit up in the middle of storms and think about him until your joints start to ache; you wait for the sun splitting the curtains, for the rise and fall of the moon, for nothing at all. This is what it's like to be alone: habit, routine, something you ease into, a comfortable empty life. Sometimes it's better this way, you think. Maybe it's easier. You can hate him and he won't hear you. You can scream his name and there will be nothing. This is what it's like to be alone. You know this. You remember this. Maybe it was inevitable. This is what it's like now: you wake alone, you sleep alone, you live alone. You remember. You wait for him. You always do. -- everything glitters at dawn when the light first hits it, bathed in gold tempting as a tomb left abandoned for the ages but the cobwebbed cavern, full of curses and scarab-beetles and the whispers of shrivelled death, tarnishes in time - * Wednesday, you eat two sandwiches at crossroads of Cortland's streets that never seem to end and watch people pass. You drink coffee and dream and think about the bills in your skinny, old mailbox. You squeeze ketchup onto napkins. You eat deep-fried potatoes with too much salt and vinegar. Thursday, you pull out a half-full pack of cigarettes and light one, breathing slowly, holding it between your thumb and index finger. You bet they'll leave grease marks on the thin, washed out paper but it's alright. Last week you saw a man, almost boyishly young, with thick dark curls and a casually affected slouch. Something about the way his jeans had sat low on his hips and the way he had smiled to himself, impossibly bright, had made you want to kiss him, gentle and laughing. Had made you want to touch the edge of his shoulder blades, the inside of his arm, to feel the bumping of his pulse at the point where his jaw and neck met against the contours of your teeth. You liked him because the frayed edges of his jeans were dragged across the pavement by flip flops, and nobody wears flip flops in New York, so you tried to imagine him as just returning from Coney Island, sitting in the subway and still being able to feel the sun. You wanted to find out if he was as warm as he looked. Friday and you still keep him in your mind, you keep all the people that stand out filed away in a little drawer. It's a skill, but there are no tricks to be learned, no clandestine tips to be traded. People either watch well or they don't and that's the end of the matter. Patience helps but if the person has a mindset for detail, for people, patience shouldn't be needed. For you, it's always the small things. The people that you think of again and again aren't always gorgeous or model thin and immaculately dressed. It's all in the line of an ankle, the tilt of a head, the way someone's hair moved in their own generated breeze. A quirk of mouth or the wrinkles of a shirt against a hip. In that moment, in that place and time, something had made them beautiful and it didn't matter if they were really irritable and dour faced in real life, because that would be the only moment you kept, the only thing you remembered, and you would never know. Two weeks ago, it had been a suited woman emerging from Starbucks in impossibly high heels and icy cheekbones. You had thought that maybe she was a businesswoman that owned half the buildings in Brooklyn or maybe somebody that took care of appointments and private matters for celebrities. She was supremely confident, you thought, assured and comfortable, the kind of woman that always had her shoulders pulled back. Next week, it could be a mother juggling a daughter and bag of groceries or a couple sharing ice cream outside Urban Outfitters. Next weekend, you'll see a girl. She'll be young, in college, maybe. The back of her sweatshirt will say 'FOREVER SMITTEN' and she will wear a white, pleated miniskirt with knee socks and ballet flats. There will be something purposefully casual about the way she stands, hand presumably tucked in pockets and knee bent, the way people do when they're waiting for something, somebody. You will like the slant of her shoulders and her short, choppy hair; you will imagine that she is one of those girls who burn CDs all the time and can dance for hours without stopping. She will make a good photograph, still against the blur of cars and smoke and people, elbows perched and foot turned out, but you won't consider taking a picture then because you know that it would never be as perfect as it will be at that point. When you go home that night, lungs seizing, you will find a bright blue envelope facedown in front of your door, dead center. -- and all that remains, left behind, are wilted petals poking stubborn through stone we treasures, we-- whose sleep is not forever but whose shine and color fade and grow again, bright even in the dismal shadows of memory's tomb. * A day before, you will cut the onions thin, edge of your nail thin, and blink at the sudden watering of your eyes; close them tight even though you know that the feeling will go away quicker with them open. Then again, sometimes you keep things close to the ribs surrounding your heart even if they hurt in the worst possible way, because at least you have them. You will squint a little at the cutting board and quickly dump the onions into the pan, crackling and popping with oil. The phone will ring, suddenly, too loud in the heavy, warm silence of the apartment. You will be almost positive that you had disconnected it the day before, but maybe you will be distracted. 'Hey.' 'Uh.' You'll lean against the counter and cross your feet, staring at the patterns the sun makes through the window and on the scratchy porcelain floor. Even the static on the handset will sound uncomfortable. 'I just. We haven't talked in a long time, and.' You will not say, eight months is not a long time when you're twenty-seven. People want to drag out time and make it insufferable, tragic romances say this in scarlet letters on the library shelf, but in your world, things come and go and it isn't hard to forget some of them. You'll hum your agreement under your breath and turn down the heat on the stove, putting a lid on the skillet; you'll frown at the grease covering it and remind yourself to wash it after you finish. Probably not. You'll search for something safe and noncommittal to say. 'Oh.' 'What are you doing?' 'Cooking.' You'll hear him smile, somewhat. 'Why?' You won't. You won't smile. You'll take the skillet off the heat before everything overcooks. You'll pour yourself a glass of water, warm and a little bitter. He'll ask, 'Will I see you? This week?' 'Maybe,' you'll say, and maybe, he should know, never means yes, and this is it, this is goodbye, this is the 'have a nice life/No, really, I did love you once, but I can't' part of the story, and you just aren't ready but it happens, life happens, and you will close your mouth before you say anything else. 'Alright.' You'll nod, remember that he can't see you, and murmur something, but whatever it is that you'll say will not matter. After you hang up, you'll put the pan back on the stove and stare at it for a few minutes, thinking, and in your mind, you'll be putting all these half remembered days like scrapbooks in a trunk; you'll be shutting the lid and twisting the lock and you'll know that he will not have the key anymore. The sun will set, hazy and drifting, and the sweet spiciness of the onions in your mouth will taste something like-- You'll remember writing half assed poetry in college, rings of coffee stains on notebook paper, remember that feeling of falling head first in love, that jittery, odd longing, and you'll wonder if you've changed at all. Maybe an ending is better than the tight silence of not knowing. Maybe. You will always want something more deep down, but over the years, you have learned to ignore the feeling. You'll eat, ignore the dishes and pans, go to sleep, wake up, and it will all be so easy, and you will try not to think of a time when it was impossibly hard, because the world was beautiful then and memories are only memories. * -thinks that sometimes, it's easier to let go. -- a wind sprang up across the sea, rustled some brilliant tapestry, the curtains pulled since half-past dawn, laughter hats and umbrellas gone, the wind has swept the garden bare of cat-white gloves and red-brown hair and now the sun is out upon the bay where weed-filled hands have gone their way and the wind is speaking a hundred things - where the ship has gone and who it brings, forgotten blue against the dusk, a ring is left - as if to rust. * A not-so-well-known fact about twenty-seven year old Ellis Nash: She still doesn't know what being in love is. She does, however, know what it's like to be loved. It makes her feel ungrateful. Something that she's not sure about: Love makes you do things that you don't have to for people, things that they can live without; things they might not deserve. Something that she knows: She loves Adam. Something else that she knows: Loving someone is still not the same as being in love. Close enough, but it doesn't equate to being able to work things out. In the end. If there's anything you have learned from Adam, it's that the easier love just isn't the real thing and that's what keeps you coming back. You've also learned, though, from everybody, that sometimes the real thing isn't worth it. It being everything else. -- and then you look in a cracked mirror, disheveled from overrun obsequies carried out in the careless desert, bodies buried deep away from the pariah dogs and women and in the smokey glass - Bookmarked on page 373 with a receipt from Dollar General for a bottle of detergent and pack of pens: 'For here was the novelty, the difference between the love that Sammy had felt of Tesla and Garfield and even for Joe Kavalier, and that which he felt for Tracy Bacon: it really did seem to be reciprocated.' you see that your own eyes have turned to leather. * There are certain principles that you act by. Not live by, but act by, because you are one of those people who are certain that life is the collection and compaction of all the events you've gone through and nothing more. Dates. Accomplishments. Places. People. There's no deeper meaning unless you make it for yourself. You have a list of beliefs in your head, neatly spelled out on a piece of lined paper, and it might, it could, look a little like this: 1. If you're going to do something, do it right. 2. There's no reason to stop trying. If you know you can do it, that is. Futile attempts are pointless. 3. Discipline. 4. You're never who you think you are. Everybody needs boundaries, that's the only way you'll ever figure yourself out, at least for the time being. And, lastly: 5. Bending the rules is never a bad idea. Holding the bright blue envelope in your hand, these things have become more than thoughts, now, they're almost feelings, rushing headily away from your heart and into your limbs. They're beyond behaviors acquired through conditioning, you think, they're something hereditary, bred into double helixes. Dominant genes and sequence codes, this is how you succeed. You operate through a suspension of belief, day in and day out, seasons melting into each other. Nobody ever pins down your ambition with 'you can't's, so you can, and you do. And you will. And as you walk through the double doors of Cortland's PD, your fingernails dig into the envelope. oh think again, don't give in this isn't what you want feelings change, morning brings rain this isn't what you want 'think again' -- teddy thompson
