Pictures From a Dream, I: Underlying Beauty

15 August 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: August 152006 Around the time I decided to go out on my own, I realized exactly how landlocked my life was. I had never seen the ocean, and even though I had always felt a slight aversion towards water, I felt that something as unimaginably vast and grand as the ocean was something that held a certain allure to a boy my age. In similar effect, the ocean called the dying back- those on their last legs with nowhere else to go and nobody else to see. As I looked back at the large, unkempt building, a sack of bare essentials slung over my shoulder, the notion that I’d never return was overshadowed by wonder as I numbered the years until the ocean would call me back. The former notion, for several reasons, was a certainty I had easily accepted. The latter was far more troubling, not because of the uncertainty, but unsettling for the collection of subtle hints I had been piecing together for quite some time. The house was brown, but not the natural wood color it might have once been. It was brown like a lung, not yet blackened, but a dusty stale from strained breathing. The owner of the house was a man who went by the name of Mr. Bismark Selig. It was by no means his real name- in fact, I’d be damned if I could remember the name I first knew him by- but I called him Mr. Bismark Selig too because I couldn’t call him dad without choking to death on my own voice. Every few years or so, Bismark would pack up his belongings and his kids and find somewhere else to go. He’d get sick of the town or the town would get sick of him or the house’s roof would finally cave in or, as was his personal favorite reason to up and leave, “the land’s all dug up.” Bismark was a lazy man, always looking for an easy way to sate his greed. He had no real professional trade, but he did have a bunch of shovels. So, he’d settle in on a place and then he’d dig, and he’d make us all dig, too. Rumor has it, he found quite a bit of gold once way back before I can remember, and that’s how we’d all been getting by, but we hadn’t found anything since, and I was sure of that because I had grown accustomed to the digging a long time ago, and I was sure about the gold because I had just found the last few pieces of it stashed away in an old cookie tin tucked above a loose ceiling panel. In fact, this coincidental discovery was the whole reason I was leaving. I couldn’t have left if I hadn’t found the last few gold pieces. Now, I can’t say I wasn’t looking for the gold because we all were really sniffing around for it in the back of our heads all the time, but I stumbled upon it while setting traps in the basement. Bismark was having fits all day that day over the “goddamned ferrets getting’ into his bathtub gin.” I doubted that they were actually ferrets, but there was some kind of vermin crawling around down there. I could tell because there were piles of shit building up along the edges of the walls, and the tile around the actual bathtub full of homemade gin in the basement bathroom was warping from drips and splashes. Anyway, I saw that one of the ceiling panels was shifted a bit, and I figured maybe that pests had a nest up there, so I stood on a wobbling chair with two legs shorter than the other two, and I reached my arm around. That’s how I found the cookie tin that Bismark had stashed up there. When I opened the tin and found the handful of gold nuggets, I immediately knew I’d have to come back for them after everyone else was asleep. If Bismark knew I’d found it, he wouldn’t hesitate to put a neat, little hole in my forehead and a much larger, gaping hole on the other side- an opening for my exploded brains to easily drip out all over the walls and floors. At least it’d be a formidable excuse to clean the place up a bit. It was an anxious few hours of avoiding Bismark until he passed out on the bathroom floor with a belly full of gin, and nothing would wake him now if his own snoring didn’t. Still, I’d flinch whenever he started mumbling unconsciously. It was all semi-coherent babble about how “if he ever found that bitch, he’d strangle her with his own two hands.” I carefully crept down the sides of the stairs, dragging a sack full of my belongings behind me, spare clothes acting as a silencer as they rounded the sides of the bag, sheltering anything that would make a sound against the stairwell. I slowly reached around, finding the tin just as I had left it, and I excavated it as if it were some holy grail before burying it in the sack underneath a moth eaten t-shirt and a pair of dirty trousers. My older sister Melanie was out working. She danced naked at a strip club, and I absolutely hated her for it. I hated that she made enough money to stay far enough away from the house for as long as she wanted. I loathed her because of it. Of course, she had to come back to take care of Jennifer. Jennifer was only eight or nine or something. I couldn’t quite remember, but I hated her for that anyway just because Jenny was too young to have to dig, and by the time she’d be old enough, Bismark’s liver will have most likely given up on him. And I also hated Melanie because every time she’d stop by, she’d bring back some new douche bag with the same ugly haircut and the same obnoxious jean jacket. And if the jackass brought a case of something, Bismark would tolerate the douche enough to get drunk with him. This would give the asshole the idea that he could have some sort of assistant managerial command over Michael and me, and Bismark wouldn’t exactly rectify this, but it’d sure remind him that he had to yell at us about something or other. Michael’s room was in the basement, too. He was only a couple of years younger than me, but the dumb shit couldn’t stop finding ways to piss Bismark off. Michael would get this funny look on his face every time he slipped up. It was as if he couldn’t help it; a half a second quicker, and Michael would have caught himself, but timing was never Michael’s thing, and then Bismark would slap him around for a good while until he had “learned him a thing or two” or until Bismark just got sick of whipping on him. I dragged my sack over to Michael’s door and shook my head. If Bismark knew the door was locked, he’d take the whole door off by the hinges, give Michael a stiff beating, and it’d be some time before Michael would be able to appreciate having back the door he so ignorantly took for granted. I could hear his girlfriend rustling a mixture of his and her clothes back onto her limbs like some fair skinned coat rack before trying to slip out the small rectangular window over Michael’s mattress that hung just above ground level. I laughed to myself at their unsurprising sexual stealth. They thought they were some kind of clever fuck ninjas or something. In truth, I only knew that he’d been sneaking Nikki in and out of his room because he came clean to me one night when he needed to borrow some condoms. I suppose though, that it was all awkward, teenage sex- easy to keep quiet because it was as scary as it was exciting, and half the excitement came from the possibility of detection. Michael opened the door wearing only his boxers. “Shit, Johnny. I thought you were Bismark.” “Bismark’s already sprawled out on the bathroom floor,” I assured my younger brother, stepping into his room and finding a seat on an overturned crate of dirty laundry. “You know he’ll tear you a new asshole if he ever finds your door locked.” “Yeah, I know,” he stated with no intent to defend himself. We both knew how irrational and paranoid he could get. Bismark always thought someone was after him. I didn’t blame him. He’d made a lot of enemies. Michael quickly changed the subject, his eyes pointed at my makeshift luggage. “What’s in the bag?” I looked down at it like even I was surprised to see it at my feet. “Just some stuff.” I brushed past his underlying curiosity, confirming his suspicions. “I’m leaving tonight.” “So you found Bismark’s stash?” The dumb shit was too smart for his own good sometimes. “No,” I obviously lied, staring at a spider crawling alongside a crack in the wall. I could have easily come clean and split the gold with Michael. He would have been good company, but I doubt he would have come along without Nikki, and I didn’t want her coming with- something about odd numbers and superstitions. Besides, a woman- especially one that was having sex with Michael- would complicate things too much. It’d get all three of us killed. Truth be told, I wasn’t too much different from old Bismark, and I wanted all the gold for myself anyway. “So I guess I’ll see you around, then,” he said, offering the closest thing to a proper goodbye that his adolescent brain could offer. His tone poked sticks at my lie, but it never blamed me for lying. “Yeah, I guess so,” I reflected, the few years I had on him failing to provide me any extra wisdom as I got up to leave. “Johnny,” his voice froze me in his doorway. “Where are you going? You know, so when I finally get out of here, I can like, find you or something.” I kept looking out from his doorway; afraid I’d end up taking him along if I looked at him directly. “The ocean,” I said bluntly. “I’m going to the ocean.” “Okay.” I could see him nodding out of my peripheral, nodding as if it would make sense as long as he pretended it did. And then after a brief hesitance, I took off, back up the stairs and out the door. I walked fifteen miles or so before daybreak to the county seat. It wasn’t a big town or a town worth remembering by any means, but it had more stores than the rest of the lonely villages scattered about the countryside. One of them took the stones off of me for fifty bucks on top of a cool grand. It was the most money I had seen in one place in my entire life; or at least it felt like it. It never felt like it, though, before because it was always somebody else’s money, and I never much gave a damn about other peoples’ money. I split the money up damn near evenly with five hundred in my left sock and five fifty in my right. I figured this way, I’d still have one chunk of money if the other one got lost or stolen, and it’d take a real wizard to steal both my socks from right off my feet. And then, with Franklins tickling my ankles, I dragged all I had over to the train station, following the tracks for a quarter mile before I found the only stop in town. “How much is it to get to the ocean?” I asked, folding my elbows, tired from all that dragging (though not that tired on account of being used to hours on end of digging), on the counter. “Which one? Atlantic or Pacific?” replied the disinterested gentleman in the booth as he barely looked up at me over his morning paper. “Either one will do,” I supposed. “I’ll take whichever one’s cheaper.” He sat up straight with a grunt and pecked away at some keys, his eyes scanning the outdated machine’s screen in front of him. “It’ll be two hundred ten dollars to Philadelphia. That’ll get you near enough to the Atlantic.” I bent down and pulled two hundred fifty out of my right sock. The man behind the counter printed me up a one-way ticket and counted out two twenty-dollar bills for change. I knelt down again, exchanging one of the twenty-dollar bills into my left sock for a hundred-dollar bill. I put this hundred, along with the remaining twenty, in my right sock; leaving me with four hundred twenty dollars in each sock. As the engine whirred to life, I glanced at the old man sitting to my right, staring at the back of the headrest on the seat in front of him. His eyes were carved out of delicate marbles, rolling around glassy in a skull that had long been a couple sizes too small for his inflated features. The drooping skin weighted the sides of his mouth into a dry, eternal frown. He could have lifted it if he tried, but frankly, it was no longer worth the effort. Looking at him made me uncomfortable. The clear lenses of his eyes bubbled out; about to burst from all the things he had seen unless he had someone to tell- to release some of that pressure building. I was a convenient stranger, reluctant, but too tactless to be unwilling. I could feel the tension building, burning through his retinas. I saw him clench his fists, green veins weaving past white knuckles. His Adam’s apple bent to a slow, salivated pulse as it bobbed forcedly with each heavy, conscious swallow. His voice leaked out through a loose seal; escaping breathily with a shaky congestion. “You on your way to the ocean?” he asked, having eyed my ticket, and then barring any response from me, he continued, “A boy your age should really see the ocean.” I shrugged a bit and shifted more towards the window, watching fields roll by until everything just balled into one striped mess of green-blue. Still, the old man kept talking towards the back of my head, his words pulling at my hairs and climbing uninvited into my ears. “When I was around your age, that’s when I went to see the ocean. Something about the water…” he sighed and trailed off, squinting his sky blue eye- sky blue on a cloudy day, “…something about the water left a profound imprint on me. I think seeing the ocean changed me a little bit.” And the more he recounted tales of how the ocean had healed his moral toxicities, the less I wanted to see the ocean. I wondered, too, if the ocean was calling him back. I wanted to go with him, then, so that I could show him how distorted his memory of the ocean was. Here I sat, having never been to the ocean before, and I thought I had a clearer image of the ocean than someone who had been there before. “The water was so blue, and the way the sun shone colorless white off of its surface…” The ocean that I would live to would be the ocean this man would die to. The ocean was always changing, aging even, and cycling over itself. But we would witness the same ocean, and he’d die pacified and I would have to live, looking out at the big, cold, heartless ocean. There was no doubt in my mind that there were plenty of things buried in the ocean, things Bismark would have liked to dig up if he had the means, but anchored down on the ocean floor, there was no beating heart, half entrenched in wet sand and seaweed. “I guess, for the first time, I saw the Artist’s signature hidden deep in the ocean. God fingerprinted the farthest corner of his masterpiece, and it made me weep.” I could smell the ocean salt of tears welling up in the reddened pockets of the man’s eyes. His breath trembled like a flag at mast in the wind; it somehow quivered. And I had to keep looking away to stop myself from laughing at him. Instead, I bit my lip and spoke up. “Are you going to the ocean?” “Heavens no. I’ve got too much to take care of before I ever go back, but I’ll go back one day.” He sighed nostalgically, and I thought we were through, but I was wrong. “So you never answered me. Are you going to the ocean or aren’t you?” “No,” I lied, mostly just to spite the bastard. “That’s a shame. You should really check it out soon.” And he didn’t say another word the whole ride. We became strangers again. He focused on a newspaper and read it as if we had never spoken. I still went to the ocean, just to see it. What the hell, I figured. I traveled all this way. Besides, I couldn’t go back, and I was halfway there by the time I got in that train. But the ocean didn’t impress me. Perhaps one day, some time ago, the ocean was awing and powerful. Perhaps it meant something. But now it was just water- water that seemed to extend on forever no matter how many globes pretentiously wagered there was something on the other side other than just an edge where everything stops or falls out. I really did look for God’s fingerprint or whatever, but I couldn’t see it. All I saw was my reflection, whirled like a colored piece in a kaleidoscope, and I wondered if this is what I would look like if the air ever rippled. I stepped in, the tide crashing against my shins, and I took off my wet socks and shoes, and I let them drift off to wherever. And then I turned away from the ocean, and it reminded me of the worn brown house out in the middle of nowhere; because the edge of nowhere isn’t all that different from the middle. It’s all pretty much inescapable when there’s nowhere worth escaping to, and you’re never in the clear until everything is absolutely still. And for a while it felt still, but I knew somewhere on the other side of the globe, there were some socks and shoes floating. And somewhere in the middle of the country, there was a man digging holes, all of them disappointedly empty except for the ones he had dug for himself and his family. Though there was still work to be done before I would return to see if they had thought of me and left one empty.
