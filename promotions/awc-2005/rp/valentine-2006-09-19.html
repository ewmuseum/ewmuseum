Valentine

19 September 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 19/09/2006 I met him in the spring and will lose him in the winter. This, you'll say, is a cliche; still, the only way I know how to begin a story is at the beginning. Then again, we might begin at his birth, or mine; we might begin at the beginning of the world. There was no spring in the first days of the universe; there were only protons and neutrons and gradually atoms, spinning and flattening into galaxies, generating the energy of stars. So here is the planet Earth, small and hot and empty, like a heart, closed and burning. Then, billions of years ago, it began to rain. This, then, for a beginning: a planet of wind and water, voiceless and warm and my heart, naked and unsuspecting. He slammed into me like a meteorite and billions of years later, lightning created something new. Scientists call it a mystery; those who know no better call it a miracle. A miracle, then. I don't believe in them, but I do believe in Brody. I met him in the spring. Brody. You are the wind that tosses the rain, the first uncurling daffodil, the light of falling stars. The mirror-places where I see myself and life begins. We met in a park. It was spring and I was sulking, throwing bread at ducks more out of spite than kindness. He caught me by surprise, at my side before I registered his presence. He had hair like a cloud. I sensed something about him; I didn't dare look at his eyes. “Tell me something,” he said. “I hate the way they pop out of the water like that, those ducks,” I said. He nodded appreciatively, as if filing this away for deeper contemplation. He took the bread from me and cast it into the pond, where it plummeted. Then he said, “Tell me something else.” I thought for a second. “That energy is lost and things fall apart, never to reunite, is an inescapable fact of nature,” I finally answered. “I read that in a book today.” “Oh,” he said, carelessly, “nature.” I was annoyed that my second fact had been taken so lightly. “It's true,” I insisted. “It's a law of the universe. Ever since its birth, we've been moving away from each other, faster and faster. While we're standing here on this bridge, it's happening.” He put his hand on my arm, then, and something flipped inside me like a jumping fish. “In that case,” he said, “things coming together is the real miracle, don't you think?” In a fairy tale, I would have leaned forward and kissed him. As it was, I scoffed; here I was, watching a pond of milling ducks searching for a lost hunk of bread, with a stranger's hand on my arm, talking about miracles. “I don't know what I think.” “Don't you believe in miracles?” I shrugged, displacing his hand. “Not really.” He laughed at me. “Love is a miracle,” he said. “I believe that.” Brody scares me, his sharp-toed shoes, his full mouth. I met him again in the courthouse where he worked. Women asked him every day to marry them. Most of the time, he said yes. They left hand in hand with their partners and he stayed smiling because he had been witness to love. All this Brody tells me, fingers curling around mine under the table of a tiny cafe. “I'm the patron saint of love,” he says. “No, you aren't. He lived hundreds of years ago.” “At least you know your stories.” His smile flashes like lightning. I can feel my heart rotating in my chest. He likes love poems, Brody does. He recites them when we fuck, though he always calls it making love; he likes the idea, he claims, of filling the world with it. “If we don't make it,” he says, “who will?” “I don't believe in love,” I say. What use is love to me? Love has an ending; everything does. “Even stars,” I say, “die. Earth itself has a final day, eight billion years or so from now.” “You're looking at it all wrong,” Brody says. “We aren't here to die. Tell me about the beginning of life.” So I tell him, my cheek against his collarbone, his breath nesting in my hair. He moves his fingertips up and down my arm while I talk about lipids and ribozymes and prokaryotic life. “Simple bacteria,' I say. “They've been around for billions of years.” Brody wraps his legs around mine. “The minute there were two of them,” he says, “two of these primal bacteria, whatever they are. It opened up an entire world.” I shake my head, still pillowed on his chest. “They didn't need anyone else,” I correct him. “They could reproduce by themselves.” He kisses me. “That's not the point, Dex.” It was August when I loved him, tawny August, ripe and decaying. He had been coaxing words for weeks, half-admissions, sudden showers of light. “I love you,” I said. My arm had fallen asleep under his body and I couldn't tell where each of us began. He had hit me like a meteorite and tilted my axis. He had changed the force of my spinning heart. He had moved me, gravity and all. Brody said, “You're learning.” I love him best in sunlight, the source of all energy, choking up the room, limbs entangled and the sun rising, casting shadows across the wall. I like his cheekbones, the angles of his wrists. Brody, I am weak for your sharpness, all the places that you bend. The tilt of your knees is deadly. The corner of your smile is worse. I love you best in sunlight, so I can see all the places that you're wounding me. When there was is rain, there is no softness; the seas wear down our planet's rough places. I am soft and you mountainous. My heart is blue and green and spinning. What do you call this, Saint Brody? I know my stories because I know my books. The day after I had met Brody, I looked up Saint Valentine. He married the soldiers forbidden to marry. He broke the law for love. I imagined him in a jail cell, his hands clasped around his own love, blind jailer's daughter that she was. “Love is a miracle,” I heard him say. “I believe that.” Poor jailer's daughter, in love with a voice, the gravity of a few words and two hands in hers. What could love do for her? What did it mean? Maybe she put her hand on his chest the way Brody did me, her palm catching the sound of his heart. Flesh and blood, mortal things. What could be miraculous there? He left her a note. How shocked she must have been to read it with her own vision, poor girl, palm clenched into a fist as if to replicate the heartbeat that had been trapped there. Was love the miracle or was he? Saint Valentine died and opened his lover's eyes. Brody was opening mine. How long can this last? Eight billion years if we let it. “Will you marry me, Mister Jersch?” A thousand women had asked the same question. He had told me about them, lining up at the courthouse, hearts in their mouths. He looked sideways at the couples. Always. New worlds. When I kill him, he will have shadows in his eyes. I will think of them forever as the shadows of death. Death, loss, betrayal - to a lover, what could be the difference? Four days later, at his memorial service, a box as his picture frame and his head shaved with hair not like a cloud, the answer will come to me. Hope. Hope, a fickle thing, creator and destroyer. Where would we be without it? There it is in our history, that bare, spinning earth, full of potential. Dry earth, open basins without water, an atmosphere without oxygen. Waiting. In eight billion years, we'll be gone. But some things will move out into space, faster and faster. Remaining particles of this round, unsuspecting planet, this now-pumping heart. All the best miracles are about transformation. Water into wine, one fish into many. The blind seeing, the world awakening. After winter, spring comes again. People get married. Things fall apart; things come together. Love can be miraculous, however short-lived, however explosive or anticlimactic its ending. The death is not important; the living is. I will learn all of this from Brody. Winter is coming. The sun rises earlier every day. It chases shadows across my walls and I think of energy and emotion. The trees are bare and blistering. Every day, I will wake up wishing I did not have to kill Brody. But the rain will come. I have faith in that. Welcome to our world.
