Faults+Regrets=Redemption

6 March 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 3-6-06 In the life I once lived in, it never occurred to me what the damaging effects it would have on me, up until now. I had occasions where I remembered something from my past, for example the death of my brother, Thomas, the beatings I had receive from my father, and the inadvertent death of my best friend, Ian McLeod. I would remember frequent flashes of those things in rapid detail. Some of the images come randomly, jumble themselves together and sometimes create a brief, broken slideshow that made no sense whatsoever. I would have these images daily. They didn’t bother me much; they dampened my mood, but they didn’t bother me. I would go on with my daily activities without any problems. They would come and go, but nothing too severe that would interfere with my daily life. Everything was going fine for me... Everything went fine up until early last week. Last week, the images were becoming more vivid in detail. I could hear the sounds of screaming, feeling pain in my head. It was interfering with my daily life; I couldn’t go through my regiments without some sort of attack from these images, these sounds and the pain that plagued my head. I would have momentary episodes of “snapping” I guess was the best word to describe it. I would clutch my head in my hands, pound my head with my fists and break down crying, pleading with whatever was going on to stop and get out of my head. It even disturbed me in my sleep. Finally, after suffering from the mental anguish, I set up an appointment with my doctor. I told him about the problems that I just mention previously. He listened with great interest and taking notes as I told him everything. He didn’t say anything until I was done. When I was done talking, he looked over his notes, pondered the possibilities I may have then nodded. He turned to face me, his face hardened into a state of seriousness and professionalism. “Sounds to me that you have PTSD, or Post Traumatic Stress Disorder,” Dr. Timothy Kennedy said. “It’s something that many war veterans go through, only back then it had many names, like shellshock, battle fatigue, etc. In your case, you’re reliving events that happened in your life that were too traumatic, too frightening for your mind to bear.” I thought about what the doctor had said. Post Traumatic Stress Disorder? It sounded serious, but I’d never heard of it before. I’ve seen reference about it in movies and thought it was a made-up disorder to give war movies a realistic feel to it. After hearing what the doctor said, that proved me wrong. “Will this continue to effect me forever?” I inquired the doctor. Tim Kennedy nodded. “It will if you don’t do anything about it. But I can’t give you a complete diagnose after what you’ve told me. Tell you what,” he said, as he was leaving his office. “I’m going to give you a test on PTSD. It’s a simple questionnaire for those who may think they’re suffering from it. When you’re done, I’ll look through it and give you a proper diagnosis. Hang on for a moment.” He left his office. Less than five minutes later, he returned to his office, knocking respectfully before he entered. In his hands were a small booklet of fifty test questions and a print-out of Post Traumatic Stress Disorder. “The questionnaire is pretty simple. All you have to do is circle ‘YES’ or ‘NO’ for the answer. If you score high on the symptoms you’re sustaining, then you have PTSD. “This is a print-out for the disorder. Feel free to highlight any of the symptoms here as well. You don’t have to though; that’s up to you. I’ll leave you to your work. I have some work to do anyhow. Let me know when you’re done.” He left his office once again. Before I took the questionnaire, I looked through the print-out first to have a better understanding of what this disorder was. I highlighted the symptoms I was feeling: disturbance in sleep, profound depression, reoccurrences of injury or other traumatic memories, etc. Now that I have a better understanding of what this disorder was, I took the test. The questions were pretty cut in dry, for example: “Have you ever felt these symptoms for more than a month?” I circled “YES.” I must’ve been feeling these symptoms for the better part of twenty years. About an hour later, I finished up the test and handed to the Dr. Kennedy. He smiled at me and told me to wait in his office. Over twenty minutes had passed before Dr. Kennedy reentered his office. He looked at me for a moment, then closed the door behind him. “It’s official,” he said, as the door clicked shut. “You have Post Traumatic Stress Disorder.” He was silent for a few seconds as he sat behind his desk. As he sat down, he looked at me, as if giving me permission to say whatever was on my mind. I took the initiative. “Okay, since I have this disorder, what are my options?” “To treat PTSD, you’ll need medication and some sort of therapy. Group therapy has proven to be the best. Knowing you for as long as I have, you won’t do it. The medication, yes, the group therapy no. A recommendation for medication is Paxil, which is proven to help with depression and PTSD. It’s FDA approved to do so. Even though it helps with young men, I think being thirty-three should categorize as ‘young.’” He chuckled at this. He liked to laugh at my own expenses. “Anyway,” he resumed, “Paxil will take about two weeks to load into your system. The only side effects I’ve heard of and seen are headaches, bouts of nausea and some weight gain, as much as five to ten pounds, which can be easily taken off by a steady diet and exercise.” He took out a prescription pad, scribbled on it and tore off the slip of paper. “That should keep you covered for awhile.” I took the slip, looked at for a moment, then nodded to the doctor and shook his head. “Just one question before I go.” “Shoot.” “Since you know I won’t go into group therapy, what would you be your best recommendation if Paxil isn’t goin’ to be enough for me?” “Well, as a doctor, I would say it wouldn’t kill you to talk to someone about your problems. But as a friend, my best I would say this - go find your father. Talk to him. Tell him how you feel. When you’re done with that, and you can’t find Ian McLeod’s family, talk to your boxing promoter. That would be a good way to remedy things there and alleviate stress, because I bet his training regiments were stressful.” I nodded and let out a laugh. “Aye, indeed they were. There were times where he almost worked me to death. I hardly moved after the training regiments I went through. I would have to be literally carried back to my home.” I stood up, nodding to my doctor. “When to ya want to see me next?” “Hmm...” He trailed off a bit, looking through his calendar. “A couple of weeks should suffice, get the prescription business out of the way. Come back in a couple of weeks and we’ll go from there. Have a good one, Mike.” “Aye you, too. Thanks for seein’ me.” “Not a problem, Mike. It’s always good to see you. See you in two weeks.” I left his office and left the clinic. I took Tim Kennedy’s advice under consideration for a few moments as I walked to my car and decided to accept it. Hell, it’s been over twenty years since I’ve seen my father, and I’ve been meaning to talk to him about how I felt but I kept putting it off. Now with my newly diagnosed Post Traumatic Stress Disorder, I can’t put it off anymore. I know what I must do. The next day, I told Zsasz what my plans were. He seemed reluctant to understand why I was going to Belfast, Ireland, to confront my father and a man who could be possibly dead, Samuel Hagen, but after I told him what had happened, he understood why I was doing this, and volunteered to drive me to the airport. About halfway to the airport, he turned to ask me a question, which had a lack of concern in it: “Are you sure you want to do this? We can turn around and I’ll take you home.” I nodded. “Aye, Jack, I can’t put this off any longer. I’ve been waiting twenty years to tell Shamus off and this is a good time to do it.” Zsasz was silent, keeping his eyes on the road. He didn’t seem to hear, or he didn’t care what I just said. “I mean,” I continued, “if you had the chance to confront your parents to tell them how you would feel, you’d do it, right?” Zsasz nodded, looking over at me with his peripherals. “Indeed I would, but I no longer have any parents to confront. Died in a car accident. You know it is.” He then changed the subject. “You will be back before the seventh, won’t you?” I looked at Zsasz quizzically, raising my right eyebrow at him. “What happens on the seventh?” “We have a pressing engagement for FRESH! this week, or have you forgotten? It’s a requirement for a roster of a major wrestling promotion to be in attendance, that includes new hired talent.” “Oh right. I won’t be gone that long. I’ll be back in a few days. After rippin’ Shamus a new asshole and talking to Sam, I’m gonna visit some friends as well.” With that said, we arrived at the airport. Zsasz looked over at me and smiled at me. Whether the smile was genuine or not, I couldn’t tell. Perhaps it didn’t matter. “Have a good flight and be careful. I really don’t want to fly out to Belfast and pick up your dead carcass because you pissed the wrong people off.” I let out a chuckle, patting a large shoulder of his. “Not to worry. It won’t be like that.” I got out of the car, got my luggage from the backseat and saluted Zsasz as he drove off. I looked at the airport, let out a deep sigh and went inside. It was early morning in Belfast, around seven o’clock, I believe, by the time the plane landed. When I got off the plane, all those memories of my childhood flooded into my head. Memories of gotten beatings from my father, calling me a “worthless lil’ shoit” in the process. Those were the times when I was roughly six years old. I gripped my head, as the memory in my head came into play. I started to remember the traumatic loss of my brother... It was the fall of 1979. I was six years old and my brother, Thomas was about to turn twelve. During that time, my oldest siblings, William and Vanessa had moved out of the house and started their own lives. Mom and Dad were out working late and wouldn’t be home until eight o’clock or so, so it was up to the four of us - Thomas, Barbara, Tammy and myself - to figure something out for dinner. Thomas had made the announcement that he was going to the deli to pick up some sandwiches for everyone to eat. I wanted to go with him and he didn’t seem to mind. We got our coats, told our sisters that we were leaving and went into the pouring rain outside. It took about five minutes to get our order in and to pick it up. We left the deli and made our way home. About halfway there, I noticed some movement to the right of me. I turned to see what it was and there was nothing. It must’ve been my imagination playing a trick on me. I was hoping my mind was playing a trick on me. Thomas noticed the worried look on my face. “Are ye okay, Mike?” I looked up at my older brother and nodded. “Yeah, I’m okay,” I said, even though I didn’t believe the conviction in my voice. “Thought I saw somethin’. Some sort o’ movement. I’m not exactly sure.” “Did ye see somethin’ or not?” I looked over to the my right again, examining area through the pouring rain. I shook my head. “No. I-I guess not.” We were no more than a quarter of the way from home until a large figure stepped in front of us, holding some for of pistol in his hand. He appeared to be a teenager, probably eighteen years of age. The hand that held the pistol was shaking. Was it because he was cold? He was nervous? I couldn’t tell. All I knew was that my brother and I were in trouble. “Alroigh,” the teenager said. “Gimme all yer money. Don’t try any phony stuff” I thought I was going to die. I thought we were going to die. With cooler heads prevailing, I heard my brother say: “Okay, take ‘er easy, sir. I don’na have any money on me. Just the food in this bag.” The teen looked at the bag, then at Thomas, then at me. “Gimme the bag o’ food,” the teen said, his eyes remained on me. Thomas slowly extended his arm, about to give the teen the bag of deli sandwiches until he shook his head. “No. Let ‘em give it t’ me.” He was referring to me. Thomas and I looked at each other, then at the armed teenager. Reluctantly, Thomas handed me the bag. “Just slowly give it t’ ’em, I have a plan.” I wasn’t sure what kind of plan he had, but I didn’t want to reveal it in my eyes. Hell, the only thing I could reveal was fear. I was scared to death. I slowly handed the deli bag to the armed teenager. He reached out for it and from the corner of my eye, I saw my brother make a dash at the armed teen. My eyes were as big as saucers when I saw the teen pointing the gun at Thomas, about to pull the trigger. “Wait, do---” It was too late. The teen pulled the trigger and an explosion rang in the air. The next thing I saw was Thomas dropping onto his knees, his hands clutching at his chest as the armed teenager ran away. “Thomas?” I dropped the deli bag and ran over to my brother. He’d fallen backward, coughing up blood. The wound in his chest was spurting blood. “Thomas? Thomas!” I grabbed a hold of him. “Thomas? Speak to me.” His eyes rolled over to meet mine. “I...can hear ye...Mike.” He coughed a few times, in a manner in a overly dramatic scene in a movie. “I...got shot...” My eyes began to brim with tears. I could see the pain in his eyes. I could see that he knew he was going to die. “Aye, indeed ye got shot, but yer gonna be just foine. I’ll get our parents an’ we’ll get ye to a hospital.” Thomas slowly shook his head. “Nah... I won’ be able t’ make it. Things’re goin’ black. I...I...” He slowly trailed off as he died in my arms. I started to cry uncontrollably, whimpering loudly and screaming. I tried shaking Thomas, trying to bring him back. I knew that he was dead, but I thought if I tried hard enough, I might be able to bring him back and get him to a hospital. It didn’t come. Thomas remained limp in my arms, his eyes looking back at me. I buried my face into his chest and wept as the rain continued to fall on me. I walked home after leaving my brother on the muddy ground. I stumbled in the house, crying uncontrollably, covered in mud, blood and sopping wet. Both Mom and Dad were there, and Mom was the first one to see me. “Michael? Michael, what’s wrong? Where’s your brother?” “He...he’s...dead,” I said. “He’s dead. Somebody shot him! Everythin’ happened so fast.” Shock went through her body. After hearing that her middle son was dead, she ran out of the house and went out into the pouring ring, calling for Thomas’ name. Less than a minute later, I heard a scream emanating from my mother, then loud crying from her, screaming Thomas’ name. I felt my father’s eyes burning a hole into me. He cocked his fist back and blasted me right in the face, knocking me onto the floor. I looked up at him with shock and surprise. Then he got on top of me,. bashing my face in with his fist. “Ye killed him!” he roared at me. “Ye killed yer brother, an’ it’s all yer fault!” After beating me up, he got off me, and left the house. I heard my sisters crying. They were standing over me, their eyes looking down at me accusingly. Then they disappeared from the house, wanting to see their brother for one last time. I lay on the floor, all alone, as pain throbbed from my face. Was it possible that it was my fault? Was it my fault that my brother died because of me? But that didn’t make any sense. But my father’s words continue to ring in my head, perhaps it was my fault. If I hadn’t gone out with Thomas, he wouldn’t have died. Then it was my fault. It was my fault. It was my regret. I was in a bad mood after reliving that day twenty-seven years ago. After looking back on it, it wasn’t my fault, but when I was six years old, I felt I was the blame for the death of my brother. I didn’t understand why I was beaten so badly by father; I didn’t do anything wrong to receive such an outlandish onslaught of fists. I was cruising through the countryside of Belfast, the place where I was born and raised. Nothing really changed when I was a little boy. There were a few new housing areas, but other than that, nothing changed. Being back in Belfast for the first time in six years made me feel like I’m home again. But that feeling had changed when I focused at the task at hand. I pulled up to the my childhood home - a twelve hundred square-foot, three bedroom house that once housed a family of eight. Shamus built this house with his bare hands and once had a dreamed to fill the house with a loving wife and a few kids. He had fulfilled that dream by marrying Wendy Cavanaugh and had six children with her. But the only problem was the house wasn’t exactly big enough to house eight people. I saw that my father’s beat-up pickup truck was parked in front of the garage. Good, he was home. It was now or never for me. Since I made this far, it was now. I got out of the rental car the airport provided for me and walked up to the front door of my old childhood home. I knocked on the door and waited. When there wasn’t a reply, I pounded the door with my fist. “Open up, Shamus, I know you’re in there,” I said. A few seconds later, I heard footsteps approaching the front door and opened it. An old man looked at me. He had short silver hair, brown eyes and had a wrinkled complexion. It was my father, Shamus, a man who’s in his seventies. He peered at me with narrow eyes. “Who the fok are ye?” he hissed. I looked down at him. I wanted to break his face with my fists, but I held that urge. I simply flashed a fake, sarcastic smile. “Hello, Shamus. It’s your son, Michael. You and I need to talk.” “I’ve got nothin’ t’ say t’ ye.” He was about to slam the door in my face, but I stopped him. My eyes were smoldering with anger. “You may not have nothin’ to say to me, but I have something to say to you. I won’t take up much of your time. Would you mind letting me in?” Shamus stared at me for a minute. He knew that I wasn’t going anywhere; I could see it in his eyes. He let out a sigh, then rolled his eyes. “Alroigh foine.” He stepped back to let me in. I took a step inside. Much like the countryside of Belfast, this house hadn’t changed since my childhood. The only difference now that it was eerily empty. Shamus was the only one who lived in this house. About two years after Thomas’ murder - no one caught the killer, by the way. Police didn’t have any leads to find the criminal - Wendy Cavanaugh had killed herself. Shamus found her naked in the bathroom in with her wrists slit and with a bag over her head. She could no longer bear the loss of Thomas, so she took her own life. When Shamus told everyone in the family that their mother had passed, his eyes remained on me. Those eyes burned me, revealing their accusations. Oh course, my father had blamed me for my mother’s suicide. “Want a drink?” I heard Shamus say, breaking me from my reverie. I looked at him for a moment, then nodded my head. I sat down at the table when he opened the fridge to pull out a couple of beers. He set one in front of me and sat down on the other side of the table, opened the tab and took a swig. He looked over at me with worn, bloodshot eyes. “So,” he began, carefully selecting his words. He knew that if he would say the wrong thing, an altercation would happen, and an altercation would fuck up his day. “What do you want to talk about?” “I want to talk about why you were such a fuckin’ asshole towards me.” He looked at me, raising his eyebrows. “I beg yer pardon?” My eye narrowed. “Don’t play dumb with me, Shamus. Ever since I was little boy, you treated me like I was your whippin’ boy, beating me up, berating me, belittling me and always blamed me for things I didn’t do. It wasn’t my fault that Thomas died and it wasn’t my fault that Mom died. Why did you treat me the way that you did? What the fuck with I ever do to you?!” Shamus remained silent for a long time. It seemed to me that he wasn’t listening to anything I was saying. He was trying to tune me out and I quickly picked up on it. “Don’t tune me out and answer me, you pathetic piece of shit!” Those words hurt him, I could see it in his eyes. I knew he could no longer bully me. He was too old to bully me anymore. He let out a sigh, then looked at me. “I don’t have any excuses fer th’ things I did t’ ye,” he began, his eyes remained locked on mine. “I know tha’ I was hard on ye, an’ I shouldn’t’ve been.” “Bein’ hard on me is an understatement,” I said, staring at my father, holding my arms across my chest. “Alroigh, I treated you horribly. I beat you up and did all sorts of mean things to you. I know saying that I’m sorry won’t mean anything, but I am sorry.” I blinked at him. This man in front of me didn’t reflect the man I once knew. He’d changed and changed for the weak. Perhaps not having Mom around nearly drained him of all life. “I’ll except your apology, but all I want to know is why? Why did you do those things to me?” He took a swig of his beer, trying to figure out what to say to his youngest son. “Like I said, I have no excuses fer th’ things I’d done t’ ye. I wasn’t th’ father ye wanted me t’ be, an’ I couldn’t show ye how t’ love ye.” “So you beat me up.” My eyes were fiery, staring at my father. Shamus slowly blinked, nodding his head. “Aye.” “Blaming me for the deaths of Thomas and Mom weren’t ways to show me love.” Shamus shook his head. “No, it doesn’t. I wish I could start all over by treatin’ ye better, treat ye like any lovin’ father would.” His chin was quivering, his eyes were brimming with tears. “I’m really sorry, Michael, I really am.” He started to cry. His tears were completely genuine. I felt sorry for the man in front of me. When I came out here, I wanted to bludgeon him with my fists, pay him back for all the beatings he’d given me. But that feeling had passed; after witnessing my father breaking down and crying in front of me, I felt pity for the man. I took one of his hands into mine. He stopped crying and looked at me through tear-bleary eyes. “You can, Dad,” I whispered, getting choked up myself. “We can start over and have the relationship we were meant to have. Would you like that, Dad?” Shamus nodded his head rapidly. “Aye. Aye, I would. Let’s start over...from the beginning...” We had a long, therapeutic with each other, starting over with everything that had transpired in our lives. We had our arguments with some things, but we took it quite well. We even had a few laughs together. For the first time in twenty years, I felt love for my father. In the summer of 1998, Ian McLeod and I agreed to have a boxing match against each other. It would be for a number one contender’s match for the bare-knuckle boxing title. We were both on top of the bare-knuckle boxing world, and we had something to prove to each other: which one of use were the better man? “Do ye think ye can defeat me, Mikey?” Ian asked before we had our match. I shrugged my shoulders. The thought never crossed my mind. “I dunno,” I replied honestly. “T’ be honest with ye, I think either of us could beat each other. I guess we’ll hafta wait an’ see.” It was time for our match. We both entered the ring separately and the bell rang. He charged at me like he did at our first sparring match. I mistimed my footing and took a barrage of shots right in the face that brought me down to the mat. I quickly got to my feet, shaking off the onslaught I’d previously felt. It completely caught me off guard more than it hurt. When I finally got into the swing of things, we had ourselves a battle. Every time I through some big hand combinations, Ian would do me up better. It was a battle of being one upped. Neither one of us could knock the other how. We bloodied either other up, but we couldn’t be denied. It continued like this for over a half-hour. We were both bloodied and bruised, and ready to collapse in the middle of the ring. We were virtually even in this match of pain. I stared at him with blurry eyes. My body was racked with pain. I didn’t know if I could continue any further against my best friend and equal boxing rival, Ian McLeod. I waited for him to make his move, but it didn’t come. I took it upon myself to take the initiative and walked towards him, keeping my guard up and was on alert for anything to happen. Ian took a step forward to throw a hard right hand. I blocked it with my left arm, stepped in and threw the hardest right hook I could muster, putting all my weight into it. I felt his jaw shatter against my fist... ....and I heard Ian’s neck snap after the vicious whiplash. He fell to the mat, twitching helplessly. I had realized what I’d done. “Ian?!” I dropped to my knees, holding him in my arms. I had flashbacks of holding Thomas’ dying body in my arms. It was the same way with Ian, but under different circumstances. “Ian, please don’ die! I beg of ye! DON’ DIE!” Ian stopped twitching... He slowly relaxed in my arms, laying limp in them. His eyes rolled in the back of his head. “No! Ian, please! Don’ do this t’ me!” I looked around the arena, trying to look for help. “Somebody help me, fer fok sakes! Somebody...” I started to cry. Much like the death of my brother, I buried my chest in Ian’s chest and I wept. A few moments later, EMT’s separated myself from Ian’s body. I watched them cover his body up and being taken out of the ring. It was all my fault. If I hadn’t agreed to fight Ian in a match, I wouldn’t have killed him. Since that day, I never wanted to return in bare-knuckle boxing. It was my fault. It was my regret... I let out a sigh, reflecting on that day. To this very day, no matter how much Samuel Hagen told me that it wasn’t my fault, I still feel responsible for his death. Even though it was an accident, I killed a man with my hands. People saw me as a murderer after that day, but I wasn’t that. It wasn’t my plan to kill Ian McLeod; my plan was to have the greatest match against my best friend and rival. I am not a murderer. “I am not a murderer,” I said, repeating my reflection. I turned to look at Samuel Hagen, who was no longer the man I used to know. Now at eight-six years old, he wasn’t the tough, hard-ass of a boxing promoter that he once was. Now, he’s a old, shriveled up old man, who lived a very long life. He still remained bald and he still had the worn, leather-like skin, but his demeanor was that of an elderly man. “I know yer not a murderer, Mikey,” he said in an ancient, raspy voice, 'yer not th’ one responsible fer Ian’s death. I’d seen several bare-knuckle boxers die in the ring by gettin’ beaten t’ death, havin’ their necks snapped from a fer-ferocious velocity of whiplash, or simply died of the pain they’re goin’ through. Hell, son, I killed a couple in me day, I even told ye tha’.” I nodded. “Aye, indeed you did.” I smiled at the old man. “Ya know, after telling you that story of Ian’s death, for the first time in my life, I don’t feel responsible for this death. For two years I did. And now, after fourteen years of beatin’ people up and winning eight titles, I retired from that profession an’ became a wrestler.” “Aye, I heard about tha’,” he said, smiling. “How’s that goin’ fer ye?” “It’s doin’ good,” I said, smiling back at my former mentor and boxing promoter. “Zsasz may not be a good manager, but he did get me into that Atlantic Wrestling Club wrestling promotion. Won me first match there.” I checked my watch. It was for me to do other errands. “Well, Samuel, it was good seeing you again. Take care of yourself, old man. Samuel cackles. “I’m doin’ th’ best I can with tha’. Come back another time, Mikey. I appreciate the visits.” I leaned in and gave him a hug. I still can’t believe that Samuel was the age that he was now. I guess time flies too quickly. As a result after what had happened during my time here in Belfast, I felt better about myself. I managed to confront my father after two decades of procrastination and found solace in Samuel Hagen. I know longer blame myself for everything that had happened, and I no longer feel bad for everything that had happened. It wasn’t my fault. It wasn’t my regret. It was my redemption.
