Date: November 14, 2006 five things logan curole misses about ellis nash 1. The clearly enunciated and practiced precision of her voice that nevertheless managed to be slow and soft and lazy in the right context and the solemn way her words dropped into the air, steady, with a certain sort of heaviness. It was a voice made for reading. 2. The way she always made coffee early in the morning, before work. It sounds like a stupid thing to miss about someone, something insignificant and meaningless out of all those fundamental things that make up a person, but it is what it is - Logan misses the line of her back, her arms, her quiet fingers, and the way she leaned against the kitchen table with a spoon in her mouth and newspaper in her hands. 3. Her tidiness. Logan is helplessly forgetful and slightly untidy, despite his best intentions, but Ellis always knew exactly where everything was, what time her appointments began, and the names of the maid’s nieces and nephews. 4. That awful habit she had of leaning on him everywhere, pressing their sides together, throwing an arm around his neck, leaning over his shoulder - the smell of her, crook of her elbow, the slight smile Logan could just catch from the corner of his eye before Ellis folded and tucked it away. 5. How she kissed. -- They’d talked about it once, only once, sitting across from each other at a cafe in a narrow alley. Ellis, fiddling absently with pink sugar packets, had looked at him over their glasses and asked, “What was it like?” And, God, it was stupid (because it wasn’t her fault, never had been, the world’s never been the way it should be) but all Logan could think about was the Nash family’s numerous vacation houses, the club memberships she’d had at the age of seven, how she could speak three languages and probably dressed in Dior as a ten year old. “Beautiful. Poor. Corrupt. What do you want to hear?” Ellis had flinched at that, a little, fingers pausing for a second, eyes glancing down. She still hadn’t learned, was still too fucking young, but she kept on going. “That’s not what I meant.” “So what did you mean?” Ellis poured the sugar into her coffee, stirred, took a sip. “I just, I just wanted--” she looked at Logan again, eyes a little angry, stubborn. “It’s not my fault.” A waiter refilled his glass of water. For a minute, the only sound was that of clinking ice cubes as Logan spun the glass around, leaving circles of perspiration on the heavy paper tablecloth. “I don’t remember much. Just the not-knowing, I guess. No one really knew. They sent us to the coast and I thought it was some sort of vacation. People didn’t start dying until much later, you know. Logan traced the words out with his finger all over the sleeping form beside him: I’m leaving, I’m leaving, I’m leaving, hoping that somehow the words would seep through the skin and into her bloodstream until the knowledge traveled to every corner and chamber of her body. Upon waking, Ellis asked him what he was doing, voice slightly disgruntled, tinged with the entitlement of youth, and Logan told her he was writing a story. “A story? What kind?” “A romance,” indulging in the drama (and masochistic tragedy) of 6 years spent together. Ellis absentmindedly tugged on a lock of Logan’s hair, curling it around her finger with a slow hypnotic rhythm before scrambling up gracelessly, all limbs and imbalance, to press her lips to Logan’s temples, her breath causing its own disaster over the delicate bones of his ear: “Cut your hair, baby. You’re turning into a girl.” In the months afterwards, Logan traced out the same words over and over while he tried to find the voice to draw his intricate pattern into reality. Ellis was oblivious as her body became bound in this net of words and disappointment, and choose instead to laugh at how it tickled just there and, while struggling to control her impatience and desire, petulantly demanded to know when this story will end. 3. “Let me see Metro when you’re done.” Ellis nodded but didn’t look up from the newspaper, too entranced with an article about grafitti and subways. If she had, she might have seen her fiance leaning back slowly in his chair, trying to surreptitiously fit his mouth softly, again and again, around that final word, trying to stop it from being swallowed by his throat, caught between his lips, ground slowly away by a jaw held rigidly closed. 3. Ellis took him to a catholic church hidden along a relatively untrodden walkway. Logan, in his surprise, could only comment on how odd it was that an Evangelical Protestant had led an Eastern Orthodox Catholic into a Roman Catholic church. It was like some scene out of a bad joke told after too much alcohol consumption. Ellis smiled but took Logan firmly by the hand and lead him into the empty building, telling him with the spark of theological purpose in her eyes that it doesn’t matter what category places of worship come under, God, their shared God, was in each and every single one of those places at once. God was everywhere. Logan knew that. He wasn’t stupid. He remembered how in the complicit secrecy of the darkness, after all the bluster and fury, he had heard the irregular, nervous clicks of one expensive rosary bead sliding against the next. Logan didn’t need reminding that like some demented inversion of Mary had a Little Lamb, God was everywhere Ellis went. Logan didn’t like being reminded of his own childish feeling of unfair betrayal, something that had nothing (and everything) to do with the fact that he hadn’t been practicing for twenty or so years now. Ellis slowly turned him around, breaking his too reflective reverie, and pointed to the confessional door in front of them. “It helps,” she said simply. “It helps to say it out loud to God. There’s no priest in there, and no one can listen from outside so I promise I won’t hear it.” “How long have you been doing this?” “I found this place after the first time. And once for each time since then.” Ellis held her hands out and shrughed, accidentally (and heretically, no doubt) mirroring the crucifix hung above her head for a brief moment. “It’s difficult to really trust telling any priests in case they recognize my voice.” The confession box inside was small, but not oppressive. There was a softness to the sunshine flowing through the small skylight in the ceiling and the padding on the seats that made Logan feel cocooned from reality which, he cynically thought, was probably the whole point to the thing: lull the parishioners into a false sense of security. There was an immediate sense of regret and self loathing at this display of derision. It was after all a holy place and perhaps more importantly, it was Ellis’ holy place. Logan closed his eyes, tried not to feel incredibly stupid, and let his words be swallowed up by the surrounding dimness. I’m leaving Cortland/this/you for Europe. There was no immediate lightness or relief afterwards. Perhaps his prolonged stay in the economic epicenter had plagued his mind with too much materialism and he needed a receipt citing each sin with its corresponding number of prayers to appear magically before his eyes before he felt that some kind of confidence had been exchanged. It was foolish to hope for a seamless divine intervention of information from himself to God to Ellis; an efficient display of collective religious consciousness. It would’ve been nice if it was that easy. 4. Logan recorded his reasons with a dictaphone, taking advantage of the cassette that had still been in there; the archaeological remnants of a typically delusional, early 80s Soviet endorsed pop group that his father had been given by the army officers during the Easter celebrations one year. His message had required a flight up to Ukraine to search amongst the eyries of cardboard boxes misted over with an interlacing of cobwebs and dust in the attic that Logan’s parents never cleaned. (His father was a mechanic and enjoyed the mess and sprawl of his family’s past laid out like the components of a car’s engine. His mother was a nurse and said that as long as the rest of the house is hers to clean, the senile idiot can keep his hobby of a room.) The tape wound round and round, accumulating time, apologies, explanations and a different attempt with each revolution of the cassette reel. But once Logan was happy with the final result he snipped his line of thought with an efficient bite from a pair of scissors and used the coils of his confession to partly cushion the box for El’s twenty-fourth birthday present. 5. It was odd how of all the different ways Logan had written it down, no method seemed to adequately convey the sorrow and intention. The man-made coarseness of the pen, the ink, the recordings: they all mutated his words by their own mechanical and physical properties and yet somehow as he stood there in their bathroom, body shivering from the droplets of water that weighed down on each individual hair, looking at the letters he’d written in the foggy opaqueness of the mirror in front of him, there was a satisfied feeling of how the words I’m Leaving seemed so right. His fingers had cut through the blurred image of himself, constructing his letters through a lack of liquid, to reveal the clear underlay reflecting disconnected portions of his body: an ear in the stem of the 't', his eyes straddling the dip between the peak of the 'a' and the 'n', a wisp of hair caught and cradled by the curves of an 's'. He was a fragmented being watching and waiting until the magic of time evaporated away the condensation, allowing the clarity of his words to spread gradually across the entire surface to reveal a fully reassembled man in none of his glory: all naked and vulnerable, but braver than he had been in the last 5 months. It was a less burdened Logan who opened the door that Ellis had been pounding on for five minutes. “Logan, what the fuck? I need to get ready for work.” A kiss and a decision and Logan could feel the coordinates of his journey finally settle in an outline around Ellis’ silhouette. He found Ellis leaning lazily against the wall, grinning wide and easy and happy. He popped the cork, proposed a mock toast, and took a swig before handing the bottle over to Ellis, who obligingly drew a long drink from it. Logan wasn’t sure why he leaned over and kissed her, then, their mouths sticky, warm, expensive, eyes half closed. Maybe it was the way Ellis still had blood all over her knuckles and wrists, how unyielding and steady her hands were. Maybe it was the fact that he knew he would be leaving, how he also knew that Ellis wouldn’t be, how strange it would be to work for another force. Maybe it had nothing to do with any of that, but as it was, he kissed Ellis full on the mouth, said El low and quiet, wrapped his hand around her fingers, the neck of the bottle, like some kind of promise. A reassurance. 2. The night Chandler was shot, Ellis’d walked into their bedroom to find Logan sitting on the bed, surrounded by messy clothes and a gun and hair products, staring blankly at an empty suitcase with a pair of socks in his hands. That was when she knew she had to do something, so what she did was drag Logan to the nearest bar and order him a few Very Strong drinks. They sat there surrounded by tourists, music loud and almost unbearably awful, staring down into their glasses and not talking. Logan made a low noise in his throat and Ellis almost panicked because it sounded like Logan was fucking crying and that shit would have been gross – but Logan was laughing. It was quiet at first and he tried to cover his mouth with his hand, but soon it was an all out, hysterical sort of laughter that left Logan with his face pressed into Ellis’ arm, breathless, shoulders shaking. This is just so unbelievably ridiculous, he said; Ellis blearily counted up the number of empty glasses by Logan’s arm. Huh. That might explain it. She didn’t feel guilty, exactly, but she did feel obligated to make sure Logan ended up back in their bedroom, whole and unmolested by creepy middle-aged women, so she dragged him step by step back to the house. In the elevator, Logan sloppily leaned into her, the bags under his eyes still a little puffy, and kissed her on the cheek, chaste and a little sweet but his breath smelling like gin and rum. The night before Logan left for Europe. There was a new awkwardness there, the realization that years of friendship and love couldn’t be recovered and reused, not after he’d known the curve of her shoulder or how well his hand fit against the line of her hip, not after that.
