The Trick Is To Keep Breathing: Act I

12 June 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 12 June, 2005 when the one you leave is the one thing you believe you say goodbye when underneath is your one belief missy higgins - drowning May 12, 2026. The jail cell smells putrid, like caustic perspiration and burnished blood and urine. You have a lot of time to think because there is no way you’re going to be able to sleep. Your head is pulsating and your knuckles are mangled and bleeding and every so often you have to breathe acutely to make sure you don’t stop breathing altogether. But it’s not the abrasions and wounds that hurt the most, it’s the betrayal, that concentrated, scrupulous stab in the back. All of it seems so dolorously ironic, given what happened. And you were right. Of course you were right about Jonathan. When have you ever been wrong about him? Sometimes you delude yourself into believing differently, but conclusively you are always justified into thinking that he is doing you wrong. But it isn’t the kind of right you can be sublime with, not something you can vaunt over. Because people got hurt. You got hurt. Though you can’t roster the coercive animosity that entrenched your body earlier that evening, you feel the anger oscillating and ringing in the pit of your stomach. It’s still there, like all of the Cortland memories you thought you left behind, like the negligible half of you that still hankers for the old neighborhood and your mom, even your dad. Like the love you have for Logan, will have, always. This entire year you have battled to keep those emotions locked inside, sober, obscured. Now you’re locked up again. You look at the saturated, dilapidated concrete beneath you and the slovenly walls and the rusty bars and you wonder, just for a second, if this is where you belonged all along. When Logan showed up, you were conspicuously surprised. It still amazes you how amicable these people are, how much they love you. You keep thinking that one day you will go too deep and they’ll cut you off entirely. “You think you can mess up so bad that we'll just give up on you? You can’t. You are a part of this family now and you are going to feel the full weight of that.” You thought today was that day. But then Logan gazed at you and you could see the and frustration in his eyes, frustration that would only grow as he argued with the C.A. who asserted he couldn’t let you go because the front detective had departed already and had abandoned the release papers. Logan is used to getting his way, you’re certain, and you felt an agonizing guilt in your heart when you saw the anxiety in his tired hues. “Don’t worry, El,” he reassured. “We’ll fix this in the morning.” Then he clasped your hands in his and added, “I am so, so sorry.” You wanted to say that you’re sorry too, sorry for being the way you are, for bringing people like Jonathan into their lives, for hindering them with all of this unnecessary drama. But the words were glued to your throat and you said nothing. Nothing. When he walked away you bit your lip and tasted blood. The image is burned in your memory, furrowed so deep that every time you close your eyes it’s there. Natalie, her slender fingers wrapped around that gun, pointing it at Jonathan, her eyes wide and anxious and petrified. You were still lagging on the ground, attempting to beckon the strength to pull yourself up when you saw her drop the gun, tears rivering down her cheeks as she collapsed to the carpet. Then Chandler and Logan appeared and Chandler helped his friend up while Logan tried to help you to your feet. You were so capricious that you couldn’t get up at first. The room was spinning and you coughed and finally, finally you stood. Logan supported you against his firm frame and you concentrated on not passing out. You know Natalie just wanted to help, that she was trying to protect you from Jonathan in the same way that you wished you’d been able to protect her. You don’t know why you feel angry at her. She’s the saint in this situation, the savior, but that stupid pride that’s always pushing at your temples, lodging in your throat, pressing in your lungs won’t let you be okay with that. You were supposed to be the hero. You know it’s bullshit but you adhere to it anyway, adhere to it because a part of you feels like your illusions are all you’ve got left. When you think about Jonathan you don’t feel anything at all, not worry or concern, not even dread. You feel apathetic, numb. You don’t want him to live and you don’t want him to die. You don’t want to know either way. You want to close your eyes and think him out of your life, throw the memories of him into non-existence. Because you’re tired of thinking about him at all. But then you think of the time in third grade when that asshole Austin Delloy was pushing you around on the playground, calling you a dork, a nerd, a loser, and Jonathan marched over and punched him square in the jaw so his head snapped back and he hit the pavement with a satisfying thump. Jonathan got punished, no recess for a week, but he didn’t care – Jonathan never cared about things like that. You tried to thank him but he waved you off, said something about how some people just deserve a good punch in the face and Austin was definitely one of them. You agreed. It seems too simple to say “things change,” or “people change,” to explain away the love you still feel for Jonathan. And it’s not because he’s your brother, not because you have the same mom and dad and shared a house and a room for years. It’s because you remember looking at him in that dim apartment, staring at him holding that gun and making threats, and in his hazel eyes you could see the best and worst parts of yourself. Jonathan’s an idiot sometimes, and he’s lost, and confused, and violent and cruel. But part of him is still that kid who always had your back in a fight, who kept your mom’s asshole boyfriends from beating up on you when they were wasted, who told you that the Nash kids were an unbeatable team, never forget that. And you know part of him still loves you, too. You think about this and you hunch over on that unforgiving stone bench and you feel tension climb your spine and then you’re shaking and crying, shaking and crying, and then crying and crying, crying and crying. You cry silently, because you learned long ago how to smother your sobs so no one would know you were hurting or think you were weak. And as the tears slip down your cheeks your body releases some fraction of the tension it’s been holding, and you think: It’s not much, but at least it’s something. At least it’s something. ----- Now. “Breathe,” he said, “you stupid little fool. Breathe.” “Why? Why should I?” The drugs were scrolling at the edges of her eyes. Ripped and gray: old-lady-in-morning-mourning veil. Like blood clots. Retinal tears; the Gothic cliché, contained behind the pupil. She would never be so obviously. Obviously. What? “I said breathe,” and she was down on the tile then, with this other person across her legs. Pinned down, butterfly on display, but she’d never had any color, black and white like a photograph: now in thirty fabrics, patterns and colors, thanks for your face kid. A bad panic-attack time, taijin kyofusho, freshman psychology — but then she said giggle, you smell sooo good. Something else. So everything was okay. Okay, ok, she’d never get it. This damned place, the airplanes that rattled her tea. Rattle rattle rattle. Shake pound sob shake pound breathe, I said breathe. Beating on her back, now she was giggling like that girl. She coughed. Sucked in a gasp before she could stop herself. Sobbed out a sob before she could reach the pills still sitting on the white plastic toilet seat. Moved. Wasn’t fast enough. Pain, little sick-red sparkles running through the gray curtain, hit her head on the basin. “Throw up, throw up, throw up, Oh God.” Off her legs, off her back, she was slipandsliding to her knees in the water, rattlerattle as the pills hit the floor, and the motion in her head was enough to set her off. The catastrophe had been averted. She was wan but upright, wrapped in a terry cloth robe, her feet in fuzzy green slippers. Tea was warm between her hands; it was dawn and the airplanes were beginning to buzz in through the window. “So,” Chandler said from the couch across the room. She let it stretch out. She didn’t really have anything to say; thanks, apology, she wouldn’t mean either. The wind curled its fingers around the living room, blinds clattering. Sunday afternoon and the sun hung against the sky’s backdrop like an unblemished coin. “I don’t know if I’m alive or dead,” Ellis looks away, the wall behind his blonde head interesting. Traffic downstairs. “You’re here,” Chandler begins, wryly his forehead wrinkling, “That’s all that matters.” It’s an amused snort that flairs her nostrils. Nostalgic. Then she collects herself and the corners of her mouth tighten. “It’s not enough.” He couldn’t say anything because he’d never heard anything so true. Logan was gone - Ellis’ heart was gone. Chandler squinted his eyes against the reflection of the sun. “What is?” Pause. Green pools flicker to the carpet. And she looked up. “Nothing is.” A saturnine grin and Ellis continues. “I told myself that I wouldn’t care if he left. I was angry, a hungry, mindless kind of fury that I didn’t know what to do with, and beneath that, hurt. I wondered which was worse, knowing that nobody could have stopped him or knowing that I just wasn’t enough. Wasn’t strong enough, wasn’t important enough. Didn’t matter to him enough.” Chandler knows things. “Maybe that was what it was like for Logan, maybe that was what it was like - not having enough power to kill Michael, not even being worthy of acknowledgment.” “It’s not a fit enough reason to leave.” She has to stop because she doesn't know what she’s saying anymore, it’s all just one gigantic mess of feeling and resentment, all tangled up like wires inside her ribs, threatening to spark. Chandler starts talking before Ellis is compelled to, and she’s grateful for that. “Smoke?” Reaches forward and lets Chandler put the cigarette between her lips because her hands are occupied with the tea. Lets him light it. Chandler is real and tangible and there, something beautiful and chipped, a magnum opus of calculations and lines. The inverse slopes of his collarbones, gravity of a spine. The crisp tilt of hands, weight of a leg, asymmetrical. Paper of the cigarette and its soft chemical glow, the burning cylinder perpendicular to Ellis’ fingers, now, nails brutally short. This is how it goes. “After he left, you know, when all the worst shit happened, I’d wake up every single motherfucking morning and tell myself that I would kill him.” Chandler chuckles shortly. Ellis takes a drag. “And if I couldn’t do that, then at least I wouldn’t care if someone else did. They said I needed convincing. Weird, that, because I was trying to convince them. Wanted to show them that I was good at this, this CIA business, before I remembered that there was no way I could ever prove myself to them, you know? Some of them never stopped being scared. But that’s what I did. Every goddamn morning for a few weeks, before I didn’t need to remind myself of it anymore. Afterwards, it became some sort of feeling. I’d wake up and it would be there. It would always be there.” Another drag and Ellis lets the smoke filter through her mouth slowly. Chandler can make out the wispy gas as it dissolves into the grim air. It looks almost white against the flatness of the room. “That’s the goddamned motherfucking problem. I’ve got all the time in the world to forget him. I want to forget him. I don't want to forget him.” Hair waves in the wind. “I don’t feel right anymore,” she says, “I feel like I’m dancing on air.” Ellis doesn't talk this much with anyone else, isn’t pushed towards these questions with anyone else, doesn’t have to explain herself with anyone else. What Chandler is for Ellis is faith. Plain and simple. What Ellis sees when she looks at Chandler is conviction, is belief, the fundamental, necessary kind. What she sees is possibility; so much of being a hero is what they call destiny, and Chandler is the inverse of that. Chandler fights against predestination every single fucking day, and it’s this kind of defiance that makes people love him, makes people hate him “Your life is not fairytale; he left. There was nothing you could do. He left and maybe it was better that he did. He would have, someday. It wasn’t like you never said that you weren't going to do it yourself, leave. But you held out hope. You were good at that. You thought that maybe you would get your happy ending; you thought that your life had been leading up to one. Maybe you deserved it; you’re selfish, you know that,” he declares. The sun is setting. “He’s the first person who has ever made me doubt myself,” she says, and thinks of sunshine. Chandler thinks of the way that danger had made his body gravitate toward Ellis’, involuntary. How he had taken those blows and those risks out of instinct and nothing more than that, just a gut feeling that said: this is your friend, this is someone that’ s worth it, this is somebody that you’ ll care about someday, if you don’ t already. “The core of the problem is that you still retain some of that former belief in Logan long after Logan has stopped believing in anything but himself.” Ellis doesn’t say anything when every rule that could’ve been broken is broken. And then she does. “Do we need milk?” Suddenly standing, dwindling the cigarette against the ashtray. The answer’s no. “No.” “Okay.” The door is quiet. A remarkable sort of girl, he thinks. Love and groceries. Heartbreak, or rather the absence of it. He knows her better now that he ever had before, and the utter familiarity has erased whatever was left of the potential between them. It’s not shocking, really; he tries not to miss things that he’d never had, and if there was anything to this situation that he regrets is the what might have been. But even that is just another thought he dismisses. Pragmatism. They are what they are. People adapt. People change. The two of them have gotten good at that over the years, out of necessity; a natural defense mechanism. There are rules that they’ve learnt from life. Not getting hurt is one of them. He gets up, places a bowl in the sink and turns the faucet. Cold water, startling. Bathroom mirror. One of the light bulbs flicker a little before steadying into a fluorescent sort of sickly light, and as he flicks the switch, he can hear the faint buzzing of an insect. A small black shape collides violently into the plastic barrier surrounding the light bulbs, seizing and lurching back and forth. Smashes into the plastic with a strangely resounding sound of destruction, amplified and a little desperate in the silence. It’s a kind of sound that slithers into his ears, lighting quick, with a hiss. The thought of it is like a snake wrapped around his neck, a tongue at the back of his ear, scales crawling through his head. He stares at his reflection, at the slightly uneven lines of his shoulders and hair bristling against the edge of his jaw. The new kind of sharpness around his face, all angles and planes, now. The mirror’s dirty, specks of dried water and toothpaste that’s collected since the last time it was cleaned. Blinks and all he can see is a face from five years back, a recollection seeping into consciousness, a former self. It’s disconcerting, to say the least. He’d been glad to lose that face, almost, glad to lose some things from back then. Chandler smiles at himself in the mirror, that old shit-eating grin he’d used to perfection. The grin he’d used to take on the world. ----- May 12, 2026. Again. Looking back on it, it was hard to describe what things had been like for them. They fit oddly, where the spaces in their words slipped together in all the wrong places, and somehow, it had worked. At least for a while. Ellis yelled too much and said all the wrong things and Jonathan always verged on the line of contemptuous, single-mindedly focused to a point of arrogance, but something about Ellis’ stubbornness and ridiculous words wore patches through Jonathan’s painted over and sandpapered exterior. It was strange, but so were a lot of things. Jonathan saved her life, once. Almost died doing so, and that’s the sort of thing that people are supposed to carry around for a long time, one of those things that equate to loyalty and debt and something like a kind of trust. It’s a matter of perspective, of linear lines converging into a solid, substantial point that marked where things had begun blurring together. Those lines never do combine. She just thinks they do. It’s an excuse for when she can’t make things out clearly anymore. The lines are parallel. They never end. They never intersect. Senses are good at secrecy, deception. It’s easy to believe, and it’s not something that Ellis would have stopped believing if Jonathan hadn’t told her. Drew a model in the sand with his foot, too, told Ellis that it was all a matter of angles and maneuvering. This is what Ellis thinks of when she remembers those years. Lives that she thought met and combined, people that she thought crossed paths. She guesses that maybe they never did, after all. Separate lives and separate futures. Time, she knows, is a strange thing. Something you don't expect; it brings gravity and grey air and scars, that’s something she’s sure of. She wasn’t very compromising then. Not exactly cooperative, rational; it's something she’s willing to acknowledge. It’s something she's learned, to some extent, and Ellis wonders if that’s an accomplishment she should be proud of or something to be upset about, the fact that growing up means abandoning that unbending, absolute sort of faith, even when she was wrong. She thinks about this sort of thing all the time. It, too, is new. Childhood is something she is convinced was perfect once she left it. Ellis guesses that Jonathan never needed any of it. He got it, though, and that’s the thing. He got everything he didn’t need and nothing that he did. It’s how the cookie crumbled. View Ellis Nash 's Biography
