Bullet

26 September 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 26/09/2006 Aren’t they strange things, photographs, you once said to me. They lie to you. They make something that lasted half a second last forever. And your brain works so that you’re trying your hardest to recall, so it is the memories that are, in general, misplaced. I looked at you that time, remembering the way you sprawled, hair tousled, coffee cup by your elbow, the way the side of your mouth tasted like espresso. Your little frown. I keep trying to fill in details, my brain like a camera, of the millions of photographs I have kept of you in the drawers of my mind, protected carefully from dust, rotted only by forgetfulness. In one, I am looking at the camera, my arm around your back, your arms around my neck, my cheek against the back of your head, the both of us posed awkwardly in this grasp. I can see my face looking up, half buried, and I can’t read my own eyes. What was I thinking then as I held you, not knowing that a moment could not last forever? The soft of your face in my gloved palm, the turn of your hip making the crease run gently up your shirt, distorting the striped fabric like the sound of your voice saying something in my ear. How much of this is falsehood? I wanted to ask you, still want to ask you, though the answer is, maybe, clear. In that photograph I am holding you. I grasp air. It's cheating my heart of its guarantee. Everything I hate about the desert I can find in you. The differences blur into triviality: I wonder whether you're simply the product of dust and mirage. But it's not that easy, of course. No matter how hard I blink -- the insides of my eyelids sharp noncolors against my vision -- you are still there, hovering at the edges of peripheral sight. When you talk, there is nothing insubstantial about your words; they define you more than the dimensions of your physical self. It's not so much your face I recall but the way you talk, the quicksand pull of your words, habituation at its finest; I know better than to listen for too long, and most of your words slide cleanly through the hourglass of your consciousness. Others, the ones I suspect you really want me to hear, have a way of stinging my senses like thin shards of lightning-fused glass. In my own way I practice patience, if only because I have long since learnt the futility of attempting to avoid the inevitable. So I don't ask until I find you in our bedroom, examining the little-used collection of stamps in the shelves about the bedstead. What are you doing, I ask, and you don't even look at me when you reply, We always ask others the questions we want to ask ourselves. I have very little interest in verbal byplay. What do I want, then, I snap. I expect you to make something up, something slightly off from true, the way you can't quite judge distance from dune to dune. To sleep, perchance to dream, coffee, you quip. You still haven't looked up from the parchment you're holding, but the smile is visible anyway, some tiny movement of your mouth that comes and goes like the shimmer of invisible heat of sunbaked asphalt. The worst thing you can do when you're trapped in a sandstorm is to try and make your way home; it's too easy to lose your way when there are no visible landmarks, when you can't even see your own feet in front of you. I say nothing in response. The first time it happened, you had a wife and one son and your good luck charm was pressing your mouth to the base of my neck. But most of that is entirely beside the point. The first time it happened, I found you fast asleep at the kitchen table the next morning, head in your arms and a crease on your cheek from the wrinkles on the sleeve of your jacket. For some reason, you'd apparently gotten fully dressed before slipping back into sleep. Jeans, black coat, trainers. Even your watch. I woke you up with a firm hand in your hair and a laugh (I thought you'd decided to leave me in the middle of the night, I was beginning to get a bit offended). It was a Sunday morning, soft and indulgent, so I made breakfast: strong coffee, eggs, spicy dried sausage, a mango sliced in three and neatly cubed, flesh so ripe and smooth that it yielded to the slightest pressure. We ate, your jacket then hanging neatly on the back of your chair, and I teased your about this newfound habit. A smile, not the one everyone else knows, but smaller, more private, close-mouthed with a quirk at the end. So, Dex, what is your subconscious trying to tell you today? You grinned, flung a piece of mango skin at me with your fork. It soon descended into immaturity, and as I stood over the sink picking a piece of toast crust out of my hair, you pressed me against the counter, kissed me long and slow as if we had all the time in the world, as if there was no world outside of the apartment, no families, no politics or religion or expectations. Just us, the room, a sink full of soapy dishes waiting to be done. It was a nice thought, not that I would ever tell you. You had to leave, eventually. There were things to be done, hands to shake, places to be, a wife to kiss. What you didn't tell me was the unsettled feeling that had lodged itself into your mind; I don't like not knowing. It's annoying to think that there are missing hours and things I don't remembering doing, no matter how tiny or insignificant they were. When I think about it too hard, something jittery and electric wraps itself around my lungs and heart, so I don't. Your name is Michael Dex Lacey. You were born on September 2, 1977. You have a son named Jordan and a wife named Jacqueline and an affair named Brody. One day, you fall asleep with your fingers on my spine. The air is still warm and fluid with the ghost of late summer, the windows open for secrets to spill out of. When you wake up, I am gone. Wouldn't that have made for a nice truth? A better one, something excusable at least. As it is, however, there are no memory lapses, no nightmares or dreams. We sign the contracts. I'll buy a new house for my mother, arrange for our belongings to be delivered; you'll enroll your son in private school and I won't pick up all those little things I've carelessly left at yours and Jac's place over the years, will not say goodbye to the either of you. After all, goodbye means something, just like how your hands and eyes and the line of your cheek have always meant something almost like faith to me. I still harbor a little hope that maybe, maybe one day I'll go to bed and wake up next to a different person, in a different world, life even. It's ridiculous, I know. I deserve that, though. I've earned the right to harbor some unattainable dream; who would've thought that there would be anything in this world I couldn't ever let myself have? It's about discipline, I tell myself, routine and precision and punishment. My father taught me that; guns and sport and money, I think, and almost laugh at the absurdity, the truthfulness of it. The second time it happens, I find myself in the drivers seat of the car, keys and a crumpled piece of paper in my hand. Blinking, I turn on the ignition and interior lighting, smooth the scrap of paper across the steering wheel and squint. The car clock reads 2:11 in the morning. The scrawl on the paper is illegible, numbers and letters looped hopelessly together, and even though I can't read the words, I'm pretty sure I know what they mean. There's a sudden sharpness in the air when I think about you. I make an appointment with a doctor the next day and count out little white sleeping pills that night on the kitchen counter, placing one next to a glass of water. A smile, a faint memory of dreaming, a flash of yellow jumps through his mind before I swallow. Someone's hand is on my face but I'm saying Dex over and over like a prayer. Their hair tickles thick and dark, and suddenly they're saying Bro-- and I'm waking up. All I can see in the dark is the faint gleam of dark hair on the pillow next to me. I think, I've forgotten something, but I don't know what, who. I go back to sleep.
