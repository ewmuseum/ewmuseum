Date: March 13, 2006 I briefly lived in a small town in rural Illinois, back when the future was wide open. My parents bought a small farm a few miles out of the town limits to raise horses and escape the insatiably growing appetite of suburban Chicago. But I hated the country. I hated the isolationism and the inconvenience. I hated the insignificance. When I left, I swore I’d never come back if I didn’t have to. Back then, I was too busy being miserable to appreciate the space. I was appreciating it now, though. I had come back. There had been too many days when I had been half surprised to wake up. Weeks would go by in blurry hazes, and parasites were chewing holes in my memory. I needed a break from the spiraling course I was on. I had been away too long exploring the limits of my own sanity. I was constantly surrounded by the hazards of my own metaphysical attempts to escape, and I was constantly surrounded by a throng of peers, looking for the same way out, trying the same disillusioning methods. I was appreciating space, but it still felt foreign to me. I knew then that what I was subconsciously yearning for back then was balance. I’ve wallowed through emptiness and congestion. Each makes the other necessary, but the two are not combinable. And I knew all too well that constantly swinging from one extreme to another, living at the end of a pendulum, confuses the mental being. The clouds seemed much larger than I’d ever seen them before. They were so large that they didn’t appear real, as if a master painter had brushed them upon the sky. And they seemed to roll on forever. Everything seemed to roll on forever, but it all looked the same. The infiniteness of the countryside always awed me. It was so much like death. I was in desperate need of a cigarette. I hadn’t had one for hours, since I had arrived here. My dad’s nose was better than any smoke detector I’ve ever had, and he absolutely hated the smell of cigarette smoke. I still didn’t have the balls to tell them I smoked, though I wasn’t sure they didn’t know already. I was finally getting along with them, and I didn’t want to change that. I saw, through the trees, a car slowly treading across the long, curved, gravel driveway. I recognized the diarrhea colored Intrepid that wrapped towards me. The car pulled to a stop a few inches away from me. The driver was waiting for me to get in, so I walked over to the passenger side door and opened it. My friend T-Bird was there to greet me with a ridiculous handshake we invented in high school. We still remembered all of the steps, performing them fluidly unless inebriated. T-Bird was the only guy in town I still kept in touch with. He was studying to be a pharmacist. He was also the one who told me about the keg party in town. This kid we went to school with, Gavin, had just come back from serving in Iraq. I think he was in the Army, but I’m not sure. I’m too easily distracted to remember small details about distant people. I never had any personal problems with Gavin, or really anyone else I grew up with, but secretly, I felt like an outsider, and looked down on most of them. Gavin was a pretty poplar guy. He was arrogant, but in a different way than I was. All the girls thought he was pretty, I had even heard that he fucked one of his teachers. She was fresh out of college, and she wasn’t half bad looking either. When I had heard Gavin was in Iraq, I kind of felt a bit guilty for looking down on him before. I wondered whether Iraq had changed him at all. I wondered if he had killed anyone. “Hey, T-Bird, can we stop at Casey’s before we head to Gavin’s? I need to buy some cigarettes.” “Yeah, no problem, I should probably fill up with gas anyway.” So we stopped at the shitty gas station, the only one in the one-road town. I walked into the little mart, full of impulse buys and a lingering stench of hopelessness. I approached the woman at the counter. I had no idea who she was, and she had no idea who I was, though we’d probably seen each other around before. It’s amazing what three years did to my appearance. I had excelled so often in this barren town that nobody would have projected me to end up as the worn, tired, crass man at the counter, unable to wait a minute longer for the sweet cancerous catalyst to fill his graying lungs. “What’ll it be?” she asked me disinterestedly. “You wouldn’t happen to have American Spirits here, would you?” “Never heard of them.” I knew it was a long shot, but I was disappointed all the same. I had a martial-like loyalty to my preferred brand that I held for few things in life. I settled for my distant second choice. “Okay. Just give me a pack of Pall Mall’s then. Reds.” The clerk bent to find my product and reemerged with it momentarily. She rung the cigarettes up on the register. They were half the price as the Spirits, which was a pretty good deal considering the longevity of a Pall Mall over a slightly pricier Marlboro or Camel. Despite a full beard and a skin that had lost its youthful glow long ago, I was still asked for an ID. Luckily, my expired driver’s license worked without question. Gavin’s family’s house was just as I remembered it. The machine shed was larger than the permanent mobile home planted beside it. The driveway was packed with pick-up trucks and visiting sedans. Gavin’s younger brother and his friends played basketball on a dimly lit hoop on a patch of cement as familiar faces clustered in small groups clutching bottles or cups of cheap beer, chattering on about the local gossip and other things that held no interest to me. A bonfire that demanded constant attention regarding threats of suicide blazed guarded by those huddled around it for jacketless warmth. It was as impotent as any of the flames I had seen there before, flames I’d jump over when they were docile enough to leap. I was too docile to leap them tonight. I used to crave attention at parties like these, but I became more and more comfortable with obscurity as I got used to it. And that killed me. As I stood there with a bottle of Bud Light in one hand and a Pall Mall pursed between my lips, I remembered the expectations that had been placed on me by the townspeople. They were expectations I started to believe I could live up to when I was stuck in this incongruent microcosm. They were expectations that I’d never meet. I felt naïve for ever having ideals in the first place. I was silly to think that the world could change, and that I would be the one to change it. And then I saw Gavin. He was walking out of his machine shed smoking a Marlboro and holding a cup of booze. I shuffled over to meet him. “Johnny, didn’t know you were back in town.” I shook his hand, wondering. I was wondering questions that I’d never ask; questions that I didn’t think he wanted to answer tonight. “I heard you were. It’s good to see you. You glad to be back?” I could only ask questions that I already knew the answers to. “Yeah, but it’s only temporary. I’m going back in a few weeks for another six months.” He seemed satisfied with that. I never knew him well enough for him to confide in me. I didn’t know what to say. And then he asked me a question, just standard small talk. “So what have you been doing, man?” I realized then, I didn’t want to answer this question either. I didn’t want to brag about my successes or reveal my failures. “Ah, not much,” I answered. I really stressed the interjection. The length of my reflexive groan usually coincided with the socially instinctual plainness of my response. It made me realize that I had changed so much, too. I never found out if he had killed or not. A girl who was a few grades below me back in high school quickly grabbed onto Gavin’s arm and demanded his attention with her lusty, half-drunk warmth. I awkwardly saluted Gavin, a greeting or farewell that had seemed a lot more trivial to me when I had used it on anyone else. He took her into his house to fuck around, and I walked behind the shed to urinate. I put my beer on the small, flat, square top of an old, wooden fencepost and balanced the Pall Mall between my lips. As I stood there, I became a shadow of a man, alone in an empty patch of dirt that would be a cornfield or a bean field in a half of a year. He seemed the same. He had that cocky magnetism, now under the irresistible sway of uniform and duty and direction. I felt so different, and he seemed the same. Everyone seemed so stagnant, like life here was sealed in some time vacuum. Maybe they all pretended not to notice my acquired detachment. I gazed off into the distance, the fire at my back. The darkness seemed to extend infinitely, barely broken by rare headlights and vague outlines of buildings. It was so much like death. I shook my dick and flipped my boxers over it before zipping my jeans back up and heading back to the gathering. I caught up with some of the guys I used to cause mischief with and some of the girls I secretly loved; and some I still might have loved but pretended not to. T-Bird was wasted. I warned him that the Vicodin was strong. It had twice the hydrocodone and less acetaminophen than normal prescriptions. I had taken a couple already, but I was used to it. He told me I could crash on his couch if I drove him home. I hadn’t operated a vehicle in over a year, I didn’t have a valid driver’s license, and I was in no condition to be driving anyway. But I was anxious to leave. The past looks brighter and more beautiful at a distance, like the sky from earlier. It’s so big and light blue and dappled with puffy, white clouds when it’s so far from earth, but the air right in front of you is invisibly mundane. The whir of the tires on the roads was soothing. T-Bird had fallen asleep immediately in the passenger’s seat, and the motionless yellow dashes flew by rhythmically, jutting out of the immense blackness into the ball of headlight glow before me. I too, was anxious to sleep, but afraid to dream. I hoped that I’d close my eyes, and all would be black. Like death. Or like the starless country sky of a dying winter night.
