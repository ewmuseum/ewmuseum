Distorted Pictures I: As Seen Through Smoke

2 October 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: October 2nd, 2006 In the morning, I went out to the balcony to smoke a cigarette for my breakfast. My apartment was on the top floor of a three story flat, and the backdoor led from the kitchen to a wooden stairwell with a thin balcony on each floor. The back of the apartment overlooked the alley where nothing much ever happened, and above that, the clouds moved westward across the sky. They were cumulous today, mostly fluffed white clouds with one dark gray mass- smaller than the rest- that stood out in front. It seemed to be moving slower than the others, and at times it seemed to stop and linger there off in the distance. It reminded me of being young and getting dizzy looking up at the skyscrapers whenever I’d come into the city. The sky sometimes would seem like it was stilled, and instead, the tops of the tremendous towers would seem to waver and drift, and the whole sight would make me feel nauseous if I stared for too long. At the top of the stairwell, I saw that most of the wood was covered in pigeon shit. I couldn’t recall having seen any pigeons, and there were no pigeons out at the time. I didn’t much mind the white splotched droppings until I got to thinking about how there were never any pigeons around. Sure, I’d seen some nesting underneath the expressway overpasses and there were always a few spotted along the city blocks. There were none that I saw here, though, and I wanted to think that they’d stay away, but the droppings were a telltale indicator that there were pigeons here at one time, and there was no telling when they would return. The whole situation concerned me deeply. I tossed my cigarette over the wooden ledge and walked back into my kitchen, barely avoiding the overturned table I had forgotten was there. The kitchen was full of overturned furniture. I had too damn much of it. I had shelving units with nothing to stack on them, so I turned the table upright and stacked all the rest of the furniture on top of it. “This isn’t abstract art!” I yelled out. “This is shit!” There was something off about my stacking today. Usually, I found the process of stacking things on top of one another to be wholly enjoyable; almost as enjoyable as alphabetizing things. But I was too distraught to stack. I couldn’t get my mind off of the goddamned pigeons, and there was something pigeon-like about my stacking. Perhaps to the untrained eye, there was nothing pigeon-like about my stacking. On the whole, though, it was there if only in the madness derived from thinking about those torturously unflinching aviators. It was remarkably warm for the beginning of October so that when I decided to leave the irritating stack of furniture for my daily walk, I had no need for my greatcoat, but I moved it from a box of laundry onto the coat rack. Upon returning from walks, there is no greater comfort than seeing your greatcoat on the coat rack. A bare coat rack, they say, is a very unlucky thing. Outside, I lit another cigarette and began walking south towards the boulevard. Nobody walked along the boulevard anymore. People would sit outside on their doorsteps sometimes, but nothing moved that wasn’t automotive. Even the way people walked had become automotive. The rare passerby would approach with body hunched at the waist and arms thrust immediately forward. Feet dragged on against sidewalk with heels that seemed to roll. I tried my best to avoid being drawn into the same walk. Walking, you see, is contagious. At least it is for me. If I’m not paying much attention, and I see someone walking in a peculiar way, I begin to subconsciously imitate that particular walk. I had an auto-immune weakness in my spatial cortex. I was sure of it! Out of fear of seeing one of these automotive walkers, I overcompensated with exaggeratedly opposite steps. I raised each knee as high as I could, alternately, and stepped down with thunderclaps on the cracked pavement. Instead of bending at the hip joints, I kept my upper body attentively stiff and made sure my arms hung low parallel to my sides. This way, I wouldn’t be influenced by the walks of others and would avoid looking ridiculous. Still, I began to feel self-conscious. The porch sitters all stared at me as I passed. I could feel the gaze of suspicious eyes locked on my back. Sometimes, if someone had been staring at me for a while, it’d knock me over. The porches, too, were all secluded by black fences. I noticed one of them get up out of my peripheral vision. He was a young man, some sort of Hispanic, and he had a neatly trimmed goatee. He walked to the fence. “Yo, man. You got another cigarette?” He held his fingers to his lips, gesticulating to me an imaginary cigarette to drive home the point. “I suppose,” I grumbled, reaching into my pocket. I looked around to make sure nobody was watching us before slipping the cigarette in between the metal bars. People around here were always on the lookout for free cigarettes. If someone saw me slip this man a smoke, I would be engulfed in a horrific swarm in no time. “Thanks, bro,” he said before retreating back to the stoop to smoke my cigarette. I walked on, trying to forget about that son of a bitch behind the fence. There were fences all around and people within them. Most of them sat on porches, some milled around. I walked past the next yard. There was an older black man sitting down on the stoop. His facial features had been squished into a series of lines in the center of his head. His mouth was pressed into a permanent smile that smiled for no reason at all anymore. “Hello!” he cried out, peering at me through eyes slitting through excess skin that hung from his brow line. He looked as if he was made from dough. “Hi,” I answered back, nervously. I thought about my cigarettes and I thought about the pigeons and the fences, but I didn’t say anything. I stopped, but my body leaned forward with onward intent. “I’m just resting my legs,” he said, still smiling. I nodded back, unsure of what to do. “Sure is a nice day out today.” “Yes. It sure is.” Then he got up and continued walking. He only walked back and forth across the fenced in yard, though. I hadn’t seen anyone open the fence gates, nor had I seen anyone enter them. I found this quite odd. I moved on. The next few yards were empty, but behind the next populated fence, there were two men. One man waddled back and forth. He was incredibly tall with a long face that drooped towards the bottom. The other man stood clutching the fence with both hands. He had very thick eyebrows and wore a scowl and a constant gaze on his face. “Iced tea!” he barked out confidently. “What?” I asked. I should have ignored him. “Here’s a dollar. Could you run to the store and pick me up an iced tea?” There was less confidence in his voice this time until he reached the last two words of his question. He handed me what he took for a dollar. It was actually an empty matchbook. I looked at the matchbook for a while. “Sure thing.” “Iced tea!” he reminded me. I walked off with no intention to buy the man iced tea. I felt kind of bad about keeping the matchbook. Even if it was worthless to me, it was currency to him. I didn’t feel like I should have it. After I turned the corner at the end of the block, I threw the matchbook over the curb and continued on home. As usual, I made nothing out of the rest of the day. I drank until I could barely stand and headed out to the porch for another cigarette. The storms were coming in from the west, and the lightning flashed across the navy blue backdrop. There were no stars over the city. They had all been replaced by planes. I could still see the clouds. They were still cumulous, but the darkness made them seem wispier, like unraveling cotton threads. My unit shared the balcony with the adjacent unit, and that unit’s screen door was angled open, though the main door was shut. I still hadn’t seen who lived there after a month, and I was curious to see who it was. There were no signs to indicate that anyone had lived there save for a pair of shoes that was drying outside one morning. Upon the top of the screen door, a pigeon perched itself, looking out over the alleyway. The filthy fucker didn’t even acknowledge me. It just perched there, looking, like it owned the whole balcony, and I was encroaching upon its space. This pigeon had some nerve, I tell you. I barked at it and waved my arms to make myself appear more threatening. I uncoiled and frilled and waxed reptilian. It ignored me. I could tell it was intentional, too. He knew I was there, alright! I whooped until I was out of breath, and by that point, it was clear that all of my commotion was useless; not one snobby feather was ruffled. *** The next day, when I woke and ambled out for my morning cigarette, I saw not one, but two pigeons now atop the screen door. When I came out, I could have sworn that I saw them turn their heads at me and then back away real quickly. They pretended that they hadn’t, and I could see that they hoped I didn’t see them. This only served to obviously exacerbate my prejudice towards pigeons. Both were black, but one was pocked with an identifiable purple blotch on the nape and throat. The two of them talked as if I wasn’t there. “It sure was coming down last night,” the one with the purple spot said. “I wouldn’t have known. I was sound asleep.” “I stepped outside for a bit and a chunk of hail nailed me in the primaries!” “Right in the primaries?” “Yeah, right in the primaries.” That must have been the male. He was flamboyant in a masculine way. He was trying to impress his counterpart. While he looked out, she stole glances at his purple blotch, shamelessly displayed. (I would later go on to display my purple blotches to attract females, but it didn’t work. They also turned out to be herpes simplex one). The conversation was unbearable. I finished the cigarette quickly and then head out to commence upon my walk. I walked past the familiar yards again. The first man was not out, thankfully. He would have asked me for a cigarette for sure. The second man was sitting on the porch again. He was smiling his etched on smile. He waved his big hand at me. His fingers were puffed with inflammation. “Hi,” I called to him as he kept waving. The wavy bastard wouldn’t stop his waving tomfooleries. He just kept right on waving at me. “I’m just resting my legs,” he nodded to me; nodded while waving. I nodded back to him and kept walking. As I passed, he got up and started walking around in a circle. The next two were there in their yard still, the big goofy looking son of a bitch and the straight faced man with the eyebrows. Eyebrows grabbed at the black fence between us and stared at me. “Iced tea?” “I couldn’t find any,” I lied. He nodded, started chewing on nothing in particular to keep the saliva turning in his mouth, and walked off to mill about. The big, goofy looking son of a bitch came waddling up to the fence then. “Excuse me, sir. Do you have an extra cigarette that you could give me?” He had an unsettlingly high voice that sounded like he was breathing in the whole time he was talking. Something about him made me irate. It angered me that the human species could produce such a deformedly large mass. He was something so dumb and ugly that it made me sick to be human. “No!” I barked. “I can’t spare any.” He waddled away in disappointment, and I kept on until I came to a black fence with a gate that swung partially open. With great trepidation, I walked through after questioning whether or not the door would lock behind me. I decided it wouldn’t, and as far as I could tell, it didn’t. I continued on towards the building. The door was open, and I could immediately see a stairwell leading down to the basement. There were a few people there and a lot of chairs facing one wall. The people were gathered in groups towards that wall and each group conversed about something or other, but since there were three groups, I couldn’t make out one subject over another. I sat in one of the chairs. I was the only one sitting. I sat and watched the babble mingle in something stereo, but somewhat more so. Someone from the middle group noticed me. I felt like I was there to open an account at the bank, the way I was sitting there. I had no patience for banks. They’re a bunch of thieves, that lot. He walked up to me. He was well dressed, with the flash and sizzle of buttons on his shirt. He was lanky and talked heavily with his hands, though his gestures were never in very good synchronization with his voice. It was wholly distracting and highly dissatisfying. “Is there anything I can help you with, sir?” he asked me in a completely non-threatening way. This was offsetting, but I cannot say why. I have good instincts if I concentrate on them hard enough. “I was just checking out the place. The gate was open.” I remained seated, though I was trying to relay the neural information to my joints to head back for the door. It was relaying somewhat slow, as it does sometimes when you’re sitting and all bent at the joints. My nervous system was like a kinked hose at the time. “Well, why don’t you stay a while?” His eyes never left me. His gaze had a hold over my nerves. His head would turn as he talked, but his eyes would stay pointed still in their sockets. “We’re always looking for more guests at our events.” “Perhaps, but I’ve fixed myself into a pretty comfortable routine, and I don’t want to do anything to jar it.” “Let me put it this way, then,” he said while fixing his glasses with a calculated pointed finger, pressing the specs back up to the bridge of his nose. “Have you noticed that fireworks displays are getting more disappointing every time that the fourth of July comes around?” I shrugged. I was tripping pretty hard on acid last Fourth of July. It was peculiar though. That was months ago. “Well, let’s just say that it has something to do with the air.” He started pacing, still craning his neck towards me. He had a skinny neck. His Adam’s apple bobbed up and down inside it. “The founder is well respected in the sciences. It is our belief that the quality of air is deteriorating. We’re really, at the heart, an environmentalist group. We believe that advocating cremation as a post-mortem option and even in extreme cases as methods capital punishment or assisted suicide for terminally ill patients.” I didn’t have time for this mother fucker’s half assed theories about air, but I politely excused myself. “I must be on my way.” I forced myself to stand against wobbling knees, using the backrest of one of the folding chairs in front of me for support. As I walked away, each leg shook thrice with every step. The stranger called back to me. “We’re having a big event here tomorrow. You should stop by. It’ll be a blast.” I turned, briefly, and acknowledged him, but then I turned back and left. His eyes were on me the whole time I walked up the stairs. I returned home that night and smoked a cigarette on the porch. It had been raining heavily, and there were no pigeons out. I wondered where pigeons went in the rain. I hoped it would circulate an influenza among the feathery, arrogant pests and their lack of fear and respect for man *** I went out for my morning cigarette again, and the pigeons were out in full force. There were the two perched on the adjacent screen door, and there were more pigeons gathered on other balconies. They were dressed for work in laundry I had seen pinned to clotheslines; laundry that had previously seemed to large for pigeons. Nevertheless, there they were, impeccably bedecked in shirts and slacks. Some of the pigeons were out smoking their morning cigarettes as well. One puffed at a cigar. “This whole town’s gone to the pigeons, damn it!” I exclaimed redundantly to myself. The pigeons acted as if they hadn’t heard me. The unlit cigarette still wedged between my lips, I huffed back inside and headed down the stairs for my walk. I couldn’t stand to be out there. I could hear them chattering away about me as I slammed the kitchen door behind and locked the deadbolt. “There’s too goddamned many pigeons moving into this neighborhood,” I cursed to myself, circling around the now upright kitchen table. The furniture had been previously rearranged again and now lay in the path of doorways and appliances. I grabbed my greatcoat from the coat rack and barreled down the stairway. As I walked down the boulevard, the yards seemed not necessarily emptier; deader. The man who asked for a cigarette wasn’t there. I hadn’t expected him to be there. The black man with the consolidated face full of lines sat on his porch. I assumed he was just resting his legs, but I wasn’t for certain. He didn’t notice me. I thought of saying hello, but I walked past. He seemed to be staring off, but there was nothing worth staring at today. In the third yard, the man with the unruly eyebrows was not up against the fence. He wasn’t anywhere else in the yard either. Still, there was the big, waddling idiot. He started shifting his mass towards me, but I sped up and walked further on a few yards. His unfittingly high pitched inhalant of a voice lacked the confident volume to catch me as I passed. Further down, smoke was rising into the air. Everyone enjoys a good fire now and then, I thought as I pressed on. My steps hurried, though my left leg was weaker, so I had to take two left steps for every one I took with my right. I could see that the building had been burning for a while. The fire hadn’t yet engulfed the brick exterior, but the support-work had caved, and the building was falling apart from the center. The fence gate was drawn open. In the burning, I smelt something peculiar. It was akin to bacon. I wasn’t thinking of heroism at the time, but I bolted in because I heard voices. I thought it seemed like the right thing to do at the time, but then again, I never planned to stay in as long as I did or pull anyone out. It was all risky business, and the smoke was starting to irritate me. Inside, I ran to the basement where amidst the coughing and the interjections of sacrifice, there was a droning chant fit with individual bursts of staccato. There were men and women, adorned in closed robes cut like greatcoats. They were naked underneath, and every so often, one would faint, and the others would remove the fainted one’s robe and discard it without any attempt at resuscitation. With the loss of members, the intensity of the chanting swelled. “What the fuck are you doing?” I choked out, attempting only to exhale as I spoke. “Are you all out of your minds?” Some continued chanting. Others turned to me. I recognized one as the man who I had talked to earlier. Another was the man with the eyebrows. He was naked on the floor in a corner. I thought I saw his mouth move a bit, but it was probably only an illusion caused by the smoke that was drifting through the basement. The goateed Hispanic was there too. He had stopped chanting. “Ah, I’m glad you decided to stop by!” His gaze returned to me as if it had never left. And yet, somehow his eyes followed the robed gathering from the corners. “You’re all going to get yourselves killed!” I rationed. Members of weaker faith but stronger tolerance seemed to agree. Many looks were exchanged between the remaining members. I noticed some of them were even children as they were holding ice cream cones. “You all better come with me.” A few began to shift. “Nobody is going anywhere! And you’re staying here with us! It’s for the good of the earth, and besides, it’s proven by science! The founder has done extensive research on the matter, and…” He seemed adamant, but not everyone was so resistant. Some moved towards the stairs. “Where the fuck even is the founder? Not even here? In that case, you all better come with me right now…or else.” “Or else what?” defied the man with the gaze. “Or else I’ll stab all of you!” I brandished a sharp knife I carried with me at all times. These days, to be safe, you should conceal a small pistol on your person while in public. I preferred a knife though- makes you look a load more like you intend on using it. I never had actually stabbed anyone with it, but I threatened a tornado away on multiple occasions with just a knife. I was wearing an eye patch at the time. It probably mistook me for a swashbuckler. Tornadoes are oftentimes like that. “I’ll stab you in the ribcages!” I added on to make the knifing sound even more unappealing. Finally, one man went up the stairs, and others, acting upon his initiation, began to stream out behind me. The children were ushered out first, and when it became clear that everyone was leaving, the first man gave in reluctantly. “Fuck it,” he sighed. “Can you help me bring this man upstairs?” Together, we dragged the guy with the eyebrows up the stairs and dragged him across the lawn. Some of the first few to leave had already gone home. The whole idea of a suicide cult is never really much more than folly gone out of control. They had all taken the idea far enough and could now look back at it with a renewed sense of yearning for their reckless youths. The ones that stayed, myself included, watched as the foundation crumbled and the walls had fallen to ash or rubble. We could see into the basement now. One had remained. I had not seen her in the basement before. She seemed to have simply appeared there after I left. Her hair was tucked into the back of her robe. I looked at the new wreckage created by the further demise of the building. A rescue now would be far more difficult. The fire had grown quite a bit. “We have to go back for her,” I commanded. I apprehensively started towards the door, but the man with the stare grabbed me by the arm. “No! Leave her.” “I know what I’m doing. I’m an off-duty firefighter!” I lied. I don’t know why I lied. I normally wouldn’t have been so assertive in my charges. I felt it had to do with the pigeons; some sort of newfound human survivalist urge. In actuality, I hadn’t the slightest clue as to what I was doing, but if people had been setting themselves ablaze over lackluster firework displays, I needed an army to rally behind me to drive the pigeons out of the neighborhood. I figured posing as an off-duty firefighter would impress the lot of them. They seemed bored and easily impressionable. “I said to leave her! I want to see what happens.” His grip tightened around my forearm like a snake coiling around its prey. As I loosened my resistance, he loosened his hold, and both of us stood there watching as she began to ascend. Nothing about it seemed very spiritual in nature. It was all a very scientific ascent for lack of a better term. As she rose, the garment disrobed from off her shoulders. It fell into the fire and burnt easily. Her thick, wild hair flashed out in all directions and refracted a different color on the spectrum as her body angled and spun beneath the waxing sun. She looked like a mermaid, unsheathed from her wavering gill. Her face wasn’t like one in magazines. She was a remarkable beauty; perhaps the most beautiful woman I had ever seen. My still robed peers stood looking up at her ascent with open mouths. The man who stared closed his eyes. He let out tears. He bent to his knees and put one hand on the ground, feeling the heat that had conducted through the earth. Maybe I should have apologized on the strange chance that I had ruined their salvation with my misguided intentions. Instead, I walked back home and didn’t say anything to anyone. By the time I got home, I was convinced that I hadn’t seen any of it. It was impossible to tell with all the smoke and all the commotion. Or it was probably all just the pigeons. The next morning, I was out for my morning cigarette. It had finally stopped raining for a good couple of days now, and there were no pigeons out. It was like there never had been any in the first place, except for the pigeon shit all over the woodwork. When I went for my walk that morning, I started out in the opposite direction and have done so every day since without hearing any mention of the fire or suffering any disturbance from the neighbors. Everything went along quite nicely; much more nicely than I could have expected, anyway.
