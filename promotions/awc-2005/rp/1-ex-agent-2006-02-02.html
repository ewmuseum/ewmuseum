[1] Ex-agent

2 February 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2/2/06 It isn’t every day that the Central Intelligence Agency allows a field soldier out of their grasp. And on the rare occasion it did happen, the media was left in the dark about it, even though they pleaded for information on the covert operations on a daily basis. This was no exception – rumors of a defection from the CIA were surfacing, and every news team across America was getting their hands dirty for information. Of course, the competitive nature that revolved around weekly television ratings didn’t help the cause … in this case in particular; it was known that CNN – who laid claim to the news mantle – had nobody behind closed doors in regards to this potential breaking-news story. However, just like news stations were infamous for their greed, the U.S Secret Service was just as renown for its secrecy. We play with the tools God gives us. Nobody was bound to beat the CIA at its own game, but ultimately, there would be no lack of trying. January 23rd, 2006 10:52 AM Langley, VA The press conference room was packed with people. The noise surmounted, cameras flashed like taking pictures was going out of style, and reporters had their pen poised to notepads, ready to explode into words. It was clear that only the top news broadcasters in the world had access to the conference, and a few faces confirmed this. One of these faces was recognized as America’s President, who wore a fake smile and shook hands with people all over the room. Something made it clear that this wasn’t a presidential address, but the man himself was in attendance. His presence made the event that much more significant, and every news reporter in the room was on the verge of creaming their jeans upon hearing detailed answers to their pompous questions. The rooms suddenly fell silent at the appearance of a man emerging from a side door. Professionally dressed in a suit and tie, all black, his graying hair elegantly disheveled to give him the “I’m important” vibe. He ascends the short flight of stairs to a slightly elevated platform that is complete with two tables and chairs lined neatly behind them, facing the people of the crowded room. Pulling out a chair near the middle of the tables, he pulls his tie anxiously and gives a weak smile. To an observant onlooker, this was a feeble attempt at looking comfortable. The faint circles underneath the man’s eyes suggested he was tired, and like he would rather be anywhere else in the world. He sat down in his chair and wiped his brow. At the sight of the water pitcher and glass that sat in front of him, a glimmer of joy flashed across his eyes. He filled the glass quickly and drank it quickly, then glanced over his audience. “I will now-,” he said hesitantly, but trudged forward. “I will now take your questions.” Chaos erupted among the group of reporters. No physical contact was made, but an outburst of loud voices filled the room, everyone with their hand raised high in the air, clenching their pens like they were going to fly away. The man took another long drink of his water and leaned back in his chair, sighing heavily. He pointed at the reporter in the front row and motioned him to speak, and the rest of the room fell silent, with the occasional click and flash from cameras. The front-row reporter spoke quickly and clearly, “Mr. Graff, is it true that you’ve had a defection from the CIA?” Mr. Graff smiled slightly, almost chuckling. “Defection, no. Somebody leave, yes.” Once again the crowd bustled with questions. The man in the spotlight quickly pointed to another random reporter. “And what is the reason for him leaving?” he asked, scribbling furiously in his notepad, just like all his other companions. “The agent in question has a … how can I put this … controversial past,” Graff replied, choosing his words thoughtfully. Shit really hit the fan at this news. Graff, expecting this, pointed boringly at the next reporter. “What kind of controversial past?” she spat excitedly. Graff leaned forward, propped his elbows on the table, and ran his fingers through his hair. He then propped his chin on the palms of his cupped hands, and tried to answer the question. “It’s a matter of the CIA’s image. Nothing more, nothing less … I will not further explain the situation,” he said confidently, and then pointed at the next reporter. “Isn’t the CIA’s image being threatened enough by the publicity of this recent happening?” “We like to keep our publicity low, normally, but this circumstance calls for different procedure …” “You make it sound like it’s … particular,” the same reporter pushed forward, looking for answers. “Everything is particular in the CIA,” Graff explained. “Let’s try and refrain from questions that we already know the answers to, okay?” The last reporter, who is almost unfazed by the insult, doesn’t further the subject. He sits back down and scribbles something in his notebook. Graff looks at another broadcaster and nods at them. She takes the opportunity and stands up. “There is gossip that the agent in question is an American citizen, but born in Canada. Is this true? Does it have anything to do with recent events?” she shot at him, almost accusingly. Graff replies, almost taken aback, “I’ll answer the second question first. No. And if he was born in Canada, it makes little difference.” The female reporter falls back into her seat, looking unconvinced. The remainder of the audience with unanswered questions – which is a lot of them – and even some reporters wanting to ask a second question, put their hands in the air. Graff runs one hand through his graying hair, then rubs his eyes, trying to look as normal as possible. This proved to be difficult, though, because the impatient group in front of him was eyeing his every movement as if they suspected him of something. He took a long drink from his glass of water, then put one hand in front of him, motioning for silence. The reporters in front of him respected his request, and a subtle, temporary silence fell over the room. “I will answer one final question,” Graff stated simply, with the tone of man who seemed almost tired of his bidding. Nobody showed any mercy, however, and noise erupted in the room again. It seemed every individual wanting to be chosen had a question of the upmost significance. Surveying the room, Graff hoped that the obvious choice would stick out like a sore thumb. He almost convinced himself that he would have to make the decision himself, until he looked at a seated man on the far side of the room. America’s President had one hand raised in the air, his facial expression calm and collected. Graff cringed regretfully – he had been waiting for the President to put him on the spotlight, and if it was his choice on the matter, he would pretend that he never saw the President requesting to question. But he caught his highest boss’s eye, and this made the expectation binding. He felt obliged to allow a man of such importance ask his question. Graff put his hand in the air again, motioning for silence, then nodded towards the President. “Mr. President, sir?” The President let his hand fall to his lap, then looked curiously at the press conference’s celebrity. “If this situation is as controversial as you claim, then why has it only been brought to your attention now? Why wasn’t something done beforehand?” he asked, the tone in his voice friendly, but frighteningly serious. Graff avoided eye contact with the President, and tugged at his tie. He felt like a spotlight was beaming down on him … a criminal trying to escape with no way out. Only, he wasn’t a criminal … he was a mere man expected to fill the position of a powerful man. Well, with power came consequence, Graff had learned throughout his many years in the CIA. There had been times before where he had been asked questions like this, questions that he knew the answer to, but couldn’t put into words without it blowing up in his face. This situation was no different. The President had asked a very well-thought question, and expected an answer of similar quality. Graff knew he couldn’t provide that without going into painful details that would result in painful consequences, like losing his job. This wasn’t in the job description, he thought to himself, fidgeting with his hands, trying to ignore the obvious fact that everyone in the room were staring at him like a black man at the KKK. He looked at his assistant, who stood respectfully by the door that Graff had entered in. Graff wore the expression that screamed, “Help me!” but both of them knew there was nothing he could do to get Graff out of this hole. Only Graff could do that. “I was prepared for this question,” he lied, then went on, “and knew my reply would be far from easy and probably not satisfying for you people, in the same respect. As CIA director, it is my task to ensure the safety of our agents and personnel at all costs. A recent series of events have resulted in having to reconsider his position, and a compromise was made. Needless to say, the man’s past was made controversial by these events, which put us here at the CIA in a tight spot. Normally we’re better at getting out of those than we are at getting in. Nothing is simple about this situation, and I’m sure more details were expected. However, the gist of it has been made prominently known, and I hope that clears things up,” he finished unconfidently. He sighed heavily, hoping that his response was satisfactory enough for the President, and enough to prevent him from losing his job. The idea of compromise attracted him. Once again questions were thrown at Mr. Graff, even though he had stated that was he final question … every reporter seemed to think otherwise – that their question was significant enough to bend his claim. Graff didn’t make any motion to anybody. Instead, he simply pushed his chair back and stood up, which was enough to silence the bustling crowd. “I have nothing further to say,” Graff announced. He headed towards the side door as an exit, the same way he entered. He looked older, more tired, and more worn than ever. - Laura Williams was a reporter for FOX news, attending a press conference that was sure to be the climax of an intense and controversial last few days. She had spent her entire week Langley, Virginia, waiting for the day of the conference, and was aware that it could be a turning point in her career. This was something she had been waiting for almost too long. She was the type of woman that would stop at nothing to get what she wanted, and she was damn good at it, too. Think Veronica Corningstone off Anchorman, with, if possible, a little more spunk and a lot more bitch. She could plaster a fake smile across her glossy lips with the snap of her fingers – as long as it would aid her in getting her story, and it helped that her cameraman Jerry was on the shortest, tightest leash known to woman. Sitting in the back of the packed room, she was in particularly bitchy mood today, for she had been stuck with such an awful view of the front table. Mr. James Graff was the center of her attention today, just like everybody else, and Laura had taken significant time to dig up some information on him. Even after pulling a few strings it seemed she couldn’t find much on him. At least not enough for Mrs. Williams, that’s for sure. She knew that he was General Director of the CIA, lived in a condo in the suburbs, married with no children, and probably had satellite television and a goldfish. The only thing she could find about him that would raise eyebrows is that he apparently attended Kung Fu lessons every Tuesday. She knew that underneath all of the bullshit that had been swirling around over the last couple weeks was a story. There always was. Political and judicial systems were infamous for denying, lying, cheating … you name it. Anything to keep their names clear was done; she was convinced there were people hired to take care of this business. Bill Clinton provides a prime example, but Laura didn’t like to think about that – she unfortunately didn’t get to the story first. As a reporter, this was like getting picked last for the kickball game. But this, this could be the main event – a CIA agent mysteriously leaving the ranks, with no trace of motive until recently. Rumors of a defection filled reporters-like-Laura’s heads, and made them smile hopefully. This wasn’t like some FBI cop caught selling drugs. This was the big-time, baby, the mother-fucking CIA … agents didn’t just come and go like that. They were usually life-time members, who died on the job or were sensible enough to retire before people caught wind of their true identities and capped them. This was one story Laura wasn’t going to let pass her by. Other reporters may be willing to give in to Graff’s big words and glorified bullshit, but this bitch wasn’t. But still, she paid painful attention to every word that came out of his mouth, like a tyke watching Barney on a Saturday morning. She had pages of scribbled notes almost word for word what he was saying, with her own observations filling the margins (if there was any left). She noticed how nervous he was. It was too obvious to miss. Anxiousness and nervousness were two weaknesses that newscasters like her thrived on, and this guy was gravy. He wasn’t exactly the strongest public speaker, either, so his mistakes were fluent. Laura saw right through the big words and intelligent sentences, and knew they were a feeble substitute for something better. Leaning back in her chair, glancing at her last paragraph as James Graff prepared to leave the conference room, she sighed heavily. She needed to somehow get behind the scenes … bend the rules a little bit, in her favor, so she could get a well-deserved step-up on her target. Right now she was behind the game, and Laura Williams just didn’t sit well with that. Her attention turned to a man beside her. He wasn’t familiar, but his appearance was odd in a way to catch someone’s eye. Mysterious would be the most fitful word for him. This man almost seemed as nervous as the director, and Laura thought that was odd – why would a reporter be nervous? Wait, she thought, what if he wasn’t a reporter? This could be the break she was waiting for … the man clearly did not belong in the room, and Laura was convinced of this when he slipped mysteriously out of the room as soon as the conference ended. She turned to Jerry, her cameraman, who stood beside with his equipment propped on his shoulder. She bumped his arm and he leaned towards her. “What?” he whispered, wondering what was up. Laura Williams nodded her head to the back door that the man had just exited through, and stood up from her chair. “Now?” Jerry asked, almost surprised. “Don’t ask questions,” Laura said. “I’ll explain later.” Laura grabbed her coat and walked briskly out of the room as the man from before just turned the corner in a hallway; away from the lobby. Mrs. Williams’s eyes lit up, and she picked up the pace, cursing her high-heels for clicking obnoxiously on the hardwood floor. Jerry followed her, walking at a similar pace but in an awkward fashion because of the camera that was clutched under his arm. Laura was just about to turn the corner into another hallway when she halted, putting her hand back to stop her co-worker and peaking around the corner. She saw the man hesitantly look behind him and retreated behind the wall in the nick of time. Upon hearing a door click shut, she moved around the corner into the hall and walked quietly up to the door the man just entered. Taking a quick look to her right to a dead-end with an exit-door, she strained her ears for any sign of somebody coming. Once convinced they were alone in the hallway, she pressed her ear to the door, anxiously hoping to catch the tiniest detail that would make her the happiest woman on television. She tried to keep her excitement in check as she finally heard voices. “What are you doing here?” demanded a man, who Laura recognized easily as Graff. “I needed to know what needed to be done was done,” another voice replied, whom Laura could only assume was the man she was pursuing. “You could have risked everything,” Graff said, almost disappointed. In this very moment of eavesdropping that reporter Laura Williams smiled menacingly. She had a theory, and with each line they spoke added support to it. The man with Graff inside was the agent that the director spoke of in the conference. It was almost too good to be true. She refrained herself from jumping for joy and strained her ear as the conversation went on. “I’m leaving,” the man who Laura thought to be the agent, rather, ex-agent. He hesitated, then went on, “I’m going to-” “Don’t say it,” Graff interrupted quickly. “Could be bugged. Here, write it down.” Outside the door, Laura Williams almost said it out loud. Shit! She mulled over the situation quickly, however, then retracted from the doorway, motioning towards the exit door at the end of the hall to her right. She ushered Jerry to follow her, who looked bewildered. “Where are we going?” he whispered, confused. “A few minutes from now, that man is going to walk out of this door and leave for somewhere. We’re going to follow him wherever he goes, even if he jumps on a plane and goes to China,” she hurriedly explained in a hushed voice. “And who gave you that authority?” Jerry shot back at her. Laura Williams gave him a sharp look. “I did.” She turned on her heel and quietly pushed open the door that led to the outside, and the cameraman promptly followed her. “But ...” “But nothing! This is my story, and I’m going to get to the bottom of it!” - Inside of the room that had been set aside for James Graff for the day, Charles Kensrue leaned over a desk and quickly scrawled a note on a piece of paper. The former CIA agent finished abruptly, handed the note to Graff, then moved briskly towards the door while Graff examined it. Grand Rapids, Michigan Atlantic Championship Wrestling... look me up.
