I'm Mikey O'Reilly

28 February 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2-27-06 & 3-2-06 Author's Notes: This rp was e-mailed to Pierre on the 27th of February to conceal its secrecy, hence why there is two dates in the 'Date Made' section. Anyway, here's my FRESH! rp for the 28th of February. Hello. I’m Mikey O’Reilly, and I’m about to debut in this promotion called the Atlantic Wrestling Club. I have quite the interesting story to tell you, from my childhood, to my bare-knuckle boxing career and even what’s been going on in my everyday life today. The life I once lived was in poverty. Born the sixth child of Wendy Cavanaugh and Shamus Farren on January 12, 1973, you could imagine the household was quite cramped. When I was born, we lived in poverty, which I’ve already previously stated. Living in Belfast, Ireland back in the seventies wasn’t easy. My parents weren’t bringing in the money they wanted and not everyone in my family ever went to high school. The men - my father, my brothers William and Thomas, and me - never went as far as high school. When he got out of middle school, we were destined to work when we hit our teenaged years. It seemed to me that the men weren’t allowed to get an education, but to work dead end jobs that would get us nowhere, eventually leading our lives into obscurity. The women - my mother, my three sisters Vanessa, Tammy and Barbara - were the only ones in my family to finish high school. I remember stories of my mother and two of my sisters - I believe it was Vanessa and Barbara - entering college. My mother wasn’t successful, went about eighteen months then dropped out. But my sisters did four years and now living lives as doctors, I think. I’m proud of my sisters making it that far in their lives. Money was tight, money was almost scarce, but we somehow managed to live life to the best of our abilities. Trying to feed, cloth and give the education needed for six children wasn’t an easy chore. There were days I remember not eating for days, wearing the same clothes several weeks straight and bathing wasn’t easy to come by. Not only were important necessities were hard to come back, utilities were a pain in the ass. Hot water almost never existed, same with electricity. We would spend months in the dark with no heat, almost freezing to death. I remember that my sister, Barbara caught pneumonia and almost died. Doctors understood our dispositions, gave us free medicines, got us the best health insurance and had medical bills wiped clean. But enough of that past, for now, anyway. I’m gonna bounce back and forth in my life story, so keep on your toes, I don’t want to confuse you, unless you’re easily confused, that sounds like personal problem to me. Right now I want to talk about my career in bare-knuckle boxing, try to spice my life up a bit. Prepared to me amazed...or not, I really don’t care. In the spring of 1986, I was thirteen years old. I was juggling junior high school with a job delivering newspapers. I wanted to quit my job so bad because the boss I had was an asshole. Since I was a young boy he thought it would be a kick in the ass to screw me out of most of the profits I’d earned. I never caught on at first, but when I started to realize the lack of money I was getting, I had a feeling something was up. It was time to get what I deserve. Harold McGee wasn’t the easiest person to work for. The fifty-six-year-old fat bastard was sitting cozily behind his desk, reading a newspaper with a cigar in his mouth. The man had a large beer-belly gut - hence why I called him a fat bastard - bloodshot hazel eyes, thinning gray hair and had more chins than a Chinese phonebook. He was wearing a pair of worn blue jeans, a simple black shirt and shit-kicker boots. He had his feet on his desk, chuckling at the comic section he was reading. This was the most opportune time I could ambush him with my demands. I marched into his office, slapped the newspaper out of his hands and looked him dead in his bloodshot eyes. “All roigh’, ye fat bastard, gimme the money I was supposed t’ make.” His eyes narrowed with anger. He put his feet down, slowly standing up, elongating his six-foot-three inch, three hundred-plus pound frame. He slammed his meaty palms onto his desk, hard enough to make marks on it. He bent down more to become eye level with me. “Just who the fuck do ye think ye are, ye lil’ piss-ant?” he began, staring down at me with the rank smell of whiskey on his breath. “Ye don’t come into me office an’ disrespect me! I outta kick the fuckin’ shoit’ outta ye!” I stood my ground, not faltering at all. I was too angry to cower from a man the size of Harold McGee. If I wasn’t running on adrenaline at that time, I would’ve gave him and walked away from his office. But I had an agenda: get the money I deserve from the tyrannical asshole or fight for it...literally. I wasn’t going to back down from this. I made it this far and I wasn’t backing out. “Ever since I stared workin’ for ye, I gave ye satisfactory work, if ye know what ‘satisfactory’ means. I’ve been the best paperboy ye’d ever had, ye said so yerself. Tha’ made me feel good. When I got that first paycheck, I was happy to get it because my family need all the money we can muster. But after awhile, the money I kept gettin’ was less an’ less. Then I put it all together - ye’ve been fuckin’ me out of me money! Why would ye do such a dastardly thing t’ me? Why would ye jerk me around like tha’?” Harold smiled down at me, chuckling at my courage, my anger and my conviction of wanting the money I wanted. “Ye really wanna know why I’ve been playin’ ye?” “I think I have the roigh’ t’ know.” “It’s ‘cause I always fuck me employees over. Yer the first one to notice that ye weren’t making the money you should be. I admire your courage, son. But if ye want yer money so damn so fuckin’ bad, what are ye gonna do about it? Run home an’ cry to your momma? Beat me up? If it’s the latter, I’d like t’ see you try.” I clinched my fists and Harold noticed it. He started to laugh, thinking that I was stupid enough to fight him. “You’re too predictable, Mike. Do ye expect that I’m gonna believe tha---” I cut him off with the hardest punch I could throw. I caught him off guard with a straight left hand right in the face, as he toppled backward onto the floor, holding his nose. I looked over at Harold McGee, who was yelling in pain, as blood gushed out through his fingers. I punched him hard enough in the face that his nose was gushing blood. He looked up at me with his tear-filled, angry, yet fearful eyes. I half expected to see him get up and run me over with his three hundred-pound frame, but he sat here. “Ye broke me nose!” he said, his voice was muffled, as he was still holding his nose. “Ye broke me fuckin’ nose, ye fuckin’ little shoit!” I advanced toward him, cocking my right hand back, about to strike him again. He held up his hands in defense. “Wait! Don’t hit me again! Jesus Christ!” He backed away from me, getting to his feet, wiping his flattened, broken nose with his handkerchief. He winced every time the hanky touched his nose. “All roigh’,” he said in a nasally voice. “Ye win. I’ll get you yer money, every cent I owe ye. Gimme a couple of minutes. I’m gonna go over t’ me vault. Stay here.” He disappeared from view, going into the back of his office to go to his vault. I heard the vault knob clicking every time he turned it. A few seconds later, I heard the sound of him opening the vault, counting to himself then closed the vault. He came back to his desk, presenting me with a lot of money, about three hundred dollars. “There ye go, Mike. Tha’s all the money I owe ye. I’m sorry that I screwed ye over.” “Thanks,” I said. “I’m gonna take this money, go home and find meself another job. I quit.” Before I could leave his office, Harold stopped me. “Wait a minute, Mike,” he said, smiling at me. Was his smile genuine? I wasn’t sure, but I didn’t really care either way, I was out of his tyrannical back pocket. “Ye should be a boxer. That punch was the hardest punch anyone had ever given me. You have a gift, Mike, and I think you should showcase that through boxing.” I looked at my left fist. There was some of Harold’s blood on it. Perhaps he was right. “Ye think so?” He nodded. “Aye. I know of a boxing promoter who does bare-knuckle boxing. The man’s Samuel Hagen; he’ll take you under his wing. He used to box himself. Here, lemme give you his credentials, his address, etc.” He took out a slip of paper, wrote down Samuel’s name, address, past credentials and everything else I needed to know about him. He handed me the slip of paper. “Go see him soon. Tomorrow, perhaps. Don’t make it any later or ye’ll never see him. Good luck, Mike. I really mean that.” “Thanks, Harold.” I turned to leave from Harold McGee’s office. Before I exited the office, I turned to face my former employer one more time. “Ye know, Harold, yer not such a bad guy after all.” Before he could reply I left his office and went home. The next morning, I did what Harold McGee told me, look for the bare-knuckle boxing promoter, Samuel Hagen. I never told you this earlier, but I’ve heard of Samuel Hagen before. He was a big boxer back in the mid 1940’s in Belfast. He was, perhaps, the best boxer ever to come out of Ireland. He boxed all over Ireland, all over Europe, then eventually all over the United States. In 1960, he retired from the boxing world after a career-ending kidney injury that needed to be surgically removed. In 1965, he decided to become a boxing promoter, but not in professional boxing. He wanted a different change in the world of boxing and started to promote bare-knuckle boxing. Later that same year, he opened up a legit boxing gym for people to train, to learn about the fundamentals of boxing and the like. I tracked down the boxing promoter, who was standing outside his gym, smoking a cigarette and drinking a beer. The man was roughly in this seventies, but was still in incredible shape. He stood about six feet tall, maybe weighed around one hundred and ninety, perhaps two hundred pounds. He was baldheaded, with worn, leather-looking skin and pale green eyes. He had a scar underneath his right that was about two inches in length, maybe a quarter of an inch in width. He was wearing a pair of jeans, a white tank top and tan boots. I walked over to the old man, smiling politely at him, extending my right hand to him, gesturing a handshake. “Hello, sir. Are ye Samuel Hagen?” His eyes shifted over to me, gave me the once over and then looked at my right hand. He didn’t bother to shake my hand. “I might be, son. Who’s askin’?” “Name’s Michael Farren, sir,” I began, a little nervous when I was talking with the greatest boxer alive. “My old employer, Harold McGee suggested that I should come lookin’ for ye. I’m interested in becoming a boxer, a bare-knuckle boxer.” Samuel gave me another once over, letting out a small grunt. “Yer a bit scrawny to be boxer,” he said, searching for my name. “What did ye say yer name was?” “Michael Farren, sir.” “Hmm...” He seemed to trail off after making that grunting hmm. He was mauling something in his head, I could tell by the deep crease in his forehead. A few moments later, he became more animate. “Ye don’t look like a Michael Farren,” he began, looking at me with scanning eyes. “In fact, ye look like more of an O’Reilly. Aye, tha’s it. Ye look like an O’Reilly. Tha’ should be your boxin’ handle - Mikey O’Reilly.” I pondered over the name for a brief moment. Mikey O’Reilly, yeah, I liked the name. I thought it suited me well. I could read it on the marquee: Mikey O’Reilly, Irish Bare-Knuckle Boxing Champion. “I like it,” I said to Samuel. “Never been called Mikey before. Been called Michael, Mike and Mick, but never Mikey.” Samuel smiled, revealing yellowed teeth. “It suits ye, son.” He started to walk towards the building. He looked over at me, beckoning me to come in. “C’mon, I’ll show ye around.” The gym looked like your basic gym. The boxing ring was smack dab in the middle of the room, there were punching bags on the left and right walls, exercise equipment, jump ropes - everything needed to work out and train hard. There were various boxing posters from all over Ireland, ranging from Dublin, Belfast, Galloway, and everywhere else in between. “This is where we train,” I heard Samuel said. “If ye want t’ spar with any of me boxers, ye spar with gloves.” I looked at Samuel quizzically. “I thought this was bare-knuckle boxin’.” “Aye, it is, me boy, but I’m not gonna allow me boxers killin’ each other with their hands. Safety first. Anyway, the criteria here is quite simple: ye come every mornin’ at eight sharp, an’ start trainin’. Ye’ll go through a strict trainin’ regiment, tutelage by our best tutelary and ye’ll be done by two, so ye’ll be trainin’ six hours a day. Tha’s not gonna be too much for ye, is it?” I shook my head. “No, not at all, sir.” My gaze was set in the ring, where a large, blond-haired, green-eyed teenager, was sparing with someone who was close to his size. But the blond teen I was the most interested in (and not in a sexual manner, mind you; I have a girlfriend). This kid was huge. He stood about six feet five, probably about two hundred and forty, two hundred and fifty pounds of fit, athletic muscle. He looked like he was chiseled out of granite. He had short, spiky blond hair, emerald green eyes with stern, yet handsome good looks. “Who’s tha’ blond in the ring?” I asked Samuel. “Hmm? Oh, tha’s Ian McLeod, he’s my boxin’ prodigy. Not bad for a kid who’s sixteen years old. The guy he’s sparin’ with is Neil Hogan. Ian’s our youngest bare-knuckle boxin’ champion, capturin’ three titles in two years. He’s been boxin’ since he’s about twelve, thirteen years old.” “Ah, cool.” I continued to study Ian McLeod, his footwork, his demeanor in the ring, his strengths and weaknesses. I’d once made it my mission to box against him someday. He’d be a good friend, and a strong adversary. A few moments later, the sparing was over, both Ian and Ted left the ring. Ian noticed Samuel, flashed him a smile and walked over to the old boxing promoter. “Hey, Sam,” he said, toweling himself off. “What’d ye think of me in the ring? Think I can capture another title?” Samuel Hagen smiled at his boxing prodigy. “Aye, me boy-o, ye did.” He turned to introduce me. “Ian, this is Mikey O’Reilly. He’ll start training with us.” I extended my right hand. “Pleasure to meet ye.” Ian took my hand and shook it. He had a good grip. “Likewise. Yer a bit scrawny to be a boxer, kid.” “Heh. Tha’s what I’ve been told.” “Do ye have any prior trainin’, Mikey?” I shook my head. “No I don’t. This’ll be me first time when I have meself me first match, and my first days of training, I might add. Harold McGee recommended me.” Ian made a face at me. “Tha’ fokin’ weasel? I used t’ work for him deliverin’ papers. He fired me because I wasn’t doin’ a good job, when in actuality I was. I got up early, delivered papers an’ he lied t’ me about the ‘unsatisfactory’ work I’d been doin’. I told him t’ fok himself and I walked off.” He stopped for a bit, smiling at me. “But tha’ man made me somethin’ better. Made me a bare-knuckle boxer, so I guess he did somethin’ roigh’. Why’d ye stop workin’ for him?” “He screwed me outta me money,” I said. “Told me he’d been doin’ it t’ everyone tha’ worked for him.” Ian cocked an eyebrow. “Not t’ me. Unless I missed somethin’ about it.” He then changed the subject. “But tha’ doesn’t matter anymore. So yer really interested in bein’ a bare-knuckle boxer?” I nodded my head. “Aye, I do. I’ve been told I have good punchin’ power.” Ian slowly flashed me a smile. “Really?” he drawled. “Sounds like a challenge t’ me. Come spar with me in the ring.” He started walking toward the ring. When he came to the realization I wasn’t following him, he turned around to see a shocked look on my face. I remember that my eyes were about to pop out of my head and my jaw could’ve literally dropped to the floor. I couldn’t believe he’d ask me to spar with him, spar against what Samuel Hagen called “his next boxing prodigy.” “Wha’s th’ matter, Mikey? Are ye scared?” If I didn’t had control of my bodily functions, my bladder and sphincter would’ve loosened. “Don’tcha think we’re pushin’ this a little too quickly, Ian?” I looked over at Samuel, who simply shrugged his shoulders. It appeared that he didn’t care what I did. “Mr. Hagen, shouldn’t I do a little trainin’ first before I spar with anyone? I don’t have any experience.” “It’s not like I’m gonna fokin’ kill ye, Mikey,” I heard Ian chime in from the ring. “I want t’ see your so-called ‘punchin’ power.’” I looked over at Samuel again, as if seeking guidance. He nodded, gesturing his head to the ring. “He won’t hurt ye,” Samuel assured me. “He wants to see your abilities to throw hands. Ye’ll be fine, trust me.” I was reluctant at first to enter the ring with Ian McLeod. I wasn’t sure if both Samuel and Ian were playing some sort of trick on me. When I sensed no danger or underhanded trickery, I entered the ring, looking over at Ian. He handed me a protection helmet, a pair of sparing gloves got into his stance. I fumbled with the equipment, putting the helmet on, putting the gloves on and had my gloves tied by the help of Samuel Hagen. Before I was to spare with Ian, Samuel gave me some advice. “Give him everythin’ ye have,” he said to me. “Tha’s wha’ he’s lookin’ for.” I gave him a small nod and got into my stance. It was a bit sloppy. I was slouching a bit, couldn’t really carry myself and I felt if I took a step toward Ian, I’d humiliate myself by falling on my face. I loosened up a little bit, moved around a little bit, starting to get a feel inside the ring. I was proud of myself to get this far. A few moments later, I saw Ian McLeod power-walk toward me, throwing a massive right hook toward my head. I managed to evade the punch by ducking underneath it and hustled away from the hulking teenager. I discovered that I had speed and agility to evade punches. I never realized I was that fast and that agile in all my life. I really must have a gift, until I was brought back to reality by a stiff left hand, a body shot and a left uppercut by Ian. I staggered backward, holding my chin. I looked at him with wide, tear-blurry eyes. I wasn’t paying attention to Ian; my mind was elsewhere. If I kept that up, I’d be done for. I shook my head, clearing the unwanted cobwebs that had accommodated inside my head. I kept my attention on Ian, my eyes locked on my sparing partner. I started to move around in the ring again, loosening myself up. He came charging at me again, with cruel intentions in his eyes and was ready to knock me the fuck out. I noticed he wasn’t impressed with what I’ve done so far in the ring - in fact, I hadn’t done anything yet. He threw a massive right hand. Without really thinking, I moved to his left, blocked the right hand and threw everything I could with a right hand of my own. The hard right hand made its mark, and I watched Ian McLeod fall backward. He landed on his back with a thud. I had managed to knock out Ian McLeod, Samuel Hagen’s boxing prodigy. My eyes were as wide as saucers. I started looking around, trying to see if I could find any help. There were so many emotions running inside me that I wasn’t sure what to do next. “Holy shoit!” I heard Samuel Hagen say. He entered the ring, checking on his prized boxing student. “Ian? Ian, are ye okay? Talk t’ me, boy-o!” Ian wasn’t responding. Samuel tried shaking him, tapping his cheeks and even checked to see if he was breathing. He looked up at me, tears brimming from his eyes. “Ye killed him!” he yelled at me. “Ye killed him, ye son of a bitch! Ye k---” Samuel was cut off by a moan emanating from Ian McLeod. His eyes fluttered open, letting out another moan. He slowly sit up, his head was pounding and his face was throbbing. “Oy, wha’ the fok happened?” Samuel smiled at Ian. “Mikey knocked ye the fok out, boy-o,” he said, giving him the once over. “Ye okay, boy-o?” He slowly nodded his head. He felt like he was sore all over, as if he was in a grueling boxing match. “Aye, I think so.” He looked over at me, taking off his gloves and protective helmet. “Jesus, Mikey, you knocked me out silly. Ye do have punchin’ power!” He let out a laugh, then gingerly got to his feet. His balance was a little off, but recovered on his own. “I believe ye found yerself another boxin’ prodigy, Sam,” Ian said, his eyes remained on mine. “This kid’s got a gift, I’ll give him tha’.” Then he turned his attention to me. “I think Sam an’ I’ll make you a champion. It’ll take time, mind you, but I believe you’ll be a bare-knuckle boxin’ champion. Me? Being a bare-knuckle boxing champion? At thirteen, that sounded like a good deal to me. A month had passed since that day, and I was ready for my very first bare-knuckle boxing match. At thirteen years old, I was both excited to have my first match, and I was scared shitless. I would be entering a world where I would be facing the top bare-knuckle boxing contenders, all experienced in the art of the sport and would be willing to dislodge my head from my shoulders if they saw fit. I was in the locker room area with Samuel Hagen. He was taping up my fists, making them rock hard to better abilities to knock my opponent out. Ian McLeod was in a different locker room, getting ready for his main event title match against a champion called Marvin “the Hammer” Sullivan. He got that hammer nickname because he would hammer away at his opponents until he either knocked him out, cause immense bodily damage or until his opponents surrendered. “How ye feelin’, Mikey?” Hagen asked. I took a deep breath and slowly let it out. “Nervous, scared, excited,” I replied. “I’m about t’ enter me first match.” “Aye ye are, boy-o.” He completed taping my hands, checked his worked and was satisfied with it. “I dunno exactly who yer facin’ but his name is Bob Harris. I’ve heard he’s a veteran fighter, been at this profession for about fifteen years. I dunno how big he is, how hard he punches or anythin’ else. All I can tell ye is t’ be careful and move around in the ring, okay?” “Gotcha, Sam.” We left the locker room, walked down the long corridor and entered the small arena where an enormous fighter was standing patiently in the ring. “Oh, fok me!” I exclaimed. “Tha’ man’s gonna kill me!” “Not if ye move around in the ring, he won’t. Hopefully the match won’t last no more than twenty seconds if ye use yer punchin’ abilities.” “Somethin’ tells me he’ll fokin’ kill me in twenty fokin’ seconds.” I slowly enter the ring, my eyes are locked on his behemoth of a bare-knuckle boxer. He had a compact built, not very much muscle definition yet he looked very strong. A small ring announcer stood in the middle of the ring, holding a microphone. “Welcome, ladies and gentlemen.” The man had an British accent. “Tonight is our opening bout. Standing to my left, from Birmingham, England, standing six feet six inches tall and weighing three hundred and sixteen pounds. He is a veteran of this sport, winning a super heavyweight title in England in bare-knuckle boxing and cage fighting. Please welcome Bob Harris!” The people in the arena gave a small ovation for Harris, sort of a mixed reaction emanating from them. “And standing to my right,” the small ring announcer chimed in. Fuck, I don’t even remember his name. Oh well. This was what he said: “Making his bare-knuckle boxing debut, from Belfast, Ireland, standing five feet, eight inches tall and weighing one hundred and thirty-five pounds. He is Mikey O’Reilly!” Silence. Well, I was new to the sport and no one knew who I was at the time, so I guess silence was better than getting jeered out of the small building. The small ring announcer - oh now I remember, his name was Bruce Wallace! - left the ring and the sound of the bell rang throughout the small arena. There wasn’t a lot of people in the arena so the bell reverberated throughout the building. I got into my stance, loosening myself up and took a step forward, watching the behemoth in front of me. Bob came after me, with malice in eyes. He threw the first punch, which I managed to evade and threw a good kidney punch for good measure. I knew that Bob didn’t feel anything since the punch was a little love-tap, showing Bob that I had the speed and agility in the ring. Bob Harris came lunging towards me again, throwing a straight right hand that found its mark, then finished up with a huge left hook. I fell to the mat, and the referee started counting. I managed to get up after six. Those punches caught me off guard. I got back into my rhythm with blood trickling out of my bottom lip. Bob made another lunge at me, but I was ready for him. I went forward, throwing the hardest right hook I could muster, connecting right on the jaw. I felt the jaw break after contact, then I watched Bob dropped to the mat, not moving after my right hook collided against his jaw. The referee started the ten count. After he said ten, I couldn’t believe it. I had won my first bare-knuckle boxing match! But it seemed to be an effortless match to me. I expected more for the veteran but he fell like any rookie starting out in the profession. Everyone was cheering for me, and after that, everyone knew my name from then on.
