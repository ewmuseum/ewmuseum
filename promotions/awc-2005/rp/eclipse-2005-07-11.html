The grass is a thin, fading green, flattened beneath the force of a thousand wills; the sky bends and warps at the edges and Ellis dreams. She’s exceptionally good at it, which is to say that Ellis only dreams in the misty, thin land between consciousness and sleep, so that everything that runs by her eyelids is a little realer than it ought to be. More than once, Ellis has dreamed about dying, about opening her eyes only to see somebody over her and the quick, beautiful flash of light on the edge of metal before it was driven into her stomach. She had woken up with a phantom, pained sparking of nerves in her abdomen and a bloody film at the back of her throat. That day, she had watched her brother die beneath the shadow of a building, Logan at her right and Chandler at her left. They didn’t have time to give him a proper funeral, really, no time to even set up a pyre and burn him and save the ashes or anything like that; Logan had covered the body and they moved him to a place so that he wouldn’t at least be robbed in death. They moved on. It is not as hard to get used to as everybody would like to think. July, and Ellis dreams in orange and red and fire. She dreams of Logan, walking at the edge of the world, dreams of her living on, talking, smiling, shaking, fighting, cooking, dying with a knife to the back and Ellis’ name hovering on an invisible precipice in the air before it falls, crashes into silence. This is when she wakes up. It’s still July. She’s still living. Ellis sees shadows float against her bedroom wall, falling haphazard and thin. Closes her eyes. Breathes out. Steadies her hands. She closes her eyes before she sleeps. It’s the last action she visibly recalls herself doing. Her eyes shut as Chandler brings up the drapes. Like everything that he has been trained to do, she is within his pattern, and hence respectful. He walks out of the room as she opens her mouth, and with him leaves the smell of smoked salmon and the pressure of his presence. Combined: a Chandler smell, as frightening as a hit and run, only less trite, because he’s the one hitting and she’s the one running, not at all like the wind; she’s too slow even to escape. Shut-eye and running, shut-eye while running. It isn’t surprising that she loses. Ellis realizes she might be blind. She gropes in darkness and ends up with herself, but at least the blind can hear, because she hears momentarily, when her legs give way from running too slowly. She hears herself falling and voices, in between the mechanics of her movement, like multiple tunes on several pianos, stretching her eardrums like gum caught on teeth. The first time she’s scared, the fifth time not as much. The tenth time she hears less of herself than the instrumental of people. By the fifteenth, she no longer learns to hear herself. She’s out of the pattern, and out of synchronisation. Her thumb triggers and breaks coordination with the rest of her fingers. It is a cacophony on her pianos as she closes her eyes, because the songs do not play as they are written. At the end of it, there is a track of silence. When the smell of salmon leaves with the open window, she forgets to apologize for the first time. She finally knows what it means when people say that the music saves, even though she hears more noise than music. Like she always does, she learns to settle for whatever luck she gets. She lies on her stomach and smells the salmon cooking all morning. It’s an oily sort of feeling that coats her, like humidity and everything feels like summer if she puts her mind to it, or if she takes the easy way out and turns on the AC or brings out the salmon to cook. She thinks, ‘It’s all so easy though I’m harder than my own hands’. What makes it worse is that her hands are stone from working too long, too much. At noon, she draws a cigarette box from her pocket and entangles her fingers into tobacco. She lights up, but doesn’t smoke it. Instead, she simply watches it burn down. When she was young, her father cooked salmon for her in the summer. He would tell her, “It’s only you and me and the salmon.” He was a fisherman by day, a smoker by night. It is only relevant that she burns a cigarette whenever the salmon grills, as though she can somehow forgive him for burning his own lungs to death; forgive him, that is, with the salmon, because the salmon makes her somewhat happy. She thinks of it, slippery silent on the stove, muscles still twitching post-mortem, the way the summer must rather die than play a comforter in her grasp. They buried him in summer too, with his face too naked to be serene, his body too still to show that his lungs had betrayed him in the end. Lacking a visage and good breathing materials, that was why he must have passed away. Face and lungs to see you through; hands are not important. She stamps the butt out on her living room floor. A gust of wind slips into a crack in her wall and blows away the ash and the remainder of her summer. It stings her eyes. To the left, the kitchen makes a sad sound. She thinks that at last, the salmon is burning. She takes himself there. Ellis only takes herself, not heed of others, and she is willing to pay for it. The graves are lined up one after the other like the columns of the morning newspaper, filled with content, and yet to those still alive, are only geometric patterns condensed into stone. Like feelings set in stone, one cannot run away from them, and Ellis is not different. When she returns from the graves she sets her hands into water. To wash them, and also, to make them drown. They balloon in water like the skin around her eyes are red, but like her hands, Ellis isn’t crying, she’s only wet. Outside, it is raining. Inside, she is still. From the window she seems only to be a far-off figure plunging palms into water, watching the stillness of the water not move. It only takes a falling object to make a ripple. If Ellis cried she would be full of ripples, but ripples are geometric patterns set into stone. Ellis has paid for visiting the graves; they are etched into her mind. She thinks she cannot escape from stillness, but neither does she want to, because unlike the graves, the concentric circles of water, remembering is formless. Remembering is safe. And more than anything, Ellis doesn’t want to forget. *** In the mornings, Ellis hates her life. She thinks she has misconstrued the understanding of developing a brand new era. “It shouldn’t involve waking up so early,” she snaps, but later, feels better when she drinks Starbucks, yells at the secretary. Then she flips through the file Landon put on her desk, and everything pauses, rewinds, stops. “I won’t do it.” Flick of the wrist: papers flying through the air and landing on mahogany, wind dancing between each sheet, causing the tips to ruffle. “Ellis.” “I won’t do it.” Agent Landon was already having a bad day. His wife was in labor, the coffee machine was having seizures, and he was currently sporting two different colors of socks: orange, blue, Florida pride. Needless to say, the CIA head’s nerves rapidly heightened when Ellis Nash, brash and headstrong, came sauntering into his office and had proceeded to steadfastly refuse the case. “Ellis, come on.” “No.” Howard thumbed at his temples, wrinkled with exhaustion. “Why? Why not?” “Because I’m not a detective. I‘m not the fucking NYPD.” He looked up sharply and moments later, sat back into the cushioning of his leather seat. “You know this case could get you back in the field.” “Homicide isn’t my specialty, Landon,” Ellis chided, narrowing emerald oculars, “you of all people should know that.” “We don’t know if this is homicide or not.” “Hello?” The brunette grabbed the papers and held them in the air, before letting them drop with a resounding thud. “Records clearly indicate a pattern between each occurrence. It’s simply a coincidence that each victim was a part of SA?” “I’m not saying it is. But you have to look at every side.” “Every side? Jesus Christ, Landon. There’s only one side. Homicide, murder, slicing someone else’s wrist with a razor.” “You said it yourself, Nash. Suicide Anonymous.” “And it’s just typical that all four bodies were found in a bath tub.” “Where else are they going to do it? The spa?” “I’m trying to prove a point.” “Great. Now get off your ass and go prove it.” Ellis blew a bubble with her gum, smacked it against the roof of her mouth. “Don’t want to.” “Look,” Landon leaned forward with a petulant sigh, “I need, well. I need someone like you on this case.” “Someone like me?” “Someone who won’t take no for an answer.” “Clearly you are someone who doesn’t take no for an answer, as well.” “Ellis?” “What?” “I’m begging you. Take this case. You can work with Chandler, you can work with Vanessa. I don’t care, just take the damn case.” Crinkling of eyebrows, suspicion abroad. “Why are you so headstrong about this? What don’t I know?” And an unconvining alibi. “Nothing.” “What don’t I know?” She tried again. “You know everything there is needed to know. Four victims, similar wounds, simultaneous calls dispatched to the same hotline. That’s it.” “I don’t believe you.” “There is no alternative to believe.” “Will you give me a pay raise?” “What?” “Give me a pay raise,” the brunette stated, righteous. “This is ridiculous, Ellis,” the elder man declared, perturbed. A pause, and Ellis suddenly smiled, corners of lips pressing dimples into cheeks. “Just checking. I’ll take the case.” “What?” “I’ll take the case.” “Then ... what was all of that ...” Ellis ignored him, stood up. Grabbed her walkie-talkie, “I need a unit outside, pronto. Copy,” walked out. *** Translation: There is one day where she picks up a stone and throw it into a lake. The stone bounces once and it is a record throw. When she throws it again it doesn’t, it simply floats on the surface of the lake. She thought, stones should not be able to do that. Stones are heavy, stones should sink. Someone taps her on the shoulder and tells me, “You threw a leaf.” She asks, leaves are not round or grey or heavy in my palm? Should they be mistaken for a leaf? She thinks not but Ellis is blind right to the ends of the earth. Someone tells her, this is a metaphor for a leaf. She thinks there is much potential in metaphors, like lying. Lying gives her much opportunity to invade upon her own integrity the actual factual nature of a farce, and similarly, a stone is a leaf or only what she thinks it to be. A stone is not a leaf, she says, persistant. Then why is your stone floating? Someone asks. Someone points to the center of the lake. It is because it’s not, she informs the someone. My stone is skipping so fast for you to see, over and over, like how I learned to skip one hot afternoon with a skipping rope made of rubber bands, it’s skipping with all the effort it has, so that it can’t possibly sink. People go bankrupt, someone told her, and as much as she did not want to believe it, that was true too. There’s no insurance for the unthinkable.
