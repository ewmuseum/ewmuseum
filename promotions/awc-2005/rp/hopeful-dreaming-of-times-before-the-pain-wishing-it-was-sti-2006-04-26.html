hopeful dreaming of times before the pain -- wishing it was still the same

26 April 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: April 26, 2006 dean was seventeen when he won a ‘76 F150 in a fight, a fuckugly maroon-red bitch with a huge V6 inside, yeah, she could haul ass. it took more than just finagling to get the tags, the insurance, but she was his, bumper to bumper. he kept the title in his wallet, nestled among a wad of credit card receipts, each one with a different name. gonna sleep in the car, dean announced the night he brought her back; dad’s eyebrows came up but he nodded, kip’s head came up out of his book, and he nodded too. * dad used to watch his sons sleep. they both had unique sleeping positions that hadn’t changed much over the years. kyson’s was neat, pristine. his body stretched straight. on occasion, he’d curl a leg up, or move a hand beneath his head, face impassive, head tilted slightly to the side. even the blankets he slept under were neat, pulled up to his chest, straight and settled. dean’s had always been the exact opposite: mouth open, limbs sprawled, blankets messy and scrunched and untucked. he knew the way they slept like he knew how to assemble a rifle in under 30 seconds. it was a habit, something he didn’t have to think about. but whenever he did, whenever he paused to look, he’d fumble, he’d fumble so bad it brought him to his knees because looking at them there, so vulnerable and innocent, so unaware and open, he wished things could be different. he wished he didn’t have to die. it was finalized in dad’s mind that he wouldn’t be walking away from this one. there’d been a time when he thought it was possible, when he’d been able to live with missing limbs, scarred faces, broken bodies, but that was gone now and dad knew it, knew it in the bottom of his heart. his sons thought they’d been saving him by making him stay, by sucker-punching him with family ties and pleads and resistance to let him go. but that and with his sons there, together, it was the exact opposite. with his sons there, dad was sure to die. now, it isn’t that he doesn’t have the will to live, and damn if he doesn’t wish they can kill this thing and be the family they once were, damn if he doesn’t want to watch his sons go on with normal lives, get married, have kids and grandkids, but -- but there’s something far more dangerous, now, playing in his head and in his heart, something he’d felt his entire life and had wanted to keep out, away. - there was a time when they didn’t sleep in the same bed, but dean barely remembers it, barely remembers what it was like to have his own room, his own space. when he was fourteen he asked about it, Don’t you think it's weird, Dad? and dad scratched his beard and said, No, not particularly. Soldiers bunk down together. gonna sleep in the car, dean said, and kip nodded and fetched their two sleeping bags, their one pillow; it wasn’t hardly the first time. so many times they’d slept together in the back seat of dad’s Malibu, wrapped up in a nest of Indian blankets, kip throwing elbows in his sleep. nothing new except the car and the dirty illicit free feeling that came with the jingle of the keys. when kip crawled in behind him, slammed the truck bed’s door too hard, dean was pissed for about five minutes before he sighed and gave kip a shove. I get the right side, he said, and kip shrugged. S'okay, kip said. ** dad used to watch his sons sleep. he watched them shift and settle, he watched them put their lives in his hands and trust him to get them all out of this. dad had never liked lying to his sons, but this was a lie he couldn’t get around. if they knew, they’d never go along with it, and their minds would drift off each other and focus on him and it could get them killed, so he kept it to himself and silently asked whatever God was listening that one day, they’d forgive him, and that they’d understand what he had to do. one time, kyson shifted in his sleep and rolled over, eyes opening slightly. Dad? dad said, Go back to sleep, Ky, voice rough with emotion. kip didn’t pick up on it, still half asleep, and nodded and turned his face towards dean and settled down again, -- and dad watched his sons sleep until morning. -- In a cracked Motel mirror somewhere between Conroe and Los Angeles, Dean looks at his smudged reflection and wonders who’s really to blame for all of this. After a minute he decides that accusing someone won’t change what’s already happened, so he turns his attention elsewhere - and although covered in so much blood, he knows his is dry and theirs is fresh. His blood is crusted brown against his neck and shirt collar, and the wounds are swollen and pink; their blood glistens in crimson smears against his skin, wet on the fabric of his shirt and jeans. A single red bead slides down his forehead, curves across his black eyelid, and dribbles down the side of his face -- -- it mocks him, almost, like the tear of the devil, and -- and when he turns to search for a new pair of clothes, Dean’s careful to step over the body behind him. Lyle Hainsworth’s eyes stare, wide-eyed, straight at him, and -- and Dean knows his blood shouldn’t be singing from the thrill of dealing death, but he’s a Brown -- -- such is their way. *** for dean’s eighth birthday, dad gave him a gun. --- now, in his death bed, dad won’t count on his fingers the number of disappointments he’d given them in place of Christmas presents and birthday gifts. he won’t remember ky’s expression when he pushed him away with harsher words than he had planned, towards the life that centers around ky’s own dreams and desires instead of dad’s loss and fears. he won’t remember dean, petrified, pointing the gleaming gun at the man about to murder his baby brother. he won’t remember the recognition of failure and self-loathing too ancient for a boy dean’s age, twenty-nine, sinking in to dwell in his — Caroline’s — eyes, reflecting back his father’s sins for the world to see, if they only knew to look. he can’t tell kip that it killed his heart to find no stocking in sight last Christmas, and the similar wound festers when he remembers a sole misshapen slice of kip’s seventh birthday cake saved at his place on the deserted kitchen table, the cake an illustration of the painful dedication of a doting elder brother in its avant-garde decoration of Lucky Charms on uneven smears of chocolate frosting, by then all dried up and flaking off in bits and pieces. he’ll never admit that he is what he has lost, the sum of what he can no more be. **** In a plush mansion on the edge of the Pacific, Kip tosses and turns in his sleep, mouth open, limbs sprawled, blankets messy and scrunched and untucked, and the ink under his skin burns. ---- dad won’t remember the moment when the media reported the death of one dean brown, serial fighter, jackass, killer, because he won’t be alive to remember it in the first place. ***** Riverside to Los Angeles is 91.5 miles, an hour and thirty-something minutes if you go sixty and keep adding water to the radiator and you hold on to Saint Christopher so tight that you have a halo on your palm by the time you cross into Los Angeles. Dean stops somewhere along the way and pops a nickel and three dimes into a pay phone, jabs in the number he’s pretty sure is etched into his heart right about now. Riverside to Los Angeles is 91.5 miles and Dean is on Dean time while Kip is on California time, so when the phone rings Kip is halfway into his dream about the Mini Cooper with the Union Jack topper. “Dad’s dyin’, Kip,” says Dean. “Tell me some’en I don’t know,” murmurs Kip, scratching his chest. The ink still burns. He swings his legs over the bed. “We need’a do some’en. We need’a do some’em about them.” “Then we’ve needed to do some’en about ’em our’tire lives. Dammit, Dean, nothin’s changed. Dad’s goin’ die and there’s nothing we can do ‘bout it.” “There is.” And Dean hangs up the phone before Kip even begins his next breath. ----- dad wasn’t invincible. that fact became glaringly real to death after kip hauled ass out of conroe, when he hopped the car for los angeles. dean was sure that his dad had been injured before, but it wasn’t until kip left that he started noticing how dad’s left shoulder sagged a little more from an old war wound or how he didn’t seem to bounce back as quickly from being thrown into walls or through windows like he once did or, hell, that he walked with an almost unnoticeable limp when it rained, from years of abuse on his body. dean suddenly realized how much more closely he’d have to watch dad’s back, how alert he’d have to be to make sure that nothing happened to him - either of them, because now it was just them; kip wasn’t there anymore to produce long, complicated explanations to get them out of a fight, or hand them ammo when they needed to reload, or stitch dean’s wounds because dad had clumsy, awkward hands that could never hold the needle steady enough. one night dean escaped to the bathroom where everything was dirty and rusty and just fucking nasty. he leaned his forehead against the mirror and took deep, calming breaths, knowing he was going to have to face up to the fact that his father was sick. daddy brown, the toughest, bravest, gutsiest, ballsiest man he knew (aside from himself, of course) was sick and in trouble and he didn’t know how to fix him, how to make him his father again. I can’t lose you, too, he murmured against the mirror, so faint he could barely hear himself and fogging it with his breath. after he breathed deeply, splashed some water on his face and stepped outside the bathroom and watched his father shift in the bed, bathed in the yellowish light from the bedside lamp, and pulled his phone out of his pocket.
