Several Short Stories Revolving Around Elevators

7 November 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 7th November 2006 in the year of Our Lord I had spent quite some time riding up and down elevators, and I had been in most of the elevators in town and could tell you about what they all were like. For the most part, they were all pretty boxy with a few architectural exceptions when an elevator would be half cylindrical for aesthetic purposes. Some of them could fit about six people if they packed in tight enough and none were too large. Others could fit as many as twelve, even in winter when people wore bulbous overcoats. Some elevators are humble with solid walls, and others have reflective gold walls so that the cabin appears twice as big on all sides, albeit also twice as crowded. In the high rises, there were whole banks of elevators that took you to other banks of elevators, and they all had small screens in them with the weather reports. Most of these elevators were sectioned off and not available to the general public, but I had ways of getting around the barriers as anyone who wanted to ride one of these elevators badly enough could tell you. Now, when most people ride elevators, it’s because they want to go up or down, but it’s a common misconception- modern day mythology- that this is the only purpose. I have found the elevator to be a quite useful and versatile tool with numerous overlooked functions that I have taken advantage of quite cunningly. For one, a spacious elevator is the perfect place for a nap. The hums and whirs of the pulley system sing mechanical lullabies and allow a few hours of relaxation on the bobbing elevator floor, gently rocking me like a sleepy sea captain in the rising and falling tides. Elevators are also a fairly decent place to meet women. A woman can’t immediately run away from you if you meet her on an elevator. It’s speed dating on methamphetamine. Why, in one day, I dated two hundred seventeen women in three and a half hours on an elevator. Nothing serious came from any of it though. I came close once. I was on the twenty eighth floor of a building downtown, and the elevator was making its descent when the most beautiful woman I had seen all day walked into the elevator. I shuffled nervously with my hands in my pockets and looked at the screen with the weather report. “It sure is a good day outside,” I remarked, looking at the sun icon, partially covered by a cloud. “Yeah,” she said. “It sure is.” I could feel the sparks awkwardly dispersing through the cabin, or maybe it was just the paint thinner, but I knew I had to act quickly because the elevator was fast approaching ground level. “My name is Johnny,” I said with a smile. “It’s good to meet you.” She looked somewhat nervously at me and backed away a step or so, although there was not much room to back up even though the reflective walls made it seem like there was. “It’s good to meet you, too” she said, and she looked me in the eye, so I knew she wasn’t lying. But the opening doors and the dinging alert cut short that magical moment where our eyes met and briefly, in that one small piece of the universe, locked. She left the elevator, but turned a bit when she noticed that I hadn’t. “Are you getting off?” she asked me. “I will be when the doors close,” I responded honestly. She looked a bit put off as the two metal doors slid together, constricting my view of her until she was swallowed into the black crease between them. I never got her name, but she looked like an Allison. I much preferred elevators to escalators, though, as I found the latter to be dizzying and had some discouraging experiences riding them. While on peyote, once, my perception was all jumbled and I couldn’t tell which escalator was moving up and which one was coming down. Needless to say, I had left my choice up to chance and had chosen the wrong one. My pace evened out with the opposite flow of the escalator, and I ended up walking for an hour and forty five minutes without moving up or down. All the while, people were getting frustrated around me, and many of them started yelling at me until they got tired of yelling. That had nothing to do with elevators, however. Another benefit of elevators is that cell phones don’t work inside them. There’s some mechanism that scrambles all the signals or something. I don’t know the science behind it or anything, but a doctor once told me that spending at least an hour a day inside of an elevator would prolong my life by about fifteen years. All the signals and waves from cell phones and the like that can’t penetrate elevator walls supposedly cause brain tumors. All the time I spent in elevators kept my brain from developing these bubbling deformities, and I take pride in keeping my brain in working order at all times. So, I ride in elevators and on occasion, fill out sudoku puzzles. Sometimes I do both simultaneously. I was waiting for the woman who looked like an Allison, one day, but she never ended up riding the elevator. I had brought a sudoku puzzle so that she could see that in addition to my good looks, I also was a deep thinker; practically a philosopher. I was a bit disappointed that I didn’t see Allison, but I got over it quickly when another woman walked onto the elevator; equally beautiful, but in a much different way. She had short hair and a very professional demeanor to her. Usually I don’t find myself attracted to short haired women out of fear that my tastes are becoming more androgynous, but I do tend to make exceptions if the short haired women have particularly fair and feminine faces. “It looks like rain today,” I observed, noting the rain cloud symbol on the weather report screen. She kind of nodded, I think. I’m sure she acknowledged me, but maybe she was a bit irked at this reminder. I noticed she didn’t have an umbrella with her. Reacting to her body language, I quickly changed the subject while holding up the sudoku puzzle to illustrate my point. Today’s puzzle was easy, and I was able to fill in all the squares without using any even numbers. I never trusted even numbers. They were just too even. “I’m a lawyer, you know.” I held the sudoku puzzle up so that I was sure she could see it. “Oh, yeah?” she asked, but disinterestedly. “That’s nice,” she followed. But I could tell that she was just making conversation and that she didn’t think it was nice at all. It was obvious to me then that she was only interested in my body, and she probably didn’t understand the concentration and cleverness it took to fill out the sudoku puzzle. She wasn’t an Allison. She was a Colleen or a Doreen, or some other name that ended in “een.” I tried to exit gracefully as the elevator descended. I pushed the button for the thirteenth floor in the middle of the ride, and the elevator came to a stop. I walked off of the elevator, and thought of saying bye to be polite, but when I looked at her, she was staring off at one of the other walls. As I stood in front of the elevators on the thirteenth floor, waiting for another elevator to arrive, I thought of how important human behavior on elevators may be for sociological purposes. There is a certain uneasiness that comes about with elevator interaction that I have somehow transcended, probably from my high elevator lifestyle. I’ve heard barely acquainted coworkers force out the strangest topics of conversation on elevators. I distinctly remember a tall Asian man. He was young and well dressed with his hair gelled up as was the fashion. His coworker was a shorter white woman, around the same age, with nothing really distinct about her appearance. They were talking about cereal. “I saw a commercial for Kix cereal the other day. Do you remember Kix?” “Oh, yeah,” replied the woman. “Kid tested mother approved.” The Asian man laughed politely as the woman recalled the cereal’s slogan. The doors opened, and they walked off together. I found it strange there, how the two of them boarded the elevator, rode it for two floors, and left together. All the while, the sole purpose of their elevator ride revolved around a short and meaningless conversation about a cereal commercial that spuriously rose from the discomforting eeriness of the whirring, whooshing elevator cables. Many times, if there is a group on the elevator that is not too small or too large, there’s an unspoken shame, and everyone stands there in a naked, claustrophobic patience. However, if there are too many people in an elevator, everyone just focuses on their own aggravation and concentrates on how someone’s elbows keep brushing against their own. If there are too few people, however, and there is a vacuous, silent space in the elevator, people who have seen each other around become strangers in a very restricted area that needs to be protected with noise. Not everyone is as comfortable as I am with simply yelling at the top of my lungs during a long elevator descent. On continuous routes from a high floor all the way back to ground level, I have had to stop to take several breaths in between yells. I have found this to be beneficial to everyone on the elevator in most cases. Nonetheless, I was snapped from thought by the dinging alert of the elevator, coming to pick me up and take me either up or down its shafted oscillations. There was a man inside, and he held the door open for me while simultaneously and contrarily trying to divert me away. “I’m going up,” he informed me, expecting that I was going to the ground floor. “That’s fine,” I said, stepping into the elevator. He stood between me and the panel of buttons. Instead of asking him to move, I just kind of looked at him, studying him with a curious look; my head was cocked diagonally and I upturned one side of my lips. “What floor do you need?” He tried to pass his intentions off as polite, but I could see right through his transparent reasoning. That greedy son of a bitch just wanted to press the buttons. It wasn’t the childish fancy of pressing a button and seeing it light, either. He was proclaiming his superiority over me in elevator matters, and as someone who had ridden this elevator with a purposeful regularity, I took offense. I’d never seen him on the elevator before. Just who the fuck did this guy think he was? “Any floor will do.” I was trying to confuse him, and I could see it working. He looked over the panel, but he didn’t press any of the buttons. This was all fine by me. I was getting a bit heavy eyed anyway. Riding around on elevators all day takes its toll on you because of all the constant changes in altitude. It reprograms the natural body clock, and oftentimes I hop on an elevator for a quick nap when I need to calibrate my sleeping schedule in order to make commitments at times I deem inconvenient. I sat down in the far corner of the elevator, opposite the man I didn’t like who was still by the panels in case anyone else came in and tried to select their own floor. That bastard, I thought. Look at him trying to appear so polite and good natured! Look at him and his elevator interaction defense mechanism. Someone meek passenger would walk on, and he could say, “Which floor do you need?” And then the passenger would answer him, and he’d repeat the number while pressing it to make sure. Then the meek passenger would thank him, and he’d say “You’re welcome,” and the door would open. It enraged me. He was trying to pass himself off as an elevator operator, but he clearly wasn’t cut out for the job. He was a bit too stocky, and he was only middle aged. He had a big head, and the elevator operator’s uniform would stretch- the hat around the bulky girth of his head would rise high, making him look like an overweight circus monkey that should have been trained to ride a tricycle. It was these angering thoughts that slipped me into an elevator nap. I had carefully slipped into a dream state. It wasn’t a pleasant dream, and I remember something about my teeth and gums detaching from my jaw and curling around uncomfortably in my mouth so that my right molars pressed up against my left at the most inopportune of times. I’m sure there was some context for this, but I barely remember because I was woken up by the guy buy the panel whom I should explicitly mention was starting to become my least favorite person. “Oh my God! The elevator’s stopped! What are we going to do?” They say that the chances of dying in an elevator are less than the chances of being struck by lightning, although not as low as being struck by lightning while inside an elevator. I had never heard of anybody dying while inside an elevator, although there had been reports of people who had been crushed while fooling around in the elevator shaft. Those people, however, made the fatal mistake of not respecting the awe inspiring behemoth that is the elevator. I had heard of people being stuck in a stopped elevator before, but they had always been somehow excavated. I vaguely remember hearing about an older woman who had been trapped in an elevator for hours and had to embarrassedly relieve herself by doing her business in a Tupperware container. “What are we going to do?” he repeated frantically. He then took a cell phone out and starting dialing some numbers as I wiped the sleep crust out of my eyes. He ended the call and tried to resend it after the first attempt didn’t work. “I’m not getting any damned service!” “Of course not,” I sat without batting an eye. “Elevators are built with an impenetrable steel magnate that blocks cell phone signals, radioactive waves, and laser technology. Don’t worry about it.” I tried falling back asleep after hopefully pacifying the man’s fears, but he continued to panic. He tried pressing the open door button repeatedly, but we were in between floors, so the doors were sealed shut. Realizing it wasn’t working, he pressed the help button to call for assistance. The dispatcher said she was phoning the fire department to come in and rescue us. “Thank God,” he said. “The fire department is on its way.” I didn’t see the logic in this, and his composure was a disgrace to the elevator operators he had previously tried to emulate. Back before the lever and pulley system was perfected, elevator operators pulled the elevator cabin up manually by a rope. Those were real men. There aren’t many real men around today like there used to be, and he certainly wasn’t one of them. I hated this man. “Just sit tight; the fire department is coming.” He pretended that he had never been worried and that the fact that everything was under control was of his doing. What was with his dependence and unfailing faith in the fire department anyway? It’s not like the elevator was on fire or anything. He started pacing back and forth, and we hadn’t been stuck all that long yet. I wish he would have stopped pacing. He was making me nervous. If he kept on pacing, I was going to have to subdue him with force and relieve myself in his sandwich containers. “Can you stop pacing? It’s bothering me.” “Sorry,” he apologized, trying to stop. I could tell he wanted to keep walking as his hands were shaking and he kept looking from side to side, but he didn’t. Instead, he started talking to me about stupid shit like cereal. “I’m supposed to meet some clients for dinner tonight. We have reservations at that new steakhouse on the lake shore. I forget the name of it.” I really didn’t know what to say to him. I knew nothing of lake shores or steakhouses. Instead, I looked up at the weather report on the screen. “It looks like the rain is going to clear up tonight.” I said it with a defeated tone, not one of hope. This time, it was more of an attempt at ending a conversation than beginning one. I hoped the awkwardness of the entrapment would set in and we would both realize that neither of us had anything important to say to the other. He was so harried by the situation we were in though, that he kept talking, almost to himself, in order to take his mind off things. I just wanted to go back to sleep, though, and I was growing very irritated. “My name’s Pete by the way. Pete Livingston.” He extended his shaky hand out for a handshake, but I was still sitting, and my hands were covered in newspaper ink from the sudoku, so I showed him my inky palms and he ungracefully withdrew his hand with a series of half-decided motions. “Johnny,” I simply stated, trying to keep conversation to an impersonal minimum until the firemen chopped us out or floated the elevator cabin up with their pressure hoses or whatever. “Really?” he said, questioning my all too generous exchange of names. “I would have taken you more for an Alec or a Dustin or something with a little more edge.” “Hmm,” I murmured out, completely apathetic towards what he had to say. He noticed this, and tried to smoothly transition back to his worry to make it feel like he hadn’t just been adamantly disregarded. “Christ! What’s taking so long?” He was getting angry that he hadn’t heard any updates as to our condition. I could tell because his arms were now making larger gyrations while he was gesturing, and he had taken off his suit coat, revealing a very wrinkled shirt and a tie that seemed to whish back and forth along with his perception of our state of affairs. He pressed the help button again, and the receptionist assured us that the fire department was on its way and maintenance was trying to fix the problem. “I’ve got children and a wife!” he argued with the paneling. “Have they been contacted?” I did not have children or a wife, which was probably why he was arguing to the paneling instead. Still, I was level enough to coexist in this elevator. Even though Pete Livingston had dreams of being seen as an elevator operator, a man who could hold his own inside an elevator, and a man who was in charge while in the elevator, it was clear to me that I was the elevator’s true commandant. The elevator was my turf, and I did not fear it even in times of crisis. Soon enough, the maintenance men had fixed the problem, and the elevator moved again. Pete got off of the elevator, and I stayed on a bit more, looking for Allison or some other woman who might see my elevator confidence and fall madly in love with me. Neither of the women rode the elevator that day, though, and it was advised that everyone took the stairs until all the elevator kinks had been smoothed out. I never ran into Pete Livingston again. I hoped that maybe he had died, but he probably hadn’t, and it was probably just luck that I never saw him after that, because I probably would have pulled a knife on him, stole his wallet, and hoped he would identify me to the police as Alec or Dustin something or other.
