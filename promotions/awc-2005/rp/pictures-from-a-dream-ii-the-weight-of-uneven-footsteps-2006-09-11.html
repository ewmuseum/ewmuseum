Pictures from a Dream II: The Weight of Uneven Footsteps

11 September 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: September 11, 2006 There was a man I saw, some days, walking around at night pushing a rusty lawnmower down the sidewalk with a burlap sack dragging over his shoulder, bouncing lightly on his back. He wore overalls through town; overalls and a flannel shirt underneath. I found this quite odd, the overalls and whatnot, seeing as how around town, you never see anyone wearing overalls much anymore. Overalls were the type of thing you’d imagine people in the countryside to wear, even if they actually didn’t; overalls and straw hats. He had abnormally hairy arms. Most people do, but his arm hair was dark and nonetheless gritty with dirt and crust. Sure, he was crusty armed, so to speak, but who wouldn’t be given his circumstances? There he was, just limping down the road, his knobby legs creaking around in baggy pants, as he pushed his lawnmower around with a sack dragging behind. I imagine the lawnmower was off kilter a bit, having one wheel that was larger than the rest, but I can’t quite remember. Perhaps it’s just one of those images I’ve spun myself so that the whole picture fits a bit more. Some nights, as I stumbled drunkenly along the main streets, I’d see him sleeping on a bench, and I’d notice how small his frame looked, outstretched across the seating, contemporarily styled to further advance the veneer of clean modernity painted over layers and layers of other forced but failed exteriors. And some mornings, on my way to the bank or the café, I’d see him sitting asleep in front of the doorway of this abandoned shop or that one, a paper bag in his lap with his mouth wide open, breathing out fumes of malt liquor while the buzzing flies gathered. His unbent knees halved short, stubby legs. One leg was always shorter than the other, too, but as to which one bore the brunt of his limp, it was all determined by the equinox and the tilt of the earth. But despite all this, there was one aspect of his character that I found uncharacteristically obnoxious. He still had pride, and plenty of it. While the other bums bid their time deriving their pitiful routines and repeating their pleas for spare change, each stalking the ATM machine or the convenient store entrance, this deviant still had his pride. Rather than ask for change, he stood, dirty face on diminutive frame, and he swore at people who crossed his path. He cursed and he threatened, plenty of times, and sometimes he’d just stand there and laugh- a slow, graduated chugging like that of a train engine, from spacious chuckles to rattle lung coughing. Sometimes I counted to twenty in syllabic patterns when it was late and I was walking. I did this to keep people from talking to me. I did this from across the street, walking north as he walked south, pushing that lawnmower. “One, two, three, four, five, six, sev-en” “Hey, you fuck! You got a problem asshole?” “Eight, nine, ten, e-le-ven, twelve, thir-“ “Hey, asshole! Hey, you son of a bitch!” “-teen, four-teen, fif-teen, six-teen, sev-” He crossed over the sidewalk, running the off lawnmower over the high grass beside the curb. He then jumped the curb, the rickety mower crashing and clanking back onto the road’s pavement as he rolled it across the one way street, oblivious to the lucky lack of traffic that occasionally swerved to avoid his diagonally cutting (and yet un-cutting) path. “en-teen, eighteen, nineteen, twenty.” He neared my side of the road and I restarted my count as I turned my head to watch him mash the front of the lawnmower into the curb, trying to get it to bounce over with his crusty armed, aggressive thrusts. He offered a grunt with each thrust, rabid foam chalking out of chapped lips and webbing to a froth in his facial hair. And in the dark, it was very hard to tell hair from dirt as both were smeared about his face. “God damned arms. God damned leprosy!” he growled, his arms, waving like cartilage tubes. The sack still swung behind him, not weighty in the slightest, but now oscillating terribly with his turbulent attempts to push the lawnmower over the curb. I could hear the blades scraping against the angling of cement. Maybe it was the sound. It had to be. The whole grating scrape of rusty blade against cement sounded off like a beached metallic whale. It was the sea scrapes, it was! I felt pity for him. His name was Rebel; at least that’s what they all called him, and I knew from this and his pride that he didn’t want my pity, and if he had smelled pity on me, he’d only curse and threaten me, and things would be unpleasant again. I said something muffled, so muffled that even I couldn’t understand what I said. Then, I bent down to help lift the mower over the curb. The alert in me was somehow pacified by his unease and his clumsiness. His smallness was overwhelmed by his burdensome heap that he pushed and carried, not only with his luggage, but with the dirt and with the crust upon his skin and clothes. Most of all, though, his spine bent and cracked under the weight of his pride; that unnecessary volume to which he held most securely. “Git your grubby mitts offa my god damned lawnmower, you ass!” I chuckled a bit. Nobody calls hands mitts unless they are grubby, and grubby is the only adjective fit to precede mitts. Dirty or grimy or anything else synonymous just doesn’t seem to fit the island description. “What’s so fucking funny?” he barked, before forgetting the condemning militancy of his question. Because then, he started laughing that oncoming train laugh, and before long, he had invariably started coughing as the foam streamed to a drool off of his chin. The lawn mower tilted, unbalanced by the lopsidedness of its one rogue wheel, and it fell. The crash broke the fluid seam between laughter and the coughing, and we stood staring at each other. “What’s so fucking funny?” he asked again, completely oblivious to the interrupted spells of laughter and the coughing bouts. His eyes shone a fuzzy, germ colored yellow. The immediacy his eyes demanded flushed away my short term memory, and I was left unable to respond. “I’m not so certain.” And then he grumbled a short affirmation, forbidding the use of any vowel. He bent down to examine his overturned lawnmower, and I bent down with him. The blades still shook, and one was missing. “The doctors, they say I’ve got three more months. It’s the damned leprosy.” He stood up with his elbows bent and his hands on his waist. I glanced over him once to see if any miscellaneous body parts had fallen off, but he appeared to be in tact, save for a loose tooth that wobbled back and forth in the front of his mouth as he talked with white speckled spread lips. It allowed spit to push freely out of his mouth when the tooth shook forward, and when it shook back, he would jab at it with his tongue until he regained his concentration. His demeanor changed a bit. His threats and jeers failed to send me scattering, presumably due to the tactical errors of his advancements. The lines on the sides of his mouth unwrinkled a bit, and a dab of the foam fell from the uneven stalactites of his chin hair into a bubbling oval in a patch of dirt between sparse growths of grass, surviving the imperils of footsteps. “Care for a cigarette?” I asked, drawing a pack out of my pocket. I withdrew one for myself, and with a practiced movement, flipped it between my lips. I also left one pushed out of the box, halfway in or out so that he could pull it from the box himself or tap it back in. “No thanks,” Rebel asserted proudly. “I don’t accept handouts,” he continued as he took the cigarette out and bit down on the filter. I lit it for him. “You happen to have an extra cot? Something I can lay down on for a while? My bones been aching, and I should rest them proper.” I looked away, luckily in full faculty of my facial expressions. With the simple twitch of an eyebrow, I could pull off a completely different identity. While when dignified, I appeared Bohemian or perhaps a bit of a sniveling activist, a simple sagging of skin to imbalance the symmetry of my face could make me look just as vagrant as he. “No,” I lied, sacrificing the comforts of my own waiting shelter to protect my privacy. I put the pack of cigarettes in the opposite pocket, wedging them against my key ring to keep it from jingling and disclosing my lie. “Then you can stay with me. It’s gonna be awful windy tonight.” He awkwardly pulled his lawnmower back up, almost knocking it over the other way with the inertia of the lawnmower’s tilting carrying his frail body with it like a leaf in the wind. He turned the lawnmower with great effort and started pushing it on down the road as I followed him, the discrepancies between our heights furthered by the added inches of curb over the road. He eyed me suspiciously every so often, sometimes catching the corners of my own eyes wandering towards his shouldered bag, but he didn’t trust me enough to divulge its mysterious contents, and I didn’t know him well enough to ask. Instead, I made conversation about the obvious, and he obliged. “Oh, it ain’t my lawnmower. I found it out by the curb up the road a bit. It’s fucking garbage.” And then he spat, distanced from the conversation. “Fucking scumbag,” he muttered to someone passing by opposite from us. I nodded in non-confrontational agreement. “It’s always a good idea to be pushing something. Don’t matter much what it is.” His words rang inexplicably prophetic within me, but it was the kind of prophecy that only sounds nice when spoken or thought about rather than one that is imperative to follow. We walked a while more, past bars where last call lights were going on and past diner parking lots where the drunk ambled home, making brief stops to pretend to look for fights. Therefore, it was commonplace when Rebel and I jutted through the scene, him pushing his lawnmower and toting his bag, and I following behind with my hands in my pockets, smoking a cigarette through the sides of my mouth and straining to remain impartial from the smoke that rose into my eyes. “Fucking cock suckers,” he yelled at a group carelessly stumbling around one of the empty lots. From behind, I saw their button-down shirts were barely tucked in. They were all tall and well groomed despite their intoxication. There were three of them, still holding bottles of beer that they had snuck out of the emptying hub of bars. “What the hell did you call us?” one said, turning around to confirm his strength in numbers. “It’s just a couple of fucking bums, Brian.” But Brian was feeling warm from the booze. He was armored by his lack of inhibition. It was a disastrous scene; like a khaki pants commercial on a meth binge. “Why don’t you say it to my face?” And old Rebel, he couldn’t help it. He just smiled, revealing his dangling tooth, and he started laughing that oncoming train laugh again, but he stopped short of the arrival and spat to the side. “Fucking scumbags!” And then he raised a middle finger that seemed like it had a few too many knuckles. It wasn’t much of a fight. The asshole broke the bottle on the ground and stabbed at Rebel, who more or less turned into the jagged glass as it tore through his sleeve and cut his left shoulder. The three button-down shirts took off in a sprint, and they jumped over a fence before anyone who cared would notice if anyone had cared at all. Rebel stood watching the blood mat to his crusty arms, picking pieces of glass out of the edges of his wound. “Are you alright?” I asked, but he was too distracted by the sight of his own blood to care. He didn’t seem too bent out of shape over the whole ordeal. He was too proud to ask for food or drink. He had given up hope long ago and no longer needed food to live; he lived on spite alone, cursing and threatening emptily was his daily bread- and his wine, too, when he couldn’t scrape together change for the cheap wine that the convenient stores never bothered to refrigerate. “It’s that damned leprosy. The doctors say I got three months.” I offered to push the lawnmower for him, but he pushed me away. He said we were almost there. “I’ll be alright as long as I got something to push. You just leave me to my wits, and I’ll be fine.” He kept pushing the damn thing, and his bag hung behind him. We turned up a side street, and he found a dumpster, enclosed on three sides by a painted wooden fence. He stopped pushing the lawnmower and dropped his bag, and we sat down in the enclosure in the space between the dumpster and the fence. “I sleep here when it’s windy; see, because the fence blocks the wind. And then when it’s rainy, I sleep under the train overpass. When it’s cool, and I can tolerate it, I sleep wherever I damn well choose. And when it’s cold, I’m miserable, but I make it through alright.” Rebel had no use for timepieces. He needed only the sun and the moon to tell night from day. There were signals all over the place. The bars let out at two. The bells at town hall chimed on the hour. The trains passed by around the same time every day. We were thinking about the trains, I could see it in his eyes; about waiting for an open freight car and running alongside it and hopping in. But he couldn’t run fast enough on those malnourished broomstick legs, and there was really no sense in leaving now anyway without a good reason to. It’d all be too much trouble. He curled up in a fetal embrace around the bag, and I stretched on my back askew from him so that I was looking up at the sky but also so that I could see him peripherally if he made a sudden movement. I noticed that there weren’t many stars out tonight, through the tree branches, and of the ones I saw, I couldn’t piece together any constellational patterns. They all just kind of hung there in dim splotches against the blues and blacks and grays that the sky was made of. I thought he had gone to sleep; he was really good at being silent and blending in with the ground. Upright, he stuck out like a sore thumb, but on the ground he was chameleon and arboreal. Here, his dirtiness blanketed him in union with the earth and his speckles of skin that appeared through the filth of his hair looked now littered and out of sorts as he lay there. “I bet you’re wondering what’s in this bag, huh?” he startled me, the sudden slide upwards in pitch at the end seemed tacked on as if he needed to verify the own significance of his possession over the said sack. I didn’t know how to answer. It sounded like he wanted me to agree, but his defense over what was inside was apparent. I was afraid of setting him off, so I let him continue. “Well, I’ll let you know, it’s treasure, alright,” he boasted. “Not worth a lick to anybody that matters around here, though, but it’s treasure.” He sprang up with a spryness that defied his creaking abilities, and I sat up in reflex, fast as an upset mousetrap. From a sitting position, he emptied out the bag in a pile, and all it contained was leaves. It wasn’t the season, yet, for the leaves to be falling, and on the trees, the leaves were all still green. The leaves he poured from his bag were brown and crumpled. They had a brittle hardness to them, and they must have been from last autumn; almost a year spent in and out of that bag- somehow protected with the utmost care. “The fool!” I thought as I watched him there, peeking over his leaf pile. He threw the leaves in the air and watched them flutter back into the pile. He waded through it with his arms, tinges of wealth seeping through his skin and coursing up his veins through the cartilage tubes. I saw it trace through his grin, his teeth yellowing between a minted green and a late autumn brown. His loose tooth swung back and forth against the force of his inane hooting that caused his head to tilt back and his mouth to open wide. “The fool!” I thought. My blood boiled inside of me, but only inside as I appeared calm otherwise. Still, it irked me to the most irritable degree that this man could find wealth in such a depraved state. Gone were the curses and threats of a man who needed only curses and threats to live, and instead was his idiotic chirping at his trove of leaves. My veins bobbed up to the surface of my skin, toning it greener; envious that rotten leaves were enough for him to keep living. And then, I came to grips with my own sudden infuriation as I watched him note my confusion. Pity gripped me again as I saw that Rebel couldn’t fathom my lack of excitement, and guilt flooded up my spine as I had failed to appreciate the enthusiasm of his selfless revelation, however redundant or innocuously misguided as it was. “We’ve got to bury the lot of them so they keep,” he informed me, the signs in his eyes reading serious. He folded a leaf into his mouth, tonguing it to one side so that he had something to chew on. He offered me a leaf. I gratefully declined, but he insisted, so I was obliged to chew as well. It tasted bitter and had a flaky crunch to it; one that was fragile but not given to bending. “There’s a shed around the side of this building. I bet we could jimmy our way in and snatch us a pair of shovels, huh? We’d put ‘em back when we’re finished up, and nobody would know, you see?” I nodded, my mouth too dry to talk, the bitter graininess of the leaf now mixing to an oatmeal with my saliva. I pushed myself to my feet and followed him, the two of us slid in turn through a missing fencepost in the enclosure, ducking between the in tact horizontal slats. The shed around the side of the building was an old, wooden cube painted red and white, but the red chipped away and tanned to a more unnatural brown, like burnt yams. There was a hole in the roof, but it wasn’t large enough for me to fit through, and Rebel couldn’t get on the roof on account of his limp leg. The padlock hung closed from a chain, and it looked like we could knock it loose with a rock if we hit it the right way. We tried, but the padlock wouldn’t give. “Son of a bitch,” he cursed and spat, kicking at the corner of the shed with his long leg and almost falling over for his efforts. But we were tired, and we didn’t want to expend any more energy trying to find some shovels. We walked back around the building and lied back down near the dumpster. He gathered his leaves and put them back into the bag, and then he wrapped himself around it. Chewing the leaf was a tiring practice. I was confused as to whether I should swallow the gritty pieces or spit them out. Neither option seemed to desirable; they were already plastered to my gums. He muttered himself to sleep, swearing and cursing, and it kept me up for a while. After he was asleep he still swore to himself subconsciously. That kept me up for a while longer, but I was tired from all the chewing. I toyed at the leafy grains in my gum for a while. Soon enough, that lost its purpose too, and I just let the glasslike corners of the tiny flakes bleed my gums as I watched Rebel, looped and twisted around his sack as his hairy, crusty limbs stretched to their furthest lengths. There he was, the air whistled between his teeth when he inhaled, and he snored profanity as he exhaled. There he was, clutching that bag with his life. He never really slept. He was conscious of his position at all times, though sometimes he closed his eyes and kept them shut for a while. And sometimes he’d open them and he’d stare out for a bit, but he wasn’t fully aware of what was going on around him. The seeds of sleep dripped from the corners of my eyes and sowed themselves around me. The earth beneath me felt cool and dewy, and it made my arms itch. I bet his arms would’ve itched too, if it wasn’t for the layers of film. I questioned my envy for him, and I hated myself for it. And then I realized that there I was, sleeping in the same grass and chewing on the same leaves with the black sky overhead, sheathing the sun in shadow. I forgot about my itching arms and bleeding gums as I drifted off for a short while. I dreamt for once, and knew what dreams were for. I became alive in my dreams; alive and in control, more so than I had been in life. I was God and man and the devil, too, and so was he: all three or none at once. It was hard to tell which one he loved to be the most. When I woke, he was still asleep, clutching that bag of leaves. And my mouth was stinging and my skin itched. I only remembered stringy cobwebs of what I had dreamed, and when awake, it seemed so whimsical and childish that I damned it all. I threw my hat on the ground and stomped on it, because that’s what I thought seemed appropriate at the time. And then when I walked off, fitting my now misshapen hat back atop my head, I noticed that the lawn around the enclosure was groomed with more care than any lawn on the block. The weeds were plucked and laid in a pile, all facing the same way. The bushes were pruned to a happy compromise. The flowers all faced towards the sun. I imagined that this was what the landscaping was like under the tracks, too; and also near the other slumbering enclaves that he could never really call home.
