Stereophonics #0: 'Prelude'

24 October 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 24th October 2005 Friday 7th October would turn out to be a monumental day in the life of Tim Shipley – me – but as I rolled out of bed a good three hours later than usual (a delicious quirk of my sophomore year timetable meant my weekend started a day early) you could forgive me for not having guessed it. 7th October 2005 11:10 I pulled open the thin cotton blinds and struggled to remember why I was feeling so down, and then it came to me all in one and I had to reseat myself on the narrow bed I was so used to – my you-can’t-really-call-it-one-but-she’d-like-it-to-be-that-way friendship with the lady who was responsible for allocation of student accommodation had meant that I’d been able to keep my first-year room throughout the summer and now into my first sophomore term. Lectures had been running two weeks now but I’d barely been here (both physically and mentally) as doubling up with professional wrestling once more took its toll. The plan had been to bow out at the end of my contract on the last day of September but the events of that night, at AWC Testimony, had rocked the foundations of my twentynothing life and caused me to, knowing this would severely compromise my education, extend my contract with the promotion on a rolling basis. What had happened that night was the reason I was feeling so down... and you would be too, if what you’d taken as fact was shot to pieces, if man walked on water or breathed without oxygen right before your eyes. A man I had believed dead – everyone had believed dead – was alive, and it wasn’t just any man, it was my friend and tag team partner Tony Aliso, and he wasn’t just alive, he was after me. I didn’t know how and I didn’t know why and I didn’t know anything. And I don’t like that – not knowing. It’s why I studied Mathematics. The purest of the disciplines. x = 3, full stop. There’s no debate about it. It’s not wishy-washy like Sociology, where any interpretation is correct, or like Literature, where a comment as inane and inaccurate as “The placement of this semi-colon indicates anger” can gain a grade A. But now was not the time to dwell on my education, for the more I thought about it the guiltier I would feel. Today, I decided suddenly, I would put it all aside for twenty-four hours and just live. Forget commitments, forget the creeping worry that had been winding its way around me. It could all wait until tomorrow. Today, Friday 7th October, I would recapture childhood once more – no responsibilities, no bills to pay, and the freedom to do whatever I wanted. I turned on the radio, and without hesitation, moved the tuning knob for the first time in over a year. It didn’t want to budge, squirming and squeaking its desire to stay where it was. But I was firm, and I shunted it a good way along until some loud guitar replaced the static. And I smiled. I sat at my desk and symbolically pushed away my folders, clearing the space to lay my head down and take in the sounds of – well, I didn’t know what, and that was a change for me in itself. I smiled, perfectly aware of how teen-movie I was being, but happy with it. Why should I care? After all, today I was someone else. “...and that was the mighty Iron Maiden on RockNY! Next up we’ve got a mega contest, so calling all fans of the Stereophonics...” That surprised me; you don’t hear much Stereophonics in America. The new album Language/Sex/Violence/Other? had been an unashamed attempt to crack the US market but had only worked in part, so I was surprised to hear them mentioned on this radio show (albeit erroneously; they’re never called “the” Stereophonics) to which I’d never before tuned in. Perhaps my tuning knob would find a new permanent position if the unnamed disc-jockey played his cards right. “...the Welsh trio will be playing a sold-out show at Valentine’s in Albany tonight...” I sat up straight. They would? Why hadn’t I heard about this? You lose out from being on the road... Damn. A concert would have been the perfect way for me to get everything out of my system, forgetting my worries and just having fun for a change. But the DJ had already said it was sold out (as well as incorrectly calling them a Welsh trio; new drummer Javier Weyler was from Argentina)... and Valentine’s, a small venue on New Scotland Avenue, was always packed. There would be no chance of squeezing in – a notion I would never normally have dared to contemplate, but today was different, remember? My grandiose ideas of making this day dissimilar to any other were still alive and kicking. “...all you guys need to do to get your hands on the pair of tickets I have sitting in front of me is answer this one question: what was the name of the Stereophonics’ first album? Jeez, even I don’t know that one...” Well, I did. I lay back down and prepared for some Google-wired geek to call in and read the answer off his screen, so nabbing the last two tickets to the hottest gig in town. But that was what I usually did. Today... I would call in... The idea was ludicrous. Long-time followers of The College Chronicles will know about my nonsensical incompetence with something as simple as the telephone. Not long ago, the shriek of the wall-mounted outlet in my room would startle me to the extent that my heart would still be racing a quarter of an hour later. In the past year, I’d become more accustomed to hearing a high-pitched intrusion into my study, partly due to the frequency of AWC-related calls and partly due to actually having friends, but I could count the number of times I’d actually dialled someone’s number on one hand... and phoning a radio station? Completely out of the question. I could hear the sickening sound of blood pumping in my head as my sweaty hands – both of them, for security – grabbed the receiver. My vision started to star over as I punched in the number the DJ, whose name was still a mystery to me, dictated. I waited, the silence of static teasing my nervous brain. And then it rang. Twice. “RockNY Radio,” a cheerful female voice on the other end announced. “Hi...” I blurted out, then forgot what else to say. “How can I help?” she asked brightly. “I – Stereophonics competition...” “Oh, you’d like to enter the contest?” she deduced, a note of amusement in her otherwise pleasant voice. “Can you answer the question?” “Yes,” I breathed hoarsely. “Oooookay... could you do so, then?” the woman asked irritably. Unfamiliar with how live shows on the radio worked, I’d been expecting to be put through to the disc-jockey to give my answer, hence my idiocy here. “Word Gets Around,” I told her more confidently, having cleared my throat. “...actually, I haven’t been told what it is... so I guess that’s probably right –” “It is,” I interjected hurriedly. “Alrighty, I’ll put you in the queue and Devon will pick a line. Can I take your name?” “T-Tim,” I told her, a little confused. A queue? Hadn’t I already told her the right answer? “OK Tim, hold the line.” She rang off. The Arcade Fire was playing and I started to worry about how much the call was costing. It would be worth it if I won the tickets... but it seemed there was an entire collection of callers with the correct answer all waiting eagerly for the DJ – Devon – to choose their line. This was nothing more than a lottery, and I almost hung up right then. But then the song came to an end. “It’s time to find out who’s going to take the two tickets I have here for tonight’s Stereophonics show at Valentine’s, located on New Scotland Avenue in Albany and offering...” I tuned out as the DJ read some promotional spiel, ostensibly in exchange for Valentine’s having provided the prize, but my ears pricked up again with the words “caller on line three”. My hands went all clammy and the blood started to pump louder and louder and then I heard a crackle in the receiver and – Oh. I must have imagined it. Because I was listening to a caller. And that caller wasn’t me. “Hey, man!” the DJ exclaimed excitedly. “What’s up,” the guy on the other end drawled. “What’s your name and where are you calling from?” “I'm Trey, from NYC, brother,” my new worst enemy informed the listening public. “And are you a fan of the Stereophonics?” A pause. “Yeah, kinda...” KINDA? KINDA? Then hang up the fucking phone and let me have the tickets! “OK, so what was the name of the first album by the Stereophonics?” the DJ lazily read off. “Word Gets Around.” Damn. “Sorry, dude, that’s not the answer I'm looking for,” the DJ told him, sounding surprised. I was more confused than anything. What the hell was going on? And then it hit me. What might have happened... What might be taken as correct... given the unintentional international ignorance of the USA... “OK, we’re taking another caller...” And this time, no clamminess, and no starry eyes, because I was thinking hard and thinking fast, knowing that if it was my line I was about to win tickets with an incorrect answer... “Hey!” And this time, the exclamation wasn’t on the radio. It was in my ear. “Hi!” I let out a strangled cry. “Dude, shut off your radio!” DJ Devon told me. I obliged. “Sorry,” I muttered, realising too late the conflicting noises. “Do you have an answer for me?” “Performance And Cocktails,” I said quietly, closing my eyes and crossing my fingers. It was wrong and I knew it – Kelly Jones, Richard Jones and Stuart Cable had released the Word Gets Around album in 1997, a good two years before the more mainstream success Performance And Cocktails – but I was banking on the chance that Word Gets Around might never have made it to these shores. “You’re going to Valentine’s!” And it seemed I was right. 7th October 2005 11:47 I advanced cautiously into Gonville Place, building G. It was a curiously designed building on the edge of campus – of no discernible regular geometric shape, all brick pillars and whitewashed walls, small windows and grand doors. Gonville Place, its origins a hotchpotch of influences, housed most of the second-year undergraduates, except some, like me, who had remained within the central accommodation complex, and others who had chosen to rent privately off-campus. In a way, I felt I was missing out; the sophomores tended to cluster here rather than diffusing across the campus and Main Halls North got a bit lonely with only freshers I didn’t know to talk to. Okay, I'm not the world’s biggest socialite, but it had been nice last year to always have Sam, or Jake, or Mark around if I wanted them. Now a visit to any of the three entailed a lengthy trek across the muddy greens of Harbrook’s central campus. Sammie lived on the first floor – or what was here called the second floor, as we were in America after all – at the end of the corridor after mounting the stairs. An interesting numbering system meant one came to the highest-numbered room (17) at the start of the corridor, and moved down towards the lowest, Sammie’s room, furthest from the stairs. On this occasion I didn’t even make it to Gonville Place G9. I’d ignored the murmur of a male voice as I mounted the stairs, but just before I reached the first floor – or second, whichever you prefer – the peal of laughter following it was undoubtedly hers. Sammie Farrell’s peal. Frowning, I turned into the corridor – and froze. There she was. Hair chestnut-brown, falling carelessly across her shoulders. Radiant face, wide smile. Comfortable in herself, a simple white strappy top and dark jeans. But in Jake’s arms. Jake Scott. Enemy turned friend – and now, it seemed, turned enemy. One-time crony of Adam Brent, World’s Biggest Arsehole, last year turfed out by his own kind, so allowing his emo side to develop. A total wrestling obsessive, too – despite our relative social statuses, he’d been the one clinging to our friendship, desperate for words of wisdom from a real life pro wrestler. I liked the guy. Well, until now. So yeah, she looked up at me and the smile dropped right off her face like toast off your plate in the morning. Butter side down. And yeah, Jake turned round and saw me and looked uncomfortable like he was doing sit-ups in jeans or his boxers were too tight. So yeah, she opened her mouth but no words came out like she was underwater, scream to a sigh. And yeah, he opened his too but didn’t know what to say like when your best friend’s mum is told she’s got cancer. So yeah, she looked up at me, pleading and vulnerable like her rabbit’s been run over and she needs a hug. But fuck that. “Oh. Oh, right,” I said thickly. The words wouldn’t come for any of us. Breaking the silence might not have been the best idea, since I didn’t want to hear what either of them had to say. Tears pricking my eyes, which felt too big by far and pushed against the inside of their sockets, I turned to go, stumbling down the stairs. People probably thought I was drunk. Drunk at noon. Typical student. Through the door and down the pathway and away, half-walking half-running, until I found a bench and lay down and clasped my jacket against me and didn’t move, didn’t speak, tried not to think for a minute, a while, an hour or two. The whole afternoon. This was too much... too soon. My day off wasn’t turning out quite how I would have liked.
