5: the final blow

24 November 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: November 24. 2006 a. and things used to be like this: 1. Logan would rather not stay, but Ellis is off, again, charming the politicians, complimenting the wives, petting the children on their curly gold heads and asking after pet turtles and stock market foibles. She's in a dress. She traverses the boat, rides the roll and pitch of this crowd the same as she would any other. There's music now. Halfheartedly, Logan pulls a young girl along and twirls her into a dance. She giggles and grasps at his knee, hides her face in his thigh. (“Her name's Helen,” the man next to him says. “My daughter, that is. She says you danced with her? and that you asked her her name.” Logan looks up. He and the girl are standing together, hand in hand. The girl's chattering on excitedly, her little face flushed and her hair sleek and streaked with gold. In her mouth is a lollipop, the bump of it rounding her cheek. “It's Yelena in Russian,” her father says. Logan just smiles and nods. After a while a woman comes to join them. The man slides a propriety arm up to cradle the curve of her waist, leans in, and kisses her ear. She laughs. Her sundress, cinched at the waist, is the vivid white of snow under sunlight. The lace edge crumples, disappears into the little girl's fist. Now she's hiding her face in her mother's thigh; now she's playing a curious game with her father's thick, ringed fingers. Together they smile at Logan, politely, distantly.) 2. She is old, almost. Sturdy and dependably spectacular, PD's beloved Ellis Nash, but to Logan she is El with her veined ankles, the give of skin in the depression of her back, the way she groans in relief and goes limp after a long day when Logan clumsily tries to knead the tightness from the base of her neck. It was disconcerting at first, and maybe a little obscene - glorious, capable Ellis reduced to flab, the dull inertia of any sleeping body. Absurdly, he found this disappointing. But it makes him tender, too: an unsophisticated, unexpected feeling that unbalances Logan's sense of composure so that he abruptly stops stroking the back of her neck and starts playing with her hair instead. 3. Like that one time he'd kissed her (to distract her), then whipped up the hose and directed it full-blast into her face. Yelling. Ellis cursing and sopping and (she'd just changed) swiping at her face with the sleeves of her dress shirt. Then the yelling. It came in a stream of clipped, furious English, words shrunk and hardened. When Ellis would yell she would become precise, as if she would stretch you out across a drawing board and very meticulously dismember you with her incisory rage. Always she had somewhere to go, people to charm, accolades to collect, meetings to attend, interviews to give, worlds to divide, parts of her personality to auction and sign away. She must do these things. She didn't have the time to lounge around. She was responsible. Another time Ellis might have shucked off her coat and wrestled the hose away from Logan. She might've careened through the house, slip-sliding over marble, skidded down the solemn hallways and raced around corners, pattered rapidly down stairs and flung open the door and burst laughing into the open. And Ellis would've given chase. But that was in the beginning when Ellis hadn't needed him, when things had been mild and casual. Something changed, became irrevocably altered or was doctored, leading to a twitch, a convulsion, and then a rapid metamorphosis on some crucial subcellular level so that the next time they met Ellis was reckless, desperate but so serious, they did it on the bed she shared with her at-the-time boyfriend as if in this violation of what they both considered sacred she could find the strength, draw a determination from the intensity of her revulsion, for a clean break. One that could be set and then healed. 4. After dinner Ellis prefers cider, light amber in a stem glass -- to distract herself from the heavier alcohols, she says wryly, the kind Logan knocks back with abandon. They walk together, one at an even cant, the other leaning like a lisping ship, pushing the hair from his eyes and laughing. Logan, drunk, is a merciless flirt, or at least Ellis accuses him of being one. If his hand strays to touch her thigh or if he develops a sudden fascination with her fingers, it's not his fault. It may be an effect of the alcohol. “Or I could be happy,” Logan announces. “Then you're bad for me when you are happy,” Ellis responds, prying Logan's fingers from her forearm. Still, she smiles, scrubs him affectionately on the head, walks a little ahead when someone hails her and asks after her day, then slows and waits for Logan to catch up. In truth that's his favorite part of the day: when Ellis pauses in the middle of some terrible, tweedy joke, and Logan's choking with laughter for all the wrong reasons, and Ellis knows it and cuffs him across the temple in affectionate retaliation. But her fingers catch in Logan's hair and she holds on longer than she needs to, so that when she lets go, a boyish happiness has already lit Logan up inside. 5. “I'll tell you a story,” Logan says abruptly, and just like that the mood changes. “Here, listen. There was a village, right? It was a bright, muted village. Lots of good smells, bakeries, open-air markets selling fruit and clay pots and sunshine captured in jars. Don't laugh. You can do that with fireflies. But it's better to say that it was real sunshine anyway. So this village was home to all sorts of people -- people who sang, danced, conversed like rusty, dueling guitars. And among these people lived a child. He had been born in the village. He grew into a young boy, then a young man. He met a beautiful woman and they married. They had children. A girl and a boy. Pretty, fine-boned children, like their mother. Everyday under the lacy trees they had breakfast. They lived busily and happily and time passed. The father -- he was a father now -- liked to fish in the river. They had smoked fish, broiled white fish, fish eggs, fish pie, stuffed fish, grilled fish, fried fish, baked fish topped with honey glaze and sprinkled with pepper and basil. They had fish and they were never sick of it because that was the nature of these people. But one day, the father was walking by the river and saw silver fish swimming, the water sequined with them. He went closer to look and realized they weren't swimming. They were floating belly-up.” His gaze had turned inward and his words came out exact and intense. “From then on, things turned bad. It wasn't just his family. The people of the village got sick too. Some became inexplicably burned, their arms railroaded with scorch marks, their backs toasted black. Others lay in their beds, refusing to get up, bodies sick and seeping. Many bled from their mouths, under their skins, in their kidneys. Slowly the village become colonized by a fungal malaise. It infected their brains, too. Crime spread, stores were broken into and wrecked. People beat each other to a pulp in the streets and chuckled afterwards, toweling the blood from their faces. One day, the father's own son was playing a game with his friend in the treetops, laughing and crying and jumping branches. It was a game of chicken. He won. And broke his neck. That's why the man had to leave,” Logan says. “He took his wife and his daughter and left. Because he was scared. And he had to.” “It's a fairytale,” Ellis says, torn between wanting to laugh and wanting desperately to understand. Logan presses a kiss to her temple. He says quietly, “It would give you nightmares.” There's a period of time when they don't say anything. It's dark now but the moon's shining. Light idles on water like sparse tinsel. Pins her loosely against the counter and catches her mouth mid-smile, feels the laughter on her lips die a painless death and revive into a hot, clench-fisted urgency. The area over her collarbone is drawn tight and taut. El's breath hitches and her fingers convulse in his hair when Logan kisses and bites, as if her skin would split and run sweetly. Later he drifts his fingers over the back of her neck and finds the curve of it damp and just as soft.
