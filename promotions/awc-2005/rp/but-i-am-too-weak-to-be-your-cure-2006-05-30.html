but i am too weak to be your cure

30 May 2006

Date: May 30, 2006 the year dean turned three, daddy brown walked in the houston memorial day parade with his son riding on his shoulders. he wore his old uniform and held himself as straight as he could, pacing behind jimmy perkins who was carrying the TX banner just in front of him and whose left hip hadn't been the same since iwo jima. there was a troop of high-school girls in short skirts and gold epaulettes twirling batons ahead of him and a troop of boy scouts behind, and caroline laughing and waving from the bleachers along the sidewalk. she wore shorts and sandals and a bright yellow tank top, and dad thought that the sight of her long, slim legs and smooth shoulders was a damned unfair distraction to a man who still had half a mile left to walk in full dress uniform with a hyperactive three-year-old bouncing against this neck. afterwards, there was a TX-sponsored picnic down by the lake. dad changed into jeans and a t-shirt in the backseat while caroline drove, earning himself a disapproving glare from jimmy upon arrival. jimmy, dad knew, would wear his uniform all day. his boots would gleam, and his medals would clank, and the red poppy on his lapel would nod jauntily at the passers-by. he would get drunk on the free beer and mutter darkly about fool civilians who thought memorial day was about picnics and sales and determining when it was okay to wear white. dad could see his point, more or less, but deep down he was sort of glad that most people didn't understand. dad sprawled, barefoot and sleepy, on the ridiculous lace-edged picnic blanket caroline's sister had given them for a wedding present, and watched an ice cream-smeared toddler waddle past with a laughing young woman in pursuit. it would be a shitty world, he thought, if these grass-stained children and smiling mothers and sulky teenagers who wanted to be at home watching TV all had to understand what war was. and wasn't that the whole point of fighting after all -- to preserve a place back home that was untouched? “you're pensive today.” caroline came up behind him, a bottle of coke in each hand. she kicked off her sandals before stepping onto the blanket and sitting down. “everything okay?” she asked, knee bumping against dad's hip. she smelled of coconut from the suntan lotion she'd put on and of lemon verbena from that girly soap she liked to use. there were tan lines criss-crossing the tops of her feet from where the sandal straps used to be. she had painted her toenails raspberry red. “everything's great.” he replied. he took one of the bottles from her and set it aside without opening it. “where's dean?” “gone swimming with diane and her brood,” sighed caroline. she pressed her coke bottle against the side of her face. a drop of water, or maybe sweat, trickled along her jaw line and down her throat. “i should've brought a swimsuit. it's a million degrees out here.” “hmm,” murmured dad, reaching over and swiping the water drop from caroline's collarbone with his thumb. “we could sneak off someplace quiet and go skinny dipping,” he continued, and let his hand wander downward, toward the edge of her tank top. “wayne,” said caroline. she caught his wrist and held it, not pushing his hand away, but not letting him cop a feel in public either. “be good.” “i'm always good.” he ran his other hand up her calf to the ticklish spot at the back of her right knee. caroline made a strangled little sound and scooted away from him, but not very far. “you do realize there's about two hundred other people here, don't you?” “they're not looking.” “they will be if you keep this up.” “let 'em look. maybe they'll learn something,” he said, and brushed that spot at the back of her knee again. “oh, you're so asking for it,” she hissed and moved back toward him, leaning forward. dad raised his head from the blanket, thinking she meant to kiss him, and fell back with a thump. “jesus christ, woman, are you nuts?” and that was absolutely, positively, not a squeak on the last word. marines did not squeak. “you started it, brown, so how do you plan on finishing it?” “err --” dad did his best to gather his wits and think. despite his earlier joking suggestion, skinny-dipping was probably not a practical option. maybe they could go back to the car and-- “daddy, daddy!” “oh, hell.” caroline scrambled off just in time for dad to receive a flying tackle from a wet, sand-covered dean. “daddy!” dean sat on dad's belly and bounced, happily oblivious to what he'd just interrupted. he was wearing wet swimming trunks and a frayed bugs bunny t-shirt that was emphatically not the brand-new kid-sized UTAustin t-shirt dad had dressed him in that morning. “carrie's got a turtle in a box, and it's this big! It eats lettuce and... and... alfa sprouts! come look!” “er --” said dad again. his command of spoken english appeared to have failed him, which was probably just as well. he threw a pleading look at caroline, who was watching the proceedings with what struck him as highly inappropriate amusement. “a turtle, honey, really? i'm sure daddy would love to see.” evil woman, dad mouthed silently over his shoulder as he let dean drag him to his feet. she smirked unrepentantly and raised one hand to wave at him. later, she mouthed back, and blew him a kiss. dad untucked his t-shirt from his jeans and followed after dean, a little unsteady on his feet but grinning from ear to ear. * “Bro, that was prett’tight.” Kip’s cheek lolls against the airplane seat, tired. Sitting beside him, Dean fiddles with the ring on his thumb, twisting and turning it like a wheel. “Yeah?” “Yeah,” confirms Dean. He settles, finally, and looks up. Blue meets a sharp green. Kip smiles, a little slow, a little lazy. “Yeah.” “I, uh...” Dean begins, looking back down at his ring. He sucks in a breath, says, “...thanks. You know, for bringin’ me along and stuff.” Above them, the seat-belt light flicks off. Kip nods, languid, watching his brother. “Anytime.” “So, what,” says Dean, leaving his ring and instead, staring at his thumbnail, “you do this for a livin’ now, or some’in’?” “What?” “Wrestlin’. This wrestlin’ thing.” Kip’s shoulder ascend in a shrug. They stay suspended while he thinks, and he moves his gaze to the empty seat in front of him. “I guess. I mean, I don’t know. Whole thing’s pretty weird.” “Yeah?” “Yeah,” answers Kip, “It just, uh... it just happened.” “Yeah,” says Dean, “a lot of things just happen.” Kip smiles, a little bitter, a little amused, a little something. “That’s cryptic.” Dean looks up. His lips quirk, somewhat, before he looks at his thumbnail again. “Yeah. Guess so.” ** three weeks after kip was born, there was an outbreak of chicken pox at dean's preschool. dad had chicken pox as a child. caroline hadn't. as a result, caroline and the baby spent memorial day weekend at caroline's sister's house, while dad stayed at home to play caretaker to an itchy four-year-old. friday wasn't so bad. dean had only a few spots, and a single nenadryl at dinnertime seemed to kill most of the itching. the fever made him sluggish, and he was content to be put to bed early, with only three consecutive readings of the very hungry caterpillar required before dad was allowed to turn out the light. saturday was the worst. dean woke crying at six in the morning, sobbing ‘daddy, it itches!’ over and over as dad carried him to the bath. the spots were on his scalp, in his ears and mouth, on the palms of his hands and the soles of his feet and every inch of skin in between. the benadryl had no noticeable effect, and the calamine lotion only served to transform dean from a huddled bundle of itchy misery into a pink huddled bundle of itchy misery. the notes caroline had left before she took off with kip said oatmeal bath, so dad dumped some quaker oats into the tub, filled it with warm water and put dean in. it made an ungodly mess, but dean definitely seemed to feel better. or maybe he just liked wallowing in oatmeal; he'd always done a great job of smearing the stuff all over himself when caroline made it for breakfast. dean ended up spending most of the day climbing in and out of the tub as the need arose, dripping water and clumps of wet oatmeal all over the bathroom floor and the hallway carpet. in between the soaks he made dad read the very hungry caterpillar twenty-seven times. it was a great relief to both of them when bedtime finally came around. caroline laughed until she nearly choked when dad called her with a progress report on Sunday. “breakfast cereal, wayne! you bathed our son in breakfast cereal!” “you wrote down yourself --” “look in the medicine cabinet.” dad carried the phone into the bathroom, swearing under his breath at the cord, which kept looping around his ankles with clear malicious intent. he opened the cabinet. stared at the dainty little box labeled moisturizing daily oatmeal bath. looked down at the smears of oatmeal on the floor. “oh,” he said. “you're going to clean that up before we come home, right?” there was one good thing about spending a sunday afternoon cleaning soggy breakfast cereal out of a hallway carpet. at least he didn't spend it reading the very hungry caterpillar. by monday, dean was noticeably better. the spots from earlier in the week were starting to scab over and the oatmeal soak from the medicine cabinet was doing its job with a minimum of mess. in a fit of inspiration, dad mixed up a batch of the stuff in the inflatable kiddie pool in the backyard, so dean could splash around to his heart's content while dad sat nearby in a folding chair and read the paper. “daddy?” dean sat up straight, a plastic sailboat dangling forgotten from one fist. “what's that noise?” “what noise?” dad looked up and for the first time became aware of the faint strains of music in the distance. it sounded vaguely like when the saints go marching in performed by a semi-competent brass band. “oh, that must be the parade.” “parade.” dean's face scrunched up in thought. “like that time when you wore a costume and carried me?” “yes, it's just like that time.” “why?” “because it's memorial day. they have it every year.” “why?” “because it's a holiday.” “why?” dad took a deep breath. “why what, dean?” “why is it a holiday?” dad put down his newspaper and looked at dean. dean stared back, eyes wide and solemn amidst a constellation of red spots. maybe he was old enough to understand. he was a smart kid, knew how to pay attention. he'd gotten the idea behind christmas well enough when caroline explained it a few months before, so why not this, too? “come inside with me, son. i want to show you something.” seated cross-legged on the living room floor, dean watched with reverent fascination as dad laid out yellowed newspaper clippings, old photos of himself in uniform standing next to men who were now dead, a Purple Heart in its velvet-lined case. “do you know what these are, dean?” “no, daddy.” “pay attention, then. this is important.” *** Kip lugs the last of his luggage into the back of Dean’s fuckugly maroon bitch. Then, he slides into the passenger seat. The curve of it against his neck when he slouches down is something unfamiliar, but the smell of the old leather keeps him sane. Dean pops his gum into a wrapper, rolls it up, and leaves it on the ashtray ten minutes into the drive. The engine is loud and noisy with age, and strains against nearly every press of acceleration. Kip looks up. He shakes his head, just a little. “Nah. Not really.” “I am,” says Dean. “Oh.” Kip pauses. “There’s stuff back at the house... or you can just, uh, stop somewhere. And get something.” The light flickers to a bland green. The car strains forward. Dean smiles. “But it’s two in the morning.” “Oh. Right.” “Right,” says Dean, and he looks over at Kip and chuckles. Kip doesn’t know why he’s chuckling, but smiles anyway. **** six months and twenty-eight days after caroline died, dad staggered, bruised and bleeding, out the back door of an abandoned house in austin, leaving a corpse in the shadows behind him. dad's legs buckled abruptly and he sat down hard in the grass, halfway between the house and the dirt road where he'd left the car. bruised ribs, he thought. maybe broken. that sharp stab of pain across his chest every time he took a breath was not a good sign. the warm trickle of blood from his left temple didn't bode well either. his left wrist was swollen to nearly twice its normal size. dad tried to wiggle his fingers, and things went kind of fuzzy for a while. by the time he could focus again, the sky was growing pale in the east and it was definitely time to move so dad gritted his teeth and moved, ignoring the protests of bruised muscle and cracked bone. he kept his eyes down, kept putting one foot in front of the other until the expanse of grass in his vision was replaced by dirt and then by dust-flecked black metal. he'd never been so fucking happy to see his car before. driving hurt like a bitch. ot should've taken only four hours or so to get back to conroe, but six hours after he'd started dad found himself pulling over to the shoulder of the highway for what felt like the hundredth time, slumping over the steering wheel to catch his breath. he'd grown soft, that was the problem. these injuries were nothing, band aid and ice-pack territory. ten years ago, he would've laughed them off and kept right on going. but that had been ten years of easy living, ten years of laughter and sunshine and caroline, ten years of losing his fighting edge. he'd have to work to get it back now, and keep it, and make sure the boys had it when they were old enough. none of them afford to be soft now, not with caroline's killer still walking the earth, a threat to him and to his children. the last time dad pulled over was in downtown brownsville, just a few minutes away. he kept running into detours everywhere he turned, streets closed off every other block, crowds and traffic everywhere and way too many cops. he had no idea what the hell was happening until he heard the brassy notes of yankee doodle dandy in the distance and realized there was a parade going on. it took him another minute or two to recall the occasion. he was less than ten blocks from their house, and he had no idea how to get there from here. dad ran one shaky hand through his hair and checked himself out in the rear view mirror. he'd cleaned up as best he could during an earlier stop, but there wasn't much to be done about the scab on his temple or the spectacular bruising around it. hopefully he looked more like somebody who'd been in a bar fight and less like somebody who’d just stabbed a bitch. dad sighed, rolled down the window and leaned out to address a young woman who was walking by. “excuse me, miss? miss?” she stopped, polite smile faltering into nervous uncertainty as she got a good look at him. dad thought she might've bolted if they weren't in the middle of a crowd in broad daylight. she was just a kid, no older than sixteen, all pimples and braces and mousy brown hair in a ponytail. a basket of red silk poppies hung on a strap from her shoulder. “how do i get to carlyle street?” “carlyle street..” the girl frowned and chewed on her lower lip. “you have to go around,” and then she frowned some more, pointed, rattled off a long list of street names and landmarks that dad could barely follow. he thanked her politely, started to roll up the window, then stopped and gestured toward her basket. “you selling those?” “one dollar,” she said brightly. “all proceeds go to the disabled veterans foundation.” dad handed over a dollar bill and accepted a poppy in return. there was a safety pin stuck in the stem, and he used it to attach the flower to the lapel of his coat. in the mirror, the splash of red looked like a blood stain. for caroline, he thought. the first casualty in his new war -- and the last, if he had anything to say about it. he rolled up the window, and the noise of the crowd faded. only the faintest strain of music pursued him as he drove away from the parade. *** “Cinnamon oatmeal? Plus, it was on sale. Two for one.” “Well, that explains it,” murmurs Dean, more to himself than Kip. Kip’s nearly done with his Sprite when Dean finally decides on a can of beef broth. The roof of his mouth burns, and the tissue starts to peel. His face crinkles in pain as the tip of his tongue touches the area, tentatively. Dean, hands soaked in dish soap, is mildly amused. “What?” “I burned myself,” responds Kip, placing another dish into the washer. “Never learn,” says Dean, scrubbing the burned chili from the pan. Kip shrugs. He puts dish cleaner in the washer and kicks the door closed with the top of his foot. Then, he pushes a few buttons and the cycle begins. “I’m going to bed.” “‘Kay,” answers Dean, and nods and kind of smiles and is four steps out of the kitchen when Dean says, “Hey, Kip?” “Yeah?” Kip turns around. “I, uh... that tattoo.
