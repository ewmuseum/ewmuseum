One for Luck, Ch 2

30 September 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 30th September One for Luck, Chapter Two The lunch had gone smoothly. I had learnt a lot from Tom and Robert, but Selene, remained a mystery. Robert and Tom left the diner a little early and left a couple of dollars for tips, which meant Selene and I had to split the bill at the end, but I was left with one thought: “what should I say?” I was nervous around Selene; I guess we both had reasons. Mine was Lois Wood, hating me for the rest of my life and Selene’s reason; I guess I’d never know. “Do you want dessert?” she asked from behind the menu. “Em, no, I’m fine, thanks,” I slouched into my seat and constantly struggled with a battle raging in my mind: ‘say something you idiot’, reverberated around the hollow walls that belonged to my brain. “You sure?” she asked politely. This time, she was looking at me. I sat up, messed with the straw in my Cola and nodded my head. “Okay, if you’re sure,” Selene turned to the waitress. “No desserts, thanks.” “Do you want the bill?” the waitress asked. “Yeah, thanks,” Selene then focused her attention on me. “I’m sorry,” I said abruptly. “What?” she said. “For the way I was, years ago,” I sat forward and took a sip from my Cola. “Pierce, that was a long time ago,” she said. “No, Selene, I need to make amends for all the mistakes I have made,” “Don’t be sorry. I guess we made a mistake and we both hurt the same person. Must have been a record,” she added and straightened her books on the table. “Yeah, but I really am sorry,” I said once more. “Pierce, you’re mumbling again,” She smiled. Selene Kyle was one of those women that had an infectious smile along with some pretty green eyes and cute little dimples. She was the High School geek, but looking passed the glasses and Geometry books, she was the most caring, considerate and very beautiful woman, still was. “Oh, sorry, I had the straw in my mouth,” I laughed. “Careful, you’ll spew all over the bill,” Selene said and placed a five dollar bill on the small silver dish. I added my own five dollar and gave Robert and Tom’s money as tip to the waitress. We both stood up and I helped Selene with her books. The sun had settled down a little, mid-day had passed and most of the college students had finished their lectures and had now gone home, hit the books and would, eventually, party until all hours of the morning. The campus was now like a cemetery - quiet, bar the odd student canvassing the halls. The women’s dorms were on the left side of the college, a good fifteen minutes walk from the men’s dorms. “So, what did you get up to?” she asked. “You mean when I was in Albany?” “Yeah,” she nodded. “You haven’t read the newspaper? That’s a relief,” I smiled and held open the door. “Newspaper? All I heard was that you were dating Lois,” she said. Ouch, it was a low-blow. Selene and Lois both liked me, unfortunately, being a dumb teenager who knew nothing about relationships, love or anything else and being immature didn’t help. So my dilemma, which would seem obvious, was both liked me. I slept with one and then dated the other. “I was dating her, she dumped me.” “I think you are one of the first men to mention a woman’s dumped you,” she said and climbed the long stairs. Oh yeah, another thing, Selene is a Women’s rights activist, well she was in her early years. “And you? Are you dating anyone?” I asked. My mind fully focused on the large book which read “THE ANATOMY OF GEOMETRY” “Tom,” she said with a large grin. “Never saw that coming,” I smiled. As teenagers, Tom and Selene hated each other, it soon became a love-hate relationship and it made it difficult to hang-out with both of them, but in college their attitudes towards one another changed, because Selene looked after Tom, after he had the accident. I guess she was happy and I was happy for her. “Yeah, it was a sudden thing,” she said. “Here we are,” I said. I was too busy staring at the large: NO MEN ALLOWED, to notice that Selene had stepped up onto the stairs and had taken back the books I was holding. “New rule?” I asked. “Yeah, but it’s good, can’t be having those late night parties,” She smiled. “I’d agree,” I stood up straight, placed my hands into my back pockets and rocked back and forth on my heels. “Thanks Pierce,” she said and gave me a hug. “It was good to see you again,” she added and left for the forbidden room. I stood waving at a fading image and soon turned around and headed back toward the student car park. When I arrived at the car, Robert was seated on the hood, balancing an open refill pad on his left knee and writing with his right hand, whilest staring at a large book. I refused to ask and just acknowledged his presence. I slid into the passenger seat and pulled out my mobile, along with the small note Mrs. Green had given me with my mother’s new number. I dialled the area code for Los Angeles and then dialled the number. After three rings, the phone was answered. “Hello,” the voice said into the receiver. It was my aunt Joyce. My mother’s older sister. She was married to Michael Bernard - a mechanic and a very loving man, completely in contrast to my father. In their years of marriage, they never had kids and even now, still wanted them. “Aunt Joyce, its Pierce. Is my mother there?” I asked. “PIERCE!” she screamed into the phone. I held it back a couple of inches and shook my head with my ears ringing. “Wow, Aunt Joyce, relax,” I said, quietly. Although I’m not sure why, she wouldn’t have heard me anyway. “Do you know how worried we were about you?” she said into the receiver. “I-eh …” “Your mother has been climbing the walls ever since we saw the newspaper article. Where the bloody hell are you, young man?” “I-eh-I’m in Harrisburg …” “What? Couldn’t you have rung the Albany police to let them know? Your mother has had a search warrant issued. You went missing,” Joyce sounded hysterical. “Aunt Joyce, I wasn’t missing. I sold my penthouse and left.” “Why?” “It’s a long story … maybe some other time. Is my mom there?” “Your mother? Oh, sometimes I’m glad I never had children. MARIE …” Joyce bellowed and I heard the receiver slam onto the table. Okay, so perhaps now wasn’t a good time to ring my mother and her sister. I could just imagine my Uncle Mike, seated on the sofa, reading a newspaper and laughing at his wife’s hysterics, I too had to hold back from laughing. “Hello?” my mother said shyly into the receiver. “Mom,” it was all I had to say. “Pierce … Pierce … I’m so sorry,” she started off by saying. Okay, yes, my mother was an emotional wreck and it was partially my fault. “I’m sorry too, mom,” I looked at Robert and saw the large bend in my bonnet. I almost jumped through the roof; putting the phone to one side I hurled myself out of the car and around to Robert, hoisted him from the bonnet and lifted the lid, straightening the bend. “DON’T SIT ON THE ENGINE,” I said in a loud voice. “Geez … Relax,” Rob said and slid into the passenger seat, squashing my phone. “Hey, get up! You’re sitting on my mother!” I stated. “WHAT?” Robert laughed and held the phone. “Sorry, Mrs. Lavelle,” he said. “Pierce?” she asked into the phone. “Sorry Mom. Robert just dented my hood,” I said with a grin. “Oh right. Listen son, I need to go and collect your sister from school. I’ll ring you later, I promise,” the phone soon went dead. I turned on the engine, fastened my seatbelt and looked at Robert. “Oh no you don’t -” he managed to say before I swung a large U-turn and drove down the road. “I think I’m going to be sick …” he said, ducking his head between his knees. The phone rang endlessly in the hall, with Robert’s feeble attempts of yelling the word “PHONE”, over and over; hoping one of his parents would answer the phone. After a couple of minutes of my ears ringing, with Robert’s kind words, I rose from the make-shift bed and ran down the stairs, jumping two steps at a time, until I landed, two feet straight next to the table with the phone. “Mr. and Mrs. Green’s residence,” I said politely. “Pierce?” the voice said into the phone. “Mom?” “You call me mom again and I’ll kill you!” “Rebecca?” I said, laughing to myself at my sister’s cringing on the other end of the phone. “Yes, that’s better,” she said with a laugh accompanying it. “What can I do for you?” I asked. “Just checking up on my big bro!” she stated. “Em, Becca, I know you too well. What do you want?” “Em, you wouldn’t happen to have Lois’s number?” she asked. Now I was confused, my own sister had rung me, not to talk to me, but to enquire about my ex-girlfriends number. “No, I don’t have it.” “Okay, thanks bro, see you soon,” she hung up as quickly as the phone call had just started. “Who was it?” Mrs.Green asked from inside the kitchen. “My sister,” I answered back and leapt up the stairs towards Robert’s room. It began like most days, the tedious alarm buzzing in the background, signalling a new day has begun and if you don’t get your “lazy-ass out of bed soon, you’ll be late for work”. I rose from the comfort of a very hard-lined bed that Mrs. Green had kindly set-up for me. Robert snored away, completely ignoring the echoing of the alarm clock. I slid out from under the duvet, straightened my back and listened to each bone crack into place like the tracks on a tank. I pulled on the usual wife-beater and grabbed hold of the corner of the pillow and flung it in Robert’s direction, who let out a “Wahhhh” and collapsed to the floor with a thud! “Ouch,” he said while rubbing his eyes. “Sorry,” I smiled and stood up. “Yeah, funny!” Robert exclaimed. I yawned and switched off the alarm. I stared in the mirror and mess that was my hair on top of my head; the “mop” as Robert calls it. “You should go to Bob the Barber!” Robert kindly suggested. Robert had recently allowed Bob the Barber to give his hair a Number one blade. If he were to wear a white suit, he would pass for a marine officer. “Thanks for the suggestion, Marine,” I laughed. “I like it,” he said and rubbed the small bristles on his head. Mrs. Green entered the room brandishing a large tray of juice and cereal. She smiled at the two of us seated on the floor, barely awake. She placed the tray on Robert’s table and then left, leaving the door ajar. I slid over and drank some juice. Robert rose from the slump he was in and dialled his mobile. He lay down and was immediately patched into Lois Wood. “Hey Lo, how are you?” I almost choked on my orange juice and felt half of it pass into my lungs. I turned around and just as I was about to leave, Robert kindly mentioned: “Oh, I’m not up to much, just got woken up by Pierce,” he said and few seconds passed. “Yeah, he’s here,” Robert jumped up from the bed and handed me the phone. I turned around, stared at him and shoved the phone back into his chest and left the room. Okay, I wasn’t over her; I don’t think anybody would blame me, but I wasn’t ready to speak to her, yet. I clambered my way down the stairs and over a pile of washing into the hall. Mr. Green was seated in front of the television watching the early morning CNN news on Hurricane Rita that had now ripped its way through most of Louisiana and Texas. I waved to him and he replied with “morning lad” and soon returned to his very important news broadcast. Mrs. Green was in the kitchen slaving away over the stove, cooking something which smelt divine. “Pierce?” she asked without even turning around. She flipped a fried egg onto a plate along with some bacon and handed it to me. “Enjoy,” she said. “A good breakfast is always a good healer …” She smiled. “Thanks Mrs. Green, smells gorgeous,” I said and grabbed a knife and fork and sat at the small round table, gazing at the rain and high winds on the outside. The shutters on the windows rattled back and forth with each gust, the rain tapped on the window, a constant pitter-patter of droplets landing and then soon, evaporating. “Well, Robert said the dinner went well,” Mrs. Green sat opposite to me. A cup of tea, some toast and bacon. She too, divulged into the warm breakfast. “Yeah, it was nice to see Tom and Selene again,” I said. My mouth was filled with crushed bacon and mashed up fried egg, not a pleasant sight for the person on the other end of the conversation. I swallowed the mangled mess in my mouth and took a gulp of water. “Well, what is the plan for tonight?” she asked. “Well, Selene, Tom, Robert and I planned to go to the bar, Fibbers. It should be fun, although I don’t think I’ll be drinking much anymore.” “Why’s that?” she asked. Mrs. Green had always been one to keep tabs on Robert and I, ever since we were young boys. “Well, I got kicked out of college for it and since my stay in Albany, I haven’t touched much alcohol.” “Good for you,” she said with a smile. “Drink corrupts the mind,” she said. I had finished my breakfast and stayed seated at the table for that friendly conversation each one of us needs, now and then. Robert came into the kitchen, his head bowed down. His mother pointed to the plate on the counter and he quickly tended to the necessities. “I’m sorry, Pierce,” he said. “I didn’t think you’d mind talking to Lois,” He sat down and placed some sauce over his pancakes. “I’m just not ready, Rob,” I mentioned and stood up to place my dirty dishes into the dishwasher. “Yeah, I know … sorry,” he said with a mouthful of pancake. “Robert Isaac Green! You know better than to mingle in break-ups.” Robert dipped his head. Mrs. Green knew exactly how to make Robert feel he had been wrong and in doing so he immediately apologized. I smiled and said my thanks and decided to get dressed. Mr. Green was still parked in front of the television, listening to some poor reporter being blown to bits in the middle of the storm. I went up the stairs, plugged my Mp3 player into Robert’s stereo player and hit play; “Hard to Beat” by Hard-Fi had soon replaced the howling wind and torrential rain fall. I threw on a pair of black baggy jeans, a white t-shirt and a grey “GAP” jumper. By the time I had made the bed it was already 1:00 pm. Robert grabbed a towel and headed for the shower. I remained seated on the small make-shift bed, staring at the digits that were my mother’s new number. As I was about to dial, my phone began to buzz, I checked the caller ID and saw TOM LINCOLN written across the screen. “Hey,” I answered. “Hey, Pierce, how are you?” he asked. “Pretty good, you?” Yeah, it was the usual phone call; until you managed to get to the point of the why the person had rung you. “I’m fine, listen about tonight -” There we go, why he had rung me. “…Selene and I won’t be able to make it. I actually planned to take her out some place romantic,” Tom stated. “Well, I hope you have a nice time. Another time, perhaps.” Tom said his goodbyes and ended the phone call. I put the phone into my pocket and leaned back on the bed, listening to “Move on Now” by Hard-Fi. I soon began to think about things, life in general and the problems involved in life and when I realised it would just be Robert and I in the bar, life suddenly lost its appeal. I sat up, pulled on a jacket and decided to brave the bad weather and go for a walk. The rain danced lightly around my face as I stood looking into the storm clouds, watching them clash and roar, sounding like an argument between a couple. The storm was passing as sunny clouds crept over the mountains in the distance. I pulled the hood over my head and left Williamsburg’s Estates and out into the real world. The bar ‘Fibbers’, was a bar that specialised in Italian Cuisine and also played Indie-Rock and Rock N’ Roll music, so for me, it was the best bar in Harrisburg, despite the critics views. I passed by my old school and continued walking, shoving my hands into the large pockets on my rain jacket, with nothing to occupy me than the sound of rain hitting the coating on the hood. I crossed the street, looked left and right and ran across, pretending to dodge each droplet. ‘Fibbers’ was buzzing with life. People from all around Harrisburg were seated sipping hot coffee or tea, eating some lunch and some were drinking alcohol. The music was not of my taste, but I knew it would end soon. Two minutes of torment or being drenched, I’d take the first option. I pulled off the coat, shook my hair, restyled it in the small mirror, smiled at the hostess and sat at the counter. “How may I help you,” a lady asked from behind the bar. “Usual, Diane,” I hadn’t bothered turning around to realise it wasn’t Diane standing opposite me. Without a reaction, I turned around and saw a small woman, around my age, tending to a mixer. She stared at me with a quizzical look. “Oh, sorry, I, eh, I thought Diane still worked here,” I smiled bashfully. “She left,” the lady said. “Oh,” I dipped my head. Diane Philips was a sixty year old red-neck from Texas, who moved to Harrisburg when her husband died. She had worked in Fibbers for ten years and had a stone cold attitude to drunks and would look after you all night, if needs be. “Well, what’s your usual?” She smiled and put the mixer under the counter. “A seven-up,” I smiled. “Okay,” She laughed and placed a poured seven-up before me. “A heavy drinker then,” She joked and moved down the table. “Oh yeah! You can’t imagine the hangovers one would have after a couple of Seven-ups!” I smiled and sipped some of the excess Seven-up that was bubbling over the rim of the glass. The news flashed on and showed the daily weather report and the usual tedious stock market minutiae. I slid the glass aside and pulled out my mobile and checked my inbox. Robert had sent me eight messages within the last twenty minutes. “How’s the getting drunk working for you?” she asked with a smile on her face. Here’s something weird, I was actually attracted to a woman I didn’t even know the name of. To be honest, staring at this lady behind the bar was a lot better than Diane with all the wrinkles and excess red lipstick and fake-tan, it was like something out of “Shaun of the Dead”, but despite her grotesque appearance, Diane had a sweet character. “Well, I’m onto the getting high on sugar part,” I smiled. “I haven’t seen you around before!” she said. She leant forward a little, placed the bar towel over her shoulder and stared at the band now climbing onto the stage. “Yeah, I was away for a while,” I said and took a large gulp from the seven-up, trying not to swallow the ice and choking nervously in front of the young bar waitress. “Oh yeah! Where were you? Anywhere nice?” she enquired. “Albany, and it wasn’t the best time of my life,” I straightened my posture and stared at a man stagger in the exit door. He had a tattered yellow rain coat on; his beard was overgrown and drenched in booze and rain. He lumbered forward, his eyes leading him in all directions; bumping into people he would mutter words of obscenity under a heavy intoxicated breath. “Oh no …” the young waitress said. “Y-O-U …” the drunken man managed to splutter as he lunged at the bar. I leant back and almost fell off the stool. I gathered myself appropriately and watched the drunken man closely. He pulled up an empty beer bottle and smashed it against the gold bar that ran along the side of the mahogany table. “W-W-hat are you do-ing?” he said. He staggered forward a little, brandishing the remains of a crushed bottle. “I-eh-I,” the young waitress replied nervously. “Please, not now …” she whimpered under the eyes of a watchful boss and drunken man. The drunken man, losing all control, lunged at the young friendly bar tender and slashed her arm with the broken glass bottle. I dove forward, losing all sense of gravity and speared the drunken man to the ground, the broken bottle went flying in mid-air and a scream was heard from the far corner of the room. Two large body guards soon ran in and helped me up and escorted the now semi-conscious drunken man to the entrance and onto the street. “It’s okay everyone, commotion is over,” the manager of Fibbers announced. The young lady that had been friendly to me was in a hunched position holding a very bloodied arm. “Here,” I said and knelt next to her. “Let me take a look,” I smiled. I knelt next to her behind the bar, grabbed the towel over her shoulder, ripped it in two and stood up to reach the tap. She followed me and I cleaned the opened wound and with the dry towel, tied it around her arm and asked for the first aid box. “Thanks for your help,” she said while holding back tears. “Hey, don’t mention it, it‘ll hold, but you might need stitches,” I smiled back and took the first aid box off Lucille. I removed the make-shift bandage and replaced it with a proper bandage, along with some antiseptic cream. “That was a nice tackle,” she commented. “Yeah?” I smiled. “Yeah. Do you play American football?” she asked. “No, I’m a wrestler,” I finished the bandage and threw both towels into the bin. “Oh … a wrestler … I knew there was a catch …” She frowned. “Huh? You don’t like wrestlers?” “Em, thanks for the help. The bandage looks really professional,” she said. “Is everything okay?” I asked, completely confused by here sudden diversion. “I’m Kelli Stoner,” she reached her hand out to me. She had completely avoided my question … weird! “Pierce Lavelle,” I shook her hand.
