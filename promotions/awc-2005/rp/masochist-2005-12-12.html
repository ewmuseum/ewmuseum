Date: 12 December, 2005 a. blue, blue skies (i forgive you) A man pretending at sadness sings songs filled with lonely drinks at midnight and empty apartments in the subway tunnel. Ellis buys a cup of coffee, modernized and artificial with three quarters and a nickel at a bodega above the fantastical rise of underground steam like something dangerous from a staircase into the underground. Nighttime and the pavement buzzes a little beneath her feet, the buildings grow more concrete, more striking, and the people become sadder and prettier and lonelier and happier. There’s something nondescript, uniformly heavy and mechanized about the way millions of feet shake the skin of the earth, but Ellis remembers that these are people with hearts that beat erratically and malfunctioning arteries, with blood fizzing in their fingertips and heavy thin ankles. Somehow that makes it a little easier, a little nicer, a little more special. Misplaced Americans and one man acts, muttering protesters and mint gum stuck to the sidewalk: these are the mornings and the afternoons and the nights. In her car, Ellis stares at the small press of her hand against glass, how the ring is too large of a circle, too grand of a statement and how it slips to the base of her finger and stays there like a forced fit, unnatural. The frost is seeping back, pressing against the edges of her nails, swallowing the outline of another handprint. On this half lit night time, one foot folded beneath a knee and music sliding across the back of her neck, she thinks: nothing is enough, I always need more, more, more. More of these beautiful things, more of these filtered days, more of these hazy nights and rain crashing into her window. More, she thinks, more of him and more of her, more of living so that they can finally say to each other, it’s worth it, more bravery so they can finally tell each other that this is real, more of everything until they bubble at the edges and finally become something real, real. Real. What she doesn’t forget is that defiant, nicotine urge to shatter something, to ruin this, that, everything, the glass, the hollow metal, the ring, but Ellis is not brave, she is not brave, and she can’t imagine what the world would say to her for breaking something so beautiful. But she wants. She wants to. Her hands shake. What Ellis knows is that her entire life has been a melted conglomerate of desire, viscous and stinging like acid in her throat, like that hurt knot of words she choked down and could never say. She wants and has always wanted; it is that addictive need that makes her want to touch him, that makes her want to hurt him, that makes her want to see how blood looks on his mouth. Chandler once told her that drowning is like forgiving and letting go. Ellis swallows the pulse in her mouth. She is not brave. The cold whispers by the back of her knee, neck, elbow. She is not brave. Winter and tremulous hearts and unsteady veins, this is what Sunday is for Ellis, forgetting to breathe and not remembering how to run away, heat and cold and him and then she wonders, what does it mean to be more than you are, what does it mean to kill someone and say that you’ve got the right reason, what does it mean to want? Ellis’ shoulders are tense in their geometry. It starts to thunder. Somebody always has the answer. Her cell phone is ringing. Ellis twirls her ring finger around like a ballerina and stops at a red light. And ring, ring, ring, ring, ring, until the only sound Ellis hears is the press of her foot against the gas pedal and the engine corresponding. Maybe she can be an actress on the stage five blocks south of her apartment, filled to the brim with bravado and mouth red, or maybe a singer in some local band playing that Friday night, her skirt a little too short and hair chopped bluntly against her neck. Half a dozen outdated cassette tapes of Billie Holiday, the scratchy itching low-fi sound of heartbreak. Ellis pops one in and turns the radio dial towards the right, and, lady sings the blues, she’s got them bad, she feels so sad, wants the world to know, just what the blues is all about. Ellis gets home at six in the morning. The air plays like a love song. Her cell phone is ringing again. *** b. i doubt this conversation is going anywhere with the words you’re leaving out And Monday night comes digging into Ellis’ shoulder like how she digs her heels into the orange carpet of the subway, her hands in the warm and her feet in pain, sharp angular pinpricks against her soles faster than the train could make stop with his brakes. The doors open and a flurry of faces rush out, only pausing to throw out New York excreta, physical and verbal. “Hey,” Chandler is sitting, though, a newspaper displaying Friday night tragedy across his lap. “What are you thinking?” Late at night is slow to please, just words in an A/C current moving like plankton between whale jaws. Gushing. Ellis’ shoulders sag as the subway’s doors close. “Nothing,” and it’s a complicated matter of picking out what matters and what doesn’t and Ellis will put her words through her blowhole and make singing sounds like the sirens, waiting, watching, tempting. When Chandler responds Ellis can’t help but stare at his teeth, small like a row of pearls. It feels as though if she thumbs her way across his face it would be baby’s skin she would be touching, pulling his cheeks apart to make a smile, like opening an oyster, cracking open the shell to see row after row of Claire’s teeth. “Yeah. Right.” Ellis leans against the railing like a collapsed object. “What?” Callous grafitti flies by their heads like a bird with clipped wings. “Forget about it,” Chandler says, and Ellis wishes she could forget about a lot of things. “Yeah,” and then a space so wide it could take in lies and hide them in places that would validate them, “right.” Later, when they are off the subway, Chandler takes Ellis’ hand. Ellis lets him, intertwines their fingers like strands of hair and asks, “Can I ask you something?” and it’s not like Chandler has a choice, anyway. “Why do things happen?” Chandler squeezes her palm, skin against skin, friction. “How do you mean?” “I don’t mean.” Ellis thinks that Chandler answers, “You’re so filled up with love. You don’t even realize it, do you? But you are, it’s poured into you and it overflows, spills out in the way that you move your hands when you talk. In the way that people look at you and can't stop; in the way that you never ask but always get. You’re soaked in the fucking thing, you’re covered with it and you carry it around everywhere, it’s like something gold that circulates its way through your veins, like something written so plainly and so painfully all across you; like a stain, a mark, a tattoo. It’s all over you, your face your hands your eyes and you don’t even see it. The mirrors fall in love with you. It’s all over you.” But he doesn’t say anything. He only hums under his breath, hums, love will come through, sweetheart. With her free hand, Ellis tucks an unlit cigarette behind her ear. Prayers mean nothing. Gold crosses aren’t worth a dime. Burned candles won’t lift the darkness of the night into something kind. -- & the midnights come digging into Ellis’ shoulder like how she digs her hand into Dennis Prather’s throat, esophagus crushed by the press of her thumb, peristalsis trapped in sharp/jagged chokes. “I didn’t kill her!” and there is a spit bubble in the corner of his mouth, little spumes of saliva like bumps on the side of the road, “Lady, please! I didn’t!” Hand on her shoulder, squeeze on her blade, fingers curling like hair tickling the nape of her neck, and Chandler breathes security against her ear, breathes breathes breathes, and his breath is hot enough to melt a man or a murderer, “Ellis,” breathes/says, snaps like his thin wrist could, hot breath, “No.” Avalon is torrid/thronged/cramped so no one notices when Ellis relents/sends her knee into Dennis’ mid-section. Dennis bends and gapes at the air like a fish, lungs thumping like gills, suck in suck in breathe, baby. Ellis digs into her jeans. “Where the fuck are my fucking handcuffs?” looks up, irritated andor tenacious, “Fuck, Chandler. Give me your handcuffs.” Chandler does not give her his handcuffs but handcuffs Dennis himself. Ellis retrieves the cigarette from behind her ear and lights up. “Whatdafuck? You can’t jus’ arrest me!” “Shut the fuck up,” Chandler snarls, tightening the handcuffs. Ellis blows smoke from her nose. “Come on, you motherfucker,” Ellis ruffles Dennis’ hair as Chandler hauls him off, “Let’s bring you in.” Before they walk into the station, Ellis sprays Lysol all over herself. “Safety precautions,” she claims, and informs Dennis pointedly, “Never let your boss know you’ve been getting high.” “Yeah. Because, you know, smelling like a fucking tangerine is sweet,” Chandler says, shoving Dennis through the door. Ellis chooses to ignore Chandler and smacks Landon’s back. “Hey, boss man. We brought you a present.” --- “So, there I was, caught in my own little debacle. I just, you know, couldn’t choose. It was such a fucking difficult choice. I mean, you know? Black Manolo heels versus white Manolo heels. I swear to God I was sitting there for two hours trying to decide which one would look best with the new Marc Jacobs dress I bought for myself about... oh, three weeks ago, perhaps? Oh my God, it was the funniest thing EVER. Oh, man. Mannn.” After Ellis is done, Dennis stares. Ellis blinks. “What? Did I say something?” “What the fuck, lady? Aren’t I supposed be gettin’ interrogated or somethin’?” “Oh, no. Well, yes, but. I’m not the one interrogating you. Chandler is. He’s just running a little late. Chandler, Chandler. Must be using the bathroom. You know, I told him not to eat that fucking guacamole, told him it was a couple months old. Now he’s shitting a horse. You know why? Because he didn’t listen to me. No one ever listens to me. Is there something wrong with me?” Dennis crosses his arms over his chest. “You dun’ wan’ me to be the judge o’ that, lady.” “What kind of accent is that? Where the fuck are you from?” “Dunno. My momma’s too busy boinkin’ shit to ever tell me anythin’.” “Boinking is a witty word.” Dennis doesn’t say anything. Ellis shrugs. “So, anyway. I totally settled for the black Manolos. I figured white didn’t mesh as well with yellow as does black, right? I might look like a bumblebee, though. Oh shit, what if I look like a bumblebee?” “Black is the only thing that looks good with black,” Dennis offers. Ellis stares at him. “That’s true, somewhat. You’re gay, aren’t you?” Dennis doesn’t say anything. Ellis shrugs. “So, anyway. I totally settled for the black Manolos. I figured white didn’t mesh as well with yellow as does black, right? I might look like a bumblebee, though. Oh shit, what if I look like a bumblebee?” “Black is the only thing that looks good with black,” Dennis offers. Ellis stares at him. “Hey, you’re right. Are you gay?” Dennis snickers. “Naw, lady. I ain’t gay.” “Huh, that’s curious,” Ellis says, “Don’t call me lady, either. My name is Ellis.” “Ellis,” Dennis confirms, cocking an eyebrow. “Ellis,” Chandler sighs, entering the room, fixing the tail of his shirt, “Stop talking to the inmates, for God’s sake.” Holding her hands up in defense, Ellis says, “Hey, he’s no inmate yet. And who else is supposed to keep them busy while you’re off shitting a brick? I told you not to eat that damn guacamole, fucking pig.” Chandler rolls his eyes and takes the seat Ellis previously occupied. Dennis smirks at Ellis as she leaves the room. “So, Dennis. Can you tell me anything about Katherine Walter’s death?” *** c. remember when you walked right through me The man on the radio broadcasts nihilism across the airwaves, faceless with quiet cruelty. He turns up the heartbreak and smothers the quick smiles with words, jagged, uneven ones that always sound furious in Ellis’ mouth, like artificially flavored medicine, like cinnamon. Masochist, the way she is always digging for people to care about, the way she smiles and leans across tables, a little dangerous and a little sly; what with the oddly gentle line of her forearm and the sharply outlined small of her back through a thin shirt. Masochist, the way she always reminds herself not to care in the memos that take form in kisses and stained touches and the choking pressure of clean nails against the side of her neck like something heavy, too much so, heavy with love. The way she sends out postcards on the seventh of each month, stamped with fingerprints and faint tracings of her lips; words distributed like currency to buy organs and mental storage space with. Ellis would like to call this a business, the trade of hours and dyed skies and soft cloth against the inside of her thigh, dreadful in its utter smoothness, lack of resistance. Things like this, they always seem to whisper aphorisms, goodbyes, against the crossroads of her neck and jaw, teeth white and slightly serrated at the edges, breath sour sweet, like the aftertaste of acid. Ellis can feel her pulse drill through thickened skin, she can feel your eyelids shaking, tired, afraid. Man on the radio, boy at the mailbox, girl in her room: everybody cares so much and everybody is so scared, everybody wants to say, I love you, but nobody can, so Ellis whispers it when she lies in bed later that night, clutching a pillow beneath her head, clutching, drinking hope like liquor on Friday nights, and her stereo sings a love song through her hair. the blues ain’t nothing but a pain in your heart when you get a bad start when you and your man have to part i ain’t gonna just sit around and cry and now i won’t die because i love him
