Watery logic

13 June 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: June 13/06 It was raining outside. Sheets of water pounded against the street as pedestrians hustled past the cafe I sat in, with their hoods up in a desperate and hopeless attempt to soften the drops that splashed mercilessly in their face. I didn't pity them; my hair and clothes still had a thin film of water as a result of my trek through the watery outside. I had been on an evening walk to clear my head leading up to what had been a horrible ending to a day of little productivity. Sitting alone in the booth of the older-style coffee shop, I expected to feel comfortable. It was a cozy atmosphere with tasty beverages that warmed your inside, complimented by nice people with enlightening smiles. Yet as I cupped a mug of hot chocolate and peered over the rim, I was, for some unknown reason, anxious. Maybe I was nervous in anticipation of my second meeting with Alex. She was due there anytime, and I couldn't help but wonder what kind of questions she was going to ask me. I was almost a virgin to interviews; my communication skills were limitted to personal affairs. While Alex and I had been involved beforehand, and we were slowly becoming friends again, I wanted to keep this one professional ... loosely, at least. And to think that my answers to her questions were more than likely going to end up in a magazine was also intimidating; I went to shambles in public communication. I took another swig of hot chocolate as the door to the cafe swung open and a gust of wind blew in the door. I experienced a shiver as I turned my attention towards the entrance to see Alex come in, complete with a rain jacket and still soaked from head to toe. She left a trail of water behind her as she left the mat, exchanging hellos with other seated customers at the shop. It was apparent that she was a regular, which would make sense, considering she recommended it as a meeting place and it also seemed like the kind of place journalists would hole up and tackle an assignment. As the excitement in the cafe over the latest regular entering, Alex spotted me and smiled. I returned the subtle greeting as she came over to the booth. She set her notebook and purse on the table and said, 'Sorry I'm late. I never knew that I'd be fighting a tycoon on the way here.' I smiled at the joke and agreed, 'Yeah, not the best out there ... just looking at it makes me feel wet.' 'Not like you aren't already,' she noticed. I abmissibly ran a hand through my hair and ended up with a slightly wet hand. Alex observed with meager interest, then removed her jacket and threw it in the corner of the booth. She sat down across from me and before initiating conversation the waitress came over with a cup and set it in front of her. I observed with curiousity as the waitress said, 'There you go, Alex. Strong coffee. Black.' Alex palmed the cup and brought it to her lips, but didn't drink before saying, 'Thanks Amy,' with a smile. The waitress, Amy, returned it and left. Alex took a sip of her coffee and looked at me, clearly noticing my curiousity. 'I come here often.' 'Often enough that they know what you're ordering?' I asked knowingly. She laughed lightly. 'Okay, I'm here all the time.' 'That's more like it.' We both took the early lull in conversation as an opportunity to sip at our drinks. I was still curious as to what kind of meeting this would prove to be. I dreaded anything question and answer - they made me uncomfortable - but in the same vain, I perhaps was moreso afraid of having to explain my thoughts on wrestling. Unable to anticipate any longer, I went ahead and asked, 'So, how are we going to do this?' 'I don't expect to have any problems, it will be pretty simple and straightforward: I'll ask you questions about wrestling and you answer them; if you have any insight or whatnot, it'd be nice to have that too ...' she explained. I cringed, and Alex noticed. 'What? Something wrong?' 'Not really...' I hesitated. 'I wouldn't consider words my thing, especially verbal.' 'You'll do fine,' she insisted, flipping through her notebook which was crammed with ink already until she found an open page. I sighed. 'So, what's this article about specifically?' Alex shrugged. 'Basically I'm trying to find an underlying purpose in wrestling and persuade others that it's more than just a fake, wannabe sport with naked guys butting shoulders.' 'Impressive,' I commented at her good use of words and strange talent at making a drawn-out sentence comprehendible. 'I don't know how I'm going to help you with that ...' 'Nonsense. There has to be some reason you put your body on the line every night, with a high risk of injury as well as being away from home constantly. And from what I read about the AWC, the salary is within reason.' I nodded. 'It's difficult for me to explain the experience, rather, numerous experiences. It's hard to form words based on a feeling that cannot be understood even by the person that it belongs to; it's one of those complexes that nobody really questions ... it's just ... there. I can't describe the intense passion and emotion that comes with going head to head with rivals in the eyes of fans; just like how a genius can't explain their thought process outside of the numbers, experiments, and experiences turned into ideas. I get into the ring every night for the love of wrestling, something that has failed to escape me ever since I left University; even after experiencing other career choices that proved to be only momentary.' Alex propped her elbows on the table and processed my words while observing me awkwardly. I felt almost uncomfortable under the spotlight of someone who relied on her strength in words as a living; it was like challenging the Rock in trash talking or wrestling Shamrock in the Lions Den. The silence was fitting and I took a long sip of hot chocolate as comfort; she mimicked with her cup of coffee as she poised her pen and started filling lines of looseleaf with words naturally. I watched in awe. 'Y'know, for somebody who didn't know what he was going to say, you sure got the jump on me. We hadn't even started the 'interview', for lack of a better word,' she said casually as she continued to write, something that also amazed me. My talent in that area was limitted to jotting down phone numbers while being told them. She put a period on her last sentence then looked back up at me, elbows still propped on table. 'Why do you think wrestling has gained such popularity, but in the meantime, gained a reputation where many people critisize it or scoff at the thought of it?' I frowned. 'It's one of those things that you love or you hate. As far as it's popularity goes ... well ... the aspect of entertainment in a huge contributor for that. North Americans crave to see drama and fighting, which modern wrestling provides a nice mix of. The club I am currently in provides a good example of that - a deep pool of talent with a lot of great storylines developing.' 'And why are storylines so significant?' 'Wrestling has evolved with society. People love to see other people's lives messed up, even if it is on television,' I said simply. 'You should have experienced that.' She looked at me and smirked. 'I report real news, Sean.' 'Cedric,' I corrected. 'Right ... you still haven't told me that story.' 'Don't expect me to,' I stated clearly, putting an end to the conversation before it even started. 'Besides, reporting real news is the same idea. People love to read about other people's misery.' She shrugged. 'True enough ... but we're straying off topic. Tell me about the aspect of winning and losing in wrestling, and why it's so important.' 'That's ... a weird question,' I responded. 'Why?' I looked for the right way to explain myself. 'Because .. I feel like your bridging the gap between my wrestling life and my outside, real life; essentially they're both a significant part of each other, but ironically different in an important way. It's something I've never really thought about ... and never thought I would.' I paused. 'So that's why it's a weird question.' She wrote something down quickly, then looked at me again curiously. 'Well ... that's sort of the point.' I chuckled. 'That's what I was afraid of.' 'The public wants to know how much of wrestling is actually competitive and how much of it is staged,' she said bluntly. 'They're the same thing ...' I began. 'No, they're not, Cedric.' 'You don't understand. How am I supposed to talk about this and save what character I have left as well as the novelty of wrestling that's apart of my career and the league I wrestle in? You can't bridge the gap. It can't be done,' I finished on an almost furious note. 'Alright, I'll accept that,' she said, writing more down. 'Good, because I'm done here. I've said all I can say,' I said bluntly, putting a few bills on the table and standing up. 'Nice seeing you, Alex.' 'You too, Sean.' I didn't bother to correct her as I manuevered out of the booth and through the cafe. We didn't make that big of a scene; I'm sure the regulars had experienced a lot of meetings ending on a sour note. I could have cared less, and had the same attitude towards storming out into the rain without a vehicle waiting for me. The intense rain had died only slightly since arriving in the cafe, which didn't seem like that long ago, but my watch told me different. I lifted my hood over my head and walked down the sidewalk, dodging people who rushed to find shelter while wielding umbrellas. I navigated downtown Berkeley until I found a park, where an empty bench was waiting for me under the cover of the overhanging branches of a large poplar. I sat down and took in the scenery, which seemed like something I could withstand for a while; the outdoors were becoming more and more of a stress reliever by the day. And I say 'stress' as if I had it, which realistically, I didn't. Up until the week prior, wrestling was slowly becoming a chore that I had to endure by the week. I didn't look ahead nor look backward, I merely did what I was expected to do, which gave me a mixed bag of results, something that I wasn't satisfied with. But ultimately it was my own damn fault. That message was etched into the front of my head and I wanted to erase it. The Cage Carnage match was almost my claim to fame. I was a single pinfall away from competing for AWC's biggest prize against the best wrestler in the business; I almost skipped the que-line and became a main eventer in a day. It wasn't meant to be, as I was defeated by experience, and hats off to Ellis Nash. But a tie for second place sufficed, especially among a handful of respectable opponents. Winning on the Fresh to follow would mean facing Ellis Nash for the Frontier Title. It promised to be a difficult task, with names such as Anton Assault, Darcy Crisis, and Aimz coming to mind. All three of them boasted a resume that I had no hope of comparing to; they were all favoured over me simply by looking at the track records. I was the guy who slipped into the second last match of the bill due to an 'excellent performance last week', the most overrated, regretful, and risky position in professional wrestling. What, exactly, was Pearl thinking? Pearl knew I had potential. Pearl knew I was talented even if I wasn't living up to personal hype, even if it wasn't immediately admitted. It is easily said that I got into that match based on performance from the week before, but in wrestling, one meager match isn't enough to do it. I was a flower waiting to blossom. I was waiting for my turning point; the chance to go from being a zero to being something. I over-hyped myself by claiming to be under-rated, and there was only way to live up to that claim - by winning matches against respected opponents. All I needed was the follow through. I didn't expect to win. Although I was a confident man, one thing I also expected of myself was honesty and realism. Anton Assault destroyed me in what I believed to be one of my better matches thus far in AWC; he was way above me in talent, experience, and sheer intellect; Aimz was a former Frontier Champion - she knew how to pick up the torch and carry it. I also lost to Darcy Crisis in my AWC debut, although holding my own in that match, it's nothing notable. But I wasn't fighting a losing battle. Because the great thing about wrestling is that anything can happen at anytime. The variables, atmosphere, passion, and tension surrounding a matchup with four talented wrestlers all officated by one man basically spells unpredictable. Unbelievable results have been a frequency since the birth of wrestling - the underdog always has that slim chance of winning. I sat on the underdog pedestal at the time, and would ride it to the end if I had to. I didn't need the advantage. I didn't need a sense of security that I was a comparable talent to my opponents. All I needed was one shining moment. That was what wrestling was about. Write that down, Alex.
