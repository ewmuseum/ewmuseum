In The Beginning: A Lion's Tale (iii)

19 September 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 19th of September, 2006 In The Beginning: A Lion’s Tale. The thick cadence of an aeroplane engine distorts the serenity of the plains of the Serengeti as it flies high above the numinous sun scorched savannah. The plane dives and ascends like some endless yoyo, back and forth on its merry journey. It was an ancient piece of aeronautical equipment, taken from a time when air travel was a luxury and not a commodity sold en masse, it was from a time when air travel meant intrigue and mystique: a far cry from the package holiday sensation experienced today. It really didn’t matter though. The magic of such an old plane was like Mozart to the ears of a classical musician, like that sweet spot on a football only a mere God like Maradona could touch, like that old Mustang brought back to life. It was not, my friends, the vehicle or the means or the colour of the craft that was transporting them. No my friends, it was about the people on board. Such a wondrous juxtaposition of contemporary life and the ancien regime of yester year in such a confined environment: it was a journalistic dream. The one uniting feature amongst the jovial brigade was both enmeshed in a sense of respect for nature and a sacrosanct duty to the divine above. It was of course humorous to note that even though every person on this plane was indeed as devout as Mother Theresa to the cause, they were all uniquely and fiercely individual. Nestled in towards the tail end of the plane, sat on aesthetics alone, the two least extroverted passengers aboard this classical piece of engineering, a husband and wife, newly married. The husband sat relaxed with his right arm cradling his most treasured asset and she burrowed deep into his powerful arms and chest, he was of course ever the protector. Both their eyes were transfixed to a bundle located in the wife’s arms. Cradling her newborn child with the love only a mother can possess, a love so pure and wholesome nothing ever could tarnish such sentiment. The child was thickly built and already it was noticeable he would inherit his fathers strapping physique. His tuft of hair swirled with every breath from the doting parents, and he was happy and he was content. “Mr. Reihana, would you like any refreshments?” the words passed the lips of an elderly lady clad in a pristinely kept Nun’s garment. “We are fine sister, but thank you anyway.” He had a certain aura surrounding him, a presence, and it was far more than just his domineering physique. Each vowel was crisply formed, each consonant escaping his tongue with a perfect pronunciation; I mean these are certain elements that you would expect from a Harvard scholar. There was no pomp, no airs and graces around this man. He lived his life like a chocolate, dark and intriguing. Under that hard exterior was a soft nougaty centre. It was Wonka-esque in the analogy; getting to know Mr. Reihana was like winning a golden ticket. Rare. Intensely private, his business was always his own and his alone. He was one of those self made men who came from nothing to attending one of the top educational facilities this world possesses. However he was seen through the eyes of others it mattered little. He was indeed his own man, a mind carved from granite, he was set in his own ways and customs, and for a solitary second allowed his gaze from the miracle curled in his lovers arms to intertwine with his sumptuous wife. Fiji was a land absent of any gender prejudices to inculcate values of abhorrence to the female form: or worse, to see them as objects, or unequivocal in this game of chess known as existence. Little Umaga would form a bond quite permanent with his mother. His tiny neck adorned a set of rosary beads. Much was the religious undertone of the journey as a whole; his parents felt he should be sent on the spiritual conveyor belt from an early age. They were of course like all Fijians guided by the good and the grace of the divine entity above. They were firm believers in just believing; whether you called him Allah, Adonai or God, the Reihana’s felt robustly that as long as you believed in some spiritual entity, that ultimately your life would both be guided and end in a pleasant fashion. Naïve? Possibly. Noble? Most definitely. And then. As if a dude had your car, the whole world came crashing down. That classic plane had finally flown its last inch in the sky it so longed to be one with, so longed just for one second to be free in again, some fifty years ago when it was the forefront of aeroplane design, when it was that trendsetter: all that was a long cry off. When it’s your time… it’s your time. There were no screams. The plane was caught in a solitary prayer whose words were unspoken: all hoping for those precious seconds remaining in life that they were to be taken safely to another place far greater than this. To transcend this life into the next with the ease of a Garbage Bag Johnny segment, the guy was monumental, as was the journey to the next life. I only hope to emulate one day… One… last… kiss… “Goodbye Umaga… I’ll see you soon okay? You be a good boy now…” the last words from a mother who surpassed the qualities needed to take a life into the world. She had warmth so comforting… and for what… to die a painful and agonising death… a servant to God… the good die young. Fuck the good. Fuck the young. Fuck God? He wouldn’t quite dream of it… not yet anyway. No amount of undue anger, no amount of emotion could stop this glorious machine meet its impending doom. It was like watching a child play with a toy plane, changing and chopping altitude at irregular angles, before being cast aside as Buzz Lightyear becomes available. So long Woody… you are now outmoded. … Calm before storm, before calm, before pandemonium, one o’clock, two o’clock, three o’clock… rock… That solace one finds on safari. Broken. Bits of contorted metal and shards of shattered glass tore through the air at breakneck speed with a deadly inaccuracy, scattering on the sun seared earth below. This area of Tanzania was as beautiful as it was remote, as aesthetically pleasing as an 8th and Ocean model, yet carrying every part of their ineptitudes as people. This place was deadly. The eye of the storm passed. An earth shattering thud resounded across the Tanzanian plains sending the Serengeti into an earlier than expected Disney moment: the Lion King had arrived. The birds they sang, oh how they sang, like a well honed Backstreet Boy quartet their song sent messages to all the other animals causing a furore to erupt across the savannah. Herds quickly sought new territory as the large metal invader ground slowly to a numbing halt, elephants charged, and lions ran, cheetahs cheated their way across the cumbersome grasses. In those precocious moments when life was extinguished for perhaps the first time in such a magnanimous event tantamount to the biggest disaster these plains had seen since Lion King ½ there was such a thunderous amount of noise. It was the most unholy of choirs, each animal grunting in a semblance of sibilance suggesting surreptitiously certainty that this indeed was a moment that would change their animal existences… it was for some the first time they had been invaded, but by God, it would not be the last. And then. Normality. Or so it seemed. Except now here lies an invader. The infidel: colonising that coconut tree with every skill of a Bounty chocolate bar, soft and crumbly like this plain, with a coconut interior. Would the animals drive the conquerors from their territory, or suffer the same fate both the aborigines and the Indians faced. Silence. Excruciating. Death. Certainly. It was painfully beautiful that somehow now in all this tangled and discombobulated metal, despite the deaths, normality now resumed. We were no more born to fly than we were to endlessly pursue perfection. Both in this world and the next the good reaped the benefits of the seeds they sowed. I’m not made to incessantly philosophise, I don’t possess the heart or the soul or the mind for it. His heart was more like a machine than any sentient being, made and constructed to spill blood in a fist fight, lord it over his foe, then drive a sword through their very heart. He was born perhaps centuries too late, much suited to a land where the barbarians reigned supreme and samurai lived by a code of honour and everything was far greater and remote… A sound. Distinct. Like a wailing… No. Could it? From within the confines of the twisted metal, and beyond the putrid stench of death that clung to the walls and dissipated out to the surrounding area like somebody else’s cooking, that quaint cry grew ever so slightly. The bodies were lifeless… devoid of any remnants that these people were once the very best and brightest of a time where the dollar reigned supreme. Yet, there was but one life preserved. He was alone now more than other; the rosary beads all that he had left of an existence that promised much and now lie wrought in the rubble and bodies of his parents. The crimson ridden bodies of Mr and Mrs Reihana had somehow managed to protect him from the impact of the crash, he was shielded and loved eternally. His mother looked as beautiful now as ever she did, aside from the trickle of blood from her inner lip, her face was ever the canvas of perfection. Night quickly descended like a shadowy ninja from the depths of the African heavens. The darkness here was like dark you have never witnessed: so black even the soul of one Michael Sloan would have illuminated the area with a glowing light of warmth. Yes. It really was that dark. The sounds of the Tanzanian night brought into life under this clandestine carpet pervaded the senses of the child. It was like a nightmare brought into a reality too indistinguishable from the dream world whence the thoughts emanated. Inside the plane now the cold of the African air was beginning to grip the body of the child, its breath materialising in the chill of the night, dancing a mesmerising dance of hope and prosperity as it rose upward toward his fathers face. Tilted at a now abnormal angle the once stout neck of Mr. Reihana twisted, it had snapped in the crash. It was weird how the plane never caught alight. God works in mysterious ways, and maybe… just maybe… this was one of them. Over the horizon a snake of light appeared like something from the 13th Warrior, slithering its way steadily but surely toward the crash site. The snake of light belonged to none other than the quasi nomadic tribe of the Maasai Tribe, Tanzania's indigenous tribe. Their brightly coloured traditional garb brought a sense of colour and tradition to the Serengeti night as reds and yellows sought to reflect the effervescent nature of their immensely rooted culture. They had seen the crash happen from a distance away, and much like the animals they lived in harmony with, there was a certain air of unease. They too felt like some strange new person had come into their home uninvited and started to try and sell them things. It was uncomfortable. These people believed in honour, believed in instilling within their young a sense of pride and belief, believed that this life was but a step on the rung in the grand scheme of things. In many respects they were similar to Umaga’s own parents. This sense of a universal honour existing was a concept foreign to those who fought in American Sports. Anyway to win a contest, by any means necessary, that ever so popular phrase garnered for thick-witted American patriots to disguise their complete illegality in the eyes of the world. They were imbued without a sense of honour that the majority of civilisations throughout the world garnered as a cornerstone of their existence. Honour was in the eye of the beholder? Much like beauty? Anyone who cannot look at a newborn child and see the beauty of such a moment was not a human. So there was a universal concept of beauty. As there was as a universal sense of honour – yet unlike beauty, with the westernisation of civilisation, this concept was slowly being lost. Shakespeare put it most beautifully, “Mine honour is my life, both grow in one. Take honour from me, and my life is done. Then, dear my liege, mine honour let me try; In that I live, and for that I will die.” Honour was a possession of the soul as Pedro Crespo would intimate, and nobody could ever take that away from you, but you alone. As he grew up he would see men without honour, and as he entered the world of professional sport, he could count on one hand the amount of men who fought with honour… it was imbued from such an early age that it became the most salient part of Umaga’s early existence. The Maasai now approached the plane, their feet made no noise as they eloquently floated above the matted grass stalks. They would have made excellent assassins: and they did. It was both awe inspiring and a revelation to see the concurrence of old and new: the Maasai and modern technology. One man, more brave, more of a man, more of a warrior than anybody in the whole of Africa stepped forward to investigate. Gasps reverberated around him as he stepped forward to the sleeping beast, spear in hand as beads of sweat dripped down his noble brow. His name was Atapa, the finest Maasai warrior for many generations. He’d never known the feeling of fear but now it stared him straight in the face like a George Galloway argument, he had no facts of the situation, so Galloway crushed him. What the fuck was this thing? He’d heard tales of such a creature… it swallowed humans, then transported them to other places in the air… sort of like a bird. He jumped back quickly prompting the other Maasai to depart another twenty yards from the scene… “Baby… baby!” he yelled like a child discovering the pleasures of a new sweet. Soon the news broke around the travelling party as a chorus of “baby, baby!” broke out like a sweet song serenading the savannah sky. Atapa cautiously entered the plane and quickly noticed something was not right. There were people here but none of them were moving… or breathing. That was the lottery moment right there. Thinking you had won the lottery; then realising your ticket was for last weeks draw. For Atapa, death was a natural occurrence, he’d seen plenty in his young experience, and he’d been at the end of the spear of many a death. This was wholeheartedly different though; this was almost genocide. He crept silently through the cabin trying to target the sound of the child, but it had fallen silent, as if it knew it was going to be taken from its mothers arms and did not want to relinquish her grip just yet… just a few more moments… she was cold… she was dead… but in her soul was a warmth that would have melted even the most blackest of hearts. Atapa noted there must have been at least thirty dead here… at least… then… there it was: that faint wail of a child, muffled somewhat, but distinct. Atapa moved swiftly toward the back of the plane using the headrests of each chair to pull himself along, his fantastically red robe adorning his slim yet athletic body moved with the breeze. There he was. A child. Cradled in both his parents sat little Umaga unscathed from the crash… he was still content, and he let out a little gurgle of pleasure as his eyes met Atapa’s. Atapa raised a smile, he’d always wanted a child, but had little time to conceive for find a suitable wife for such a high standing member of Maasai society. He looked down at Umaga and read the medical bracelet around his chubby little right wrist… “Umaga…” he struggled ever so slightly with the pronunciation, but it was evident he spoke English… and he spoke English well. “You will be my little Lion…” he quipped, happy at his own deductions. He spoke in Maasai tongue offering thoughts to the parents and to all on the plane that they may pass to whichever God they believed in safe and harmonious fashion before removing little Umaga from his mothers grip. There were no cries, no blubbering from the little Lion: I think deep down he knew he was going to a happy place, and well… it was a necessity anyway. These people were taking him to live and to operate in their society as an equal, he would thank them later for saving his life, and ultimately thank them for imbuing within him a sense of right and wrong, of morality and ultimately of life itself. Atapa came from within the confines of the belly of the beast cradling little Umaga Lion over his shoulder prompting a roar of approval from the awaiting party. A quick shake of the head soon silenced the party, “many… dead…” that sombre feeling gripped the party around the collective throat and throttled viciously, the moment every parent of a solider dreads, that knock on the door… your son/daughter is dead, knocking every last ounce of wind from your sails. “Little Lion is all that survived…” he once more looked out across the sea of faces, “At least… at least this survived.” Atapa raised Umaga over his head, his Polynesian complexion glistening in the reflected firelight. “My little Lion… my Umaga.” The crowd broke out into song… the circle of life… it moves us all… etcetera… except they didn’t. There was a sort of reverence of the now orphaned cub… as if now a certain aura exuded from his very tiny pores infecting the nearby like sickness to the down. That night the little Lion and his adoptive father made the long and arduous trek home in the bitter tinges of a Serengeti night. Though a longer journey was about to begin. There were stark differences between little Umaga and the other children of the Maasai tribe, namely in size. Umaga was already the size and strength of an eleven year old at the age of seven, dominating the other children in all aspects of physical encounters. Furthermore, Umaga was a gifted and skilled learner, picking up the need for morality in a society and even upon request of Atapa being taught by missionaries the ‘real’ world. He grew up as if he were indeed Atapa’s own son and he enjoyed the laid back lifestyle he experienced here. After experiencing the peace and tranquillity of a Tanzanian indigenous life, it is of course no great shock to see how maladjusted Umaga is in contemporary society. He shuns everything about the western world: the glitz, the glamour, the money and the fame. However it would be two days… two days in Maasai history that would forever change both Umaga and Maasai folk law for generations and years to come. At the age of eleven years Umaga was the size and strength of someone six years his elder: “they’re big boys…” O’Driscoll quipped when playing the Fijian national team… they were big boys indeed. The crucifix beads had never left his neck; they were of course eternally fused to his existence by the paternal bond that kept burning way beyond the prescribed limit. He prayed every night that his parents were proud of him, and that they loved him still. They reciprocated this love more than Umaga ever really comprehended, and even more so after Umaga became a hero among the Maasai people. It was late at night when it happened… the screams of several villagers were met with widespread panic. People ran for their lives: and this was no euphemism. A lion had crept into the tribe late at night and savagely attacked a child… or so it seemed… as Atapa burst into the hut where the lion attacked, crimson stuck to the earth like syrup over a nutritious American breakfast. He let out an anguished cry before noticing something both astonishing… and terrifying. For here… in Umaga’s tent was a lion as dead to the world as the heart of Darth Vader. In the corner cradled against the wall sat Umaga… his hands smeared in blood… his dreadlocked hair covering his eyes… “What happened here… Umaga?” Atapa’s voice was quivering: he had his suspicions but did not want to believe them. Umaga raised his head but was cut off by the voice of an elder in the community, “He saved us Atapa… he saved us all… the lion… it would have killed us. Umaga he… he… killed it.” The woman was clearly shaken… Atapa looked at Umaga with a face that said only disbelief… “come Umaga… come walk with me…” the path cleared quickly for the Fijian Lion – his moniker that night would be set in stone. As he left the hut he could hear their whispers… “How?”… “He’s only a boy…”… and he was… but he was more than just any boy… he was a Lion. “What happened in there Umaga…?” the words slipped from Atapa’s mouth. “It just… it just tried to hurt them Ata… and I couldn’t let it happen… and I just… I just snapped. I don’t remember much… but I think I broke it.” He looked up for some approval… “you’re not mad are you… Ata?” his voice was that of a tiny child still. Atapa looked at him in disbelief. He was but a boy… what courage if anything… but pure animalistic strength was so rare… “I’m not mad Umaga… but you see, you killed a Lion today, bare handed… it’s just a little shocking. How could I be mad at you? You’re my little Lion…” he raised a smile through the pain. Umaga was a hero now… but Atapa realised then and there little Umaga… his little lion was a warrior: with the right training… he could make… no… or could he? “Ata… I’m sorry. Please. Don’t look so sad.” There was tenderness in Umaga that reflected so much about his parents. It’s funny what prison will do to a person… every last ounce of morality and goodness within him seemed to seep through the bloodied cracks in the wall in that fucking place. The news filtered through the Tanzanian savannah and into the capital cities and beyond much like Z-List celebrity gossip at a Big Brother reunion, and word spread fast… prompting her arrival. She arrived by armoured Land Rover… as if the Tanzanian people automatically were going to open fire on her on the Savannah. She though radiated that same energy his mother had done for the years of her existence. She had this because she was family… well… practically family. She was the maid of the Reihana’s and had inherited the Reihana estate upon their passing away. “Umaga come here… I have someone who wants to meet you.” The glint in Atapa’s eyes had vanished. The Lion could tell something was wrong… She saw him emerge from the hut… it was like looking into a portal… he was the image of his father. “My… how you’ve grown… I must say you are the image of your father…” her voice was well spoken and educated. “Still wearing the beads?” Umaga reached up for the beads that clung to his neck… “who… who are you…” but he subconsciously knew… Atapa was letting his little Lion go today… go back home where he belonged. He’d always be a part of his life, the lady had expressly told him he was welcome to come whenever he saw fit. Although Umaga was unsure, he’d tell him to go… to make something of his life far greater than a nomadic farmer on the Serengeti plains. Anyway, the lady promised to send him to the classes he’d suggested… deep within the spirit of Umaga was a warrior, and she should bring that out in a controlled fashion. After the Lion incident he was always slightly fearful of Umaga’s strength and internal demeanour. It worried him that someone so young had such an explosive power in them that they could, single handed, break a Lions neck. “We’re going home to Fiji Umaga… home…” she smiled at him as if he too were her own. “Mei…?” his eyes lit up. How did he know… he was barely born when Mei first laid eyes upon his beautiful face… “Why yes… but how…” she stuttered… Umaga looked to Atapa and gripped his rosary beads once again. Atapa smiled and knew that this child was special: he was a great spirit, a warrior… he would go far. He sat now in 2006 a completely different character, still clutching his rosary beads, still that little Lion… he was now the Relentless Champion in a wrestling federation. It seemed like a division so tailor made for GA it was almost too perfect to be real… he could fight till his heart no longer moved… none of this wrestling bollocks. He had predicted he’d straight up Rice Krispy someone… but this week it was all about making someone Flake… like that Cadbury treat, the Captain was going to crumble. Sure he was defeated last week… but the match was effectively two on one throughout, and he’d only been pinned after being practically decapitated with a title belt. Twice. It was a hollow victory but victory nonetheless. Gabriel as he was now known was beyond that, he had his honour to uphold… and honour to the Maasai was everything, and honour to Gabriel was everything and Atapa in his eyes was his all and Mei too… he had but one love in his life encompassed under the friends and family appendage of life. The Empire was looking strong for this show, the new addition Kintu looked like he had a point to prove… Afeaki could care less if he inherited the muscle role. He was hardly the competitive type… and he was slowly losing heart in the organisation. This was not what he set out to achieve… a tool for Harber’s use, and a toy for Lavelle to get his kicks out of with. There was something wholeheartedly evil about Kintu, he was bigger than GA without question: but pairing the two in a cage would be a different matter. He’d seen them all before in Flatlined fighting… and one after another after another fell… Kintu had a jaw. That was all that counted. He yearned to return to the cages and all that they inspired within him. Less of the flashing bulbs, less of the razzamatazz, less is more… more or less… his eyes skimmed down to the title… as he remembered his favourite quote… “… there are moments when the telling of my own story gives me the most honest of honest pleasures, perhaps because I feel so far removed from what I am telling that I seem to be repeating a story from hearsay about some unknown person...” How fucking apt he thought… how fucking apt indeed. .::oti::.
