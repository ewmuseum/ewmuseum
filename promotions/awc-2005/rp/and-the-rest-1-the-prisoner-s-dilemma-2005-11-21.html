And The Rest #1: 'The Prisoner's Dilemma'

21 November 2005

Date: 21st November 2005 19th November 2005 10:45 Tap tap tap tap tap tap tap... pause... tap tap... pause... tap tap. Listen. Drum drum drum. Wait. Itch itch itch. Wait some more. Sigh. Hang up. 19th November 2005 11:34 Tap tap tap – Wait. How do I use redial again? Ah, fuck it. Tap tap tap tap tap tap tap... pause... Oh yeah, I remember. ...tap tap tap tap. Listen. Drum drum drum. Wait. Bladder needs emptying, stomach needs filling, work needs doing... Wait some more. Sigh. Hang up. It’ll have to wait. 19th November 2005 12:09 Kung fu, do what you do to me I haven’t been the same since my teenage lobotomy Full on, I moved to Hong Kong With Bruce Lee’s brother and Johnny Wong I think it’s strange, he’s friends with Fu Manchu And he thinks he knows you Oh oh oh oh oh oh Oh Daniel San, made in Taiwan Come on Jackie Ch – “NOISE POLLUTION!” “Ruuuaaaarghhh!” I let out a startled gasp and span around in my chair. My eyes rolled back into my skull as I saw Henry Archibald Bennington poised by my hi-fi, one hand resting pompously on his hip and the other returning to his side having just committed the heinous sin of turning off Ash. His eyes were bright and his chest puffed out with self-importance, a white shirt stuffed roughly into his corduroy trousers as it strained to hold in his decidedly chubby upper body. His hair was combed immaculately, hairline receding at eighteen but it didn’t matter because not a single one was out of place, and his cheeks looked, as usual, as if he’d never had the need to shave in his life – shiny and rosy and empty of stubble. I knew for a fact, though, that he spent forty-five minutes every morning in the bathroom down the corridor with foam on his face and a razor propped expertly between his fingers. I knew this because he insisted on locking shut the entire communal bathroom while doing so. Not the wisest move when you’re a freshman at college and you’re supposed to be sharing said bathroom with six peers, all of whom are busy making the friends that’ll last them a lifetime and won’t include you... six peers, and of course that strange sophomore at the end of the corridor who chose to stay in his first-year accommodation. Freak. But I was of use to Henry, because like him, I was an Englishman living in the States, and also happened to be his designated “parent”. Like many European universities, Harbrook operated a “family” system, in which all new first-years were assigned a “father” and “mother” from the year above to help them through their first term at university. In practice, no one bothered with it; the previous year I hadn’t even known that I had “parents”, and wouldn’t have made use of them anyway. Henry, though, was going to make full use of me. He was determined to grab all he could from what Harbrook had to offer, and in a way, I admired him for that – but the loud, incessant knocks at the door as I was in the midst of proving some facet of number theory did start to drag after a while. My solution to this: coaxing a spare key to my room out of the receptionist – who seemed to worship my every movement, so it wasn’t too difficult – and forcing it onto him while explaining clearly that whenever he wanted me he could walk straight in, but he should be silent until I acknowledged him. Needless to say, this hadn’t really been working so well, and it was often quite a shock to the system to hear a sudden exclamation right by my ear, Henry having crept in without my noticing due to the music pumping out from my hi-fi. I looked down at my page despondently; my ballpoint pen had ripped right through it from bottom to top and there was no way Richard (Sir R. Blakey, my favourite professor) would even consider giving this a look. I resigned myself to copying it out and carrying on later, and turned back to Henry, but he wasn’t behind my chair anymore. “This wine tastes like water!” he announced, peering suspiciously into a glass of clear liquid from which he had just taken a sip. “That’s because it is water...” I said nonplussed. “Aha! So it is!” Henry exclaimed, and set it back down, satisfied. “Where’s the wine then, old chap? Break out a bottle! We’re celebrating!” I sighed. “We are?” Henry perched himself on the side of my bed, resting his elbows on his knees and his head in his hands and rocking from side to side. “Naturally! Be thankful, Timothy, be joyful!” “Henry, what are we celebrating?” I asked patiently. I honestly hadn’t the faintest idea what he was on about. “Well... you know!” he said brusquely, waving a hand at nothing in particular as he straightened his back. “There’s got to be something...” Henry jumped to his feet and began pacing around the room, casting challenging looks at my desk and my blank walls, undoubtedly trying to sniff out where the wine was hiding itself. Shaking my head in sheer amazement, I turned back to my problem, trying to remember what the HELL the difference between äx and dx was. No sooner had I put my mind to it than he was barking another question in my general direction. “Where’s the fridge, old chap?” “No fridge, Henry,” I said listlessly, gritting my teeth. “What? No fridge?” He sounded truly gobsmacked. “Henry, you know very well I don’t have a fridge. I don’t think a single student room in the college has a fridge,” I addressed to him rattily, slinging an arm over the back of my chair. He stared at me open-mouthed, his lips oscillating as he slowly digested what I had to say. Suddenly, it dawned on him that my words made some sense when put together in the order in which I’d said them, and he nodded vigorously. “Right you are! Right you are, old chap!” As you might have already inferred, Henry came from a posh background. He claimed to be an Old Etonian, but seeing straight through this I’d made some inquiries (here the receptionist came in useful again) and found that he had in fact been educated at Oundle. Oundle, if you don’t know it, is like an old-fashioned public school without the quality education but with the extortionate fees. It’s a place for those slightly dim boys whose parents have trained them their whole lives for their Eton or Winchester interviews, but at the end of the day not been able to knock enough intelligence into them to fool the admissions tutors into a costly decision. Often, a hefty donation to the science department would still do the trick, but evidently in Henry’s case Mr and Mrs Bennington hadn’t been able to balance the scales, let alone tip them. “Henry...” He turned to me, ceasing the random mutterings under his breath and the somewhat worrying movements of his arms that might have been construed in some circles as a modern form of dance. “Yes yes? Yes yes yes?” He shook his head as he spoke, his jollity all the more forced. “Why are you here?” That one stumped him. He stared into space for a good ten seconds before realisation of his situation finally struck him like a bolt of lightning. “Ah!” he exclaimed, galvanised by at last having a purpose. “Aha! Yes, yes! Now I remember!” “Good,” I murmured. “So...?” “Patience, old boy! Just coming to that! No need to get your knickers in a twist!” He strode away from me as he spoke, striding out of the corridor and throwing a hand to his head. “Oh, no,” I muttered under my breath, and leapt up to stop him. Once Henry Bennington had decided his hair needed seeing to, it took quite some convincing to stop him locking himself in the bathroom for a good hour. I really needed to work on getting the janitorial team to remove that lock... “Henry! Wait! Your hair’s fine!” I chased after him, breaking into a jog as he gathered pace, his brisk strides exuding class, or so he thought at least. “Henry! Stop!” Finally I caught up with him, grabbing the back of his shirt with a firm hand to prevent his progress into the bathroom. A toilet flushed, and a second later a short petite blonde was giving us a mighty strange look as she edged out of the bathroom and back to her room. “Be sensible, Henry, you’re embarrassing yourself,” I muttered, and he didn’t protest as I dragged him back to my room. My inkling was that Mr Bennington had the beginnings of a crush on our unnamed blonde. Pushing him onto my bed, I shut the door, ignoring the sexual connotations the narrative had just thrown up, and hopped back onto my desk chair to lean forward and look him in the eyes. “So.” “So,” he said morosely, looking at the floor. “Calmed down a bit, have we?” “In a manner of speaking...” “Good. Now tell me what you want, and I’ll try to help you with it.” “I –” I leant closer. “Yes?” “I don’t –” “What don’t you?” “G... g-game theory.” “You don’t what game theory?” “Don’t under... underst-stand.” “What in particular about game theory don’t you understand, Henry?” “The p-p-prisoner’s dilemma...” He suddenly sniffed loudly, and his timing was fortunate, because he wouldn’t have been all too happy at the semi-chortle that erupted from me against my will on hearing what was troubling him. The prisoner’s dilemma was a basic staple of game theory, one of the bases on which more complex theories were built. If he couldn’t do that... well, he was going to struggle. “The prisoner’s dilemma,” I began wearily, recalling the content of the short game theory module that I had completed in my freshman year, “is a basic game in which there are two prisoners – let’s call them Tim and Henry, OK?” His head whipped up, nodding as he watched me. “Jolly good. Go on.” “So let’s say we’ve... been found guilty of... I don’t know, smoking pot in here or something. The Disciplinary Board takes us away and keeps us in separate rooms. They’ve got no evidence but they know we did it. We then each have two options: we can confess to it or deny it –” I stopped, seeing the sorrowful expression on his face. “What?” I snapped. “But... but Tim... I’ve never sm-smoked, not pot, not here...” I grinned. “It’s hypothetical, Henry.” “Gotcha,” he announced, though he didn’t have a clue what I meant. “This is just for an example. I don’t mean we actually did it.” “Right... brilliant. Top notch. Carry on.” Raising my eyebrows, I continued. “So the Disciplinary Board decides to offer us both a deal – though we don’t know we’re both getting offered it. They come to you and they say, if you confess and I deny it, you get suspended; if you deny but I confess, you get expelled and go to jail for five years; if we both confess, we’re both expelled but they don’t get the police involved and they give us... er... a small cash reward for confessing; and if we both deny it, we both get suspended, for... ah... other crimes they have evidence for.” Henry’s expression was blank. “So if you were acting rationally,” I went on, “what would you do?” ”We’d both deny it,” he began. “Well, that’d be the best outcome for the group, yes. But you don’t know what I'm going to say. Let’s take the two options. If I confess, then your two options are confessional or denial. If you confess, you get expelled; if you deny, you go to prison. What do you do?” “Confess,” he said at once, and I breathed a sigh of relief. “Right. And the other situation is if I deny it. If you confess, you get suspended but you get some money; if you deny, you get suspended. What do you do?” “Confess,” he said again, more slowly, and I smiled. “Right. So then flip the situation around and look at it from my point of view. I act rationally too, so I do exactly the same. Can you see that we’re both going to confess?” “Yes,” Henry confirmed. “So the college gets what they want. A double confession.” He sat in silence for a few seconds, gazing at me with what looked horribly like admiration. “That’s really clever!” “Indeed. But it also explains why in the real world, firms try and get together when making decisions – you’ve done collusion in oligopoly?” Henry nodded. “Go back to the prisoner’s dilemma,” I said decisively, “but this time, we’re able to talk to each other. What will we do?” “We’ll both deny it, old chap!” Henry informed me with the air of a genius. I nodded appreciatively. “That’s right. And so, with collusion, we get the best possible outcome for the group as a whole. When businesses collude, they can set prices and output to maximise profit for the group as a whole.” “But isn’t that illegal?” he asked in surprise. “Sometimes, yes, but there are some situations when it’s perfectly legal, but still a bit shady. OPEC?” I threw the name out, hoping he’d grab my line. “The oil countries?” he ventured questioningly. I nodded again. I felt just like a teacher. “Cheers!” Henry said suddenly, standing up and grabbing my hand to shake it vigorously. I blinked. “Wotcher!” he called over his shoulder as he steamed through the door and away. “Bye...” I murmured, raising a hand to the door as it swung shut. The prisoner’s dilemma was all well and good as a theory, locked away in a textbook where it never really mattered. But when in real life you’ve got a situation that desperately requires collusion but the person in question won’t cooperate, it all gets a bit frustrating. You need to communicate in order to get the best possible outcome for the group, and if you could only tell them that, you know they’d be more than happy to do it... but if contact is impossible, and what’s worse, you’re competing against an established cartel that knows every trick in the book... you’re going to play right into their hands. You’re going to act in your own self-interest. You’re both going to confess. Tap tap tap tap tap tap tap... pause... tap tap tap tap. And if Pierce Lavelle didn’t pick up the phone... we were going to lose to The Unfuckables.
