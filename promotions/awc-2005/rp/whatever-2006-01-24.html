Whatever

24 January 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 24 January, 2006 a. unfulfillment is killing you Ellis is preoccupied by the thought of murder the entire day. It’s not a bothersome feeling; just a sense of predestination, or maybe even nothing more than a simple, plain sort of rage. She doesn’t function from step to step constantly wanting to kill someone: it's more that there’s some sort of filter over her vision that sharpens all the details into a rugged, dangerous clarity that makes every open space an escape route and every pair of hands a weapon and every tiny window something to shatter and every path a possibility. Choices, she thinks. It’s not something she’s had much of lately, at least not in the real sense, for a while. It’s hard to get away with murder, though, so instead Ellis makes a list of what she would have done different on her last day at PD so she wouldn’t be having homicidal thoughts in the first place: Things I Would Have Done Differently On My Last Day At PD 1) kill howie before he can fire me 2) or at least tell someone else to do it 3) tell laura to get a breast reduction 4) steal more pot from kennard’s desk so i have a stash to use when i do get fired 5) steal some posts-its, too 6) kill howie Markedly absent is any desire to kill Howie. What Howie doesn’t see, though, what Howie doesn’t know about Ellis is that she sees everything wrong with the world and it makes her angry, not resigned. There is a difference there. She is so painfully and intensely aware of all the injustices, so much so that Ellis wonders how she herself can stay furious for this long. It is exhausting but this is how Ellis lives, furiously and sharply. She is so very vivid and there that it seems like a crime. But words never make things easier. Ellis has found they complicate matters far more worse. Surefail and spitfire; dressed up in the gentle noise, words are there as an oblivion, a black hole of words, and lies, and all they are are ugly, bruised; they seem to cause more problems, and Ellis wishes she could transmit all she felt in one single glance. Maybe she’d try to be philosophical. Maybe not. At night (and the night is dangerous because it is a realization of how small you are in contrast to the world around you) Ellis’ phones buzzes. For the eighteenth time that half-hour, she presses ignore. For the third time that half-hour, she lights one up. When she sits on the balcony, there are always flies. Sometimes she kills them, had once squished one against the old brick with her thumb. She has thin fingers and quick eyes and empty worlds to fill. When she’d taken her hand away, all that was left was the small explosion of dark blood and organs smeared across the fading gray, the skinny lines of its legs in a mess of angles, thin film of wings. Ellis had looked at it and then wiped it off with a tissue, washed her hands. If she didn’t think about what it was, it wasn’t that disgusting. But there are no flies today. The cigarette carelessly dangles, red-orange a contrast against the black backdrop. Ellis nonchalantly watches as the smoke bellows and swallows her whole. Beneath her, the ground buzzes like an ultimatum. Her toe taps with the rhythm of New England rain, taps a melody that sounds like man, fuck, God, damnit, what the fuck am I supposed to do. What the fuck am I supposed to do? “Howie wants to know how you’re doing,” Chandler sits next to her later. Ellis doesn’t reply at first. Her nail polish is chipping. She scratches at it until bits of violet blotch the concrete like freckles. Everything in her head is spinning, dancing; fucked up, confused, lost, unsure, scared, enraged, ill, put off, self-conscious, hurt, pained, dead. Closure is bullshit. Closure is a lie. She answers, “Shit.” The wind is heavy. Goosebumps prickle her skin and now her sixth cigarette is dwindling so Ellis flicks it over the railing. They are three stories up so Ellis can see it prone on the sidewalk, the last glow of orange glittering against dirty/abused gray until some fat bitch steps on it. “Figured as much,” Chandler says. Ellis shrugs in response. The whores in her head are screaming: their bodies writing on the bed of her thoughts, naked flash rubbing her mind raw with holes that caused all of her insecurities to escape like fairies coming out for a night dance. A minute passes. It was deafening silence: the perversion of no sound, lack of anything – just the sound of the void rising up around, sucking things down into the depths of oblivion. Chandler coughs, wipes his mouth and says, “So, uh. I tried calling you.” Says/squeezes from the void like a cavity between her lips, (cavities in her teeth, cavities in a body, cavities in the ground like a succession of causes and effects, maybe, she wonders, holes in your mouth and holes in your body for blood to flow from and holes in the ground to bury the dead) “Yeah, like fifty times. I noticed,” and there are moments in time where the world seems to have an answer that rests just at her fingertips, the weight thereof that like a whisper: soft and delicate, just a rustle of wind that whistles though the leafy boughs of the tree of life, murmuring the eternal tale of the planet earth. Time comes to a still and everything begins a slow revolution through dreams and nothingness, breaking through the oblivion: establishing order to chaos, and all of the movements are led by the point of a finger, which is twirling against the air like a broken pinwheel, but the answer doesn’t come. “Why didn’t you answer?” “Didn’t want to,” and the answer is simple like shooting a gun, like walking into her office and collapsing into her chair and spilling coffee on Laura’s blouse and snapping at the assistants for fun, like pretending that the world hasn’t ended. “It’s not like the world’s ended, Ellis. You’re a big-time cop. There’s jobs out there with your name on them.” Ellis watches teenages make-out in the opposite alleyway. “That’s not it.” But Chandler keeps his eyes on her. Struggles have made living possible. It’s unsettling to think of their lives without struggles. “Talk to me.” Now the wind is slow and lazy. Ellis itches. There is something terribly and fundamentally alive about failure. “It’s not something I want to talk about.” And Chandler can’t blame her. It’s not something he’s good at because Ellis has never been righteous but he knows that she is more than just what he sees when he looks at her, tan skin and dusty hair, all angles now. She could kill him without thinking. He knows her, though; there’s a difference between what she is and what she could be. There will always be. He says, “There’s soup in the kitchen.” “Thanks,” and she could be impractical, too, but Ellis could be whatever she damned well pleased, and if someone wanted to disagree: could they please tell her why they sounded like blown speakers, their voices simple whispers carried on the wind; of no consequence, of no care inside her imperfect world of losing the most important things to her? “Yeah, well. I know you can’t cook for shit.” Ellis smiles at him. It’s something she still believes can be done and the funny thing about Ellis is that she stills honestly believes in some things. “Too busy saving the world,” she says and her words tilt like a lazy poem, rising and falling in pitch to Shel Silverstein, a poem about a hot air balloon and the little girl who rode it to the sun, burning up upon contact with the person her earth revolved around: she was her own constellation. The spoon settles against the edge of the bowl with a clean tap of a noise. There’s a terrible somber air settling in the room and it’s not something that Ellis can explain, not really. Maybe it’s the rush of tap water into the drain. Maybe it’s the strangeness of their lives. Maybe it’s the funny way that the world looks, dingy and battered and cheap. Ellis doesn’t know. She tries to drink the soup quietly and realizes that she’s never been good at dealing with only one other person in the room; she’d always had two, at least, and that had made things easy. Comfortable. Even when she hadn’t had two, she’d had their shadows, echoes and, now, she doesn’t even have that. It’s Chandler and it’s the only person that she shouldn’t feel awkward and loud around, but she does. He places a hand on the side of her neck and looks at her. It’s warm and Ellis circles her fingers around his wrist and, as gently as she knows how, removes it, watches his hand fall to rest on the edge of the chair. She breathes. She smokes pot. She wears earrings five times the size of her lobes. She speaks in mixtapes: masterpieces of sound and fury, signifying nothing, but telling everything. Everything that could be said had been by other people with far more eloquence, and a bit more intelligence. They eat breakfast late the next morning. The TV is on. After, Ellis leans against the kitchen counter, feet bare, and unpeels a banana. Chews loudly. She sits at the table and props her feet up on an empty chair, drinks tea from teabags and microwaved water because she can’t be bothered to care. A few minutes later and Ellis grabs an apple, one elbow propped up lazily on the counter and the other arm busy with a knife, cutting quickly through the flesh before she hisses, soft. Swears, not as softly. Chandler sees blood from her finger transfer onto the waxy skin of the apple. Red and green. There’s a memory there that won’t wash off. Ellis sucks on her index finger, brief, before she sets the knife down and turns on the tap. Her back is to him, head bent down, the soft fibers of her shirt settling across her shoulders in a comfortably close fashion. There’s a bit of a pause as a John Basedow commercial plays and the faucet runs. The plates are still drying on the dish rack. He makes a note to put them in the cabinets later. “Are you alright?” “Yeah. Just a small cut.” Chandler will get up to find a band-aid, anyway. They do things the normal way sometimes. It’s reassuring. He grabs the remote control and turns the TV set off so the world doesn’t pause; the war doesn’t end. The next morning, Ellis will get up and do everything all over again. Everything will still be the same, and Ellis will never be able to make herself change that. She’s brave in all the wrong ways but she lives in this peace and she has an aura, (they call her a bodhisattva, or little Buddha, Siddhartha, because she is so in tune with herself) and she has to laugh because she feels like she deserves a fucking Oscar for all of her performances. Life ain’t nothing but a game. She just wants a little more. She is all of the above. The vulnerability has to hide.
