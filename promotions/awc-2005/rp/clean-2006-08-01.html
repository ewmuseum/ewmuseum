OOC: because everyone else is putting them here and for the sake of consistency/makessenseville/lia's quickly dissipating sanity, the actions of this entire roleplay will take place after fresh!special: live on huntington beach regardless of the residuals. -- all the war horses wore rubber bands to hide their hooves from sinking sand the commander in charge was hoping that destiny was not to die 'one day i slowly floated away' -- eisley ** Thursday, high, Girl stands on the edge of her balcony with the world at her feet and sees Brad Pitt sitting cross-legged on floating poems. She reaches out to touch his face and sees him look, warily, at the raining men drifting from the clouds. They wear yellow hats, that much she is convinced of, and hum. This is all before a gust of wind comes and Brad diffuses into the artificial city lights like the stars in the sky. She shouts, where are you going? and he says, Away, just like in a melodrama of war and smallpox where people mean what they say. After this, she feels strangely defeated. Her elbows race to knock at the other and there is no more marrow to suck out of life except for the noise she threatens out of her iPod and the sobs she forces out of her lungs after watching seven hours of Golden Girls. Midnight, and the warm air, floating in from an open window, is an insiduous and calm destruction. It pries the floorboards apart, slips into cracks, whistles into ears like too-loud secrets unable to properly unfold. The curtains suck this way and that way, and the lightbulb in the kitchen finally goes out. Ellis burns her fingers momentarily trying to take it out. One in the morning, she’s been reduced to a shamble of numbers and vulgar words and barfing in the toilet. She clutches her head on the bathroom floor and recites fuck fuck fuck like a mantra to the Gods or like there’s a palm tree in her mouth and it’s swaying from one end to the other with moisture lacing its leaves. Occasionally, a palm fruit falls and rolls between her teeth. She crushes it to death and bends over with her head between her knees. Voicing how she feels would be too many run-on sentences for her own good. 3:00, four packets of pills to cushion her to sleep as though she’s Cleopatra and a casanova on a lazy summer day, swallowing grapes and being a lover and -- Asleep on the bathroom floor, Girl dreams that -- breathless -- her and Boy fall off the roof of the apartment building, wind cutting notches into their fingers so they bleed a violent shade of red. They’re the aberrant suicidals, for at some point she thinks she can stand on the flat of his belly and surf him on the next gust of air. They both know she will hit the ground first; he is too light to really fall at all. He abashes the forces of gravity and makes Newton flip cartwheels in his tomb. The wind rushes against their lips and stretches them into smiles. Someone pokes their head out of the window and shouts, but they don’t notice. He holds out his hand and she takes it, and he pulls her down into a mouth of the earth. -- she has lost the string that Hope gives, has dropped it on the floor and stepped on it and static brought it out of the front door, caught and dragged off from the spike of her heel. This is no story ending; Girl is no Casanova. ** Friday, 8:00AM. Night has died, but they hardly mourn her. Fresh days are always about sun and about red things and boiling skin and slicing eyes and burning smiles onto public faces. Ally does not like the way Ellis smells: mango over cocoa butter over papaya over pot over vomit. There is no consistency. Nevertheless, instincts shove at the seams. She wets a hand towel, folds it, places it on her sister’s forehead and whispers, “This is a new low for you.” Eye barely cracked open, Ellis sees a silhouette by the corner of her face. It’s talking and she’s replying, What?, and suddenly she wants to barf again. For a moment she feels lop-sided, bent toward the right or left or one side but she can’t remember which. This appears to be a key point, because if she can remember which side she should turn toward maybe she’ll notice that she likes the shade of green of the ceiling but it’s harsh against the blue of the toilet seat, colors screaming for her attention so that halfway through all she can really justify is the pounding in her head and heart. Instead, she scrambles to the toilet and barfs intenstines and Thursday’s Pom. It sort of smells like olives, the kind crushed between fingers and that linger, the kind once nearly lost between the smells of their grandmother’s thick treacle of incense she burnt to Gods they did not believe in. Ally winces. Her hands go to either of El’s cheeks and glide back, cupping hair along the way. Two minutes later, Ellis leans on her heels and swipes her mouth with the back of a hand. Haze tickles her eyes. She touches it with her tongue, chokes on the fragments, and goes to barf again. Ally grazes her back with a palm, soft and kind and uninvited, and when Ellis is done she swipes it away only because her skin burns beneath sweat like unchopped firewood under haze. “I’m only trying to help.” Ellis, rubbing away the stray tears and simultaneously trying not to feel like the most repellent and disgusting person in the world, says, “I don’t need it. Help.” Frowning, Ally waits and then, says, “Come here.” So she does. Goes, and crumbles in her sister’s arms and Ally puts a chaste hug around her shoulders, something simple and emotional like a bootleg of a movie only much faster, less than an hour and twenty minutes and not worth anyone else’s time but theirs. The problem with such a movie is that people leave before the viewers can solidly conclude anything. A good film is the buff man in the tattoo parlour trying to make his mark on you. Ellis closes her eyes, clenches her fists. The veins bring the blood back to her heart. Voicing how she feels would be too many excuses for her own good. -- Friday evening. She’s bored of poking holes into her own judgement and wishing that outsmarting herself was equivalent to outsmarting someone else. Right now it’s not even dark and already she feels like sleeping fast enough to race someplace in a dream, but it’s not the right time for anything but to talk and be a little stupid. The weather is fairly rainy, less heartbreaking than anywhere else. Ellis’ nails are long and square and she chips them against her knees while muttering, “I don’t even want to go.” From behind the wheel of Ellis’ Infiniti, Ally says, “Too bad.” The aches keep splitting her head in half, like cutting open a watermelon in one chop. Some children are scooping her out and spitting out the seeds as they eat. Some children are her. She can’t think; her brain is in her gut, pockets of memory saliva-coated in soil. They’ll grow to become trees that tower over the skeletons that fall out of her closet. She says, “Why are you doing this?” “Because not even I puked that much when I was pregnant,” responds Ally, making a sharp left, “and I was on coke.” Ellis looks up, pauses, watches a streetlight flick from yellow to red. “Something’s wrong with me?” “I don’t know,” Ally answers, honest, dangerously so. The car humps its way up a hill. Time trickles like sand and for every second that passes, Ellis falls. It’s because the gravity of the situation is acting against her. Six minutes away from the hospital, Ellis puts a palm on her stomach and watches it grow, waiting for some irritation to remind her that it’s time to be responsible for herself. There is no excuse to kill it now. It would stain like guilt, if only because she would not be able to regard it as something dead in the earth. The irritation doesn’t come. There is only a swell of pride. -- In the waiting room, Ellis sits in a strange sort of suspension, mouth slightly agape with her head resting on the crook of a bent knee, hair mussed with boredom. Ally asks if she’s okay. Ellis does not look at her. Instead, her eyes fall somewhere between the sky and space and she says something along the lines of No. It takes forty-two minutes. A shaft of light throws itself against Ellis’ eyes when the door is pushed open. She squints when the nurse says, “Eleanor Nash?” Ellis waves a hand, but doesn’t smile. A bad film is one that leaves you wondering why you were stupid enough to have cried when he tried. -- She sits fidgeting, frightened at the intellectual concept of doctor talk. A talking at, a talking to, a monologue in stagelight posed as a conversational orgy. She’s not really listening to Dr. Shurste as much as she’s watching his bowl hair and large spectacles. The spectacles look make him look like he’s wearing bowls on his eyes; transparent bowls viewed from below, and his eyes as if they are the food she is supposed to eat. She gives him another three minutes after six before interrupting. Ellis says, “So?” Half an hour later, Ellis will walk out of the hospital with her held high and her face streaked grossly with red. It will be one of the terribly rare times that she’ll cried because she’s sad and happy all at once, so much so that there will be barely enough time for Adam to rush through her head. ** The rain outside washes away history and white fences built to border traditions lost in modern age and what she’s forgotten and she hasn’t. The sidewalks are not edged with grass, instead edged with the reflection of a blue sky wallpapering a world hung low on the horizon, crimson streaks coloring the line between Ellis and eternity. In the passenger seat of her own car, it's easy to face forward and let her eyelashes color her cheeks grey, background set in softness and pastel lights filtering through levels of complexity, color, life. She finds herself imagining more than thinking, creation resounding off air stacked densely in days that are too long and days that are too short. It is inevitable as much as impossible and eight minutes into the drive back home is when Ellis realizes that words spun off obligation are worth more than ones spun in earnest, that optimism has lines drawn in thin yellows and whites and blues stolen from silver mirrors, that life, with its graceful poise and clumsy steps, is wrong in all the right ways. ** The thunder god plays with his nausea early Saturday morning, his roar bursting padi stalks from beyond history. In the apartment, on the phone with Adam, Ellis dodges his initial inquiries and goes about telling him what Dr. Shurste said like this: I think our relationship is shaped like a Rubik's cube -- made to be perfect, but so difficult to work out. I almost want to leave it how it came (perfect, you know; rainbow organized plastic wrapping), except then the purpose would be defeated and I know you're worth it anyway. I remember playing with one once; I was on vacation in Dad’s lake house and I found it in the grass by the stones -- that I used for skipping, by the way, because there's just something about not sinking -- edging the water. It wasn't particularly interesting, and I only got to one side when I quit have I told you how much I hate the color orange? I used to think the sum of one and one was two, but we add up to something more, me and you. I never quite mastered the art of skipping stones, but for you, for our Rubik's cube, well, I'm willing to paint all the sides orange. Then she tells him that their baby might be gone already, and the dial tone couldn’t have stung her ear drum any quicker. Into the reciever, she recites fuck fuck fuck like a mantra to the Gods. -- There is something about film. There is something about sitting in a dark theater where suddenly everybody becomes a stranger, a certain number of slides per minute moving through unreal time. For a moment all that matters is this thread that ties one life to another, another life to yours. Take your pick. You can be any number of them, but for a hundred or so minutes, it is absolutely required that you, in all earnest, are devoted to them. Film is like a temporary religion. For the first time in your life you're smoking crack, or someone has ripped you opened and told you they love you. You're reading a book, or losing your memory. Someone comes up to you and tries to hold your hand, and then puts their hands around your neck to keep their palms warm. A good film is the buff man in the tattoo parlor trying to make his mark on you. A bad film is one that leaves you wondering why you were stupid enough to have cried when he tried. A bad film deceives you and leaves you disgruntled in retrospect. A good film deceives you and you're happy to be the victim of the latest bilk or fraud or break-up. This is no good film ending; she is not waiting for Prince Charming, an entity pressed soft onto her skin by a mother's voice, clasped, prim, folded, and smoothed. This is no good film ending; Girl is no Casanova and Boy is no King and Baby is no Saving Grace. A bad film leaves you with no definable ending.
