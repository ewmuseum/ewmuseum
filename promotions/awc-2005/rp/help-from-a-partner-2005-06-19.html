Help from a Partner

19 June 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 19-6-05 Help from a Partner written by Brendan S. For AWC’s Fresh! Tuesday, June 21, 2005 Matches: Relentless Title Match and Alliance Championship Round II Prologue – Help from a Partner -- To Know Someone Cares When I was fifteen years old, the 'crazy old lady next door' died. Sadly, not many people seemed to care. However, even at fifteen, I knew that I would forever miss her. Not that I even knew her name-- she was always the 'crazy old lady next door' to me, but everyday on my way home from school she would ask me how my day was. Knowing the consequential response, I usually mumbled fine continuing down the sidewalk as quickly as possible to avoid answering the inevitable. 'Why?' 'I don't know.' I would say. Her response: 'There you have it, another wasted day.' I got out of bed one Saturday and went down stairs. Mom cooked me the regular Irish sweet sausage and some fried potatoes. Mornings at our house usually had Mom cooking at the stove and Dad at the head of the table reading the morning’s paper before turning to the latest wrestling magazine. Today Dad was still in the paper when I got downstairs. He turned the page and picked up his glass of fresh squeezed orange juice. “Oh me lord.” Dad said as he put the paper down. “What is it, honey?” Mom said putting her hand on his shoulder. Mom and Dad were always the best couple. I felt they could get through anything together. It wasn’t until later that even without Dad, Mom could carry herself well knowing that her strength still came from him from beyond. “Our neighbor died.” The world froze, my heart sank, and fingers went numb quicker than a lightning strike. My jaw dropped. I didn’t hear what my mother said; it was slow, deep, and imperceptible. “Yeah, yesterday morning. Heart attack.” Yesterday? I didn’t even notice that she wasn’t there after school the past two days to ask me how my day was. What about three days ago, did I mumble the usual response and disappoint her again? I was shocked that I was thinking about her—thinking about her like she was family. “Poor old lady, must have been so lonely.” was all my mother could say. “She was crazy. She didn’t know any better.” Dad said. “Don’t say that.” I said in a half-mutter half-shout. My mother and father froze and looked at me across the table. I shoved my plate down the table a ways and stood up. I looked back at both my mother and father who stared at me blankly. I turned and ran up the stairs to my room. I jumped on my bed and began punching my pillow. Mom quickly came into my bedroom and sat on the bed. She put a hand on my back and began rubbing. I stopped hitting the pillow. It was probably then that I found out that Mom had enough strength to give me. It was later when she died, that I realized that, the strength was really mine and I didn’t really have that much. “Honey, the pillow didn’t do anything.” She said in a sweet “mommy” voice. It’s strange that mothers are always there when you need them as a kid. Mommy can fix everything. She can kiss scraped knees, mend broken hearts, and buy you a new G.I. Joe when the old one breaks. Mothers are everything to their sons. “I know, it’s just… Dad called her crazy. She’s not crazy. I mean okay maybe she is crazy, but we have no reason to call her that. We might very well be just as crazy, but that woman was nice to me. Talked to me every day and invited me into her home. Yeah she might have been a little mean sometimes, but we have no right to call her that.” I said, throwing my pillow across the room. My mom sighed; she knew this was going to be a tough one. She put her arm around my shoulder. “Your Dad was just… he was just…” “Your Dad was just wrong.” My father said standing in the doorway. “I didn’t mean it son. It was just something that slipped from the tongue. Had I known it was going to hurt you I wouldn’t have said it. Your mother and I had no idea you and Ms. Jenkins were so close.” Dad sat on the edge of the bed. He wasn’t the touchy-feely type. He didn’t need to be. He had his way with words. “We weren’t really. I don’t know.” Both my parents looked at me somewhat befuddled by my actions as of late. “Do you want to talk about it, honey?” Mom said, making me feel a little more comfortable. “There’s not really much to talk about. I mean I didn’t know her that well. It’s not even until Jamie told me to be a little nicer to her that I got to know her. Even then I didn’t even know until a couple of minutes ago her name was Ms. Jenkins. She talked to me everyday, usually the same things, but I don’t know. I don’t know.” I said, really not knowing. I didn’t know what these feelings I had for Ms. Jenkins were. I didn’t even know where these feelings were coming from. It was all very new to me. I wasn’t used to having connections with people I didn’t really know. “It’s alright, honey. Sometimes it’s really hard to explain or describe our feelings. Mostly because sometimes we aren’t suppose to. It’s like they say back home, ‘May the road rise up to meet you.’ We aren’t suppose to know what’s a head of us, that’s what makes life so special.” “Son, I know that sometimes it’s hard to deal with the things that happen to you, but you have to rely on the good things that have happened to you to make times like this a lot easier. It’s all a part of growing up. It hurts sometimes, but in the end, I’m sure you’ll be grateful of the things that happened to you that allowed you to grow into a man. But don’t forget that even men make mistakes and even men have their hardships, and it’s okay to admit it.” Dad said genuinely—this being one of the only times I remember Dad’s voice sounding soft and comforting instead of like a stern solid. We used to call Dad the rock when my sister and I were little. We never saw Dad cry or even shift. Since dad was a devout Irishman he enjoyed the reference to home. “Dad,” I spoke, unsure if the words would come. “Will you take me to her funeral?” Dad took in a deep breath leaving an awkward silence. Dad looked over to my mother and said, “Yeah.” He nodded his head up and down a couple of times, letting the silence roll over the room. “Yes son I will take you. We will go together.” “Thanks,” was about all I could mutter. The next day I got into my Easter suit and got ready for the funeral. I had been to a couple funerals before when I was little, but I didn’t remember exactly what they were like. I was a little nervous, but Mom assured me that I would be all right. She gave me a couple extra kisses on the cheek that Sunday morning. I guess she thought I needed a little extra encouragement. She did not ask to go. She knew that I would tell her that this was something Dad and I needed to do. She wanted to be there to hold me in her arms when I didn’t know how to react. She had spoken to my father for a long time that night before they fell asleep. I had heard them in my room. She told him what to do if I cried or if I tried to run out. He told my mom that he could handle it. He planned on keeping a firm hand on both my shoulders and this was a relief. I always felt stronger in the presence of my father. I knew that if the rock was strong, so could I be. When we got to the funeral home, I began to feel a little week in the knees. It became hard to walk, like my feet were concrete and the ground already trodden by concrete footed men. I was reminded of the doctor’s office by the way the funeral home smelled. I thought it ironic that considering doctors are suppose to make you think of life that such a place would smell the same way. Dad led me over to the line for the viewing of the body. This tradition never really made any sense to me. I always thought the casket should be closed and a bunch pictures of the person be shown as you remembered them. No one ever looks the same lying there in a casket. The line was shorter than most of the lines I remembered from going to funerals when I was younger. I guess not too many people came to see Ms. Jenkins. When we got to the casket, Dad had to push me up a little closer to the casket. He pushed me down to one knee and he himself got to a knee. He took one of his hands off my shoulder and stayed there kneeling with an arm around me. He genuflected and began to sing a prayer. “May the road rise up to meet you. May the wind be always with you. May the sunshine warm you always, ‘til we meet again. May the rain fall softly on you. May the hand of God uphold you.” My Dad was not the greatest singer, but I began to cry. Dad stood up and brought me up with him. “It’s alright, son. It’s alright to cry.” Dad turned me around and led me down the aisle of chairs set up for the actual funeral. I was beginning to turn when we reached the back when I felt Dad turn me back around to a woman sitting in the last row. “We’re really sorry for your loss.” I didn’t know who this woman was, and I was pretty sure my Dad didn’t either. I didn’t know why he was talking to her. All I knew was that I wanted to go home. “Thank you. How did you know my mother?” my Dad gave me a pat on the back. This was my cue to speak. “She was our neighbor. She and I used to have little talks everyday when I was walking home after school. She used to ask me how my day went and I would tell her. She’d ask me if I’d learned anything from my experiences. It was really nice knowing that everyday I came home there was someone there to ask me about my day.” “So you really enjoyed my mother’s bickering?” she said, quite frankly, which took me by surprise. “I used to think of it as a pain, but I eventually realized that it was nice having someone who cared.” I said. This was the first time I became aware of the actual connection I had with Ms. Jenkins. “You know, most people think my mother was a nutcase. Most the people here probably thought it. Hell even I thought it sometimes too. I’m glad someone other than me began to realize that my mother was not such an awful person.” She said, seeing that I pass the test. “I didn’t see her that way. She had a different way of expressing herself, but she was a loving person none the less.” “Thank you very much.” Ms. Jenkins’s daughter said with a smile. “I’m glad you came and I’m sure my mother is too.” Dad steered me through the rest of the funeral home and outside. As we got to the car Dad let go of me and said, “I’m really proud of you today son. Maybe you don’t understand it today, but you did a lot for Ms. Jenkins’s daughter back there. I guess, we all need a little reminder of the way people should be remembered.” Flashback (Jessica) – Help from a Partner – To Return Home I was seventeen when I got my first apartment. Anyone else my age would have thought it the best thing in the world, but I was dragging my heels every minute. At seventeen, your own apartment could grant you the best parties, freedom from your parents, and god know what else your average everyday teenage would get into. However, I wasn’t so lucky. My apartment was granting me shelter and separation. The things I needed, not the things I wanted. The apartment wasn’t the nicest. Actually, it was a dump. Fully furnished meant a bed, a couch, a furnace that also served as your heater in the winter, and a TV that didn’t work. It was a roof over my head that’s all I asked. I would say that it was a ground under my feet, but the literal undertone of that cliché made me want to vomit. It had been a year since my mother died and maybe a month since my sister’s death. My sister took over after Mom died. I spent the next four weeks being juggled around by relatives, the state, and whoever else felt sorry for me. Then I got my hands on my inheritance and jumped in my car and drove as far away as I could from the beach on what was left in the tank of gas. I still wonder why I didn’t fill up and go another couple hundred miles, but I guess going to far way from the bad memories only makes it worse. I guess you could say I was running away. I didn’t see it that way. I was saving myself. Saving myself from bad memories, bad karma, bad everything. I was saving myself from death. I quit school. “It’s not THAT big of a deal.” I used to say. It used to piss people off when they would say, “But you only had a short time left, why didn’t you finish.” Well the truth was, I didn’t have a short time left. I was pulled from school at sixteen to go to some stupid ass big city where apparently “the best doctors in the world for this kind of thing” were. What a load of shit that was. I had more than two years to do before graduation. I didn’t want to be the twenty-year-old freak at graduation. The apartment had its good things going for it. I had to clean a couple rooms of the apartment complex’s staff and the public areas like the lobby and what not, but it wasn’t hard. Free room and board was all a little run away orphan could ask for I guess. Especially considering my family was either literally dead or dead to me any ways. I got to know most of the staff there pretty well. One of my favorites was a character named Sam. Sam worked security, but he was sort of a little off. Many people in the complex didn’t like how Sam ran things—his way. However, the boss would always tell dissatisfied residents “Sam has been the best thing to happen to this complex. He’s kept the trash out that our janitors didn’t get.” Sam was not your ordinary security guard. Sam had an inoperative arm. It was quite obvious by looking at him. His left arm was significantly smaller and out of proportion to the rest of his body. It wasn’t really like Sam tried to cover it up. Sam would often wedge a cigarette or lighter into his immobile fingers while trying to smoke. Sam wore the same thing every day. A blue shirt in the morning and a yellow shirt with the word “staff” written on the back at night. Sam carried a belt with a flashlight, pocketknife, and small handgun on him when he was on duty, which ran from 7 p.m. to 7 a.m. but Sam normally considered himself ruler of the premises the other twelve hours of the day also. Sam painted a bad picture of himself to most people. However, to those he got along with, Sam faired quite well. Once you got to know Sam, you were quick to find this rough looking gray haired man had a heart made out of butter. Getting to know Sam on my sleepless nights when I first moved in are probably one of the helpful times of my life. I remember the most life altering night the best… “What’s got you down, beautiful?” Sam said. “Oh nothing’s wrong, Sam.” I replied. “You can lie to yourself, but I see right through that.” He said somewhat challengingly with a sense of sincerity. “No, really, Sam, I’m fine.” “Bull shit!” He said firmly. “Your average seventeen year old girl doesn’t stay up until five in the morning on her porch when she’s got to start work at eight.” “I’m not tired.” “Right. And I am Oprah fucking Winfrey.” Sam said rolling his eyes. “You’ve been sitting here staring into the pool all night. I have a feeling you aren’t considering skinny dipping either. Although I wouldn’t mind watching a pretty young thing like you glisten naked in the moon light.” “Sam, you are such a pervert.” I jested, jokingly. “No, just a man.” “Well, doesn’t that man have some security type work to do rather than bother poor little me?” “What? You don’t think I have everything under control?” I just shrug. “Oh, let me quote another famous guy. ‘Oh, Ye of little faith.’ It’s a quiet night and I’m done in two hours anyhow. So give me something to do. Tell me what’s wrong?” “Can’t a girl just have a sleepless night? You stay up all night with no problems. I bet you get your sleep during the day.” “I wish.” He sighed. “I’ve never told you this, mostly because I don’t want your sympathy. I may put on an act of confidence out here when I’m on duty. But when I get through those doors, I let myself go. I have cancer and I’m terminal, everyday is a struggle for me. Five years ago they gave me three months to live. A year later cancer free I went to go see my doctor to rub it in his face that he was wrong. When I got there, I asked if my old doctor still worked there. The nurse told me he died, of cancer. I’ve lived past my expiration date five times. I don’t give up, but at the same time, I don’t care either.” “I’m so sorry. I had no idea.” I said, truly shocked. “But, really there’s nothing wrong.” “I know what you are thinking. I am going to say, ‘what does this little girl have to worry about.’ Well I’m not going to say that.” “It’s just I have some things that I need to deal with Sam.” I said staring back into the pool. “What? And I don’t? You really think I am an angel, kid. I have done a lot of thing that it takes me every part of my body that isn’t already in pain to deal with. I stole, cheated, and everything else when I was running away.” “You had to. Its your duty to do everything you can just to stay alive.” I said, noticing I was starting to say a little more of what was on my mind that I was able to admit. Sam began to notice. He looked at me, trying to take in what I had just said, shrugging it off he continued. “I took a little more than I needed.” “You were only twelve.” “Okay. So I’m not bad enough of a guy, yet. About twenty years ago, when I lived in Philadelphia there was this serial rapist going around raping women and then straggling them to death. I walked in when this guy was raping my wife. I got to the drawer of my dresser pulled out my gun and shot the guy six times.” “You were only protecting your wife.” “I don’t see it that way. Of course, that’s what the courts believed too. I should be in jail right now.” “I don’t believe that. You were justified.” “There are a lot of other things I’ve done wrong in my life too.” “I don’t get it. You blame yourself for everything? Then how do you go on with your life?” I said, realizing that I was answering one of the questions I had in my head while I was staring at the pool. “I don’t. I pray everyday that God will give me five minutes with my wife and kids in heaven before He sends me to hell.” Sam said grimly, starting to stare into the pool himself. “I can’t believe that, Sam. I have to believe that if there is a God up there, that He must be an all-forgiving God. Just that you are sitting here right now regretting your decisions and trying your best to make yourself a decent person now is enough to be with your wife and kids for all eternity when you die.” I said almost ready to stand up and give a full sermon. “I really appreciate that kid. I really do. You know what? I know that I am an old fart who you think doesn’t know a damn thing, but-” “I don’t believe that Sam.” “Okay. Let it go.” Sam stopped and there was silence. “Whatever you are fretting over. Whatever he did or whatever he is doing. Let it go. It’s not worth it. Take it from me, if you sit here every night and bitch and complain to yourself nothing is ever going to change. You are going to feel a lot better about yourself if you try to make whatever is wrong better and fail than you are if you let yourself never see him again.” Shocked, I sat there and took in a couple of deep breaths. I knew Sam was right, but I was still to young and stupid to admit it. “You get in there and get an hour’s sleep before you start cleaning my room.” Sam turned to go away. “Sam… How did you know it was a guy that was bothering me?” Sam turned around and smiled at me. “There might have been some other things to get you pissed off at him, but you women thing everything is a guy’s fault and not surprisingly, the guy thinks it’s his fault too. Goodnight, Jessica.” “Goodnight, Sam.” I said. “I really want you to know, that what you said tonight, that I can be forgiven and that it’s not really all my fault. I really appreciate it. I’m going to remember that for the rest of my life, however long that may be.” “I want to thank you too. I think you’ve changed my life too.” Sam did change my life. The next day I called in sick and went to see Crimson for the first time since I found him in his apartment ready to die. Sam died three months later. A man trying to burglarize an apartment had shot him two weeks after our conversation. That didn’t kill him; the cancer finally took his life. I had a conversation with the owner of the apartment complex; he said that he could have fought the cancer. However she said that for the first time in the three years she had known Sam that Sam seemed to think that dying was not the worst thing in the world. He had even told her that he was starting to look forward to spending the rest of his life with his wife. I thought after Sam’s death that I would be mourning for a long time. For the first time in my life, Sam’s death was something I could deal with. The rest of the deaths I had faced had been something I could not cope with, but with Sam he taught me that I should live my life and try to motivate the rest to live theirs. I guess Crimson is my testimony of inspiration. Present day -- Help from a Partner – From Beyond the Grave Crimson walks through the graveyard. It is around dusk, it is light enough to see where you are going, but the sky darkening as the sun bows out for the evening. Rain clouds roll overhead bragging of the downpour it laid earlier in the day. The clouds fly slowly threatening to open the floodgates if anyone would dare to snicker up at them. Rain clouds taunt like that, they know that within their power is the ability to ruin birthdays, weddings, sports events, or anything they disagree with. They are conceited that way. He is wearing his Sunday’s best and carrying a small brief case. He walks with purpose not spending time looking at any of the other headstones surrounding him. He has a blank expression—no thought crossing his mind as he walks. He comes to his destination and his feet stop suddenly in the rain sullen dirt. His feet begin to sink in as if becoming one of the statues or part of the scenery. His chin stays parallel and he does not look down at the head stone he has stopped in front of. Crimson loosens his tie allowing himself to breathe a little deeper. Crimson takes off the bowler hat on his head and clenches it to his chest. He sighs while removing the shield from his heart and letting his arm and hat drop to his side. Crimson drops the hat on the ground. He takes off his jacket and spreads it out on the ground. Crimson takes a seat on the jacket. He sits with his knees to his chest, arms wrapped around his shins, and his toes pointing up in the air. “He Jamie.” He says softly, a little rasp in his voice. “It’s been awhile since I’ve been here. Okay, probably not since the funeral, but you have to understand. They took you too soon. I hope you are not disappointed in me and my actions after your death.” Crimson bites his lip and looks up to the sky. “It was all just too much to handle anymore. After Dad, Mom, Your Mom, and your Dad, and everyone else… Then you…” Crimson bites his lip again and takes in a deep breath through his nose. “I’m alright now. Atleast I think so. Look at me now, I can come here, speak out loud. I’m out of those white rooms with the rubber walls. I’m doing well. I still live at Rolling Creek. It feels like home now and I don’t want to leave. I don’t really think I need to stay there. Jason, he’s my doctor and my friend; he reminds me everyday that I can leave at any time. I don’t need to come back. I think I’m doing alright.” Crimson lets his legs stretch out. His muscles become less tense and he begins to relax. “Your sister and I are getting along quite well. Actually she’s the one who came to save me. She really helped me get over my troubles. She and I have grown really close. She comes to visit me almost every day. She brings me notebooks to write my poetry in. She wants me to move in with her and move out of Rolling Creek, but I’m no so sure that’s a great idea. I don’t know; sometimes I think she has feelings for me. Sometimes, I think I have feelings for her, but I imagine its just me seeing her in you.” Crimson nods his head up and down as if confirming this to himself. “She’s grown into a beautiful young lady though. She looks just like you. She’s a good person, too. She deeply reminds me of you. She helps me confront the past instead of bury myself in it. She is healthy and going back to school. She has been proven free of the disease for the past five years. I think she may be the one who stops the curse.” Crimson lets out a forced breathy chuckle. “I’ve gotten back into wrestling. I’m actually on a professional circuit now. It’s one of the things I’m doing to confront my past. It’s helping me come to terms with the way Dad died. I’m doing pretty well. I am a champion already. I got a title shot a month into my contract and one the “Relentless Championship.” I am really proud, but I’m not letting it get to me head. I know that Dad would be proud too.” Crimson reaches into his briefcase and pulls out the Relentless Title. “This is what I won. Shiny thing isn’t it?” Crimson actually laughs as he puts the belt back into the briefcase. “I have to defend that title on Tuesday. I have a weapons match against this hot shot who likes to run off at the mouth. I can handle him though. He’s nothing but a bunch of hot air. He doesn’t even respect the wrestling club and it’s staff. He’s a despicable sort, gets on my nerves. I’ll enjoy using him to boost my career -- not that my career is in bad shape. I already am in a competition for two other championship belts.” Crimson reaches into his briefcase again and pulls out a photograph of Paddy O’Shea. “I made a new friend. This is Paddy O’Shea.” Crimson turns the picture to the headstone and then smirks at his own actions. “He’s an interesting character. He reminds me of the people we used to meet back in Ireland. He’s a pretty heavy drinker, but what native Irishmen isn’t? I like him, he’s funny. There’s something to him, but I can’t exactly put my finger on it. He and I formed sort of a team, like a duo of sorts. We are probably going to win another championship belt for each of us.” Crimson puts the photo back into the briefcase. He looks into the briefcase and puts his hand on something inside. With his other hand he wipes his brow. “I want you to know that I still love you and I still think about you everyday. I wrote you this poem.” Crimson pulls out a piece of paper from his briefcase and lays it on the tombstone. “I better get going. I have to make the long drive to Winchester tonight.” Crimson gets up and beings collecting his things. He takes his foot and messes up the grass where the grass is matted down from his body weight. He puts his jacket over his arm, his hat back on his head, and carries his suitcase. He begins walking back the way he came. The rain begins to pour washing away the letters on the paper. Washing them into the ground only to be returned to the skies and read by the angels.
