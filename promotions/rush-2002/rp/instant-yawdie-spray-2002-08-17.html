Instant Yawdie Spray

17 August 2002
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 17th August 2002 Somewhere, presumably in the rolling hills of America, sat a small and lonely child. He crossed his hands over his lap as he rocked back and forth in his Grandfather’s chair. His blonde mop of hair glistened in the sun, but his face was sullen and his eyes wept with bitter tears. The small room, which was adorned with knickknacks and photos of relatives from the old country, was the eventual tomb for the old man who wheezed heavily on his bed. He raised his frail hand and beckoned the small boy to move in closer. Young Timothy nodded gently as he pulled himself from the wooden rocking chair and tip toed over, sitting at the very edge on the mattress. He held the pale and bony hand of his Grandfather tightly in his grip, as if trying desperately to hold him in this world. The old man coughed from the back of his throat, his lungs making a fragile croaking noise. His wrinkled baldhead moved slightly as he prepared to speak. “Timothy… Timothy, is that… you?” He asked through a set of three yellow teeth. “Yes Grandfather, it is I.” Replied the young lad. “Timothy… I want you to call… this… number. Tell the man on the other line to come here straight away, he is the only person who can save me now.” Whispered the old man. “Is he a specialist doctor, or a voodoo master of some sort?” Queried young Timothy. “None of those, just call him and you will learn the truth.” “I will do Grandfather, if he is as good as you believe then surely he is the only man to save you.” Timothy ran from his Grandfather’s side and left the small shack they lived in. He ran as quickly as his legs would take him, skipping every so often at the happy thought of good health. His white socks and black buckle shoes clapping against the cobbled pathway as he journeyed to the nearest public telephone. * It had been days since Timothy’s telephone conversation with the man from the strange land. He had dreamt every night since then of steel drums, gunga peas and curried goat. Not knowing where such obscure sights could be seen, nor where or when they had entered his mind. Putting it from his thoughts each sunrise as he dabbed his Grandfather’s head with a damp cloth and told him of how he would find peace. The day when the knock arrived, that ever so loud rapping against the wooden door, Timothy leapt from his seat. Unable to stop his body from rushing over and opening the shack to the outside world, and salvation. The man, who stood before young Timothy, had dark skin and a smile on his face. Timothy looked up in awe at the man’s long hair, which resembled a lion’s mane atop the grinning man’s head. He wore odd clothing, printed with images of lions and a man similar in appearance but with a microphone held closely to his mouth. Timothy gulped, his nerve lacking from his voice. “He… hello, sir.!” Squeaked the young boy. “Wa gwaan? Ya mus’ beh Timmy.” Replied the man, standing in the doorway. “That is I, you are the man who my Grandfather seeks as his saviour?” “Yeah mon! Mah name Joe Bananas, Ah a come ta fix up ya grandpops.” Said Joe, patting the small child’s head. The old man lifted his head, weakly smiling at the man he now could see. He clawed the coverings and sheets from him and he edged closer to the side, his pale blue pyjamas in full view. “J… J… Joe?” Asked the old man, wincing as he tried to further his focus. “Yo mon, Ah here ta fix ya up an den we gan have ourselves a bashment up in here!” Wailed Joe, his arms flaling wildly above his head. “Please… I can feel, I have little time left.” Spluttered the old man, leaning back into his pillow. “Ok mon, ya nee’ ta do e’ert’ing dat Ah say.” Joe said, a more serious look on his face. Joe waved his his hands above the resting body of the elderly man and began to chant in a low drone. “We Jammin’… Jammin’… an’ we hope ya like Jammin’ too.” Chanted Joe, young Timothy in amazement at the strange incantation. Joe came to a stop and revealed a can of some kind of can with a nozzle on top from inside his shirt. He shook it in his strong hand violently before pushing down on the nozzle and releasing a cloud of purple smoke, covering as much of the old man as possible. He then dropped the spray can and sat down, cross-legged, on the floor. He clenched both his fists and pushed them together, the bent fingers meeting with each other and whitening the knuckles with pressure. Joe closed his eyes and continued to chant. “I shot the sheriff, but I didn't shoot no deputy, oh no! Oh! I shot the sheriff, but I didn't shoot no deputy, ooh, ooh, oo-ooh.” The old man, covered in the mist of purple, grinned madly as he sprang up in his bed and waved his arms around. He slid off the side of his bed and helped Joe to his feet. He performed some strange handshake with him, locking hands together and twisting fingers around until seemingly satisfied. Joe leaned down to the floor and lifted up a folded shirt, which he helped the old man to put on over his pyjamas. It was a plain white t-shirt, which was much shorter in length than the blue garments underneath, and had ‘One Love’ printed in red letters. “Wa gwaan Joe?” Asked the old man, his voice overtaken by the same accent as the man he was addressing. “E’er’ting’s alright mon, ya wan a go back ta yawd wi ya Tim fa nah?” Asked Joe, a grin of success across his face. The old man nodded eagerly, turning to young Timothy. “We gwaan a Jamaican Tim!” Exclaimed the old mon. “I… don’t understand.” Said Timothy, confused. “Ya will do, ya will do.” Said Joe, leading the trio out of the shack. Left behind was the half empty can, rolling along the floor, the front of which had an image of a man with long dreadlocks and a beard, his fist raised in his red, gold and green string vest. Above his head were printed black letters which read ‘Yawdie Spray, become a yawdie wi jus dis spray.’
