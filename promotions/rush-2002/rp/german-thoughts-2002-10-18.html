German Thoughts

18 October 2002
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 18th October 2002 German Thoughts NB: The original format of this piece was written in German. It has been checked and translated into English, for the benefit of a predominantly English-speaking audience. Ever heard that track that The Police performed all those years ago? “An Englishman in New York”? Well, it was either The Police or Sting…one of the two. Well, for many years I was “A German in Manchester” It became my second home and despite the fact that I was an “outsider”, the Salford folk made my life that little bit easier, before the jump over the Atlantic a few months back. Now, I am “A German in New York”. I haven’t exactly heard the track in detail, nor have I really followed the lyrics but I’m suspecting that Sting was talking about himself, living in a foreign country and adapting his life to a different lifestyle and atmosphere. When I left Hamburg at age 20, I never thought that I would be a professional wrestler, earning my trade in the USA and supporting a loving wife and children with a decent wage. But it was tough in England. For those of you that aren’t aware, there’s no love lost between the English and the Germans. Stemming back from the world wars, through the infamous 1966 World Cup and today. Whereas the hate between the two was political in the past it’s now all about one thing that most men believe is the most important thing in their life…football (or soccer to my American/Canadian readers). Of course, the hate is only during a time period of 90mins. But nonetheless, the general feeling on the other was negative. Being an Englishman in Hamburg didn’t seem to be a problem, as there are thousands and thousands of British military personnel and their families stationed in various parts of Northern Germany. Hearing the tone of a cockney (Londoner), a Geordie (Newcastle native) or a Scouser (Liverpudlian) was second nature to us…because they were everywhere. However, noticing a slight pronunciation difference of words with the letter ‘w’ in Northwest England wasn’t so common. I was singled out, when living in the city that gave George Best his legendary status. From the minute I opened my mouth when looking for accommodation, I was destined to be on the receiving end of some…lets say….stick. Of course, the majority was all in good taste and a day wouldn’t go by when a mention of Adolf Hitler or the Nazi’s or the 66 World Cup or the recent 5-1 hammering in Munich would ring in my earholes. The fact that I am a big football fan, made it even worse. At first, I took the jokes and whatnot because I had to. I had no choice. I wanted to live in England, therefore I had to suffer the consequences of being a German. But eventually, the jokes turned nasty and there were times when I didn’t attend the daily routine at the nearby wrestling school due to threats made. “We don’t need you krauts here, German scum…fuck off!”. If I were to ignore these, then I would be on the receiving end of not only mental abuse…but physical too. It wasn’t just a certain group of idiots at the school; it would be my neighbours too…spray painting “Get Out, Kraut!” on my front door, which took hours upon hours to clean. Kicking my car to pieces with steel toe caps on and the occasional brick through the window. By the way, “kraut” is a nickname for the Germans used by the English. Some look at it in a derogatory manner, hence the constant use. The English translation is “cabbage” Not much of a taunt I know, but some Germans take great offence…and for a number of years, only based on what I was brought up on, so did I. I took my rage and frustration out on sparring partners at the academy, by executing certain painful moves which target the bones and limbs of the body. As I slowly moved up into the world of Independent wrestling, I was more exposed to the taunting by the rest of the locker room and because I was touring with a different company virtually every month, I endured more and more. Looking back, I don’t think I would have made it through all the torment without Stephanie, my wife. We met in Manchester and she’s a local girl. To this day, I don’t know if she felt sorry for me or whether she was generally attracted to me. My senses tell me that it was a mixture of the two. I’ll probably never ask her, plus it was a good years ago and I don’t believe it’s worth delving into the past and digging up certain points. She was supportive, caring and gave me a shoulder to cry on when I really needed it. It made sense that we would spend the rest of our lives together. She also had her problems, what with a racist father and an ignorant mother giving her constant hassle. So I tend to think that we were there for each other and for the majority, we only had each other and nobody else. Our relationship is now stronger than ever and with two children being an important part of our lives; it’s safe to say that neither Stephanie nor I will be going anywhere. When the opportunity arose to leave England and start a brand new life in the United States, we both jumped at the chance and packed our bags. Of course, I was cautious and slightly paranoid about moving to a new country. Afterall, my first few years in England were a nightmare. However, we’ve been here for a good few months now and even though I’ve spent most of the time on the road, the family have settled in nicely. There’s no murmur of racial abuse as our neighbourhood is full of Hispanic and Irish, so there’s a real international atmosphere and we’ve been made to feel really welcome…even if the Irish didn’t speak to me during the recent World Cup in Japan, heheh. After the disaster of last September, it’s made out family and the neighbourhood stronger than ever. Like one huge international family. They all know that I’m in a different city every day, so they keep a close eye on them to keep me calm. I have to admit that was scared about living in a big city, especially the one of this size and reputation. You hear so many things every day about murders, shootings, etc, etc…but thankfully, I don’t expect those things to happen in a suburban area. So, I am now on the proverbial ladder. Not just in life, but in professional wrestling. I’ve made the leap across the big pond and my name in this business is more open to local and possibly national exposure…whether that will harm or further my career is another thing. I’m hoping and pushing for the latter. These are my general thoughts about a foreigner in a new country. I’ve experienced the positive and negative effects of this and I’m proud to say that I am a stronger man because of the past. This great city, as well as the country itself, has opened its arms to Stephanie, the children and myself and made us feel welcome and happy. I guess I’m a German in New York now, eh?
