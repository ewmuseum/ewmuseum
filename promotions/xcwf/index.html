Ran a fixed monthly pay-per-view calendar behind a weekly Saturday Night Fear broadcast for a year and a half, crowning a twenty-reign Extreme Championship lineage that changed hands five times on one October night in Vancouver and three times on one Cancun beach show six months later, and handing out 44 of its own annual awards across a roster that passed through 130 recorded shows. Written by AI, 2026

Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

XCWF opened on 18 March 2001 with the Georgia Dome under it and a sixteen-man Royal Rumble to crown its first World Heavyweight Champion, and its own broadcast that night made no secret that it was a re-launch: a two-month-dark predecessor the fed's own commentary calls only “the CWF” had just reopened “with a new name… and it’s a hell of a lot tougher than before.” Da Masta won that first Rumble and the fed's first Intercontinental Championship on the same night; five months later, at ResurreXtion in Madison Square Garden, it crowned a second belt that would become its own richest lineage — the Extreme Championship, won by Freak in a four-way ladder match that opened with three men colliding in midair before the bell had properly rung.

Nine championships survive in the fed's own title-history pages, recovered whole: the World Heavyweight, Extreme, Women's, Tag Team, United States, Junior Heavyweight, Intercontinental, InterUnited and N.E.W. Championships, eighty-eight reigns between them. The Extreme belt alone accounts for twenty of those reigns in eleven months — three changing hands in a single week between Bayamon, Puerto Rico and Cancun, Mexico, and three more on one Cancun night alone, a Spring Break-Out card the fed's own commentary remembered the following year as the show a storm had once cancelled outright. Its InterUnited Championship, created in June 2002 to fold the United States and Intercontinental belts into one, opens on a reign the ledger's own key calls a unification match against a wrestler, Brian Arson, who appears nowhere else as a titleholder in the surviving record — a loose thread the fed's own paperwork never ties off.

The fed staged its championship matches as often on interference as on a clean finish. Its own Vice President, TJ Faith, wrote himself into ResurreXtion's own first World Championship match as a surprise special guest referee and finished the challenger with his own move to hand Royce Mathers the title; at Desperation, the President struck Mark Estep with the belt itself in the closing seconds of a Last Man Standing main event, and a masked ally at Kamikazi's side shoved a ladder-climbing Mimic down to settle the Intercontinental Championship the same night. XCWF joined Primetime Central in January 2002, by its own account, and its held roster is entirely original characters — no real-world wrestler is billed under their own name anywhere in the surviving record.

The record does not agree with itself everywhere. Primetime Central's own dated profile of the fed, from 20 April 2002, names several champions who do not match the title-history ledger's own reign dates for that same week; both readings survive and neither is resolved here. How XCWF itself ended is not in the surviving record. Every one of its nine title-history pages stops recording new reigns on or by 25 August 2002, in Indianapolis — four different belts changing hands that same date — and the fed's own undated Champions and Contenders page lists every title at exactly that final reign. One document runs later: a Fear of 1 September 2002 the fed posted as a highlight reel after apologising for never airing that week's show, closing on an advertisement for a Redemption pay-per-view in Montreal on 29 September that nothing else in the record shows happening. The free host the fed's site lived on eventually lapsed, and a later address, xcwf.theewz.com, is a domain-parking redirect by the time of its own last capture.

Storylines

XCWF gave out its own X-Awards three times in the surviving record — the first ever in September 2001, a third edition in January 2002, and a fourth from a beach in Cancun that April, the last of them voted on by the fans at the fed's own xcwf.tk address. A fed naming its own Best Feud, Most Extreme Wrestler and Best Match is saying out loud what it thought it was making. The arcs below are led by those categories and recapped from the cards they ran across.

Total HaVok, and the fed that opened by calling itself a rebirth Written by AI, 2026

XCWF's first night, Total HaVok at the Georgia Dome on 18 March 2001, opened on a recap of somebody else's year. Clips ran of a Zone match, a cell match and an eight-man Royal Rumble — all of them from a promotion the broadcast calls only the CWF, which it says had been “shut down for almost two whole months, and left in the dark. After the wait it has returned with a new name… and it's a hell of a lot tougher than before.” Signs in the crowd read “[X]CWF: The Rebirth.” Predator, who introduced himself as the first CWF World Champion, hosted the show from a desk at ringside rather than wrestling on it, while sixteen men fought to become the first XCWF one.

Da Masta won both belts on offer. He took the Intercontinental Championship off ConVict in a Georgia Strap match earlier in the evening, entered the Rumble at number thirteen on the strength of it, and finished by dropping Mark Estep over the top rope with the Vindicator. The fed's own president, Thomas Mason, came to the ring to hand him the belt. What the card does not settle is anything about the CWF itself: no CWF document of any kind survives in the record, and later commentary dates that fed to October 2000 without saying more.

The record then stops for ten weeks. Cards run weekly from March into 16 May 2001 and do not resume until 24 July, and when they do the fed is describing itself as having re-opened again. Its first act was to put the World Championship into a sixteen-man tournament, which is how Kamikazi's own reign ends in the ledger — not lost in a ring, a point he was still making a year later.

The tournament, the Canadian Elite, and the referee in the XCWF jacket Written by AI, 2026

The tournament final was the main event of ResurreXtion at Madison Square Garden on 5 August 2001, and it put the two founders of the Canadian Elite against each other: Royce Mathers and Mark Blaze, inside a cell, both men insisting beforehand that the friendship would survive it. It did not survive the referee. The fed's Vice President, TJ Faith, walked out before the bell, tore off an XCWF jacket to show an official's jersey underneath, and announced himself into the match: “NOW WE'RE READY FOR HELL!!!!”

He then worked the match rather than officiating it — leaning on the ropes to knock Blaze off a turnbuckle, handing Mathers a chair and pulling it back again, lying to Mathers about what Blaze had called him to get both men onto the roof of the cell, and counting slowly for each of them in turn. Mathers threw Blaze off the cell through the announce table and missed his own dive after him. The finish was Faith's: he laid Blaze out with his own move, the Seek and Destroy, and let Mathers cover him. The rest of the Canadian Elite came down to answer for it, and what answered them was a run-in of the fed's and the CWF's old champions — Kamikazi, Da Masta, Mark Estep and Predator — who cleared the ring, put Mathers through a table, and stood in it with Faith with their hands joined.

Two other belts were born the same night. Lil Guido beat Jake Simmons to become the first Junior Heavyweight Champion, and a four-way ladder match crowned the first Extreme Champion in Freak, who took the belt down after giving Justin Pearce a tornado DDT from the top of it. That match had already run through a stack of tables, two ladders, a step ladder, a fire extinguisher and four men sword fighting with steel chairs before either of them got there.

Title versus title in Nashville, and the night the record disagrees with itself Written by AI, 2026

On Fear of 7 September 2001 in Nashville, Freak defended the Extreme Championship against Al Magz and the United States Championship in the same hardcore match — a challenge Magz had issued at UnleAsheD, and the fed's own first title-for-title bout. Asked beforehand what he would do with the second belt, Freak said the United States Title was “of no importance” and left the rest at “You'll see Matt… You'll see.” He won the match with a hurricanrana off a ladder, was handed both belts, looked at the second one, and dropped it across the body of the man he had just beaten before climbing the turnbuckle with only his own.

The fed's own United States Championship ledger records that night as Al Magz taking the belt from Freak. The card records Freak beating Al Magz. Both are true of what the fed staged, and neither is corrected against the other: the reign row follows the ledger, the match row follows the card, and the fed's own answer to the contradiction is the pause Freak took on the turnbuckle before he threw the belt away.

Sid, and the WWCW Elite Written by AI, 2026

On Fear of 21 September 2001 at the Saddledome in Calgary, the fed's own owner Thomas Mason stepped down from the presidency in the middle of the ring and named a successor: a man billed only as Sid, who introduced himself as “the former WWCW President and Owner.” The commentary desk, marking the moment, put a date on the company that appears nowhere else in the record — that Mason “started the XCWF back in October 2000 as the CWF.”

Mason came back on 9 November in Edmonton to say he had been wrong to go: “That was September twenty-first on Fear. Tonight, is the ninth of November and I regret what I said.” Sid kept the office anyway, and on 7 December in Chicago he brought a company with him — seven or eight men into the ring behind him, and a line the show went off the air on: “As long as I am here… The XCWF will go under!! And they will go under to the WWCW ELITE!!”

The Summit in Houston on 30 December 2001 was booked as eight matches of XCWF against the invaders, and the invaders won the one that counted: Royce Mathers took the World Championship off Zac Moore with Sid at ringside. Moore won it back at New Dawn five weeks later. The fed's own X-Awards that January named “XCWF vs. WWCW” its Best Feud or Angle, and by the middle of that month Mason was signing matches again; how he got his office back is not in the surviving record.

The Extreme Championship, and the rule that made it fair game Written by AI, 2026

Twenty reigns ran through the Extreme Championship in the eleven months between Freak winning it off the ladder and Royce Mathers winning it in Richmond in July 2002, and most of them are owed to one announcement. Ahead of Total HaVok II on 7 April 2002, Thomas Mason taped a statement making the belt “fair game at any point in time, on any XCWF broadcast program. That includes Pay-Per-View events such as this one, Warfare, Fear and Shockwave.” It took effect the same night: Destroyer came out of a bathroom stall, put champion Curt Rock's head in a toilet and then a mop bucket, and pinned him there.

Seventeen days later, on Warfare in Bayamon, Puerto Rico, the belt changed hands three times inside one six-man tag match. Curt Rock rolled Destroyer up in a school boy; Darkside slid in and hit the new champion with a swanton off the same corner Destroyer had rolled into; Destroyer got up and superkicked Darkside under the jaw for a third fall before the ring announcer had finished with the second. He then ran up the ramp with the belt while the two men he had left behind fought on their knees.

Four days after that the fed took the belt to Spring Break-Out on a beach in Cancun — the April slot on its own fixed annual calendar, and the first time that slot had actually run. The desk explained why on air: the 2001 edition was “canceled. There was a violent storm in Florida and the stage was tossed around beyond repair,” costing a Mark Estep–Kamikazi main event the fed still called “a great show that sadly never happened” a year on. The 2002 one ran, and Destroyer turned back Curt Rock on it in a match fought in the sand and off a balcony into the water. He carried it across the road to Fat Tuesday's afterwards, set it down on a table and ordered a drink; the bartender turned around, and the bartender was Duff, with a referee already in the bar. Chris Collins took it off Duff with a wooden chair before Duff had finished the free beer, and Curt Rock took it back from Collins in the street with a surfboard and a referee of his own, then left Cancun immediately — because, as the desk put it, he knew he was fair game.

Kamikazi hands the belt over Written by AI, 2026

Kamikazi held the Intercontinental Championship for 126 days across the winter of 2001–02, which he called the longest title reign the fed had, and won the World Championship a second time on Fear of 13 April 2002 in Oklahoma City. Four days later, on Warfare in Fayetteville, he came out wearing it and retired instead of defending it, citing a knee: “I have decided that, I'm not going to be around for the life the XCWF lives, though my name is etched in its immortality… But rather, I need to find someone willing of that stature to keep the spirit alive.”

He picked Al Magz — his own tag partner in the Veteration, and the man he had beaten for the belt four nights before — kissed the title, and laid it on his shoulder. Magz took the microphone and said he would rather have had the partner: “tonight I am losing my best friend on the road here, and my Veteration partner… and all for this title.” The fed's own ledger records the reign as ending exactly that way, handed down in Fayetteville rather than lost in a ring, and its X-Awards eleven days later gave Kamikazi Most Inspirational, Best Promo and Most Devastating Finisher on his way out the door.

The Board of Directors Written by AI, 2026

Mimic won the Circuit at Total HaVok II — a thirty-man battle royal across two rings inside one cell, which he was awarded twice, once when he threw Daddy-O through four stacked tables and again when Thomas Mason overruled Mark Estep for entering himself as a thirty-first man. He never took the title shot he won with it: he was hurt in an accident in Miami and the Spring Break-Out main event was cancelled. He came back on 18 May, lost the rescheduled two-out-of-three-falls match to Al Magz at MayDay!, and blamed TJ Faith for it. On Fear of 8 June 2002 in St. Louis he arrived with a lawyer and a briefcase. Thomas Mason had already been laid out by a returning Royce Mathers with a chair; Mimic had Menace and Bruce Blade prop him up in a chair at a table in the middle of the ring and told him the paper was a rematch contract. Mason signed it, shook his hand, and was told what else it said: “I now OWN… Your half of the XCWF!” Police escorted him out of the building on the strength of a clause he had not read. Backstage, Mimic named what he had been building — “The Board Of Directors is now in charge of the XCWF” — and booked his own challenger into a two-on-one handicap main event the same night.

Untitled in Toronto on 30 June 2002 was advertised on its own opening voice-over as the B.o.D. Hostile Takeover, and it opened instead with Mason on a screen reading a court ruling that split the company three ways — a third each to himself, TJ Faith and Mark Estep, with Mimic recognised as no part of it. The B.o.D. said in their own locker room that the plan was going ahead anyway. It was: Mimic was disqualified out of the main event when a chair appeared beside him, and the chair had been slid in by Phil McCann, who had spent the previous ten minutes appearing to help him. Mark Estep then levelled Mimic with the championship belt, had McCann put him in a submission hold, and explained on a microphone what he had actually built: “You have no clue what I had to go through to sucker TJ Faith into bringing back Royce Mathers. And then to get Psycho Kid. And you know how hard it was to find Phil? And above all else… How hard it was to set up Tom into losing the company?!” He finished by firing him.

The B.o.D. ran the fed for eight weeks with Estep and McCann at the head of it. McCann took the World Championship off Al Magz at UncoNS2XionL on 21 July, in Washington, in a match both men bled through. Menace won the first PTC Extreme Championship on the same card, beating Jackson Rust of the DWO in the final of a thirty-two-man tournament Primetime Central had funded — and then dropped Estep with the Menace Crusher in the middle of the celebration. Mason and Estep signed the answer to that together, and TJ Faith read it out on 10 August: a four-way, double-pinfall elimination match at Unleashed for both the World and InterUnited Championships at once, Estep and McCann against Al Magz and Menace. Al Magz took the InterUnited belt out of it and Menace took the World Championship, pinning McCann while all four men were down. Those are the last two title changes the fed's own ledger records.

The InterUnited Championship, and the man on the executive board Written by AI, 2026

XCWF folded its United States and Intercontinental Championships into one belt in the summer of 2002, and its own InterUnited title history records the first reign against a blank — champion Menace, defeated opponent “n/a,” with a key at the foot of the page reading “Unification Match with Brian Arson.” Brian Arson holds no reign of any belt anywhere in that ledger, which is why the row reads the way it does. What he was is on a different fed's page: Primetime Central's own member profile of XCWF, dated 20 April 2002, lists him on the executive board as Talent Relations, alongside Mason, Faith and Estep.

The match itself is on the Untitled card, and it is a real one: he kicked out of the MenAce Crusher, hit a slam Menace kicked out of in turn, waved the B.o.D. away from ringside when they came to help, and lost to the Renegade. A week later, on Fear of 6 July, his music played in Boston for a match against Jackson Rust and he did not come out. The desk said they would try to find out why. Nothing in the record after that names him again.

That same PTC profile is also where the fed's paperwork stops agreeing with itself. Its champion list for 20 April 2002 names Menace, Victoria Vengeance, Rage, Shawn Boyd and the team of Stryder and 3rd Degree Burns in five belts that XCWF's own title histories put in other hands that week. Both documents belong to the fed's own record, and neither is corrected against the other.

The Innovators of Violence Written by AI, 2026

Big 'N' Bad — Stryder and 3rd Degree Burns — had traded the Tag Team Championships with the Nation of Darkness across the summer of 2002 and been beaten up by them at Untitled. On Fear of 13 July in Green Bay they kicked the Nation's locker-room door in with JC Mayhem alongside them, and came to the ring later the same night in red and black with three more people the fed had not shown before — a woman called Fyre, Marcus X, and Savage Wolverine. Burns unfurled a flag with an emblem on it and gave the group a name: “you have to deal with the IoV…The INNOVATORS OF VIOLENCE!”

What the IoV did with the next seven weeks was take Crimson Lord apart. It finished on 1 September 2002: Scott Amazing beat him, the group came in afterwards, and they worked on his leg and foot with two chairs and a baseball bat until the paramedics came out. Darkside met the stretcher on the ramp, pushed the medics off it, and shoved it off the stage. Burns took a microphone and renamed what he was doing while the wreckage was still being cleared: the IoV, he said, now meant the Inception of Viciousness as well, “because a new wave will sweep through, and it will come crashing down on the XCWF!” The acting president walked into their locker room to tell them they had crossed a line and left without an answer he wanted.

The show that never aired Written by AI, 2026

Thomas Mason had a heart attack on the fed's own programming on 10 August 2002 and was written into a hospital, leaving TJ Faith as acting president and Mark Estep as commissioner and the two of them arguing about which of those outranked the other. That argument is where the last document in the record opens.

Fear of 1 September 2002 was never broadcast. The fed posted it anyway, under an apology: “The XCWF sadly apologizes for the miscommunication and problems that came with not airing this week's episode of Fear. Conditions beyond our control brought upon this, so I figured that for what was put out, a highlight show was owed.” What follows is a full show written out as a recap — seven matches, a returning Chris Metal working a long bit about suing Darkside over the Junior Heavyweight Championship a shoulder injury had cost him and then admitting he was not suing anyone, the destruction of Crimson Lord, and an opening segment in which Chris Collins refused to wrestle at all.

Collins' complaint was about the fed itself: that XCWF “has become too political, becoming a TJ Faith vs. Mark Estep program rather than the XCWF superstars themselves.” He talked Johnny Reno and Bruce Blade into laying out the referee instead of wrestling each other, cut a deal with Faith to be a lumberjack in the main event, broke it, and called for a strike from the middle of the ring before Al Magz pulled the rope down on him. The show ends with Evan Darkness standing on the ramp having laid out the InterUnited Champion, Collins smirking at the bodies, and the acting president sweeping his own desk onto the floor.

The last line on the page advertises the next pay-per-view: “XCWF Redemption! Live on Sunday September 29th, 2002 from Montreal!!” Nothing else in the record shows it happening. Every one of the fed's nine title histories stops on 25 August, and its own undated Champions and Contenders page lists every belt at exactly that reign.
