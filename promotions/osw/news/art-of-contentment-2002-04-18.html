Art of Contentment

18 April 2002
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
I have been here for only a few short weeks now, and my experience in America has been an interesting one to say the least. However, it has also been a very enlightening one, as I have learned quite a bit in my short time here. My name is Yoshi Kimawa, and I am the great sumo warrior Kimbusa's mentor. In our preparation to come to this country, we spent a great deal familiarizing ourselves with your language. As a result we are now bilingual, a trait I have always desired. I do enjoy being able to communicate with such a larger number of my fellow human beings, even if I have found their customs to be quite disgusting. I admit, my own patience for your country is considerably more than Kimbusa's. He refused to reside in an American home in an American city, and as a result we have found ourselves in a valley in the mountains, living in the comfort of our Oriental home. In fact, it seems quite like home, a trait that I think could be the worst thing for my pupil. But Kimbusa is still not content, something that I have found most of you Americans to share. I have always tried to teach Kimbusa the art of contentment, the beauty of one's own satisfaction with their lives. Americans are always moving, always working, always trying to do things faster, quicker, and more efficient. Do you not see the futility in your lives? Every day this country goes to work in an attempt to make things smaller and faster, cheaper and more efficient. You build your technology, you manufacture your computers, you create the most superficial and insignificant products in the world, and you let them dictate your life. But you don't realize that there is no fastest computer to strive for, there is no ultimate gas-efficiency to build for your car. There is no greatest economic victory there is no safe cigarette. In the end, you continue to make things faster and easier for yourselves, but there is no end. Just an eternal struggle for something better, leaving your lives totally devoid of meaning. How can one be content with a life spent trying to capitalize on the marketplace for faster and cheaper products? Each and every American is a malcontent, trying to satisfy and unquenchable thirst for an imaginary need for material objects. In Japan, our own traditional lifestyle is slowly fading away into that same capitalist nightmare that you live here in America. But true contentment can still be attained. However, it is a true art form to put aside the growing influence of the cheap goods the world revolves around today, and find an inner meaning and become content with one's self. My pupil is not yet content, but we have found an outlet for his rage. You, too, must find an outlet for your greed, somewhere to let it all flow out, leaving room only for the simple yet important meanings of life. Then, and only then, can any of your hope to find real peace. We shall make our first appearance in true battle this coming week, and I truly hope that Kimbusa will be an example for you all. A way to release that which prevents your contentment, and then move on to a state of total enlightenment and inner peace. Watch closely, because the change you see will be quite visible, and you might just learn a thing or two. -Yoshi Kimawa
