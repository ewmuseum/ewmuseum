Searching for Answers and finding more Questions....

9 August 2002
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 8-9-02 Who would have thought that after forty years of faithful service to his Motherland, Ivan Stanislav would find himself at odds with her? It is a shame really when I think of entire situation. For so long, Ivan Stanislav has been hated by so many people outside of Russia, and now he finally has found himself embraced by these people and trusted, and then who turns their back upon him? Mother Russia! And then, to think about Tempests’ impeccable return at such trying time has only proven to be more of a burden upon The Russian Bear than was needed. Things within Russia were deteriorating quickly. The relations between the State and Ivan Stanislav, mainly after his suspension, were negligible at best. I never thought I would see the day whenever Ivan Stanislav spent more time in United States than in Mother Russia, unfortunately I do not have that luxury as well. Everybody in Russia is walking on eggshells, so to speak. The upheaval of the officer corp. was one that no one could have foreseen. Every day within Kremlin I would see empty desks left and right of officers that had jobs day before, only to be found gone the next day. Many of them, unfortunately, were Communists. The old guard was slowly being moved out and being replaced with younger, more Western people. Even I at the young age of… well, wouldn’t you like to know my age? I think not. Even I, younger than Stanislav and the other hard lined Communists, was afraid to lose my job. You must understand, of course, that to lose a job of my level does not mean cleaning out your desk. It means disappearing forever, permanently. Permanence plays major part in my line of work, mind you, but I do not believe I will be losing any level of security any time soon. I attribute this to one Capitalistic Igor Karpov and his overseer, General Leonid Tomifei. To think that Leonid once was a communist makes me sick to my stomach. When the wall fell and the Iron Curtain crumbled, he was one of the first to change his dedication to one of The West. And to think of Ivan’s position, with deteriorating relations with the country that could make or break his future truly worried me. I knew where I would find the answer: The Archives. When a document goes to The Archives, chances are it will never be seen again. Documents from Cold War, ages old now, are still lost deep within the file folders and cabinets of The Archives, signed in the blood of thousands of Russians, all written by Stalin himself. It does not matter who you are, you do not see the documents again after they are sent away in file folders in boxes. Praporschik, Lieutenant, Foreign Minister, President, it does not matter who you are, when they head into the building and beneath the surface in which they are hidden, they are meant to be lost forever. Of course, while I am not Praporschik or Minister or President, I am Alexei Ruslan. I have my ways of finding things out. I always do. Power in Russia comes in many forms. Most notably Political, Military, Monetary, and Social power. To say that I have Political, Military, and Social power would be understatement. If the order was signed and sent to the Archives, it was going to be carried out, no question. So I decided to head to the building in which the Archives are kept. Do not think for a moment I will tell you where this is, I’d have to kill you. When I got there, Eugeny Borishnikov just so happened to be on break, smoking a cigarette away from his post. That cigarette was to give him much trouble, at least for thirty minutes, which was more than enough time to poke around a few folders and take a look at what dirt I could scrape up. Throughout my relationship with Ivan Stanislav I have always been his friend. However, when it comes to handling matters involving orders, it has always been strictly professional. No need for my emotions and feelings to get in the way of the job at hand, that would be folly. The orders in PCW were only to be carried out for few months, then the seeds of our communistic might would work the rest. A year later, we find ourselves in sister federation of PCW. It is strange, the orders seem secondary to merely participating in wrestling federation. The orders, who come from Tomifei himself, are hardly even mentioned anymore and with good reason, for Tomifei is capitalistic jackass. When I look at our work now I find that somehow it has become personal instead of professional. OSW has almost become a home in a way, to destroy it would be stupid, we have our roots now and it should not have to change. Things have become personal. Never would I go out of my way like this if it was anything else. But I am getting off of subject, am I not? Where was I….oh yes, The Archives. While Eugeny was busy smoking, I snuck into the cold, dank building. Thousands of folders, all of which lives were granted and lost before my eyes, many of them of which I have signed, no doubt, were before me. It was easy to find the newest documents though as they had not been filed away yet and remained in the boxes in the corner. It is a shame how slowly things run in this country, a true testament to capitalisms failure. After some rummaging and a blasted paper cut, I found what I wanted. A list of names from the officer corp. of who would be in the next line of releases, suspensions, and terminations. The prolonging of Ivan’s suspension was very irregular to say that least. I found the folder which of course, I searched for Ruslan. Such a shame. Niki Ruslan was a good soldier, shame he had to smuggle money, but luckily, no Alexei Ruslan was to be found. So, I replaced the names of ‘R’ and found the ‘S’ folder. It took quite some time until I managed to find the Stanislav’s. I had no idea how many were actually signed to the military. It was then that I ultimately got my answer to a question I thought I would never ask: Who would have thought that after forty years of faithful service to his Motherland, Ivan Stanislav would find himself at odds with her? The answer to my question startled me, shocked me. After that, it was a blur. I left the Archives and returned to my office. Stanislav was in America as usual looking over documents about Corg no less. His focus was on a match that he had no stake in as opposed to his real match against PHD and Mammoth. It was there, in my office, that I had to make a choice. The answer I had found led me to another question: To tell a Comrade, or to hope for the best?
