John Vent — history (January 2004)

10 January 2004
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
History For a man with some much aggression, John Vent’s background is not one you may associate with the kind of man he has become. Raised on a local farm with his two younger brothers and a strong pair of parents, Vent grew up with the morals of decent working people, his farmer drilling into him the fact you had to earn your way through life and his mother emphasizing the fact that even though it is nice to be nice, there’s only one person you can rely on in the world and that is yourself. Fast forward 25 years or so and find Vent working steadily in the factories of Glasgow, the 3 hour journey from his hometown to his found place of work being quite a wake-up every morning. Around the time of industrial breakdown in the early 90’s, Vent finds himself as an unemployed steel worker and with plenty of time on his hands. Opportunity knocks in the form of up-and-coming wrestling promoter Jeremy Carter, who would later become the UK’s biggest promoter, but that’s a different story. Carter saw potential in Vent, who at the age of 39 weighed in at a mighty 396 lbs and stood with the build only comparable to a brick wall. With a little convincing, Vent was persuaded to enter the squared circle as a trainee at one of Scotland’s only wrestling schools, the wrestling industry still young in the UK at this point. Within 6 months training, the middle-aged Vent was now ready to make an appearance to the crowds of the sparse UK Wrestling Scene. A smooth debut and numerous matches later, Vent started to pick up a reputation around the scene as a dedicated worker, but also one with a harsh outlook at wrestling. Much like wrestlers do, a mistake earned Vent the nickname “No Sell” John Vent after a piledriver went wrong and gave his opponent a severe stinger which lasted for a month or so. As a promotional tactic, Carter, who was gradually becoming one of Britain’s top promoters, had Vent play off the “No Sell” gimmick as a heel, giving the impression he was deadly serious about wrestling and did intend to injure his opponents. The truth was: Vent was the gimmick. The piledriver incident was no accident: his opponent at the time had been running his mouth about Vent’s lack of training, in his opinion, and how he was a danger to others. Little did he know that he would be the object of Vent’s destruction later that night. Carter was still under the impression that Vent’s aggression was part of a gimmick, and believing Vent was a natural, he gave him a push and put him up against the promotion’s top guy at that point, their World Champion who’s name isn’t really of much significance. In a gruelling main event match, Vent shown his true colours, bringing the match to a halt after taking the contest to the outside and using the announcer’s table as a canvas to smash the champion on top of, and ultimately through. After the DQ decision, Vent couldn’t handle the outcome and went crackers on the ringside equipment and it’s occupants, severely breaking kayfabe and pretty much screwing any chances he may have had in the promotion in the future. But Vent was to be handed the proverbial “Meal Ticket” as an American promoter scouting from ringside during the event saw Vent’s downright aggression and power as he went through destroying the ringpost and crowd barrier. Managing to fob off security and other precautionary methods, the American promoter managed to get backstage and have a word with Vent, who was still in a tizzy. Catching him in his element, the promoter’s eyes practically lit up as Vent agreed to compete in a new American promotion, but not one in which you may think. Vent agreed to compete... In a fighting promotion. With no particular style to his name, Vent competed as a Street Fighter in an early incarnation of what would later become the popular promotion, UFC. Titles were of no significance to Vent anymore after the fiasco in his previous promotion, so fighting was the number one priority for John. Royce Gracie, UFC’s creator and promoter, worked alongside Vent to turn him into a “One-Man Army”, as many referred to him as, and after a year of combat, Vent was established as UFC’s resident tough man. The year is now 2003. Early 2003. March. Vent, at the grand age of 45, is now working as an Ultimate Fighting specialist, training young hopefuls at summer camps and working alongside current UFC combatants like Tito Ortiz to develop the next generation of UFC competitors. A phone call from an old buddy back home brings back some good memories for John and with the offer to get back into the ring again, Vent isn’t stupid enough to decline and willingly accepts and makes his way home to Scotland for some recap training before heading over to the US to compete. The promotion: One known as OSE.
