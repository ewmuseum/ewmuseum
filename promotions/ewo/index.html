A Toronto-staged roleplay federation that ran four months in 2004, closed when its own founder was written out in a warehouse explosion, and was bought out of dormancy three years later by a wrestler who had signed with it and never had a match. Written by AI, 2026

Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

eWo opened in August 2004 and crowned its first champion the month after, in a two-out-of-three-falls tournament final that its own history calls the true start of its success: “The Idol” Bill Dynamite over “The Dealer” Dylan Dunn, refereed by Erin Jacobs. It ran four months. In December the promotion closed, and the story it told about why is the one it kept telling for the next four years: the chairman, AJ Epic, went missing, and a disgruntled ex-wrestler took him and a man from another federation to a warehouse where a gunshot found a drum of gasoline. Three men died, none of them real.

What survived the first run was a belt. The Primetime Championship began in eWo when Johnny Legend won a ladder match at Bound for Glory, passed through Gabe Shelley, Lawrence Jarvis and Seth Thomas, and then had nowhere left to be defended. Shelley, holding it when the doors shut, carried it out of the promotion into the Blazenwing Wrestling Federation, which bought the rights to it outright and defended it until spring 2006; px:w bought it next and activated it there; when px:w closed it went back to BWF. The promotion’s own account of it is precise about what happened in the end: it “is one of a select few championships that has gone through many federations,” and “many believe it lost it’s prestige and it has since been abandoned.”

Cody Clark had signed with eWo in that first run and never had a match. In 2007 he spent his savings buying the dormant company, had an arena built in Toronto he called the Thunderdome after the Mad Max film, and took a briefcase to another federation’s pay-per-view. At BWF Danger Zone 2007 he sat at ringside with eight people, waited for the main event to finish, walked into the ring and handed out two eWo championships; The Legend put down the BWF title he had just won and left carrying eWo’s instead. The promotion’s own site went back up on 2 August 2007 under a headline that read “The Epic Can Never Be Killed!”

It ran weekly, from the Thunderdome, on a show called Violence, with a pay-per-view at the end of every month. Its own rules explain what it was for: no in-ring promos, because it ran no house shows for anyone to cut one at; strategies and segments filed by Friday midnight; a Dynamic Booking System that placed a match on the card by how hard everyone in it had worked. It said so outright — “eWo’s style has always been that of storytelling” — and then it named its weekly show Violence, gave out a Gutz & Glory Championship, ran a BodyCount match with an award for most eliminations, and announced an all-barbed-wire pay-per-view from the AstroDome.

The second run closed on 25 November 2007 with a story about a father’s signature, a stolen company and a Canadian courtroom under a gag order. It reopened in March 2008 with The Crowning: Epic Homecoming, and ran seven more weeks. The last thing eWo left is a card: a barbed wire cage match, an electric barbed wire ropes match for the eWo Championship, Sunday the 27th of April 2008, from Houston. Whether it happened isn’t in the surviving record, and neither is how the promotion ended.

Storylines

The belt Cody Clark carried in a briefcase Written by AI, 2026

The Legend walked out of BWF Danger Zone 2007 an eWo Champion who had never wrestled a match for the company. His own roster card picks the story up from there: the belt had been his “since January,” and his first challenger in the Epic was the last LWA Champion, Xaria Linette. He beat her, and at eWo’s first pay-per-view of the reopened company, Hell & Back, he defended in a four-way against Czecher, C.M. Vyrus and James Dean. Czecher won it — not by pinning Legend, but by pinning Dean — and a 117-day reign ended in someone else’s fall.

Legend got it back at Body Count, with a finisher new enough that his own card still calls it his “new finisher”: the Red Lottery, thrown at Czecher for the win. What followed was the longest stretch of his career. He beat Erin Jacobs at Supremacy despite a broken nose, countering her Quickiller into a top-rope Requiem that put them both through the ring, with special guest referee Cody Clark counting the fall. He beat Lawrence Jarvis at License to Thrill while Reno Drake and Legend’s own tag partner, The Executioner, brawled to the back around them. He beat a triple threat at Guilty As Charged — Alexis Sykes and Xaria Linette both trying to take it off him at once — and, in the build toward The New Black, beat Sykes again, alone, in an Ultimate Tables Match she had demanded herself. None of those five shows survives as its own document; every beat of them survives only because Legend’s own roster card kept score of who he’d beaten and for how long, in prose too tangled in its own references to fully untangle here.

It ended at The New Black. Frank Murdoch won a number-one contenders match and then did something nobody else in eWo’s second life had managed — he pinned Legend clean, closing out a 126-day reign the fed’s own account calls the only one to break a hundred days twice over. eWo shut its doors two weeks later, on 25 November 2007, and Murdoch is the last champion the closure left standing. No roster card of his own survived to say what happened next; he is known at all only because two other wrestlers’ own cards mention him in passing — Legend’s, recording the loss, and Xaria Linette’s, which names him as the husband she married in Toronto on 1 March 2008, in the gap while eWo was dark.

By the time the company reopened, the title had moved again without anyone recording how. eWo’s own front page dated 6 April 2008 lists C.M. Vyrus among its reigning champions, five weeks after The Crowning: Epic Homecoming had reopened the company on a card whose own closing match — an eWo Championship triple threat — survives only as an unlabeled line in a chapter index, naming no one. Three weeks later, on the There We Be Blood pay-per-view card, it is Kyle Cross billed champion (c), defending against Legend in an Electric Barbed Wire Ropes Match main event from the AstroDome in Houston. The only match on file between Vyrus’s own listing and Cross’s is a non-title Violence card bout between the two of them a week before the pay-per-view. No results page for There We Be Blood was ever captured, and nothing in the surviving record says whether the show happened at all.

The Takeover, and the shirt that replaced them Written by AI, 2026

On the 11 November 2007 Violence card, billed under the header “The Road To No Return…,” the eWo Tag-Team Champions were a team called The Takeover, defending against The Baseball Furies — neither team’s own members named anywhere in the surviving record. It was the last tag title match the promotion’s first-run-into-second-run history would ever record a result for, and even this one has none: eWo closed a fortnight later, on 25 November 2007, the show it had been building toward, No Return, unrun. Xaria Linette’s own roster card confirms the closure caught the company mid-build, saying eWo shut “before No Return could commence.”

By the time the company had a store again, the champions were someone else. eWo’s own merchandise page sells a t-shirt for Nuclear Wynter — C.M. Vyrus and Ryley Wynters, a partnership Vyrus had built out of Wynters’ own violent streak back in the LWA, broken when Vyrus disappeared, and reunited on eWo’s own 2008 return — describing them outright as “the Tag-Team Champions of eWo.” No document anywhere in the corpus records the match that took the belts off The Takeover, or the one that put them on Nuclear Wynter; the record simply shows one team holding them in November 2007 and a different team holding them, with a shirt to prove it, by the following spring.

Crossover booking with eWo (2004)
eWo’s own About page reproduces in full the closing segment of BWF Danger Zone 2007’s main event — the “Walkout,” at which Cody Clark left BWF for eWo along with nine others, staged as an interpromotional angle that ran on afterward as a feud between the two feds.
