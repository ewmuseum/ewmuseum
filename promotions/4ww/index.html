A hybrid roleplay-and-angle federation out of a converted warehouse in Phoenix, Arizona, running one Sunday show and four compass championships from July 2003, and opening its first night by putting its top belt on Jay Phoenix. Written by AI, 2026

Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

4 Winds Wrestling opened on Sunday, 13 July 2003, out of a converted warehouse in Phoenix, Arizona it called the 4 Winds Arena. Its own Company Information page is unusually frank about what that meant: “The 4WW, or 4 Winds Wrestling, is by definition, a small federation. It's just starting right now” — an old warehouse bought and converted, seating about 2,500, “not unlike a certain bingo hall, but a bit larger,” certified for small pyrotechnics but not large ones. It planned, in its own words, on one day growing up.

It was built as an argument. Its own Mission Statement calls the fed “an experiment within the E-Wrestling community... an experiment to try to get the best of both worlds. The roleplaying based federation and the angle based federation,” and then makes the case against the thing it thought was wrong with both: “We don't want this to become a writing contest. We don't want this to be about the win-loss record.” The complaint it says it kept hearing from members of other feds was “I didn't read the card. Only checked to see who won and who didn't.” So it scored angles at half the mark and roleplays and match strategies at a quarter each, published the result as a HEAT figure, and put a Who's Hot table on its own front page.

Four championships named for the compass winds ran the roster: North, South, East and West. The North Wind belt was the top of the card and it was decided on the first night, in a gauntlet, and 4WW gave it to an outsider. Jay Phoenix pinned four men to win it and then told the fed's own interviewer, on the following card, that he had only signed to take something away from the man who owned the place: “I suppose it would be fair to say that I don't see this belt as a title at all, but more of an opening volley in a war against Childress.” The belt changed hands four times in the eight months after that, and every change turned on interference the fed staged on purpose — a coal miner's glove passed at ringside, a run-in from a man who had been written out weeks earlier, a stablemate swinging a title belt, and finally a guest referee counting three in a match he had every reason to want to end that way.

It joined Primetime Central at the end of August 2003 and stayed in for the rest of its run; its own 2005 staff advertisement puts it at “a member of PTC for all but it's first 2 months of operation.” It ran three Maelstrom pay-per-views inside its first nine months, the first of them out of the Orleans Casino in Las Vegas, and topped the Revolution Wrestling Network's first ratings board after that network's own rebuild — a 4.65, printed on 4WW's own newsdesk with the whole scorecard beside it, segment and match subtotals included.

The thing it could not keep was a tag division. By February 2005 there were two teams left and the fed put its own East Wind titles to a members' poll: keep them, suspend them, or turn them into a ladies', pure-wrestling or cruiserweight championship. Thirteen people voted. The owner argued to keep them, on the ground that killing the tag division would cost 4WW its place in Primetime Central's.

How it ended isn't in the surviving record. The community forum's last capture reads March 2005; the roleplay board carries one further post, dated 23 August 2005; and a second site the fed had started building on the same server, a Mambo install with its own boards, was still showing an “Opening Soon” splash at the host root when the crawl reached it.

Storylines

The opening volley: Jay Phoenix and the North Wind Championship Written by AI, 2026

4 Winds Wrestling decided its top championship on its own first night, 13 July 2003, in a gauntlet. The card itself is not held; the video package that opens the following week's Tempest recaps it fall by fall — Shocker pinning Fagen Gilchrist, Syxx Figures eliminating two men, Patti Anderson pinning Shocker and then Syxx, and then Jay Phoenix going through Patti Anderson, Johnny Beckett, Nick Raynes and Jaime in succession to take the belt. The awards site the fed ran the following year bills him in one line: “Jay Phoenix (First Ever NWT Champion).”

The same package carries what happened next. Fey McClancy-Childress met him in the ring: “Bu' ye know wha' Jay. Yer on top, bu' nothin' says yer goin' t' stay there.” Syxx Figures came from behind, hit a superkick and a vertibreaker, put the North Wind Title around his own waist, and dropped it on Phoenix on his way out.

Tempest gave Phoenix a long interview and he used it to explain what he was doing there at all. He listed what he had won before — a Chris Williams TV title, a first world title in the PWW, the whole set of belts in the PWW and the GIWF/UWF — and said this one felt different: “This time I wasn't going out to be the best wrestler in the industry, or even to be the best that I could be... I went out there to win that title to TAKE something away from Ian Childress.” The reason was a man named Johnny Blaze, and what Phoenix said had been taken from him: “his blood... his sweat... his tears... his career... his health... his life.” He also said, on camera, that his contract gave him a cut of the revenue he brought in and that all of it was going to Johnny's medical bills.

The belt lasted three weeks. The card 4WW ran on 20 July booked a non-title Coal Miner's Glove match, “Jay Phoenix vs. Opponent to be named on Tempest!”, and Tempest signed off promising it. That card is not held either; the fed's own preview for the following week settles who won it, in a card-change note that also writes Phoenix off the next show: “Due to Mr. Phoenix's injuries sustained in last weeks match, the doctors have not cleared him to wrestle. Therefore, Mr. Childress has decided that Psymon will come off his victory over the North Wind Champion last week.”

On 3 August Syxx Figures took the title in the main event. Phoenix had a leglock on and would not let it go; Impulse climbed the guardrail, pulled Syxx free, and slid a coal miner's glove onto his hand. Syxx hit him in the face with it and covered him. Afterwards the two of them held Phoenix up, put the belt in his face, and dropped him head-first onto it.

He came back on 7 September, unannounced and disguised, kicking a chair into Syxx's knee in the middle of the main event — “JAY PHOENIX!!!! Jay Phoenix is back in the 4WW and he is making his presence felt right away.” Jaime took the title out of it. The show closes on Phoenix sitting in the ring watching Jaime walk away with the belt, and the commentary is careful to say he looks shocked.

A week later, on 14 September, he and Jaime worked a tag match against Ritoru Joufu and a partner Syxx Figures would not name. The fed built the whole thing on Joufu having broken Johnny Blaze's neck — “Johnny Blaze, the dear friend to Jay Phoenix,” as its own commentary put it. The mystery partner walked out to “Hotel California”: Todd Dream, who the commentary says was also one of Johnny Blaze's friends. Phoenix went to meet him and they talked, calmly, and nodded at each other. Then Dream took him over the guardrail with a belly-to-back suplex, powerbombed him onto the floor, and pulled off his shirt to show an Ego Trip shirt underneath. Joufu blinded Phoenix with red mist on the outside; Dream pinned Jaime in the ring.

At Maelstrom I: Critical Mass Phoenix was booked into the North Wind Title match and the fed wrote him out of it in-fiction instead. “Where are Jay Phoenix and—” “Well, to be honest, we don't know right now. As I understand it they both made the pre booking muster yesterday. But neither has been seen since. Mr. Childress currently has Las Vegas Metro out there looking.” Todd Dream beat Jaime for the belt without him.

The last thing 4WW's own record has him do is count a fall. At Maelstrom III: Legacy in March 2004 he refereed the North Wind Title main event between Todd Dream and Patti Anderson, and Dream spent the week before it saying out loud that the match was rigged: “I find it odd how you signed this match and had my mortal enemy Jay Phoenix ref it.” Phoenix counted three for Patti Anderson. Dream and Jerms came to argue about it; Jerms hit him in the back of the head with the 4 Winds trophy belt, and the show went off the air with both men stomping him.

The compass belts, and the division that emptied Written by AI, 2026

The other three belts ran their own courses. The South Wind Championship was decided in a ladder match at the end of two mini tournaments on 20 July 2003 and went to Patti Anderson, who is billed on her own roster card as its first ever champion; it then moved faster than anything else in the fed, through Chris West, Mike Marvelous, Jerms, Jade, Bullet, Chris Sanchez, Darek DeAngelo and Caitlin Fairchild inside a year. One holder did the arithmetic on the fed's own board and worked out that at that rate there would be fifty-four South Wind champions in twelve months.

The West Wind title was where 4WW put its gimmick matches: its recorded changes include a Flaming Cross match and a two-out-of-three-falls bout fought over 220 light tubes and barbed-wire ropes with an I Quit third fall. The East Wind titles were tag belts, crowned at Maelstrom II: Eruption in December 2003 after a nine-man gauntlet and a tournament final, and given to Draven Lox and Dark Angel. By February 2005 the division behind them had two teams in it, and the fed ran a poll on what to do about it. The owner voted to keep them and said why: “If we kill our tag team division, we also kill being in PTC's tag division as there would be no means of proving a tag team to them to use.”

Above all four, eventually, came the 4 Winds Championship, decided in a tournament across Maelstrom III: Legacy and won by Jerms — who then used the trophy for something other than holding up.

What the fed thought of itself Written by AI, 2026

4WW ran one full awards season. Nominations were collected by e-mail through November 2004, the top two in each category went on a hosted ballot alongside a third pick by the owners, and members voted. The categories are the fed's own reading of what it had made: Wrestler of the Year, Best Exposure, Angle of the Year, Best Match Writer, and one Best Champion category for each of the five belts. The Hall of Fame section of the same ballot put six names to a vote, each with the single line the fed thought worth printing beside it. The first of the six is Jay Phoenix.
