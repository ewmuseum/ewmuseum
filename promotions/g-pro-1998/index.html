A Japanese-style puroresu federation broadcasting weekly from Tokyo, Osaka and Yokohama from July 1998, crowning its first slate of champions across two title-tournament brackets and a series of interpromotional supercards. Forty-five wrestlers and six shows are recorded here. Written by AI, 2026

Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

Gunryo Pro Wrestling opened in July 1998 as a Japanese-style puroresu promotion, its own founding chairman posting a blank application form that asked every hopeful to invent a wrestler's name, look and moveset from scratch. It settled into a four-day weekly cadence of its own devising — Battlefield, its live Saturday show; Recap, a Monday interview packet; a Wednesday newsletter; and Battle Stations, a Friday preview carrying every wrestler's own promo for the coming card — broadcasting out of Tokyo, Osaka and Yokohama.

Its debut Battlefield, live from Budokan Hall on 8 August 1998, introduced a hardcore division it called Jisatsu — Japanese for “suicide” — opening with a barbed-wire baseball bat match under its own house rules for the division.

Its first title-crowning supercard, that September, saw Zokugun Sangai win the new Trios Championship in a twelve-man, four-team cage war. Its next, Blast-Off, out of the Osaka Dome, crowned two more champions by tournament final — Kashan Akuma over The Banshee for the Super-Middleweight title, and Jason Storm over Frazer Fury for the Asia Pacific Heavyweight title — on a card that also saw the Jisatsu champion, “Demon Boy” Ishrinku, who had won the belt in a five-man scaffold battle royal fought above a ring of tables, defend it in a three-way billed as “Bloodmania” against Ghost Warrior Rudiger and Keiji Asamiya.

Jason Storm went on to unify his title into a Triple Crown Championship; its first defense, against Jisatsu champion Bram Black in February 1999, was disputed on-air by a rival who called Storm a cheater and accused his manager, Charisma, of interference. The same broadcast crowned a new Combative Tag Team Championship for two of the three wrestlers making up the promotion's own Daimyo trio.

Its founding chairman announced its closure on 15 March 1999, citing “fedhead burnout” after “seven months of operation” and thanking its handlers, staff, writers and fans for what “has been fun while it has lasted.” Broadcasts under its own Battlefield name resumed that May and continued into August 1999 with no reopening notice and no successor named; who kept it running after its own chairman left is not recorded.
