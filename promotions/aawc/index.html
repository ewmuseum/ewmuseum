A Prodigy-era federation billed as the place where the Real Men Wrestle, which sold its own presidency on camera in 1995 and ran a two-night Oktoberfest out of the New Orleans SuperDome in 1996 — a haunted arena, a commentator who vanished in a blackout, and a Television title handed over in exchange for a bottle of water. Written by AI, 2026

Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

The All Action Wrestling Conference ran on the Prodigy online service and billed itself, in nearly every segment, as the place “where the Real Men Wrestle.” It carried four championships — a World Heavyweight title, a United States Heavyweight title, a Universal Tag Team title and a Television title, with a Universal Heavyweight Championship and a Unified Championship on its own earlier cards — and it staged itself at stadium scale: Three Rivers Stadium in Pittsburgh one week, the New Orleans SuperDome the next, with its own President addressing the fans who had bought the show on pay-per-view.

Two of its cards survive. The later one is a night of Oktoberfest ’96, subtitled SUPERDOME SWEET DREAMS, posted to Prodigy’s own wrestling board on 25 October 1996 in twenty-nine numbered parts. An old Creole gypsy woman was carried out of the locker room warning that the arena’s spirits had promised retribution; the lights went out four times over the course of the night; and the colour commentator screamed once in the dark and was not on the broadcast platform when they came back on. The fed replaced him on camera with Jim Mora, billed as the former head coach of the New Orleans Saints, who spent the rest of the evening rating referees, entrance music and his own old team.

Underneath the haunting, the booking was a heel stable’s night. Dr. Silver went into it as World Heavyweight Champion, having taken the belt off Marcus Price in a match Price wrestled with his own finisher suspended — Silver had filed a protest with the fed’s offices calling the Fashion Statement a chokehold, the office suspended it pending an investigation, the referee enforced the suspension, and Price answered by applying the hold to the President. Silver spent Oktoberfest filling other people’s matches with his own men: a missing tag team’s slot went to his Elite Guard on the spot. He closed the night collecting the Television title from a triangle match that turned out to have been his all along, when Bloodhunt tagged himself in, flattened the champion with the Headshot, and rolled a barely conscious Xavier Virile on top of him for the count.

The earlier card is fourteen months before it, and belongs to a different AAWC. On 5 August 1995, at Cameron Indoor Arena, the promotion staged its own sale: its President, Robert E. Schmid, had sold the office to CS Enterprises at the end of WrestleFest, been double-crossed by the buyers, and been booked into a tuxedo match with his own million dollars on the line against the woman who had left him. He lost it, kidnapped three broadcasters and both new owners in revenge, and was last seen fighting one of them with a lightsaber in a warehouse off a North Carolina highway. The main event was a steel cage for two World titles at once — Tom Adler’s AAWC Universal Heavyweight Championship and GUNS’s Enterprises World Heavyweight Championship — and Adler left with both, dropping down the far side of the cage while a commentator held GUNS off the floor on his shoulders.

The two cards belong to two different AAWCs. The first ran from at least October 1993 — when its own World title was merged into a ten-title Unified Championship, its champion at the time a wrestler called Hot Shot — through a Unified Championship of its own built out of three lesser belts, through WrestleFest ’95, which merged that title with the World title to crown a single Universal Champion, and ended with Under New Ownership. The buyers kept the promotion and did nothing with it. On 19 March 1996, in another company’s ring, Schmid quit them on the microphone and announced he was restarting it: “come check out the AAWC...no, not where the BIG BOYS play...but where REAL MEN WRESTLE!” That is where the phrase running through every segment of Oktoberfest ’96 comes from. The second run was still going in the summer of 1997, with its own Television and World Tag Team champions named elsewhere; by January 1999 it was spoken of in the past tense.

No founding date and no closing date survive for either run. The Oktoberfest card ends mid-count during the United States title match, so the fed’s own last recorded word is a referee’s “ONE...TWO...THRE” — the announced main event, the tag title defence and the whole second night are past the edge of the page.

Storylines

The arcs below are recovered from full reads of the two surviving AAWC cards, 5 August 1995 and 25 October 1996, and of the championship history that dates the promotion’s own 1993 title merger.

The plant Written by AI, 2026

Xavier Virile’s first night in the AAWC ended with Dr. Silver’s entire army beating him, and the beatings did not stop: Bloodhunt interfered in his match with Lex Taylor in Pittsburgh, and the Oktoberfest ’96 broadcast introduced him as a man finally getting his hands on the man who had been attacking him since he arrived. He got a Television title triangle match instead, and won it without knowing he had. Bloodhunt tagged himself in as Taylor hit the Lex Plex, put Taylor down with the Headshot, tagged Virile’s arm and rolled him, barely conscious, on top of the champion. Silver took the belt off the referee, and Virile — handed a bottle of water to carry to him by Hell’s Caretaker — traded the water for the title and hugged him. The commentary worked out on air what the booking had been doing for weeks: the beatings were the cover, and Virile had been Silver’s from day one.

The suspended hold Written by AI, 2026

Dr. Silver filed a protest with the AAWC offices calling Marcus Price’s finisher, the Fashion Statement, a blatant chokehold. The office suspended the hold pending an investigation, which meant Price defended his World Heavyweight title in a triangle match against Silver and “Superstar” Scotty Michaels without it, and lost the belt. Price’s answer was to apply the suspended hold to President Schmid at ringside. The President came to the ring at Oktoberfest ’96 to rule on it in person: the Fashion Statement is legal, he could not lawfully ban it however much he wanted to, Price would be fined but not suspended because the fans had been promised two title defences and would get them, and Price and his manager the Angel of Mercy were on strict probation. It bound the rest of the night: when Price tried to walk out on Mike Randalls late in the United States title match, it was Schmid who met him in the aisle and pointed him back to the ring.

The pillbox hat Written by AI, 2026

Bloodhunt sent Lex Taylor a package, and Taylor opened it on camera to find a pink outfit and a pillbox hat — a Jackie Kennedy costume, sent to the Television champion about his own valet. Taylor arrived at the SuperDome telling Extasy to go home; she refused, said she was an adult and could make her own decisions, and followed him in. He walked to the ring alone. She came down the aisle in the middle of his title defence wearing the pink outfit and the hat, flattened Bloodhunt with her purse, and then stripped out of the costume to reveal black leather reading PRIMETIME. Taylor, screaming her own name at her from the mat, picked the purse up and knocked Virile cold with it; she was on the apron distracting the referee when he did. Both halves of the double cross were running at once, and the champion lost the belt inside a minute of it.

The haunting of the SuperDome Written by AI, 2026

Before the first match, security dragged an old Creole gypsy woman out of the locker room; she took the microphone on the way and warned that nobody should be in the building, that there were spirits there and they had promised retribution. The lights went out four times over the night. On the first, “Slick” Vic Watters screamed in the dark and was gone from the broadcast platform when they came back on, and was never seen again on the card; on the second, the announced opponents for a tag match could not be found and the World Champion filled their slot with his own Elite Guard; police questioned Saafir Muhammed about the disappearance and arrested him backstage, which took another match off the card. The missing tag team walked out at the end of the night, in the World Champion’s own group. The card never says whether anything in it was supernatural, and Watters is not accounted for by the last surviving line.

The sale of the presidency Written by AI, 2026

The 1995 card is built on the AAWC changing hands. Robert E. Schmid sold the presidency to CS Enterprises at the end of WrestleFest — the same show at which he lost to Tom Adler and Adler won the Universal Heavyweight title — and the buyers immediately double-crossed him, writing him into a tuxedo match against Teri Melton, the manager who had used and left him, with his own million-dollar payoff at stake. He lost, and spent the rest of the broadcast taking hostages: the play-by-play man, then Teri, then the colour commentator who came after her, then both new owners, all of them ending up in an abandoned warehouse with a vat of chocolate pudding and a threat of carbon freezing. Sixteen months later, at Oktoberfest ’96, President Schmid is announced to “Hail to the Chief” as the sitting President of the AAWC again. Nothing held explains how the office came back to him.

Affiliations

CSWA

Shared continuity with AAWC (All Action, 1993) (24 Oct 1993 (title unification); also co-listed Jul 1997)
The AAWC World Championship was one of the belts merged into the CSWA Unified Championship at LAST CALL II, 24 October 1993 (Hornet's own IRONMAN of CHAMPIONS unification, alongside the IWCCW World and ten-title CSWA Unified championships) — one continuous title lineage crossing both feds' own kayfabe records. Also independently co-listed with CSWA as a fellow Prodigy Wrestling BB league in the 9 July 1997 register.

ChampionShip Wrestling Alliance (CSWA)

Crossover booking with AAWC (All Action, 1993) (1993–1996)
The two federations booked into each other across the Prodigy years, and CSWA’s own title history dates it. The AAWC World Championship was one of three titles merged at the IRONMAN of CHAMPIONS at CSWA’s LAST CALL II on 24 October 1993; the AAWC’s own World Champion Hot Shot wrestled in that six-man field; Hornet captured the AAWC Universal Championship in a Unified Championship defence at Thanksgiving Weekend 1995; and a full AAWC card of 5 August 1995 was hosted on CSWA’s own Prodigy directory.

PRODIGY's Wrestling Bulletin Board (WRESTLING BB)

Community — AAWC (All Action, 1993) was present here (1996–1997)
The board’s own League Info register of 9 July 1997 lists the AAWC with its own World War Games 3 card and its own named roster; its one surviving night, Oktoberfest ’96, was posted to the board on 25 October 1996 in twenty-nine numbered parts.

Ultimate Wrestling Alliance (UWA, 1992–97)

Crossover booking with AAWC (All Action, 1993) (1996–1997)
Xavier Virile, Nathan Storm, Mike Chain and Jimmy Ray Vaughn are all billed on both held Prodigy-era cards, sixteen months apart — the AAWC's own Oktoberfest '96 (25 October 1996) and the UWA's own Random Rumble (24 February 1997). The UWA card also refers to “<> X members,” a group the AAWC card names as the “<> X-Change.”
