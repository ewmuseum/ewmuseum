FLATLINEDfighting staged mixed martial arts in prose rather than on a results card, with no vignettes and no television — and its roster wrote 220 chapters about it in four months, against fourteen shows. Written by AI, 2026

Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

FLATLINEDfighting was not a wrestling promotion and said so on its own About page: a concept “based on real mixed martial arts (MMA) federations such as UFC and PrideFC”, where “winners aren't decided by pinfall or countout” and “you won't see combatants ‘selling’ high risk attacks or grapples.” Three rounds of five minutes in an octagon, no biting, no weapons, and a towel its fighters' own cornermen could throw in.

What it ruled out along with the wrestling was the television. “Vignettes, or televised segments are purely non-existent in these fighting organizations,” its own About page says; the segments it would run “stress more of the real-lives of the characters off camera.” So it wrote chapters instead. Its own index counts 220 of them between the first day of 2003 and 12 April, against fourteen shows in the same window: Lucien Keenan wrote 29, Frank Minio 26, Chester Ramis 23, and one man wrote seven in a single shape, each of them a Something with Something — Safety, Unity, Coldness, Searching, Loss, Death.

The Heavyweight Championship went through four men in four months. Takeo Brown had it on New Year's Day and Liphe Gonne took it off him the same night; Javier Garcia dislocated Gonne's shoulder to take it at Pride & Victory, the fed's own first pay-per-view, whose full results turned out to be hiding in plain sight on its own “Latest PPV” page; and on 23 April, reported only by the front page, Frank Minio beat Garcia with a right fist thrown to counter a blow that would have won the fight. Garcia stayed on his feet after being knocked out and challenged for it again before he left the cage. The Lightweight Championship had one holder in its whole life: Chester Ramis, who reached it after a chapter in which he wakes up sick, five pounds lighter, and works out that he is about to drop a weight class.

It kept the paperwork of a real fight promotion and filled almost none of it in. There was a medical-history register, a win/loss record page for every fighter, a Released Fighters page with one name on it, and an Injured Fighters page whose one entry read “Last Name, First Name.” Its own staff page promised biographies for its officials and used the owner as the example of what one might contain.

How it ended isn't in the surviving record. The last thing it wrote is the news of 24 April 2003 announcing the new Heavyweight Champion and a CLASH in May whose date it never gave; every page captured through the middle of that month still carries the same item at its head. After that the domain lapsed, and everything the archive holds of the address is somebody else's search page.

Storylines

The arcs below are recovered from a full read of all 35 held documents and all seven surviving roleplays.

Frank Minio's promise and the Heavyweight Championship's four owners Written by AI, 2026

The belt starts the year moving fast: Takeo Brown holds it on New Year's Day and loses it to Liphe Gonne the same night, and Brown spends the next two months trying to force a rematch — bribing Enrique De La Mora to injure Jamie Pierce after Pierce takes his spot on a magazine cover, and losing to Pierce himself at Pride & Victory when a knee to the nose finally drops him. Gonne defends the title against Javier Garcia at that same Pride & Victory — trainer Colonel Judge Harris throws in the towel a beat before the referee can call it, after Garcia dislocates Gonne's shoulder reaching for what looked like a knockout blow. Weeks later, Frank Minio — a one-win, four-loss fighter managed by Horendo Lorenza and Ramon Pirenzo — stands at a press conference and promises to beat three heavyweights in a row, each inside the first round, and then take the title from Garcia. He makes good on the first promise that same night, knocking out Arson Zanders at 4:12 to save Zanders's own job in the process. The surviving record catches up with him again on 23 April: Minio beats Garcia for the title with a right fist thrown to counter what would have been a winning blow, and Garcia, still on his feet, challenges him again for a CLASH the fed never dated.

Amanda Ling's Lightweight Title tournament, run in defiance of the boss Written by AI, 2026

Amanda Ling sets up a Lightweight Title tournament without Jared Davidson's sign-off, tired of the Heavyweights getting all the ink — production manager Roy Widrig backs her over the objections of nobody who dares object. The bracket becomes the story of Ijinza Yebanka, a debuting fighter who tells his cousin and trainer Fejona Min he is destined to win it while she smiles along, quietly hoping he loses. He beats Kristofer Niedzwiedz in his debut and draws a bye into the final, where Chester Ramis — in the tournament at all only because a bout of flu dropped him a weight class — knocks him out with a spinning elbow he calls the Paradoxical Touch to become the first Lightweight Champion Flatlined ever has. Running alongside it is Christopher Broward's quiet takeover of the roster: he talks his way into managing Alexander Morgan, books him into three fights in one week over the objections of Morgan's old trainer John Lee (whom Broward knocks out for saying so), and by 27 March walks into Damon D. Jackson's own locker room to announce he is Jackson's manager too, over the protests of Jackson's lifelong friend James McKirkland, whom he throws out on the spot. A debuting Demond Wilson walks out to Damon D. Jackson's own entrance music by mistake and loses to Niedzwiedz anyway.

Montgomery Benson's Hollywood fight, and Umaga Reihana's road home Written by AI, 2026

Montgomery Benson, a television star trailed by real-world tabloid cameras, signs with Flatlined over the doubts of his own trainer Graham Robinson, who tries to talk him out of his debut against Alexander Morgan and is told to go back to Australia if he won't shut up about it. Morgan wins by TKO after Benson ruptures his own inner ear falling down repeatedly, and the fed's own doctor spells the injury out on camera: this isn't television, the punches are real. Meanwhile Umaga Reihana spends the same stretch of the record trying to walk back a corrupting endorsement deal and a knockout of Eric Abram he privately can't stop feeling guilty about, confronted backstage by a heckler over it and talked through his own doubts by Seminalia Babakanu, the woman who raised him. By 27 March he tells her he is going home to Fiji to rediscover himself; the surviving record ends five weeks later, and never says whether he goes.
