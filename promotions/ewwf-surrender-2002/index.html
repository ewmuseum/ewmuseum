A 2002 original-roster promotion built around a weekly broadcast called Surrender, presided over on-screen by its own President and World Champion, Adam Mitchell, and staffed through much of its held run by performers the text itself ties to the recently closed SEWF. Written by AI, 2026

Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

The EWWF opens, on the earliest surviving broadcast, already a going concern: a weekly show called Surrender, staged from a venue it calls the Great Pyramid, run by an on-screen President and World Champion who also signs the card's own signature moves. Its two-man announce team called the 16 April 2002 edition a night of “PURE GOLD,” and the five matches that followed bore it out — an opener decided by a bat handle, a title switch turned on a surprise run-in, and a main event fought in a parking lot under a ladder holding a championship twenty feet in the air.

The broadcast's own commentary keeps drawing a line back to an earlier company it calls the SEWF, crediting several of the men on this card with having “kept the SEWF thriving” before it closed. One competitor is introduced outright as an ex-president of that earlier company, and another is told outright that he “signed with the SEWF, and wasn't given a chance,” only to make his own chance here instead. The EWWF never claims to be that earlier company reborn under a new name; it presents itself as a fresh address for talent that company could no longer hold.

The Extreme Championship went up for grabs the same night in a ladder match fought entirely outside the building, and it turned on an outsider: a debuting challenger to the reigning “Nature Boy” billing choked the champion's own opponent unconscious and dragged him off, clearing the way for the title to change hands unopposed. The broadcast's own commentary ties the ambush straight back to the owner's office, framing the newcomer as a recruit brought in specifically to settle a naming dispute over whose nickname it really was.

Redemption, the promotion's first named pay-per-view that June, widened the SEWF connection further still, staging a rematch billed as a sequel to a bout that had given one wrestler his only World Championship in that earlier company, and the United States and World Championships both changed hands there and on the broadcasts that followed with no document in this packet ever showing how either title first reached the men defending it — an honest gap the record leaves open rather than guesses at. A weekly hardcore rivalry between two other newcomers, one fond of a jetpack entrance and the other of extension cords plugged into the house lights, ran alongside title matches decided by feet on the ropes, mandated instant rematches, and at least one deliberately self-inflicted disqualification.

Kingdom of the Wicked in August, split across six postings, still awaits a full structuring pass of its own. The last surviving edition of Surrender ran that December, with no closing notice of any kind following it in the held corpus.
