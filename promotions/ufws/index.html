Depth
documented

A play-by-e-mail federation that published its own weekly broadcast for nineteen weeks in 1991 — play-by-play, interview segments, a results desk and a ranked league table with won-loss records and its own "Hype" scores — and renamed itself from the PWGeA to the UFWS in the middle of it. Written by AI, 2026

Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

It began as a rulebook. On 6 April 1991 its operator posted version 1.1 of the Professional Wrestling Gamers’ Alliance to rec.sport.pro-wrestling — a dice-and-power-points role-playing game for people who wanted to run a wrestling promotion at their kitchen table — and promised another post within the hour. Two days later that post arrived on rec.games.pbm: the same rules, but played by mail, by strangers, with him keeping score. Three wrestlers a player. One match each a week. Four championships. Sixteen singles slots and eight tag-teams, and no more. Within forty-eight hours he was posting a notice that the federation was full.

On 22 April 1991 he filled all four belts in one night of tournaments and wrote it up as a television broadcast, and that was the shape of the thing for the next four months: an announce desk with a straight man and a heel colour commentator, an interview show that changed hosts when its host went back to wrestling, one or two feature matches called move by move, a results desk that gave every other bout of the week its finish and its time, and — every issue — a ranked league table with each wrestler’s alignment, won-loss record and “Hype” score. Nineteen weeks of it survive. This is a federation that published its own standings before most of the hobby existed.

It also kept changing its mind about what it was called. The reports run PWGeA through 18 July and UFWS from 29 July, and the changeover is a single subject line — UFWS Report #11 (UFWS = PWGeA) — over a body that opens “the twelfth UFWS Report; actually the first one under that name.” The weekly numbering never breaks. Neither does the operator, the roster, or the announce desk.

Its people came from everywhere and went everywhere. Wrestlers walked in from the WeWF and the EMWA and walked back out again; whole stables arrived mid-summer; the announce desk itself turns up on other federations’ broadcasts. In July 1991 it co-presented the Summit of E-Wrestling with the WeWF, an interfederation event large enough to have become its own institution (its own wing, id summit-of-e-wrestling), and its own July championship changes happened there rather than here. The last surviving issue is dated 29 August 1991 and promises another one on Thursday. The play-by-mail hobby’s own directory still listed the federation as open to players in May 1992.

Storylines

Every arc below is drawn from the federation’s own weekly broadcasts and cites their own words. Nothing is reconstructed from outside them, and where an arc runs through the Summit of E-Wrestling or through the two issues that do not survive, the gap is named rather than bridged.

The Jungle, the Allied Forces, and the flag match Written by AI, 2026

The federation’s longest feud started as a handshake refused. In Report #2, Sargent Glasnost — a Perestroika-era Soviet the crowd cheered — would not shake hands with The Russians before his feature match, and Bear hit him with a hammer behind the referee’s back for it. Glasnost won anyway; The Russians and The Spewmaster beat him down afterwards until The Masked Missiles ran in, and that save became a standing alliance. Chief Tomahawk and The Patriot cut a promo in front of American and Soviet flags with Glasnost between them — “the Allies will sweep you. Period” — and challenged The Zookeeper to a Survivor Series match at the first SuperCard. They got a flag match, and they swept it: three eliminations, none for the Jungle. The Spewmaster handed the winners the flags himself, spat green mist in his own manager’s face and ran, and the stable broke up in the ring. The Soviets kept going without him, under a new manager, the Zampolit, and finished the summer being recalled home to a treason trial — their last match escorted by six men in gray coats who turned out to be somebody else entirely.

Club Anarchy, from winless to world champions Written by AI, 2026

Captain Necropolis introduced himself on Jimmy Vendetta’s segment by calling wrestling a violence movement and his own matches pieces. For two months the pieces lost: five straight defeats, a handicap match against the Born Loser built around brass knuckles hidden in a jello-filled doll and titled A Modest Proposal, and a nineteen-minute mat pounder against The Masked Missiles that Necropolis said taught him how to hurt people properly. He went away and trained, lost ten pounds on camera, and came back: Club Anarchy beat The Masked Missiles on 28 May and put The Patriot out of wrestling for a month doing it, then won three in a row and took the World Tag-Team Championship off Crime and Punishment on 16 June. They held it six weeks, through a defence against the Summit’s own Road Warrior Cup winners, and dropped it inside the issue that does not survive.

Baron Von Klaw, and the title shot he never got Written by AI, 2026

Von Klaw won the first championship the federation ever awarded — the All-Jobber final was called before either other final — and spent the next four months arguing that he deserved a bigger one. He pinned the World Champion at the first SuperCard in a Survivor Series match and got nothing for it. He answered a challenge poem through a lawyer. He hid under the ring to attack two men after a title match with an SS helmet. He joined Jack Moran’s KO Corral for the promised title shots, and when Moran told him to go easy on the All-Jobber Champion instead, he sent in a videotape from his own war room, tore up the contract, spat on it, and moved the stable’s photographs to his enemies wall. The KO Corral beat him up on camera the following week; the federation called it their face turn. At the second SuperCard the man leading the KO Corral to the ring under a mask, and the man who threw in their towel, was Baron Von Klaw.

The Four Corners take the World title Written by AI, 2026

Smooth arrived in the last week of July with a talk show and three wrestlers, and teased a fourth. Running Eagle Feather was World Champion by then, two weeks into the federation and managed by Jack Moran. What happened to him is the most-narrated finish in the whole record: the Unknown Masked Wrestler and Smooth chased Moran round the ring, the referee turned, the SandMan came out from under the ring and hit the champion with a roll of quarters, Billy Thunder hit the Thunderstorm, and the fourth Corner was Billy Thunder. Four men then beat the unconscious former champion with a spiked piledriver they called the BottleOpener while the announce desk called it a disgrace. Jack Moran challenged them to a War Games match with both managers stranded on a scaffold above the ring, and at the second SuperCard he throttled Smooth with a hangman’s noose and backdropped Billy Thunder twenty feet into the ring — and still lost, because his own towel came in from ringside, thrown by a Jack Moran who was not Jack Moran.

The All-Jobber Confederation Written by AI, 2026

The federation ranked its own wrestlers and marked some of them jobbers in its own standings, and in July 1991 the jobbers signed a treaty. Every wrestler carrying the mark joined except Baron Von Klaw, who had held their belt twice and would not. Their first act was to put Sargent Glasnost in the Aztec Mummy’s corner for a North American title defence; their second was to assault the World Tag-Team Champions as a body before a title match, which took twenty-five minutes to break up and ended in a disqualification; their third was to walk the Soviets to their farewell match dressed as police. By the last surviving issue one of them was North American and All-Jobber Champion at once, another was All-Jobber Champion, and a team of two of them had reached a championship final.

Affiliations

The Summit of E-Wrestling

Member of UFWS (1991) (10 July 1991 – 30 December 1991, as the served corpus runs)
A founder member under both of its names. The PWGeA is one of the three federations named on the first Summit’s own opening night; the same federation, renamed UFWS in July 1991, is one of the four named on the second Summit’s. Its ring announcer worked every night of both Summits, its North American Championship changed hands on the first Summit’s opening card, and its World Champion wrestled the first Summit’s main event.
