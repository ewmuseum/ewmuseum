An interfed supercard institution that pulled three e-wrestling federations onto one card in July 1991, four onto four nights that December, and gave the hobby the trophies it went on running for years. Written by AI, 2026

Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

In the summer of 1991 three e-wrestling federations agreed to put their wrestlers on the same card. The first Summit of E-Wrestling ran over three July nights — Chicago, Buffalo, Montreal — and it worked the way a joint venture works: the WeWF’s announcers called the matches, the PWGeA’s ring announcer read the introductions, and the EMWA sent its head of security to keep order, which he mostly failed to do. Four championships belonging to two different federations were defended on the opening night and one of them changed hands. The main event put the two federations’ World Champions in a ring together with both belts on the line and ended in a double count-out, which is what a shared card does when neither side will drop its own title.

It came back that December, four nights instead of three and four federations instead of three, and by then it had turned into something else: a place where a federation could hold its own founding tournaments. The UeWF was five weeks old. Across the second Summit’s four nights it crowned its first Atlantic Champion, its first Pacific Champion and its first Ladies Champion, ran its World and tag-team brackets down to two finals, and advertised those finals for a fifth night. The fifth night is not here. Neither is a Slick Ric trophy tournament, nor an Iron Lady tournament for the ladies’ tag belts, both promised in the same closing sentence.

What it made and kept were the trophies. The Road Warrior’s Cup went to the Soviets at the Olympic Stadium in Montreal on 12 July 1991, in front of a billed sixty-seven thousand; the Von Erich/Freebird Memorial Trophy went to the Big Three at the Rosemont Horizon that December, after fourteen trios and seventeen matches in one night. Its own Ultimate Heavyweight Championship was made for two men who had each held major titles in two federations at once, and awarded after both had lost them, on the reasoning that losing a belt does not undo what winning it proved.

It did not stop. A wrestler retiring on someone else’s broadcast in April 1992 challenged all comers to a battle royal “at the summit of e-wrestling in June”; a champion the month before had promised to defend the Road Warrior Cup at the next one. Four years later a writer finished the seventh Summit’s Road Warrior tournament six months late, apologised for it, and signed off telling readers to watch any of the fine Summit Alliance federations. Two of the ring names on that card had wrestled the first Summit’s opening night in 1991, out of a federation that no longer existed, billed now as the Original Shadow Assassins. Somebody was still asking for a copy of the last Summit at the end of 1996.

Affiliations

Members

Every member promotion this alliance's own record names.

World Electronic Wrestling Federation (WeWF)

Member (10 July 1991 – 30 December 1991, as the served corpus runs)
A founder member: the WeWF is one of the three federations named on the first Summit’s own opening night (“we’ve got wrestlers from the EMWA, the WeWF, and the PWGeA on hand”) and one of the four named on the second’s (“pitting the best of the WeWF, the UeWF, the UFWS and the IWF against each other”). Its own announce team called the July cards, and its own champions defended the WeWF US and Tag Team titles on them.

Usenet Free Wrestling Simulation (UFWS)

Member (10 July 1991 – 30 December 1991, as the served corpus runs)
A founder member under both of its names. The PWGeA is one of the three federations named on the first Summit’s own opening night; the same federation, renamed UFWS in July 1991, is one of the four named on the second Summit’s. Its ring announcer worked every night of both Summits, its North American Championship changed hands on the first Summit’s opening card, and its World Champion wrestled the first Summit’s main event.

E-Mail Wrestling Association (EMWA)

Member (14 June 1991 – 12 July 1991, as the served corpus runs)
A founder member of the first Summit, named in its own opening night's roll call of the three federations present and again in its own pre-event Update, which prints the battle royal and tag tournament fields against the federation each entrant represented. Its own World Tag Team Champions were seeded first into the Road Warrior's Cup and its Women's Tag Team Champions wrestled the only women's match on the card. It is NOT among the four federations the second Summit names six months later.

Universal Electronic Wrestling Federation (UeWF)

Member (27 December 1991 – 30 December 1991, as the served corpus runs)
A member of the second Summit, named in its own opening line, and the federation whose own inaugural championship tournaments the Summit staged inside itself across four nights — World, Atlantic, Pacific, Ladies and World Tag Team. Three of those first champions were crowned there.

IWF

Member (27 December 1991, as the served corpus runs)
Named as one of the second Summit’s four member federations by its own host in the first line of its own opening night — “this week-long extravaganza pitting the best of the WeWF, the UeWF, the UFWS and the IWF against each other” — and never named again anywhere in the surviving record. No wrestler, title, card or result is attributed to it, and this IWF is a distinct promotion from the two other, later website federations that share the initials.

AWI

Member (at least 30 November 1995 – 14 October 1996, as the four served editions of the alliance's own membership list run)
Allied Wrestling International appears in all four surviving editions of the alliance’s own membership FAQ, spanning November 1995 to October 1996. Its own blurb runs unchanged across the year: “A ROLE-PLAYING GAME based upon the WWF Rules and Colisieum rules, the AWI strives to be the epitome of realism and originality in the world of e-wrestling... Cards come out roughly twice a week.” Its contact changed hands between the March and June 1996 editions, and a rival federation’s commissioner names AWI, alongside the EWF, as one of the two Summit federations the alliance’s smaller members sheltered behind.

EWC

Member (at least 30 November 1995 – 4 June 1996; gone by 14 October 1996)
The Electronic Wrestling Council appears in the alliance’s own membership FAQ from November 1995 through June 1996, then drops out of the October 1996 edition. It calls itself “the oldest of the active Summit feds,” formed “in the wake of the MBWF, one of the old Summit Feds, by two of it’s former members” — which makes the alliance older than any federation in it. Its own heavyweight champion is billed on the Leprecharnage card as one of “the two biggest stars of the Summit Wrestling Alliance.” Its listed contact is the same person the October 1996 edition lists for the Keystone Wrestling Federation, at a different host — this federation’s departure and that one’s arrival are the same person moving on.

The Electronic Wrestling Federation (EWF, 1994–96)

Member (at least 30 November 1995 – 14 October 1996, as the four served editions of the alliance's own membership list run)
This EWF, the Electronic Wrestling Federation, appears in all four surviving editions of the alliance’s own membership FAQ, spanning November 1995 to October 1996. Its own blurb dates it against the alliance’s own calendar: it “got going during Summit VI,” and by November 1995 it had “quickly amassed over 100 wrestlers.” Its wrestler the Electric Youth is entered “representing the EWF” in another member’s interfederation tournament in September 1996. This is a separate promotion from the held Elite Wrestling Foundation and the held World Electronic Wrestling Federation (1990–92), which used the same initials in different eras.

EWWA

Member (at least 30 November 1995 – 9 February 1996; gone by 4 June 1996)
The Electronic Women’s Wrestling Association appears in the alliance’s own membership FAQ for its first two editions (November 1995, February 1996), then drops out. Its own blurb calls it “after EWC, the second-oldest Summit league,” “started in June 1994 to give the women a place to compete,” “the true ‘league of their own’” — the alliance’s own women’s federation, and the hobby’s earliest. A separate 1994 Summit member list independently attests the alliance two years earlier still, the earliest sighting of it anywhere on record.

IeWS

Member (at least 30 November 1995 – 14 October 1996, as the four served editions of the alliance's own membership list run)
INTERNETional e-Wrestling Superstars appears in all four surviving editions of the alliance’s own membership FAQ, spanning November 1995 to October 1996 — five months past the federation’s last other sighting on record, 27 May 1996. Its own heavyweight champion is billed on the Leprecharnage card as one of “the two biggest stars of the Summit Wrestling Alliance,” against the EWC’s; that match was advertised but does not appear in the surviving part of the card.

SLAM! Madhouse Wrestling

Member (at least 30 November 1995 – 9 February 1996; gone by 4 June 1996)
SLAM! Madhouse Wrestling appears in the alliance’s own membership FAQ for its first two editions (November 1995, February 1996), then drops out in the same turnover that took the EWWA out. Its own blurb: “a renegade fed being run out of an RV as it travels the highways and byways of America,” monthly cards, WRENCH 1.0 / WrestleScript 2.0 rules, run by the in-fiction “Amazing Mr. Jones.”

Universal Superstars of e-Wrestling (USeW)

Member (at least 30 November 1995 – 14 October 1996, as the four served editions of the alliance's own membership list run)
USeW appears in all four surviving editions of the alliance’s own membership FAQ, spanning November 1995 to October 1996. Its own blurb: “One of the more developed leagues as far as having e-wrestling legends is concerned, but always looking for new talant. This league takes a backseat to no one.” It is the federation that staged Leprecharnage on 18 March 1996, its own interfederation supercard, drawing crowds and champions from five other member federations.

VCW

Member (at least 30 November 1995 – 8 December 1997, as the four surviving alliance FAQ editions and two further 1997 newsgroup sightings run)
Virtual Championship Wrestling appears in all four surviving editions of the alliance’s own membership FAQ, spanning November 1995 to October 1996. Its own blurb, rewritten in the first person for the June 1996 edition, dates the federation: “This federation was started in January of 1995 to test out the FastMatch! rules... As of this writing, June 1996, we are changing rules to BlitzMatch! since I got bored with FastMatch!” In September 1996 it hosted the Nine Worthies Cup, an eight-ring twelve-man interfederation tournament billed on its own air as “some of the best that SWA wrestling has to offer,” with entrants announced by federation from four other members — the last interfederation event of this alliance that survives with a date. A newsgroup exchange of 22 September 1997 independently still describes the federation as "a good fed although it seems very isolated from" the alliance, and a further exchange of 6-8 December 1997 lists it among a named group of federations before a reply confirms that same group is the alliance under its own full name -- corroborating the federation's continued membership more than a year past the alliance's own last surviving membership FAQ.

KWF

Member (at least 14 September 1996 – 14 October 1996; the last federation recorded joining this alliance)
The Keystone Wrestling Federation appears only in the alliance’s own final membership FAQ (October 1996), listed under the same contact person the earlier editions list for the EWC. It’s attested a month earlier outside the FAQ too: on 14 September 1996 its wrestler “‘The Strutter’ T.R. Parker” is announced “representing the KWF” in VCW’s Nine Worthies Cup and wins it, and the same document names a KWF event, “the Night of the Legends in the KWF.” This is a distinct promotion from a same-named Keystone Wrestling Federation attested six years later, at a different address.

FWA

Member (by 19 June 1997 – at least 6 December 1997)
Named among the alliance's own member federations twice in 1997, once by a co-fed-head's own community voice (19 June) and once in a further member roll (6 December). This federation does not appear in any of the alliance's own four surviving membership FAQ editions (Nov 1995 – Oct 1996), so its own join date within 1997 is not otherwise dated.

the renamed KWF (Atlantic Wrestling Alliance)

Member (by 21 October 1997 – at least 22 November 1997)
The alliance's own last-joined member (see the frontier row "KWF (1996)") renamed itself on camera in October 1997 and continues in the alliance's own standing under the new name, attested by a community fed directory of 25 September 1997 and a community Hall of Fame nomination of 22 November 1997.
