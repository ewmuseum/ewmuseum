Making the Scarecrow - Part II

15 July 2002
Content noticeA period document, served as recovered; withheld details are marked. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 7/15/02 I was just 8 years old when I watched him die. My Father. He was a fantastic man. A smile that could light up a room. Quick with a joke, or a card trick. He was always ready to make someone laugh. He was the coach of the little league team everyone wanted to be on. He coached High School football. To me, he was Superman, and more. Until he showed his mortality. I remember it well. Being just 8 years old, and still, 17 years later, I haven’t forgotten a thing about that day. It started off normally enough. It was a Summer Saturday. July 21st, 1985. Dad and I were outside in the back yard, working on my fastball. I wanted to be a pro ball player, and Dad wouldn’t stand in the way of anything. The week before, when I wanted to be a cowboy, he took me to the stables and let me ride the horses. He never pushed, but always drew off your enthusiasm. There I stood, on my makeshift mound. I threw a couple warm up tosses in to him, to loosen the shoulder up, and started. “Jason,” Dad started. “Make sure when you throw the ball, your hand comes over the top of your shoulder, and your hips stay with the ball, ok?” “Sure, Dad” I answered back, glaring at him like I was Jack Morris ready to mow down the Kansas City Royals. He crouched, and I looked over at the Imaginary first base. George Brett had a hell of a lead, but He wasn’t who I was worried about. Steve Balboni was at the plate. Balboni, was somewhat of a legend in the Cleveland area when I was a kid, being that even with 2 broken legs, and 2 broken arms, he could probably still hit a homer off our pathetic pitching staff. Sure, Jack Morris wasn’t a Cleveland Indian, but in my mind, he was. Silently, I looked in for the count. MY father held up two fingers. I shook it off. I wanted to give Balboni the high heat. My father put up one finger, and I nodded. I wound up, and delivered. In my head, I could hear the wonderful, rich voice of Herb Score with the call. “Bases Loaded. 3-2 count on Balboni. O’Neil winds and delivers. SWING AND A MISS! THE INDIANS WIN THE PENNANT! THE INDIANS HAVE WON THE PENNANT!” I pumped a fist in the air, as my dad ran out, cheering with me. He picked me up, swung me around, and gave me a big hug. It would be the last time he could ever put his arms around me again. I went to Billy [name withheld]’s house just a bit before Dinner. We sat out back, and played some catch, hit a few balls, and talked about GI Joe, Transformers, and all the other things the cool 3rd graders talked about. “Did you see wrestling at the Coliseum the other night, Billy?” I asked. We were big Hulkamaniacs back then. “Yeah, my dad took me. We sat in the upper deck, and got hot dogs. Did you?” “Yeah, we sat ringside! Matlida almost bit my dad!” “Wow. You’re so lucky, man! Your dad does everything!” “I know. Tonight, we’re going to the Indians Game. They’re playing the Tigers.” “The Tigers! Wow! I never saw the Tigers. My dad only takes me to see the crappy Orioles.” “Want me to see if you can come with us?” “Yeah! That would be great, Jay! Thanks!” “Sure. I don’t think dad would mind buying another ticket for…” Then I heard it. The unmistakeable, pristinely clear sign that my mother wanted me home. I heard her whistle. Still to this day, when I hear a whistle, I turn my head, and almost run home. I threw Billy the ball, said good-bye and I would call him with what Dad said as soon as he gets done mowing the lawn, and I sprinted up the street. I passed the Webb’s house with blazing speed. The Cain house was in sight now, and I tore past it. I was Tim Raines with my lightning speed! I turned the corner, leading to the street I lived on just like Tim Raines would have and saw an ambulance with it’s lights on infront of my house. I slowed down to watch it.. At first, I thought it was cool that they were there. I thought maybe Old Man Mara, our neightbor finally kicked the bucket. But as I got closer to the house, I realized they were at my house, and someone was in trouble. Mom was running towards me, tears in her eyes. The lawnmower was running in the front yard. I stopped and just stared as a man was taken out of my yard on a stretcher. Another man was on his chest, pumping on it. I stopped and watched it all in slow motion. 2 men hurried the stretcher along, but I remember every millisecond it was on the ground. The man on the stretcher with the bag on his face, and the man on his chest, slowly turned his eyes my way. We locked eyes for a split second. It was my father. I ran over to him hoping that with just a touch from me, he would be fine, leap off the stretcher, and everything would be ok. Before I got there, Mom had me in her arms. I fought the tears and started screaming at the ambulance. I screamed out my fathers name as he looked at me one last time. Tears poured from my eyes as I fought as hard as I could to get to him, knowing that I held the key to saving his life in my tiny, 8 year old hands. My face was getting redder and redder as Mom held me tight, and didn’t let go, holding me close, crying as well. I remember seeing the ambulance door closing, and realizing I would never see Dad take another breath. I looked at Mom as she watched her life and mine get taken away in that split second. Her tears were something I had never seen before from this pillar of strength. She was our rock, there through skinned knees, broken bones, and hard bumps on the head. This time, I put my arms around her, and we both cried, sitting in the middle of the street as the police cruisers came. Neighbors just stared at what just happened in our quiet little neighborhood. They stared at the mother and son in the middle of the street, holding each other, wishing that one would suddenly wake up, and realize it was all a dream. That we were still together as a family. And that My Father, Peter O’Neil, was still here, ready to take his only son to see the Detroit Tigers. I’m still waiting to wake up. Three days later, we buried him. It was a heart attack. Never saw it coming. No symptoms, no stress. Just…boom. He was healthy as a horse. Maybe even too healthy. I watched as they lowered him into the ground, dressed in my black suit, not realizing that this would set the tone for my entire life. I held mom’s hand as we stood, in the rain. I stared at the shiny mahogany coffin as tears welled up again. I stood and watched as they lowered him into the ground, and don’t think I blinked. I wanted to be with him. But he was gone. Before they covered him, I threw in his glove, a ball, and a bat. Because when I get where ever he is, the second thing we’re going to do is play catch. The first would be something I haven’t done in years. Give him a hug. Because I miss him. My father.
