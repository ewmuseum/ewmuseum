Date: Today As is so often the case, we seldom reflect back on the minor successes of our days. In such a hectic and often depressing world it is easy for our perception of the day to wash out as another failure. I bet you might not even identify the victory of these tales, but rest assured they are there. ***** The lazy haze of the early summer sun-soaked skies had followed Kirsten down the first four aisles of the grocery store. Michael, her toddler sitting in the cart she pushed, was upset by this and that every coloury item on the shelves was not added to his list of play things in the front of the buggy. His big sister Marissa added to the nightmare when her four year old voice very loudly stated her desire for the most sugar filled, high priced cereal down that aisle. In short, for Ms. Kirsten Attwood it was a typical Saturday morning of grocery shopping. ‘Just five more,’ she thought; a small prayer to the Saint of Good Children. The list in her left hand was not so long as to give the two-some any great amount of time to give Mom any more silver hairs, but you’d be surprised to remember how much trouble you could cause in the few instances afforded you as a toddler. Marissa: Speed Demon. A blur of blond hair shooting further and further away from her Mommy until she was at the corner, daring to break away out of sight. It started as a soft calling, progressed to a harsh whisper and concluded with a sharp tongue demanding the child, now sobbing, back to the side of the cart. The stares of other parents felt like daggers, not that they could do any better themselves. Crouching beside the little girl in the sundress she used the softest octave in her pallet to explain that good little girls should stay with Mommy. She turned a smile at the still teary eyed four year-old and at last bribed her with the candy bar of her choice for behaviour well enough to get this trip over with in a calm. This always worked. The last added to the cart were the eggs carefully placed and the small family was ready to visit the checkout. The line consisted of one man with six items left to scan and a smiling seventeen year old with a jet black ponytail at cash. Kirsten grabbed a divider and began to unload the load with the assistance of a near giddy Marissa, to whom she gave the chips, the bread and the cookies to put up. Once the cart was empty of all save her brother, Marissa darted over to the gumball machine. "But don’t you want a chocolate bar?" asked Mommy. A head shook from side to side. "Okay," she said, looking through her purse for the quarter. "Hello," the teller said in a friendly - but said already way too many times today to be sincere - way. Kirsten drew her attention from her oldest to the five foot five girl with the brilliant blue eyes. She had thought back to a time not too long ago when she was in a similar place. But that was a lifetime ago. She was a mother of two without a man of the house; an old maid at twenty five. She looked upon "Lisa", the name tag read, with envy for the freedom she assumed the child must enjoy. A glance back to Marissa to see a growingly impatience saw Kirsten holding up the quarter and the swift movements of a child with candy on the mind. The final ‘beep’ of the scanner and a tension fell on Kirsten’s shoulders. This was the time she hated. "So your total comes to $132.87 please. Debit? Okay." And slender hands ran the plastic over a reader and handed it back to Kirsten. Swatting away the curious hands of the 18 month old, she entered the four digit pin and felt time slow down. A list of the last twenty transactions from her chequings account ran in her head, a series of expressions to figure out if she had enough this time. Fifteen seconds past and a bead of sweat formed around Mommy’s temple. A small sigh of relief when the till sprang open and she was being handed her receipt. Now in bags, the groceries were loaded back into the cart and Marissa was warned that Mommy was leaving, breaking her deep gumball focus. They made it to the car, one chore done. **** From behind a grand maple desk Mr. Owens sat sucking back the tar filled smoke like it was the cure of his worries. The afternoon sun shone through the corner window and the rest of Toronto. His office was on the fifteenth floor and hid behind a door with a golden name plaque on it. But the great view of the metropolis and lakefront could not draw his attention away from the clock. It read 4:25 pm, and Mr. Owens had been spending the past hour hoping it would hurry up and be 4:30 pm. Earlier in the day he had made appeasements to the investors with a pen stroke eliminating 20, 000 jobs. It was a last minute effort to save the stock value in uncertain times for the firm. It was 20, 000 people unemployed, with lives shaken, shattered because of a poorly managed budget. It was not employee morale that Mr. Owens dreaded, it was the closing bell. But four thirty came ‘round and there was a tapping at his door. "Come in," summoned Mr. Owens , C.O.O. When it swung open behind it was a smiling colleague, Mr. Sawyer, C.F.O. It was the sign he’d been waiting for. They had made the right move. The stock finished up ten cents on the trading day, and the brass finished up the day with cigars and cocktails. **** It hadn’t been the easiest of years for Jake, just another casualty to the economic downturn. It wasn’t the first time he had been fired; it was just that there was nothing else to jump on. Part-time employment was the best available so like so many others, he became a member of the working poor. The career stall only seemed to further the butting of heads with his father. He couldn’t remember the last time the two had seen eye to eye, Pops usually looking through a set of beer goggles. But rivalry aside, he was still his father, and why the news that he had lung cancer at the age of fifty-seven hit him deep. It seemed that everything in his life was gradually getting worse, and Jake was convinced that it would only sink further into a pit he couldn’t get out of, if it weren’t for Julie. She was the one thing that set it all straight. In his girlfriend he saw the chance the world would be made right. He smiled every time he saw her. It was that smile that kept his face from the succumbing to the frigid airs of middle February as the two walked along the snow dusted paved way in the park. They joined rainbow coloured mitt to leather glove as they watched their breath rising into the air. It was two minutes into the ice march when Julie finally asked, "Jake, what are we doing out here?" He said nothing, but coerced her a little further along until they were before a snow covered bench under a mighty oak tree. "Do you remember this place?" "This was where we first met. I was jogging along the path and you, you were sucking air on the bench," she said with a playful nudge to the coat padded shoulder. A grin his response. "That was what? Two and ... " her words trailed for a moment. "Jake, what are you doing?" It would be obvious to the onlooker; a man on bended knee, tiny box held open. "Julie, you have easily been the best thing to happen in my life. Will you marry me?" Tears streamed down her cheek, mascara running. She stood speechless for the short while. Then grabbing the man up from the ground hugged him with such intensity that he’d reckon it to be a bear hug from his older brother, and kissed him so passionately that his toes tingled with warmth in the face of the wind-chill. "So that’s a," he was cut off. "Yes." **** The organ pipes come alive with the notes of another hymn. He stands from his seat and slowly makes his way across the front, behind the altar and stopping to genuflect to the tabernacle on sore, aged knees. The acolytes met him at the pulpit and the hymn ends. He stares out to those gathered for the Sunday morning Mass before starting into this week’s gospel. Oh how they had dwindled. The sparsely filled pews were a far cry from the hearty congregation of the Church he’d preach to when he entered into the vocation. As attendance waned so too did participation in parish organised groups and charities. These were indeed trying times for a man who saw himself as a shepard. Always acutely aware of the evil that sought to tear apart the Church he had cringed at accusations and scandals of abhorred behaviour carried on by trusted officials. Understanding that these men had a special place in Hell waiting did little to bring the people back. It was a cancer that was not handled with in a timely matter as to not infect the entire body. The people had struggled to put their faith in God when the men preaching His word could not be trusted. He did rejoice for the ones who had remained, for he had not changed what he was preaching. It was still the truth he spoke to the people in his homilies. It was not always a pleasant truth, but it is not a subjective creature. He concluded his homily on the one baptism for the forgiveness of sins and invited the church to reaffirm the commitments they had made for them during baptism. With each statement, three accepting Christ, three denying Satan, the crowds responded, "Amen." **** Life in Washington, D.C. could be downright frightful for any outsider unlucky enough to happen upon it. In a juxtaposition of the verbal assault and behind doors dealings of capital hills, the urban area was more of a violent world where wearing the wrong colours was risking your life. It was necessity to run in a pack, a gang. Memorial Day weekend was chillier than past, but Ben, Andre and Steve sat out on the stoop of Steven’s grandmother. They were revisiting stories of their past glories, which at eighteen years of age was and extensive list of assaults, robberies and running away from the police, even if only half of the stuff they were saying was true. This was a community marginalised and forbidden accesses we, the majority, have enjoyed for so long, so they decided it obligatory to make their own. When a black Cadillac Escalade rolled slowly down 53rd Street and sprayed the air with white hot lead, not much was made of it. Ben, Adam, Steven all laid face down and leaking blood onto the concrete. The SUV sped away as the world first when fuzzy, then black. Waking up with tubes sticking out of his veins spooked Ben, but remembering what had happened scarred more than the stitches ever would. The monitors beeped with his heart as it beat. He wanted answers. He needed revenge. While I am sure you have been told before the happiness is the best revenge, that simply did not apply in Ben Smith’s world. He wouldn’t be happy until he had hunted down everyone responsible for putting him in a hospital and, as he would later find out, his two best friends in graves. It was another week before the hospital would let him leave. Once he was out the first thing he got was his gun, the rest would come to him as it happened. So he crossed that boarder from one gang turf to another and looked for the black SUV with the tinted windows. When he found it with it was the wheel man for the drive-by, all grins to see Ben limp his way. Reaching into his waistband he pulled out the nine millimetre pistol and pointed it. Unfortunately he was too focused on the wheel man to notice the other gun walking up from behind him. The last thing that went through his mind was the bullet. **** It was a world populated with half naked men and women who wore less. This was a great caldron of exaggerated personalities ready to ignite at any moment. It was a locker room that reminded me every chance it could that I wasn’t welcome. I laced up my boot and peered across to a man in a blackberry mask staring me down. The prayer in Latin did little to cast away those looks. I kissed the crucifix that hung around my neck before removing it, placing it in my gym bag. Then to the upright six foot three and a swagger to the doorway. No one to wish me luck, not that I’d have it any other way. From there I walk through drab hallways to the spot behind the stage. I am handed a comically large belt and gaze to the gold plating over steel. This title that meant to little to me when I didn’t hold it, but now means my security in the company. It means that I am the best pure wrestler this company has. It did not come without sacrifice and suffering, but every time out to that ring meant pain. Whether it was grumpy old men travelling in Southern Ontario who worked so stiff I still wrestle with a mouth guard, or the slimy group efforts and general disinterest in rules that I have encountered in this place, the one certainty I walked out there with was that I was limping back. Just to look at my left leg and understand that underneath that kneepad was a set of scars, reminders of where the metal went in, was enough to remind me of the sleepless nights and struggles to get out of bed that lay ahead. But when your grimace is more like a grin you are in the right business. And then Jason Myers gets on the P.A. System, "... He is your LiveWire Champion! He is Mitcheelll Quinnnlaannn!" and the crowd cheers I remember why it is all so worth it.
