Date: Today Hoist That Rag Story Three The Ambrose Papers: Volume 3. Hoist That Rag. May 2008. In which, desperate times call for desperate measures, and desperate measures cause quite a commotion. "Glance into the world just as though time were gone: and everything crooked will become straight to you." - Fredrich Nietzsche --- So Long, Space Cowboy The peyote caved in around noon, just as the sun was reaching its peak. Every man and every woman, at some point in their lives, stands up for something they believe in, whether for good or for ill. If it rends their mind to a tattered, weathered thing, then that’s all for the better; at least they tried, and trying is always more important than actually succeeding. There was a strange wind blowing across the desert, like a storm was brewing, except there were no rain clouds in sight. Call it foreboding, call it a bad omen, call it common paranoia, but it came on thick and vicious, like at any moment it could pick me up and drift me off to some foreign place. Jeremiah had come down as well and, quicker than I expected, the grim realization of my formidable task was sinking in completely. He was sputtering and gushing, telling me that I was out of my head, that the drugs were finally getting to me, but I just grinned and dodged his words of reason. "Listen, Jerry," I said, my voice coming out halfway yelling, if only to be heard above the raging winds. "There is nothing you can say or do to talk me out of this. I love you like a brother, but you should really just shut the fuck up." We were somewhere outside of San Antonio, where the desert was the most desolate and the sun seemed to shine the brightest. I could almost see the city if I stood on my tiptoes, but it seemed like it was light-years away. Somewhere in that city – the very city that birthed me, mind you – there was a relic built with brick and mortar, old and ancient, standing as a monument to all that was patriotic, all that was red, white, and blue, a true representation of the American Dream: The Alamo. It was a glorious place, a place where real legends existed and the past felt so close that you could almost touch it, if you could just reach your hand out far enough. And my plan, of course, was to blow it up. Jerry kept asking me why, and I kept explaining it to him as well as I could. The America Dream was dead and buried, nothing but worm chow and false hope for people who still bother being proud of their country. The Alamo, it was just a shell of its former self, a vulgar display of what America could have been and never really was. All of its glory got sucked dry the second it became a roadside attraction, some evil scheme to capitalize on the bravery of our forefathers. It was a nothing more than a tourist trap now, a place for family photos and childhood memories. Those sightseeing, day-tripping, rubbernecking, scum-sucking road whores we call ‘tourists’, they’re nothing more than vultures, just hopeless scavengers feeding on the carcasses of the good ol’ U.S. of A. I was blowing up The Alamo to save it from itself. I’d put that final nail in the coffin and sleep a little better at night. Jerry didn’t get my way of thinking, but I guess that’s what you get when you pal around when a drunken, Bible-thumping, Mexican. I knew that I would most likely be vilified and denounced as a heartless degenerate. I knew they’d try to burn me at the stake. But someday, years from now, I knew I’d be called a true patriot, an American hero, a man who stood for the truth and told the truth, even when the truth was a rough, heartbreaking thing. The only people I expected to disagree were Republicans and the rest of the people in power. A revolution almost always happens when people get the straight, honest facts. Some people may say I’m too preachy, too self-righteous and stubborn, maybe even stark raving mad, but really, the only difference between Sane and Insane is the former conceal their thoughts like the skeletons in their closets and the latter give those same thoughts utterance and declaration, air to breathe and life to live. But in this generation of swine we live in a kingdom of fear; fear of life, fear of death, and fear for whatever we have in between the two. So, call me what you will and judge me how you like, but know that you’re wrong. When you finally go over the edge, I hope you wave up to the cliffs above and look forward to the Hell below, without any sort of horror or cowardice, because then and only then will you understand how my mind works. Who knows? Maybe you’ll embrace ‘insanity’. As we loaded ourselves into the convertible, the road to the end of our savage and perilous journey stretched out in front of us like some darkening chasm, I grinned and looked over to Jerry, my eyes gone wild and my words laced with all the fury of God almighty, "Buy the ticket, take the ride. Turn on, tune in, drop out, and explode." Jerry had given up trying to talk me out of it. He’d slithered back into his shell, loaded himself up on as many uppers as he could, and reclined the seat back to enjoy the ride. The trunk was packed full with five sticks of homemade dynamite, a gas can filled with lighter fluid, a pipe bomb made out of PVC, and a box full of sparklers for the triumphant celebration that would follow. In other words, we had one hell of a massive explosion on our hands. With the wind still howling and the weight of the world settling on my shoulders, we entered San Antonio proper. It had been years since I’d been there and everything seemed to have changed, not necessarily for the best. The old barrios had given way to gentrification, Hispanics and blacks alike replaced by young, beautiful, lifeless white hipsters. Cultural zombies following the latest trends, sucking the blood out of The American Dream, their very existence driven by some sick sense of irony, living as parasites sustaining themselves off the scraps somebody else left behind. Like some weird new Manifest Destiny, but dripping with more malice and ignorance than I originally thought possible. It was disgusting. I felt sick to my stomach. Jerry, he couldn’t see it for himself, but I had the right kind of eyes and all the memories to back it up. Good people were being replaced by bewildered cowboys, trying to make a life out of a stereotype. All truth and genuity were hidden behind a thick veil of deceit and hypocrisy. Places I held near and dear to my heart had been repossessed by strip malls, fast food restaurants, and shithole theme parks trying to capitalize on all the things that made Texas famous. My eyes were watering and a brand new animosity had found its way to the forefront of my brain. With that savage anger shaking me to my very bones, I knew beyond a shadow of a doubt that what I was doing was right, just, and good. But all of my ranting and raving was pointless. I was just preaching to the choir, letting my Gospel fall on deaf ears. Talking shit never really matters. Real action, that’s where all the true grit lays and what headlines are made of. As we drove through the city, I chain smoked to keep my nerves at bay, staring up at the skyline with a foul contempt, but remaining silent if only to keep Jerry from raising another argument. But all that quietness, it sent the cogs in Jerry’s head to spinning, causing weird thoughts and unanswered questions to come out like woodwork. "You know," He said, licking his lips and scratching his chin. "Maybe I’m missing something obvious here, but you never really told me why you wanted to drag me along on this misadventure of yours. I mean, all things considered, you could have easily done this without me. You’re insanity is only trumped by your independence, after all." "Honestly? You’re one of the only people I can trust, Jerry," I paused for a moment. I was never very good at explaining myself when it came to friendship. It made me feel vulnerable. My lack of many real friends should stand as a testament of that. "You’re the Lennon to my McCartney, that little voice of reason in my sea of madness. You keep me from going completely off the deep end, you know? You keep me from doing anything too crazy." He couldn’t help but laugh at that, tilting his sunglasses and looking over at me with a raised eyebrow, a look of mild disbelief on his face. "I keep you from doing anything too crazy? Well, what do you call this, Vic? I mean, far be it from me to pass judgment here, but if you ask me, this whole big scheme of yours is just a little bit more than cuckoo." "Well, that’s why I didn’t ask you," I replied, rolling my eyes. He was building up for another lecture, I could already tell. "We live in the margin of society, Jerry. We’re cast to the side, swept under the carpet like some guilty secret, when we should be the ones in the halls of power. We’re the ones who hold the keys to the future, but every time we start to speak up, we’re put to silence by the powers that be. Don’t you get it? This is it. This is our last stronghold. This is where we finally rise up against the forces of Old and Evil. This is where we change the world." He was quiet for a moment, lost in deep thought and consideration, measuring my words with his morals. He knew I was right and he knew how important this all was, but he was afraid to admit it. I was waiting for him to admit defeat. I was hoping he’d agree with me and join in on this grand crusade. But when his words came out, I was shot down. "…you’re a fucking lunatic, Vic. I swear to God, you’re a fucking lunatic." My nostrils flared up and my upper lip snarled, grumbling to myself for a moment before I looked over at him, sneering, "You know what, Jerry? Fuck you. This entire trip, you’ve been nothing but a constant downer. If you don’t understand where I’m coming from, fine. That’s cool. But for once in your goddamn life, could you just keep your fucking mouth shut? You’re just jealous because you don’t have the balls to do something like this yourself. So get the fuck off your high horse. This is important to me. This is going to put me in the history books." There was a moment of silence. To be honest, I felt bad for my outburst, but it had to come out eventually. A man can only be talked down to for so long before he finally snaps and starts biting at his opponent’s jugular. The only thing that had kept me from reaching that breaking point sooner was the fact that is was Jerry, but even friends need to be put in their place from time to time. Occasionally, however, trying to shut someone up only exasperates things, leading to further arguments. From time to time, someone will say something that will stop you dead in your tracks, leave you dumbstruck, and make you question yourself. When Jerry opened his mouth, his words hit me like a ton of bricks, "You know, I wouldn’t be so adamantly against this if I didn’t know you were so full of bullshit. Let’s just stop the charades, Vic. You and me both know this isn’t about killing the American Dream or whatever fucking stupid shit you’re spouting on and on about. This is all about you, Vic, you and your own selfishness. You just want to get your name in the headlines, claim your place in the history books. You’re finally trying to make a name for yourself, but you’re twenty-nine years too late. You’re getting old, Vic, and this is your way of grasping at what little relevance you have left. You keep on putting out this American hero image, but really, you’re just living a lie. You never were satisfied with just being normal; you were always trying to be different and unique, but let’s be honest here and cut the bullshit for a second. You’re just as fake as everyone else, you’re just too ashamed to admit it." I’d like to say he was wrong. I’d like to, but I can’t. Fact of the matter is, he’d probably never been more right in his entire life. He hit the nail on the head. My jaw had dropped, my head started spinning, all my anger gave way to something else, something different. It hurt. It stung like a thousand beestings. I tried my best to come up with a coherent reply, but all my words came out as vowels. He had me on all fronts. He had me backed into a corner, a cat tangling with my tongue. There was nothing to say and no need to defend myself. With a heavy sigh and a nod of my head, I gave in to that earth shattering defeat. I felt a certain sort of guilt building up in the pit of my stomach, moving through my bones, pumping through my bloodstream. With the radio turned off and the all our convictions stacked higher than a house, we drove in silence, just the howl of the wind and the noise of the city bellowing all around us. The truth holds no prejudice; it’ll cut us down, one and all. --- Land of the Free… All of history’s greatest atrocities were backed up by someone who thought they were doing the right thing. Hitler thought the Holocaust was just the next logical step in human evolution; to clear out all racial impurities and cultural differences to make way for a utopian, albeit totalitarian, future. Lee Harvey Oswald only wanted to make a difference, change the world, and finally have a meaning in life. Even Charlie Manson didn’t operate on the basis of thrill kills and empty revenge, he was only preparing himself and his ‘family’ for an inevitable race war he was positive would take place. Whether misguided or simply the inventions of dilapidated minds, the point remains that nobody ever thinks what they do is evil. Even when a man questions himself or his actions, he almost always falls back on the certainty that what he did or what he will do is for the ‘greater good’. In a way, I can relate. I’m sure that a quick observation would make most people think of me as a madman with lunatic notions; but if you put it underneath the microscope and look again, you may see things differently. Whether or not my plan was some weird result of self-indulgence and a overwhelming urge to achieve stardom was beside the point; I honestly believed that I was doing the right thing, that this was what was best for America. After all, you always hurt the ones you love, but it’s usually for that so-called ‘greater good’. America had become a broken machine. A tired old legend steeped in faux patriotism and dead traditions. I knew I couldn’t be the only person who saw through the façade. I’d watched my country go through a weird retrogression, deconstructing itself to fit the backwards ideas of religious nuts, politically correct dumbasses, and right wing agendas. To light the spark of a revolution and put an end to all the stupidity plaguing this country was my goal. Any fame I might achieve during the process was just an extra incentive. Or at least this is what I kept telling myself. We stood on the on the street outside the Alamo, acting as discreet and anonymous as possible. Jerry stood off to the side smoking a cigarette, making sure no curious eyes were nearby, while I crouched behind the car, loading an unassuming messenger bag up with as many explosives as I possibly could. Nobody watched. Nobody paid attention. Nobody assumed a thing. There were too many distracting things around for anyone to worry about two scruffy ‘tourists’. Hell, right across the street, there was a row of gift shops and tourist attractions, just waiting to take people’s hard earned money. Why worry? Nobody saw it coming. They’d all hear a massive explosion, a boom that sounded more like thunder than a bomb going off. They’d gasp and scream, run for their lives, only to realize, oh my god, somebody just blew up the Alamo. I’d grin, light a sparkler, dance around in some weird revelry, and then we’d blend into the crowd, run and scream, and make our great escape. The primetime news would have my police sketch all over the place. ‘Terrorist attack or American hero? You be the judge.’ I’d turn myself in, become a legend in my own right, and have my name live forever in the bowels of history. At least this was how I had it all planned out. It seemed plausible, at the time. With the bomb bag slung over my shoulder, we passed through the gates without so much as a question from one of the attendants. Jerry kept asking if I was positive I wanted to do this, and I kept nodding my head. As we walked through the gardens leading to the actual fort, there was a sense of pride, some weird sort of feeling that justice was finally being served, once and for all. Parents and their kids stood around, taking snapshots for the family album. Shady characters seemed to be around every corner, selling counterfeit shirts and broken trinkets. Men dressed in 1800s attire reenacted and told stories about the Alamo with more inaccuracies than actual facts. Honestly, I didn’t know whether to laugh or cry. Everyone around me seemed happy, content, accepting of what America had become; even Jerry wanted to stop to buy an Alamo-shaped coffee mug. But for some bizarre reason, I simply couldn’t accept it. I didn’t want to believe that my country was as shallow, heartless, and capitalistic as the rest of the world made it out to be. Maybe it was a neurological disorder or a simple case of a Cassandra complex in action, but it was there, and it was real, and it wasn’t something I wanted. As we walked through those sacred gardens turned roadside attraction, my fingers quivered on the lighter in my pocket, flicking at it and preparing for the coming chaos. Jerry looked at me and said, tilting his glasses downward, "So, you do know I’m not actually going to have any part in this, right? I’m just with you. I’m just going to be a fly on the wall, an innocent bystander." "Yeah, I figured as much," I replied, nervously slipping my last cigarette between my lips as we passed through the final gate approaching the Alamo. I could see it in the distance, battered by the absurdity of man, but still standing as a powerful monument of the American way. Even I could feel my heart sink to the pit of my stomach, knowing it wouldn’t be standing for much longer. "You don’t have to be involved, don’t worry. Just be a tourist, like everyone else here." "No hard feelings, right? I mean, even though I don’t agree with you, I don’t want this to be the straw that breaks the camel’s back. You’re still my best friend, Vic," His voice seemed to quiver and bend, weighed down by his own words. I could tell he felt guilty for his previous arguments with me, all those angry words and turbulent moment bringing the fear of losing a friend to a not so unlikely outcome. "No hard feelings, Jerry. I know you were only trying to be honest with me. How could I hold that against you?" I smiled, looking over at him with a raised eyebrow as we came to stand on the Alamo’s lawn. It stood in front of us, the beautiful remains of a bygone glory, and I half expected its legendary skeletons to rise up from the grave to defend it once again. Its cannons pointed straight at us, its walls seemingly impenetrable. Even with its current uselessness, its long lost grandeur still seemed to shine through all the muck and waste. "Things will be fine, Jerry. We’ll be fine. Don’t think of this as the end of a friendship, just the beginning of something new. We’ll never be the same after today, I know, but that’s not necessarily a bad thing, is it?" "I guess not," He muttered, hanging his head and sliding his hands in his pockets. He stood in silence for a moment, the sun seeming to send a spotlight down on him, giving him a holy aura. When he finally spoke up again, his words dripped with some archaic wisdom, something Jerry wasn’t known to have, "Word to the wise, though, Vic: Don’t expect things to go as planned. Nothing this crazy can be anything less than complicated. Hope for the best, but prepare for the worst, yeah?" I smirked and looked over to him, raising an eyebrow, my cigarette slowly melting down to its filter. "Haven’t I taught you anything, Jerry? A word to the wise is infuriating. Quit being so goddamn cynical, will you?" He laughed and shook his head, reaching over to pat me on the back. Somehow, it felt like this was our final goodbyes, some strange foreboding, a hopeful calm before an inevitable storm. "Don’t ever change, Vic. You’re too weird to live and too rare to die." With one last laugh, we shook hands and he walked off by himself, disappearing into the crowd. With a deep breath and one last draw from my cigarette, I unzipped the back and reached in for the first stick of dynamite. My motto has always been, when in doubt, make no sense, because no sense is good sense. Somehow, I thought that fit in with the situation perfectly. As the first few people noticed the explosive in my hand and began to mutter amongst themselves, a simple precursory state of confusion that would soon give way to an unavoidable state of panic, I grinned a little to myself. I held the dynamite up in the air as high as I possibly could, displayed proudly for the entire world to see, and muttered beneath my breath the five words that seem to sum it all up perfectly. "Let the good times roll." --- …Home of the Brave Bravery is a rarity in today’s society. It comes and goes depending on which planets are aligned, which way the wind is blowing, and whether or not someone ate their Wheaties for breakfast. When someone does feel that sudden gush of courage flow through though, it starts a chain reaction. As soon as one person tries to be a hero, another person follows suit. It multiplies, like the heads of a hydra. You take one down, two more take its place. As I fumbled with the lighter, trying to light the fuse on the dynamite, chaos begun to erupt around me as soon as people realized what was happening. Women screamed, children cried, and people started to run for their lives. As soon as someone yelled, ‘He’s got a bomb!’, all hell followed. Everything was going according to plan, until I felt the breath knocked out of me and found myself falling towards the ground below. The dynamite skidded across the concrete in slow motion. I felt like I was bleeding from the back of the head. My bag full of explosives was torn away from me and tossed out of my reach. As I rolled over and looked up, I saw some muscle-bound idiot standing over me like a knight in shining armor, his fists balled and his eyes glazed over with some undeniable rage. When he spoke, his voice came out with that stereotypical, thick New Yorker tough guy accent. "Yo, guys! Come help me restrain this bastard and call the cops!" In one fell swoop, I saw my entire plan falling apart around me. People started clapping, people started coming around and looking down at me with more anger and hatred than I’d seen in my entire life. I could already hear the sirens in the distance, coming to take me away once and for all. It would take a miracle to make it out of this one. I was dizzy. I felt sick. I gave up all hope and felt the heavy weight of defeat descending down upon me. And that’s when I heard a gunshot, followed my more screaming and panic. The New Yorker started running for his life and the next thing I knew, Jerry stood over me, extending his hand down to me as he said, "If you’re going to go through with this shit, you better get up and do it now." "Jesus Christ, Jerry!" I yelled as he pulled me to my feet. "Did you shoot somebody?!" "No, just a warning shot," He replied, looking over his shoulder to make sure the cops hadn’t arrived yet. "It should buy you enough time to do what you have to do. Now hurry the fuck up and finish the job, Vic. We’ve got no time to waste. This whole place is going to be full of pigs in a few minutes." I grinned wide and said, "You son of a bitch. I thought you wanted nothing to do with this." "I don’t," He replied, rolling his eyes and sneering. "But I’m not going to stand by and watch my best friend get ripped apart by these savages. What do you take me for?" "Way to be a hero, Jerry," I said, smirking as I walked over and picked up the stick of dynamite that I had dropped. Looking over my shoulder at Jerry, I cocked my eyebrow and said, "Care to do the countdown?" "Fuck off," He rolled his eyes once again, some weird and nervous paranoia kicking in. "Now would you hurry the fuck up? I think I hear sirens." Honestly, it wasn’t just his paranoia. I could hear them too and they were closer than I wanted to admit, speeding madly through the streets in some grim attempt to stop me. A weird and terrible sort of dread started to crawl up my spine, a strange foreboding that was full of bad omens. My wrists started to tickle and shake, already feeling the cold metal of handcuffs wrapping around them. It wasn’t so much fear as it was a sudden shock of distress, a preternatural thought that I might not accomplish what I’d set out to do. It was unlike me, but in the most desperate of important situations a man can become volatile, neurotic, and overwrought with the fear of failure. I became twitchy, fidgeting and fumbling as I fished a lighter out of my pocket. In all that nervous agitation, I felt one thing even above all other emotions: Anticipation. As I looked at the crudely made stick of dynamite in my hand, the fuse twisting and curling out of its head like a pig’s tail, I couldn’t help but smile, a giddiness overtaking me. However, and as unfortunate as it may be, even the best laid plans of men can fall to the will of God. As I struck the lighter’s wheel and the flame heaved out of it to touch the fuse and make’s it dent in history, those powerful winds from the desert finally seemed to catch up with me. They came out of nowhere, almost knocking me off my feet, cruel and wanton, with no reason or intention other than to smother the lighter’s flame and dash my dreams against the rocks. I guess it has to do with karma. Somewhere up above, I’d like to think, at that most loathsome of moments, that whatever God or higher power keeps this world spinning was laughing. He just stopped everything he was doing, quit listening to the prayers of his faithful, and took a moment out of his ceaseless existence to laugh, and not at all the possibly comical things happening right then and right there, but only at me. Just me. Insignificant, unimportant, meaningless me. As I struck at the lighter, desperation setting in fast, my eyes went wide and my voice rose with the winds all around me, saying, "C’mon. C’mon. For fuck’s sake, c’mon." But nothing happened. And there was no explosion. Just the wail of sirens. That feeling of failure. All hope lost in one instant. I looked over my shoulder to see Jerry holding his hands in the air, dropping the gun to the ground, and tears streaming down his cheeks, three cops standing behind him, barking out commands and holding guns to his back. I heard the stampede of black shoes across the pristine pavement, and I knew they were rushing for me, but I still kept striking at that goddamn lighter. I refused to believe this was the end. That feeling, that crushing blow of defeat, it overwhelms the mind. It sends you into a frenzy. You lose all sense of yourself and who you are. Everything goes slow motion and nothing really matters. As I felt arms wrap around me from behind, a coldness grew over me and my head went dizzy. As I fell backwards, everything was blurry. I couldn’t feel any part of my body and I barely knew what was really happening. It felt like I was staring into the gates of heaven while falling into the depths of oblivion. I went limp, the world just seemed to stop spinning, and then everything just went black. Just silence, darkness, and nothing. --- The Ambrose Papers: Volume 3.Come On Pilgrim. May 2008-April 2009. "And I heard, as it were, the noise of thunder: One of the four beasts saying: 'Come and see.' And I saw. And behold, a white horse." - Revelations 6:1 and 6:2. --- The Good Ol’ Days Back in the late 90s, I’d left home and moved to San Francisco, living in the Haight-Ashbury district with some misplaced idea of the hippie legacy still going strong. It wasn’t, as times had changed and so had people, but it was still nice there, there was still something special, something undeniably dreamlike and surreal. I ran around with a group who called themselves The Merry Pranksters, raised on Beatnik poetry and free love, the misconception that peace could exist and that all you really needed was to believe. While I didn’t agree with a lot of their thoughts and ideas, they were good people, deeply rooted in the psychedelic movement and always in possession of as many high powered drugs you could care to ingest. It’s been years and I’ve forgotten most of their names, most of their faces, and most of what we did. It’s all hazy, weathered over by age and drug abuse, just a fond memory that seems a forever ago. I do remember that I was happy back then, satisfied with spending my days tripping out in some weird drug binge, surrounded by people I loved who loved me too. Of course, like all good things, it didn’t last. Between politics and the police, people either ended up in jail or skipping town. In a few cases, some people built rafts and let the messianic tides of San Francisco Bay take them out to nowhere, never to be seen again. Since I left, I haven’t been back, almost scared that things would have changed too much and taint my memories of that holy place. All the same, I can’t help but think about it from time to time, but not that often anymore. While I’ve become hazy on the facts and all the names have escaped my mind, I do remember one person; perhaps the only person who really matters. His real name was Timothy McCoy, but everyone called him Stanley for some reason, probably some long lost in joke that just stuck to him. All the same, no matter what he went by, he was a godsend, that once-in-a-lifetime special person you meet who hollows out his own little nook in your head, changing the way you think and the way you live. He seemed to breathe electricity, his voice and words capable of striking sparks and setting the whole world on fire. He existed in the highest echelon of human beings, coinciding with all the great minds and thinkers of this or any generation. You see, back in the late nineties, there was a culture war taking place underneath everyone’s noses, between the young and poor and the old and rich. We all drew lines and took sides, setting up our camps and digging out or trenches. It wasn’t an open battle, just a war of words and ways to live. Exact opposites can only coincide alongside each other for so long before a calm, steady hatred sets in and both sides start to argue back and forth. While we were sitting around in opium dens, dropping acid and taking pills just to get by, they were having rich and whimsical in their mansions, chocked full of debasement and enough cocaine to get half the northern hemisphere higher than a kite. There wasn’t any sort of jealousy or envy involved, we didn’t want that life and they didn’t want ours. We simply didn’t get along. Two different lifestyles tied together by a common love for drugs, danger, and destructive behavior. The thing that made Stanley that separated him from the rest of us was the fact that he managed to bridge a gap between the two sides, capable of moving back and forth between us without anyone so much as raising an eyebrow. We called him our most important ‘spy’. We’d sent him on reconnaissance missions, parachuting acid into their cocktail parties only to come back with new information, little tidbits and secrets that nobody was supposed to know. For example, did you know Willie Brown, the then mayor of San Francisco, had a deep rooted love for amphetamines and black tar heroin? Neither did we, until he told us. Nobody else knows either, it was never covered by the media and it never came out to the public, but Stanley knew, so we knew, and that was all that really mattered. Stanley lived on a whole other level, far removed from the rest of us. He held more intellectual fortitude than I thought possible, but he went about it all humble, as if he wasn’t seeking to impress or even to be well known, but only to live and to be loved by his peers. He didn’t just change minds; he changed lives, and did so daily. His name has become legend now, though it’s steeped in part myth and part rumor, with very little truth in between. The sad thing is, once the rest of us left, he stayed there only to find out we were irreplaceable. He could have started living the good life twenty-four-seven, going to fancy mansion parties and becoming just another yuppie by the bay, but he couldn’t live happily surrounded by those vultures, with their Gestapo mindset and exclusivity. He simply couldn’t accept that, and I can’t really blame him. In the end, he became just another silver bullet suicide. He died alone and left behind no suicide note, just a blood stain on the wall and a crooked grin on his face. The point of this whole story isn’t just me dwelling on the past, though. What I’m trying to say is, even the people who matter most to you, all those legends and heroes who have shaped your life and molded you into the person you are, even they’re just mortal men in a depraved world that has a strange tendency of setting you up for failure, heartbreak, and ultimately, an irreversible depression that could very well be the death of you. So, you have to wonder why we even bother. If all of our heroes can’t compete with the way the world works, how are we expected to make any sort of difference? What makes us so special? So swallow your pride, fight back those tears, and just accept the fact that, in the end, your whole life amounts to nothing. God must have a really sick sense of humor. --- The Lonely Desert I woke up in a fever, my eyes cracking open to look up and see the moon and the stars hanging overhead. I had a weird feeling in the pit of my stomach, that terrible sort of nausea you can’t have unless you’ve been through a serious, multi-day drug binge. It wasn’t really a hangover, but more of a sudden realization that you’re sober, and you hate it, and you wish you’d taken more drugs while you had them. As I sat up, I felt dusty dirt beneath me. As I widened my eyes and looked around, I saw all the things you’d expect to see in a desert. There were cactuses, there was dead plantation, there were even tumbleweeds blowing in the distance. As I looked around, more confused than concerned about my well being, I realized I had no idea where I was or how I had gotten there. Jerry was gone and I was alone with my last conscious memory consisting of being dragged away from the Alamo by a group of cops after attempting to blow the place up. My watch was missing from my wrist, so I had no idea what time it was or how long I’d been out of it. Judging from my condition, however, something strange had happened. The thought crossed my mind that maybe I died and this was my Hell. Maybe, just maybe, in a fit of rage and restlessness after my disappointing failure, I’d grabbed one of the officer’s guns and let that thing sing sweet mercy against my head. Maybe, but probably not. My lips tasted like dried blood. I could tell I had a black eye and I assumed it was swollen due to the throbbing pain pulsing from it. I was stripped down to my boxer shorts, all my other clothes gone missing, save a pair of cowboy boots that didn’t belong to me and a large American flag I had wrapped around me like a blanket. One of the lenses was knocked out of my sunglasses, causing everything around me to have a weird bright-dark effect. My chin had grown rough with stubble while the rest of me seemed like it had been dipped in a pool of sweat. Thankfully, I spotted my pack of cigarettes a few steps away, along with a lighter. I still had some luck left. Looking around, the desert seemed endless, like some never ending purgatory left for the most wretched of men. It was cold and the wind came whipping across the sand. I didn’t see so much as a road anywhere in my general vicinity. With a heavy sigh, I pulled the flag around me tighter and started to walk, my legs as wobbly as gelatin and every joint aching in some righteous fury. As I walked, step by uneasy step, I assumed I was still in Texas, probably somewhere outside of San Antonio. But I couldn’t help but notice that the dirt looked different, thicker and slightly redder when compared to the typical Texas dust. There were more hills than I generally remembered too, standing up tall in the distance. Scratching my head and pushing on, my teeth chattered and that cruel chill crawled its way up my back. The moon was full in the sky, brighter than I’d ever seen it. In a way, despite the cold, this was all almost nice, somehow soothing to be the only living soul for what seemed like miles and miles. It was rare to find that kind of solitude. Isolation in America is harder to pull off than one might expect, unless you’re satisfied with living your life in the middle of nowhere, living off the land and generally considered a madman, which was a label I caught enough of as it was. "This is what you get for selling your soul to the devil," I muttered to myself. "You should have known, you selfish bastard. God’s a dick when it comes to punishment." The worst part of it all wasn’t that I was in the middle of nowhere; that, I didn’t mind. It wasn’t even the walking or the cold winds blowing. The worst part, the only part that really bothered me, was being left alone with my thoughts. I kept replaying what had happened at the Alamo in my head, how things had come so close only to be ripped away from me at the very last second. I felt weighed down by the heavy hand of disgruntlement and my own chagrin. I couldn’t understand why or how it happened, but it did and I was going to just have to learn to accept it, I supposed. No burying the American Dream. No going down in the history books. No fame, no fortune, no revolution. I had failed in every aspect of what I had set out to do, and that was that. With my head hung low, I suddenly a sound growing up over the hill in front of me. At first, I assumed it was just that constant pounding in my head, but as it grew and grew, I realized it was something else. It sounded like… drums. With a certain sort of curiosity setting in, I began to climb the hill, hearing the drums grow louder, along with a chanting that began to ascent as well. As I got closer and closer to the very top, I noticed a flickering orange light pulsing just over the hump, shining bright in that savage darkness. As I reached the peak, looking over the hill and down into the valley below, I saw something straight out of a dream. Bizarre and surreal, as unexpected as it was shocking, I had managed to stumble across what appeared to be a modern day desert tribe; people who had forsaken their former city lives and made their way out to nowhere, living communally in peace and harmony, disassociated with the rest of the country. Possibly a cult, but the likelihood of that was rare at best. There seemed to be at least thirty people, gathered around a large bonfire, most of the women dancing around it in some weird drugged out frenzy. The men, for the most part, sat around playing instruments or watching happily, talking amongst themselves, probably planning on which woman they’d be taking back to their tent that night. Tilting my head to the side, I threw caution to the wind and started walking down the hill toward them, not to interrupt their celebration by any means, but to ask for directions or even a slight idea of where I actually was. As I walked toward them, they seemed to pay me no mind, only ignore me and continue their singing and dancing. As I walked past them, through their crowds, it was as if they couldn’t even see me. For a moment, the idea that maybe this was one mass hallucination crossed my mind, but I didn’t think it was. At least, I hoped it wasn’t. When one finally seemed to notice me, he simply smiled and lifted his beer, nodding his head at me. Raising an eyebrow, I approached him cautiously. This was a strange place and these people couldn’t be trusted, despite their seemingly innocent endeavors. The desert was the last known home of the Manson Family, after all. "How’s it going?" I asked, putting on my best smile. I didn’t think he would mind my appearance, despite how bizarre it was. He had dreadlocks halfway down his back, wearing a kilt and staring with wide eyes, occasionally twitching his head sideways. "Uh, not to disturb you guys or anything, but uh, well, long story short, I have no idea where I am. Is San Antonio anywhere around here?" "San Antonio?" He asked, seeming almost like he was about to go into a fit of laughter. "Oh man, you’re about ten hours west of there. You’re in Nevada, man. About 5 miles from Las Vegas, actually." What? I paused and cocked an eyebrow, my jaw dropping open. How in the hell did I get to Nevada? Why in the hell was I in Nevada? "Wait, wait. Stop. What is today?" "June 1st, bro. You must be really out of it. Have a seat, take a load off, all that good stuff." "…June 1st…?" It had been four days since the Alamo incident. Four days since I passed out. Four days that I didn’t remember. I assumed that I had gotten blitzed out of my mind on drugs, lost in the some mindfuck utopia that only just came out of. But the question remained, how didn’t I get arrested? And, once again, what the hell was I doing in Nevada? My world was spinning. Even without all that surrealism around me, I felt like I was going out of my mind. Slowly, I sat down, scratching my chin and trying to figure things out. As the hippies danced and the band played on, I wondered, what next? --- Sin City After a useless hour of sitting with these hippies, ingesting as many multicolored drugs as I could, and occasionally considering just saying fuck it and staying with them, I bid my adieu with my heart set on Las Vegas. There was something there, though I wasn’t quite sure what it was. It was just a feeling I had, some weird hunch. The hippies had given me clothes to wear, but I still looked like a complete wreck. It didn’t matter though, I supposed. Just another freak on the strip. No big deal. I walked through the desert until I saw those towering buildings in the distance, all covered in bright lights and that warm neon glow. It was an elaborate and stunning sight, but I knew in my heart of hearts that it was just another burial ground for the American Dream. Nodding my head to myself and sliding my hands into my pocket, I began the final trek across the desert, walking along the interstate into Hell itself… Usually, in most sensible situations, Las Vegas would be the last place I would go. It was all fake, a plastic city built by plastic people. All those elaborate shows, those towering hotels, that bleak casino glow, it all just perplexed me. Sure, it was an impressive sensory overload, but what drives a person to drive cross country to go there? I didn’t understand and I didn’t really care. Even if I did, it was all beside the point; I had no choice. This was my only place to go, my strange and terrible last resort. Thirty minutes later, I was walking down the sidewalk on the Sunset Strip, catching wide eyed stares and sending people into fits of laughter. Las Vegas was a town of beauty and elegance, or so it liked to think. If you didn’t fall in the same categories as everyone else, then you were either a sideshow freak or a complete social outcast; both in my case. All the bright lights and wall-to-wall advertising made me squint my eyes. I could barely see where I was going, all the colors mixing together and blurring. I had no idea where I was going or what I was even doing, but if worse came to worse, there’s always an empty alley you can sleep in or at least desperate mid-western housewife spending her honeymoon alone because her husband won’t leave the casinos; she’ll usually let you catch a few hours of sleep as long as you fuck her well enough. Las Vegas was the only place in this country that you could burn an American flag and people would think it was some new form of high performance art. Usually, they’d even clap and cheer you on. It’s also the only place where you can see a polar bear fucking a donkey while a man teabags a picture of Jesus, but that too remains high performance art in the minds of these people. Las Vegas takes away all sanity, all perception of how you think the world should be. Lose all morals, forget all your convictions, and fall apart with the rest of us. Even I can appreciate that, despite my displeasure with the place. I kept having this overwhelming urge to hang my head and let out that common sigh of disappointment. It happened in waves, coming and going as it pleased. Nothing seemed to trigger it, it just seemed to pop up every time I let myself become undistracted. I kept wondering what I was supposed to do now. I was out of money, half a country away from home, probably wanted in Texas, and with absolutely no idea what to do with my life anymore. I had become an old bull in a young man’s bullfight, getting stabbed repeatedly, time and time again, trying my best to build up the strength to gore the matador. That strength is elusive, though. It evades every attempt and opportunity I see. Instead, I should just accept that I’m dying. I should lay down and give in to that slow, painful death… but instead, I just keep on trying. Keep on pushing forward. Keep on hoping that something will change. I had sold my soul to the devil for fame, and this was the best he could do for me? Where were my fortunes? Why wasn’t I surrounded by beautiful women? Why wasn’t I a household name yet? It stung. It burnt. I felt betrayed, but then again, I suppose a betrayal by Lucifer is a bit more acceptable than a betrayal by your wife or your best friend. At least with the devil, you know it might possibly happen. All the same, I always thought he would be a man of his word. That’s how the legends described it, anyway. If he made a deal, he kept it, even if it went against his wishes. I guess new times had called for new policies to be implemented. Now he was just another salesman, making promises he couldn’t keep just to make sells to keep up with his quota. With all these wicked thoughts in my head, there was really not that much room for anything else. Things like where Jerry was didn’t concern me right then. I was too strung out in my own self-loathing, being just as selfish as everyone said I was. In all honesty, Jerry was in the back of my mind. I can’t say I’m the best friend a person can have, but I guess the list of flaws is ever growing, isn’t it? At that most self-deprecating of moments, falling deeper and deeper into the depths of depression, something happened. Something unexpected. Something that made a sudden impact on me for no reason whatsoever. As I lifted my eyes up from the sidewalk, taking in a deep breath, I stopped to smoke a cigarette and mop up my mortal remains. As I took heavy drags, feeling that ethereal burn in the back of my throat, I looked up at the massive building across the street. It seemed to stretch up into the heavens, beyond the clouds and beyond the reach of space. But it wasn’t the sheer height of the building I was interested in. Honestly, it didn’t impress me at all. Hanging from the side of the building, stretching out over the street for everyone to see, there was the run-of-the-mill advertisement board. It was huge and proud, an ungodly behemoth that stood as a monument to just how controlled by media America had really become. But all the same, there was something aesthetically pleasing about it, something that took me by the shoulders and shook me to my bones, lighting a spark in my head that quickly spread to a raging inferno. It was a picture of a wrestling ring, a lone fighter standing in the middle with a spotlight shining down on him. He was proud of himself and who he was. He was the very vision of godliness, some holy ruler disguised in the flesh of a mortal man. Underneath him, the sign read in bold lettering: SIN CITY CHAMPIONSHIP WRESTLING: GLORY. It was simple and should have been underwhelming. Just some wrestling company slinging around the word ‘glory’, implying that they had something special that the competitor didn’t, and generally lying to everyone. However, there was something about it, something that struck me and lit a lightbulb over my head. As if the devil himself was whispering in my ear and guiding my way, I realized what I needed to do in that one polarizing moment. Tossing my cigarette butte to the ground and smiling contently to myself, I slid my hands in my pocket and began to walk once more. Fame. Fortune. The American Dream. Something told me SCCW could cover that for me. --- Parting Is Such Sweet Sorrow A couple months passed by. I had went through a few training classes, managed to get a hold of a few people in corporate office, and I had signed a year-long contract with Sin City Championship Wrestling. A lot of people who knew who had to ask themselves how a drug addicted loser with no formal training in fighting managed to get signed to a well renowned and well known wrestling company. Well, the devil works in mysterious ways, doesn’t he? If that explanation doesn’t strike your fancy, just go ahead and blame luck. It was January 2009, just a few brief months away from my debut. The last few months had been a whirlwind rush, everything happening fast. I had gotten a lot of things in my life straightened out – drug use notwithstanding – but something untouched remained in my mind. Something important that I should have done a lot sooner. As I stepped out of my car, lighting a cigarette, an unwarranted nervousness set in. I took slow, uneasy steps up the driveway, trying to be as quiet as a mouse just in case I needed to run. As I stood at the doorway, I took a deep breath. Butterflies were going crazy in my stomach. All my nerves of steel came undone. With an awkward little hesitation, I slowly pressed my finger down on the doorbell and waited, a lump in my throat and a cat holding my tongue. I heard footsteps on the other side, faint against carpet. A familiar bellowing voice shouted out ‘Don’t worry, honey! I’ve got it!’. Taking one last deep breath, I watched the door open in slow motion… and there stood Jerry, smiling until he looked up to see who I was. His whole face contorted. He went wide eyed, his jaw dropping. As he reared his fists back, I covered my face and waited for the blows to strike me… only to feel two fat arms wrap around me and a head on my chest saying, "Victor! Holy mother… How is this possible?! We all thought you were dead!" Trying to push him away, I patted him on the back and began to swat at him, muttering, "Yeah, yeah. I keep getting that." He saw me in and we sat down in his living room, which had become some sort of religious temple in my absence. Religious objects were strewn all over the place, from crucifixes to pictures of the Virgin Mary. We caught up on what had happened while I was gone. He had become a preacher like he said he would and he couldn’t help but laugh when I told him I was going to be a wrestler. He had gotten off drugs and joined normal society, trying to get as far away from his past as possible. He did a brief stint in jail over the Alamo fiasco, but nothing too serious. As far as how I had escaped, he had no explanation for me either. He expected them to haul me in a few minutes after him, but I just never showed up. Once again, I guess you can blame the devil for that one. As night fell and those few brief minutes I had planned to stay turned into hour stacked on hour, I decided to hit the heart of the matter, the whole reason I had come here to begin with. Licking my lips and taking a deep breath, twiddling my thumbs as that uncharacteristic nervousness reared its ugly head once again, my voice came out barely above a whisper. I told Jerry, "I… the real reason I came here, Jerry is… aw, fuck it, I’ve never been good at this sort of thing…. I just… well, I wanted to apologize. I know I made a lot of mistakes. I know I dragged you down with me. I know I’m selfish and I’ve never really been as good a friend to you as you’ve been to me, but… yeah. If I’m ever around these parts again, maybe we can go get a drink together or something, eh?" "No," He said, hanging his head. I was slightly taken aback, but then I noticed a sly grin spreading across his lips. "I don’t drink." With that typical cocky wink, we both laughed together for the one millionth time that night. He sighed and leaned back in his recliner, letting out a heavy sigh of contentment. "You know, Victor, if you were anyone else, I’d probably just tell you to get out of my house and never even think about coming back… but since you went to the trouble of rising from the dead to come make amends, I guess I can accept that. All water under the bridge, Vic. You’re welcome here anytime." We talked into the night, about the past and the future and all of our plans. We both realized how much we had changed and we both realized there was now a gap in our friendship, but something told us both that it was all going to be okay. Something said it would all work out. Something told us it wouldn’t be the last time Victor Ambrose and Jeremiah Garcia would got themselves into some trouble. He would have said it was God telling him. Me, I like to just call it intuition. As we both stood to leave, I extended my hand to shake his, but he pulled me in for a tight hug. He told me if he ever needed anything to give him a call. I did the same for him, and I honestly meant it. We said our goodbyes and he led me to the door. And for once, even if I’m the most cynical person in the world, everything seemed right. --- Glory Three weeks. Three was all it took. I found myself skyrocketing through the ranks of the SCCW elite. I was climbing up the ladder quicker than anyone. Pushing myself through the No Limit tournament was one thing, but all of a sudden, I was in the main event match with a shot at the Universal Championship. Suddenly, I was being recognized in the streets. Corporations and big businesses started calling me, hoping to get strike a sponsorship deal. Suddenly, I was famous, and I had to admit, it felt good. As I walked through the locker rooms, an undeniable pride hung over me. As I climbed up the steel steps, coming to stand behind the curtains that would lead out to the rampway that would lead down to the ring that would lead me to glory, I air felt electric. I could hear the fans cheering just a few feet away, chanting my name. I could feel every single drop of sweat pouring down my forehead. As the arena darkened and I heard the first few opening notes to my theme song, I closed my eyes and time seemed to freeze for a moment. Everything flashed through my head in one whirlwind motion. Everything spun and echoed endlessly, reminding me of where I had been and what I had been through. And then I opened my eyes and I stepped through the curtain. As I stood at the top of the ramp, looking out at the audience and only seconds away from the most important match I’d probably ever be in, I took a deep breath and muttered to myself the only thing I could think of to say. "Let the good times roll."
