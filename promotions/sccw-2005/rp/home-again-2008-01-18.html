Home Again.

18 January 2008

Date: 01/18/08 Finally. It had felt like years since he first won the opportunity to face off against Jason Cruise for the LiveWire title, because in the amount of time that he had, so much had happened on and off-camera that every day felt like a new year, and every new year felt like a rebirth. It was a constant struggle to find himself, while maintaining some semblance of a professional life in the process. He felt on the cusp of something great. His sessions has been improving dramatically, and with every day he came closer. In the ring, he had waged wars, and constantly showed improvement in his performance and marketability. He had made a "to-do" list, of sorts, and after every Uproar, he crossed something else off that list. Paul Cain had been dismissed when he didn't have a lead pipe as a tag team partner, and he quickly disappeared after his lackluster showing at the Young Lion's Cup. Duncan Drake had been consistently inconsistent, and after this week, Elliott would focus on ending their spat for good. But for now, his mind had to focus on one thing, and that's winning what he had never won before, what he had never even had the opportunity to win before, and that's a title belt. It was a shame that he had to take it from someone like Jason Cruise, but that's the way the business goes sometime, and he would do his best to make it known that it was nothing personal. .you need to go home again. "I don't need to do anything." In his mind, that was the last thing he needed. When Margaret Thapes suggested that Elliott return home, they were sitting in her office, which he was becoming well-accustomed to. By now, every corner of the room, every artifact had been examined multiple times, and if he were to go blind he would still be able to maneuver the room effortlessly, which is what he was doing now. He avoided the couch as he paced, tugging at his black button-down shirt, his tan slacks choking his waist. He unbuttoned a few buttons on his shirt, revealing a white Bad Brains tee shirt underneath. Even his image consultant couldn't break him from old habits, nor could the woman sitting behind her desk, watching as he silently stalked his shadow. "Did you hear me?" she asked sternly, still a little hung up on the outfit he was wearing. He nodded, biting at the skin at the corner of his fingernails. For some reason, for him, the word 'home' didn't carry the comforting thoughts commonly associated with it. "What are you thinking?" she asked. One step forward, three steps back. "I'm thinking that's a bad idea," he said softly, still pacing. She watched him as if she were front row at a tennis match. "Why is that a bad idea?" she inquired, trying to get something she could consider a reasonable answer. "What good would it do?" he asked. "What harm would it do?" she responded. "Don't answer my question with a question, please," he said, and then paused for a moment. He turned to her, but once his eyes made contact with hers, he immediately began pacing again, his eyes returning to the floor. She stood from behind her desk, and while he walked towards the wall, she positioned herself behind him. He swung around, and was met chest-to-face with the woman who had been trying for years to help him. "You need closure," she said. He tried walking past her, but she mirrored his moves. "You can't find that in an office or a different state or even a different country. You can only find it in Pittsburgh." He grabbed her by the shoulders and lifted her effortlessly out of his way, continuing his pace. "I was just there," he said, his back to her. "I didn't find shit." "You weren't looking," she said. "You went home because your father died, and you didn't even handle that well. The only thing you gained from that trip was a train stub and a cold." "And that's all I'd get this time," he said as he turned around. She looked at him, her jaw slightly agape, and her hands at her hips. "Why are you so fucking stubborn?" she snorted. She caught herself off-guard, and held a hand to her mouth when she realized what she said. He stopped in his tracks, and had a look on his face similar to the one he would have if Mother Teresa had called him a cunt. "I'm sorry," she said immediately. She dropped her shoulders and made a beeline towards her desk chair, her eyes never leaving the floor. Elliott just smirked, and then cocked an eyebrow. He was silent, but not because he didn't want to talk, but because he didn't know what to say. .you can't go home again. William "Slick Willy" McDormant was selfish. That's why, when his client posed the idea that maybe he should return to Pittsburgh before his match, the answer he gave was a resounding 'no.' He had been out there with him at the last Uproar, he heard the ovation they gave him when he entered the ring, and the buzz that he created when he spiked Ray Pierce on his head. The last thing he needed was his client traveling cross-country days before what was the biggest match he's had in FUSE, and quite arguably the biggest match of his eight-year career. "Thapes said it would be good for me," Elliott said, as they browsed the new release section of the video store. Over the past few weeks they had actually become buddy-buddy of sorts, but never in his life did Elliott think they would be renting movies together. "She also said 'fuck,'" McDormant retorted. "She wasn't thinking clearly. She's probably on drugs." McDormant flipped over the case of what appeared to be a romantic comedy, and then flung it back at the shelf when he saw there was no nudity in it. "Does she realize how important this match is?" McDormant continued, as he followed a few steps behind Rollins. Rollins turned around to him now, and looked at McDormant with furrowed brows. "Do you realize that there's more to life than wrestling?" Rollins retorted. McDormant threw his hands up in the air and shook his head. "Bullshit there is," he exclaimed. "If you're a wrestler, you're life is wrestling. I don't care if your mom's dead, your dad's dead, your dog's dead, you've got the HIV, you've gone blind, AND-OR you have a malignant tumor the size of a grapefruit, all that shit can wait." He paused, took in a quick, sharp breath, and continued. "Title shots don't come around every fucking day, dude, unless your name is Sean Sterling or BloodAngel, explain that one to me." He cocks an eyebrow. "Other than that, if you don't take the opportunity when it's presented, you might as well plunge yourself asshole-first into a prison shower stall, 'cause you just got dicked." He neglected the fact that they were standing in front of Shrek the Third and he should be mindful of his language. Once he started, there was no stopping him. "And lastly," McDormant persisted, "even if you make it back in time for your match, you'll probably be drained from not being able to sleep in that hobo shuttle they call mass transportation, and you need to be on top of your game if you want to beat a guy like Jason Cruise." "Are you done?" Elliott asked. "For now," McDormant responded, red in the face. "Good," Elliott said, "because I didn't even say I was going. I was just mentioning that it was a possibility I may be going. The last thing I want to do is venture up there, but, and this is meant as no offense to you, I'd be more willing to take her advise than yours." "Fuck off," McDormant said, then continued to walk the shelves of lackluster cinema Hollywood put out these days. "Everything I've done for you over the past month or however long it's been has been spot-on, genius management on my part. You're a fucking stud now, and I'm not laying claim to all of it, you're responsible for most of it, but I helped get you there. Why would you take her advise over mine?" "Seniority?" Elliott said, although it sounded more like a question. "Look, I've known her for years, and it seems like just now things are starting to run smoothly between us, and we're making real progress. I don't want to mess that up." "Are you married to her? Jesus Christ," McDormant snapped. "And what's this 'progress' bullshit?" He scoffed as he walked away, his eyes returning to the shelves. "You want to talk progress, this is it. I mean, it's not every day I browse Blockbuster with someone who doesn't have a vagina. Unconfirmed, of course, but I'll take your word for it." Elliott smirked. "I'm flattered." "All I'm saying is..." McDormant paused as he stopped once again, and turned to Elliott. "All I'm saying is I don't think it's a good idea. I don't think it's a good idea at all." .you should go home again. "I'm sorry," she repeated, as her eyes still roamed the carpet. "You don't have to apologize," Elliott confirmed. "I'm actually impressed." She sat back down in her chair, and then looked up. She was slightly embarrassed, but it had only been because the man sitting across from her now had pushed her to a point that she rarely allowed herself to reach. "Impressed?" She looked at him, and he nodded. "Impressed with what, may I ask? The fact that I've been able to maintain my composure for this long without one of these sudden outbursts?" "No," he said, shaking his head. "The fact that you can actually show some emotion every now and then." He didn't think much of it, but in her mind this was a great offense. "Well you really gave me no choice," she said, pushing her glasses up further on her nose. "You've been doing so well, you've been making so much progress, and you come in here today and it's like you're the same man you were three months ago. I just can't understand, for the life of me, why you're so resistant to change. To growth." Elliott smirked, which was not the reaction she had hoped for. "What do you want me to say?" he asked through a dry smile. "I am how I am, you're the one who's supposed to figure out why that is." She sighed. "Unfortunately, I can't do that," she said, her eyes looking down at her desk. He looked at her, unsure of what she meant. "This will be our last session," she explained. "You're going to have to find another doctor to treat you." It was hard to tell with the way the desk lamp caused a glare on her glasses, but if you looked at just the right angle, you could see tears forming in the corners of her eyes. Tears that she quickly wiped away, before Elliott could notice. "I'm taking a leave of absence," she continued. "I'm not sure for how long. It may be permanent. All I know is that I can't do this anymore." Elliott stopped his pace, and sat down on the couch across from her. "Do what?" he asked, a genuine look of sympathy on his face. "This profession," she sighed. "I've lost too many battles." He was silent for a moment. They both were. "I'm just..." she hesitated. "I'm tired, Elliott. I'm exhausted. Too exhausted to fight anymore." She looked at him, and he looked at her. "I want you to know," she continued, "that even though I may have been harsh, or too honest, everything I did or said was for your benefit. Everything I've tried to do for you was for your own good." He nodded. "I can see it in you," she said, and half-smiled. "I know what you're capable of. Sure, there are hurdles in the road, but you are capable of so much if you just open yourself up to the possibilities life has to offer." "I have faith in you," she said. .home again. He had neglected his managers advice. The bus trip was an exhausting one, longer than he remembered from last time. A road trip from Orlando to Pittsburgh, including stops, was about sixteen hours all-together. Sixteen hours of silence, the only sound coming from the hum of the diesel engine as it roared down I-95. For the first time, he had tried to hold a conversation with someone on the bus. She was a frail, emaciated old lady with hands so brittle he feared her fingers would snap when he shook it. She had spoken to him about her grandchildren, her late husband, and her former profession, until she fell asleep about a half-hour in to the trip. That left Elliott with fifteen and a half hours of nothing to do. When the bus finally pulled in to the terminal in Pittsburgh, he stood on rubbery legs. It had been dark when they left early that morning, and it was dark when they arrived late that evening. When he walked down the steps of the rickety bus, he spotted Hannah Rose standing there. He had forgotten he had called her, which made seeing her that much more pleasant. He smiled immediately, and she smiled back, her hair whipping in the harsh January wind. She stood there in a black overcoat that hung to her knees, a dark navy skirt, and red leggings that tucked their way in to tan boots that climbed to her shins. She had an eclectic style that Elliott missed even more now. "Hi," he said sheepishly as he approached. She nodded to him, but said nothing. He had only packed for a few days, in the blue duffel bag that he had slung over his shoulders many times before. They walked to her car in silence, neither saying anything. When they reached it, he threw his duffel bag in the back seat and slumped down in the passenger's seat. It wasn't long before he was asleep, and she navigated the confusing Pittsburgh streets with ease, occasionally looking over at him with a smile. He caught a smile out of the corner of his eye as he started to drift -- "Elliott?" she whispered. He snapped up quickly, nearly strangling himself in the seat belt. She laughed quietly as he did his best to orient himself as quickly as he could. He rubbed the sleep from his eyes, and then looked out the window at a small apartment complex he had been unfamiliar with. "Where are we?" he choked out, his throat dry. "We're at my apartment," she said softly. He nodded, and stretched his arms above his head as far as the ceiling of the Ford Taurus would allow. "You're staying here, right?" she confirmed. He nodded. .the day after. They had spoken very little that night. They didn't need to say anything. She invited him in to her bed, where he quickly fell in to a deep sleep once again, his arm wrapped around her and her head tucked in to the curve of his arm. When they awoke, they did their usual routine as if the two of them spending the night together was a common thing. When they were ready, they made their way to the cemetery. It was exactly how he remembered it, except now there was another headstone next to his mother's. Even though they had separated, his father and mother had remained close. So close, in fact, that they still decided to be buried together. One day, he would be laying between them. He rubbed his fingers through the grass growing in front of his mothers grave. Hannah hung back, watching him from a distance. When she felt she had given him adequate time, she joined at his side. She looked at the graves somberly, her eyes beginning to tear up. "You okay?" she asked, wrapping her arms around his waist and putting her head to his shoulder. He nodded. "How do you feel?" He smiled at the irony, as even six states away he was being asked that question. "I feel fine," he said, his smile widening. "For the first time in a long time." They sat there for awhile longer. Elliott felt no remorse, no sadness anymore. He wasn't sure what had inspired it. It wasn't an immediate thing, but instead a collection of everything he had been through over the past few months. He knew it as he could actually sit there and smile while looking at the graves of his mother and father. He had reached a level of acceptance that he never thought he would. Finally.
