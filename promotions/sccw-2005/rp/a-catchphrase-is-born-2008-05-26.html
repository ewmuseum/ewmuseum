A Catchphrase is Born

26 May 2008
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 5/26/08 It's times like this that I'm fully aware that I ain't no cowboy. Sure, I look the part. Dress the part. Hell, I can e'en talk the part purdy well sometimes. But I'd be lyin' if I said I didn't miss back East. This is Chicago. Mid-west. 'Bout halfway back to the shore I called home until the government told me I couldn't make my livin' playin' poker no more. Hell, until I signed up with Sin City, I ain't been outta Vegas in two years. Lot of things change in the world. Gas prices go up. Neighborhoods get better or worse. I thought this was gonna be a gig to get me some poker money. Give me some cash so I wouldn't be broke ass, livin' or not eatin' on the turn of a card all the time. An' then, I set up shop here, an' all Hell's done broke loose. I lost my first bout to Jay Draven, a vet. An' I was pissed, you know? Pissed I lost. Pissed I couldn't do no better on that day. Ain't here just for money no more. Here to make a livin' on somethin new. Week after, I met with the head honcho, the big cheese...or at least, he's s'posed to be. Smitty, good guy an' all, but he ain't runnin' shit. The Hand is. The Dead Man's Hand. Aces an' eights. Two pair in poker, purdy good one. In SCCW, though, it's a royal friggin' flush from the looks of it. Smitty's bowed. He's fucked up. Cain't take them out through the normal channels. Gotta go about it all sneaky like. I wonder why he bothers. Cain't help but feel like I'm bein' watched backstage. No matter where I am, there's a finger of the Hand there. Watchin' me...and I don't matter worth shit yet. I'm a dumb rook with four or five moves and a chip and a chair. An' I'm STILL bein' watched. God only knows what Lambert's goin' through. Or the New Life guy. Jesus. Gotta three way dance comin' up. Two more newcomers, but Hell! This time I'm practically the vet. Got one whole more SCCW match than they do. Gonna make all the difference in the world! Yeah fuckin' right. -- P.B.K. ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~ With a snort, Phillip Benjamin Kennedy looked over his writing one more time before he closed his journal. Shaking his head from side to side, he reproached himself, yet again, for his complete inability to master his chosen language. 'Goddamnit. Why does it have to be so hard to talk like an undereducated hick from the hills?' Seated in the relative splendor of a Chicago-area hotel room, Mr. Kennedy was less than pleased. He dressed the part, to a certain extent, the black Stetson on his head clear evidence of that. Speaking it, though, would always be difficult for him. 'I'm a guy from Maryland. I can't talk like that. Maybe I need a speech coach..' Unable to resist a quick peek, he reopened the journal, reading over the words written in a messy script, in a language he couldn't speak especially well. Damned if what he wrote wasn't true, though. Wrestling DID mean more to him than he ever thought it would, and that wasn't just after he saw what he made a week nowadays. Sure, it had only been one match, and he hadn't performed especially well, but the adrenaline of the moment had been a rush that before, only poker could provide. Practice was what he needed. And wrestling wasn't something you could really practice so much. You could train, lift weights and such, but matches were what one needed to truly get better. And that chance would come on national television. Spike TV, no less. Television for men. When he thought about it, he supposed that he was Spike's target demographic. A man in a cowboy hat who played poker, liked beer, and lusted for women. Just because he wasn't TECHNICALLY a cowboy didn't mean that he had no cowboy spirit. Taking the hat off his head, he swung it around in a circle, mimicking a lasso. 'Comin' out to that song was the best part,' he grinned to himself, replaying his first ever professional wrestling entrance in his head. Other talent had better pyro, or better reactions, but he quite liked coming out to Big & Rich for the very first time. The song, of course, had been featured on World Series of Poker broadcasts, and he'd found it to be a pretty little ditty. And it psyched him up, far more than probably most would think possible. 'Kinda sucks not being able to play, though,' he noted. That was perhaps the hardest part of being on the road: no poker. He could play online, of course...but with the government cracking down on real wagering, all you had were idiots who'd go all in every hand. With the money had gone the skill, for the most part, to real card rooms. Of course, they ALSO had their fish every now and then, something Kennedy was more than grateful for. A single fish could be milked for enough to keep him going for a week or more, back in the old days. There was something to be said for stability though. As much adrenaline coursed through your system when your poker performance determined where you stayed and what you ate...the worry that such a life brings makes things difficult. 'And then, there's the Hand,' he mused, a sick smile on his face. The journal, again, was absolutely accurate. They were frightening in their power, in the authority that they seemed to wield over the promotion. They were not, though, the primary focus. Simon King and Tina Davis were. Two newcomers. Two people who would soon learn what they had stepped into, the veritable hornet's nest that was SCCW. 'I ain't no wrestler,' he began, trying to get the drawl back in his voice. 'I'm a fighter. That's what ah do in the ring. Ah fight for my paycheck. Simon, Tina, ya two wanna stand between me an' the winnah's share?' Despair at not knowing quite WHAT accent he was speaking faded away as conviction replaced it, a smirk curling the corner of the former Maryland native's mouth. 'When it comes to this match, kids...I'm holdin' the nuts.' He blinked, and then smiled. 'Not bad,' he had to admit. 'The phrase at least. Maybe I should use that.' And so was born a catchphrase. Not a good catchphrase, really. But not a bad one either. It was a perfectly mediocre catchphrase in the grand compendium of them. For a newbie, that has to count for something.
