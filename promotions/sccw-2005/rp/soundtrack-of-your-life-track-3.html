Date: April 28/29 "Pain" by Three Days Grace (One-X, 2006) April 28, 2009 As the car came to a rolling stop, I hadn’t paid attention enough to know that it had reached its destination. It took me a moment to notice the driver’s darting glare through the rear view mirror to get the hint. I still couldn’t reconcile that I was no longer hearing the noise of seven thousand, and was instead listening to the latest from Britney Spears. So when that hit me, I glanced back at the man at the helm and nodded my head, while he shook his. "This is the part where you give me the money." With that one line he managed to make me feel like a five year old, so like one I stuck out my tongue as I routed through my pocket. It was much easier to get to that damn wallet was I finally undid the seatbelt. I squinted my eyes to see the meter reading $22.17, and then pulled out some cash and handed it over to him. The rear door swung open, and I stretched my poor legs. My feet planted on the concrete sidewalk, I raised to a standing position, a little more gingerly that normal. The cool night breeze passed through my short hair and made each lock dance a dance. In that moment, as my lungs filled with the type of air that is cool enough to burn your chest and make you feel alive, I thought that this is the rush a smoker must get. I patted down a few pockets looking for a light before I realised I don’t smoke. "Hey, thanks a lot mister!" the cabbie bellowed from his yellow chariot. He held in his hand the cash I had left with him to compensate for my transport right next to the cheshire cat grin. He had already started halfway down the block before I would clue in to why the man was so elated with his latest fare. Damn this money you use. This silly money where every bill is coloured the same so that in the twilight a man cannot make distinction between a twenty and a fifty. I made some man’s night all because of an American minimalist approach to colour, and some silly statute that because I was not yet twenty five, I was some how not responsible enough for a rental. I would have loved to spend the night focused on these minor grievances if my mind was not wiped clear of any other thought than pain as I took stride toward the motel entrance. I took my lower lip between my teeth and twisted my face into a grimace. Quickly to the right foot. Short step on the left. Pain. Quickly back to the right. And so it was with this pattern that I made my way through the lobby of the Motel 6, garnering some attention from the attendant at the desk, but not enough for her to offer any support. I reached the elevator and pressed the up button. My lodging that night was only on the second floor, but I knew better than to test the stairs in the condition I found myself that night. In short moments the opening of those sliding metal doors was preceded by a pleasant "ding". The tiny shaft was filled with the annoyance that is elevator music, enough to make me wish that this short ride was one in silence, and I rarely like silence. But it was during this gentle addition of potential energy that I sorted through the events of the night to determine the source of this uncomfort, and there it was. It was those damned kicks to my leg. Although I am sure that I might have worsened it during my abrupt landing. Who put that announce table there any how? When I finally made it to the room, it was a B-line to the bed, where I let my two hundred and forty pound body fall. It was away with the pair of jeans I wore since changing from the spandex, and I hurriedly rubbed over the scars on my left leg. Those little spider-like impressions of red forever etched into my skin, the big healed over slice that ran over my knee cap. I could feel the steel rod behind my knee and I knew that the passing of the night would not be so peaceful. All the same I choose that night to forgo getting up to brush my teeth, or any such task that required me to put weight on lefty. I thought my bladder was sufficiently empty, certainly empty enough that any bodily functions might wait until day break. What I wanted in that moment was to get to sleep, and let the battle my body wages with the alien metal intruder go on while I was unconscious. I cozy’d up to that bed, that having watched enough CSI to know was more than likely covered in a filth I would under ordinary circumstances avoid. I was tired, I tried to tell myself. Just go to sleep. When I lasted checked the alarm clocked positioned next to me, in red it read: 11:13 pm. I awoke to a room still dark, untouched by morning light and a sunrise still hours away. I was hit immediately by how hot I seemed to feel. The sweat that ran down my face was an item of repugnance. I kicked at the sheets to have them off. Fuck. I stopped kicking with the left, and with my hands ripped at the heavy comforter on top of me until it was underneath. I stripped myself down to just the boxers I wore to combat the over production of heat my body had decided it was not soon going to stop. This was all before I parted my lips and found my mouth parched. I wanted back to sleep, but I knew I could not reach this state without first refuelling on the liquids that had found themselves sweat stains in this communal pillow. So I dragged myself to my feet, and I felt every millilitre of blood rush past the swelling in my left leg. I did my best to make the instances where I was on it short, and even began to hop on the good leg to my destination, that proved equally as painful. But in that mini-refrigerator I found the bottle of water I deposited earlier in the day, or previous day I would come to realise when I saw the alarm clock in red now read only: 1:55 am. But I chugged back what was left of the water, and made my way back to the bed, cursing for every step. It was a struggle to get back to sleep. I turned on the television and found that late night television sucks in South Dakota too. The warm glow of the box filled the room, and danced on the ceiling as I flicked about the stations. When I finally found a show that was not porn or an infomercial, I set the sleep timer, and enjoyed ten minutes of a Married with Children rerun. I might not have been awake for all those minutes, as I cannot recall the TV turning off. I would see the clock a few more times that night before I would see dawn. It was a restless night spent tossing and turning, trying to find a position where my leg wasn’t screaming in pain. It was not the first time I had spent a night this way, and I am sure it will not be the last. But the morning finally did come. The plane ride back home that was scheduled for 9:15 am was quite unlikely. I decided to skip that flight, as it was past noon before I was able to walk on my left leg. And it was only at noon that I found out when the maids came round to clean the rooms, and to yell at me in Spanish. I cannot speak the language, but I knew enough to guess those words were not kind. So I grabbed my belongings and high-tailed it. It was another cab ride, and this time a careful inspection of the bills I forked over. It was an afternoon spent in an airport, waiting for the next flight out to Hamilton. It was an afternoon spent reminiscing about one fateful day in 2004. ------- October 5, 2004 "Mitchell, you are doing alright out there, but if you would just hold on to that ball for a few more moments, things are opening up down field," my coach told me. I thought about yelling to him that maybe if my line could hold a block, then I wouldn’t have been getting rid of that ball so very quickly, but I held my tongue. Even with my quick release that game, the first half of the game had seen me sat down so many times I had begun to loose count. And not one of them where called for a late hit, either. I hate these referees. They hate us, that is why it is easy to hate them. The halftime intermission is winding down, and the boys are squeezing water bottles that were emptied five minutes ago, looking for a drop of refreshment. I down what is left of the lemon lime Gatorade I have and just smile to myself. I get off my left knee, and take a look at the crowd that they managed to pull together for this game. The seats are almost full. These kids will do anything to skip some school, even if it means buying out of class to watch their team get whipped by the rival school. They were the only other catholic high school in town, so they were made our defacto rival. I didn’t even know any of the names of the kids on the other bench. All I knew was that I was supposed to not like them, and after the beating that they had given me in the first half, that was becoming easier by the down. The referee sounds his whistle to tell each team that halftime is over, and the teams vacate the end zones for the benches once more. I had my helmet on and ready to go, watching our return team take their positions on the field. We had the ball first this half, and I was determined to make something happen with it. And hey, a little luck and a good return had us set up at the 40. Time to go to work. "Mitch, remember what we talked about. Alright, first play, let’s run it to the right." "Alright coach." And so I trotted out to the huddle where my offence surrounded me. It was always difficult for me to get my voice at just the right volume: Loud enough for the boys to hear, but not those Eagles. But I tell them the play with what I think is the right pitch, even though the receivers lean in to hear it, "I tight right 34 lead, I tight right 34 lead, on one, on one, break!" And the huddle breaks. The other eleven in the same silver jerseys and blue helmets walk to their places, and I saunter past the running backs, up to the line. This looks like it should work. Our running game was the only thing to give us any measure of success in the first half, and we were due to break a big one. "Down! Set! Hut!" I pull my hands back from Telfar’s ass with the ball held tight. I turn to my right to see Calder breaking open the 4 hole, and Marlo is just in time for the hand off. There is no greater sight I’ve seen than just handing off the ball and watching a running back carve up a defence for the long scamper. Unfortunately I do not get to see that this time. By the time Marlo hit the hole, it had collapsed with a pair of defenders ready and willing to drive him to the ground. Guess that play didn’t work, and we were lucky to pick up the yard we did. The scrum separates and we go to either side. "Huddle!" Telfar yells, but with his mouthpiece in anyone who hears it swears he is calling for a group "Cuddle!" The slot receiver switches, the sub coming in with the next play. I don’t know why my offensive co-ordinator had never bothered to set up hand signals. Why start halfway through the season though, I thought. I get told the play, and then I tell the team. When I hear the play, I first glance back to my bench. Nothing. So I guess this is the play, even though I don’t like it. "I tight right, motion right, screen right, I tight right, motion right, screen right, on set, on set. Ready? Break!" We get back up to the line and everyone takes their place. I kick back, and send my slot receiver in motion. When I see him cross behind me in the reflection of my center’s helmet, I start into my cadence. "Down!....Set!" The quick count catches the defence, and we are a step ahead of them. I drop back, looking at my wide outs running the flies, just trying to take the corner backs down field. I look at my running back. He is behind the half of the line that has floated over to protect him, and I dump the ball off to him. I see the ball into his hands before I get hit again, and sent to the ground. Something isn’t right about it this time. I didn’t even see the kid that hit me. All I know is that I am hurt. I begin to crawl my way off the field, before I hear the coaches yelling at me. "Mitch, stay there! We’ll come to you." I didn’t really understand the emergency in their voice, but when they do make it out to me, I answer them with a little humour. "It’s something with my leg isn’t it?" "Yeah Mitch, it is your leg," my offensive co-ordinator told me. "I don’t suppose you would let me play on one leg? Heh…" "Yeah Mitch, I think you are going to have to sit this one out," he said reaching into his pocket. He pulled the cell to his ear, having already dialled, and he starts talking to who ever was on the other end. "Hi, yeah, I am in Wayne Gretzky Field. I need an ambulance." That was one of the best parts of having half of your coaching staff as police officers. The team gathered around me and were talking to me. I tried to answer them, but I think they understood that I really wasn’t in the mood for talking. I began with a variety of curse words, but soon came to an end of the list that I knew, so I repeated a few of my favourites. But all that was short lived and rather pointless. I turned my thoughts to healing, even though I had no idea what had really just happened. Pray. That was all I had left to do in that moment while I waited for the ambulance to show up. The Our Father. Hail Mary. Glory Be. Repeat. Each time I said them, my voice was getting quieter, softer, weaker. Where the hell was this ambulance? Someone put a coat over me. I thought it was too keep me warm, but later I might think it was to cover the sight from the crowd. I had my knee bended up toward the sky, and my foot was on the ground. With the high socks that I wore as part of the team uniform, they couldn’t tell if it was a compound fracture. I overhead the word and began to panic even more than I was before. Honestly, when a cop calls an ambulance, how long does it take them to show? Answer: Thirteen minutes. I lay on that field, leg in two, for thirteen minutes. Thirteen minutes for everyone in the stands to gasp and awe, become bored with the sight of me, and then grossed out all again. But after thirteen minutes the ambulance and its attendants are on the scene. And now begins the awkward part. They tried their best to get me onto that gurney with little pain, but once gravity pulled the blood back past the break site there was nothing but pain. They got me on the gurney, and a few of the boys walk over with me. I tried to yell at them something motivational, I forget exactly what it was now, but I knew then that it sounded less like a yell and more like a scream or a screech. The shock was taking its toll on me. So I didn’t come away as cool as I wanted to, there was still that iconic pose of football; the injured player being carted off, thumbs in the air letting everyone know it is alright. Now is my time for that, but wait. Those jerks have secured my forearms down. I got the thumbs up on my hand, but I cannot put it in the air for anyone to see. What’s the point? So they load me into the back of the ambulance, and start their way off the field and to the hospital. One of my coaches has hopped into his squad car – I didn’t even know that he was alloyed to take that thing when he wasn’t on duty, but any way – and he escorts us there. By this time my eyes are starting to play tricks on me, and I am struggling just to keep them open. I was surprised to know that I was paying any attention to the path when I received my first bit of relief. A red light! We stopped for a red light! They didn’t have the lights on. They stopped for a red light! This baby wasn’t compound! We made it to the hospital in good enough time, even obeying the rules of the road. They quickly whisked me from the back of that fancy white van and into the emergency room before I could even realise that it was my mother there waiting for me in the emergency. Apparently during his calls, Kevin, my coach/cop, had called her, and here she was. Upset and supportive at once, if you can picture that. The hallmark of the entire incident for me can be summed up in one word: waiting. I waited hours for a room to open up for me. When I was given the grim options I had, choosing to opt for the surgical repair job, rather than the cast and maybe surgery should the cast not work deal, I had to wait twenty seven hours. Twenty seven hours from the time I was admitted to the Brantford General Hospital until the time a merciful aesthetician put me in la-la-land. It was two weeks after being released from the hospital until I was actually assigned a physiotherapist. It was six weeks before I took my first steps all over again. It was a lot of time spent waiting. I was conscious of every passing second of it. ----- April 29, 2009 I spent another afternoon waiting, thinking, planning. As the busy people hurried past me, going to a thousand different destinations, I could only think of home, and what it meant. Home was where I was going to go to recover. I would mend to these injures, and come back stronger than ever. I would train to get back to what I had achieved before these set backs, and what I had achieved was hearing the cheers of those fans. Hearing the cheers of thousands of people, who paid money to watch a show, is what I had. I will draw those cheers again. I will do it because it takes me away from my own head. I will do it again because it gives me a rush like nothing else I have ever felt. I will do it again in Utah. Under the bright lights and the pressure of a pay-per-view, in the middle of a dried up inland sea turned desert, in the heart of Mormon country, I will hear those cheers once more.
