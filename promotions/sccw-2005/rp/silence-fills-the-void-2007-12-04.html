Silence fills the void.

4 December 2007
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 12/4/2007 There comes a time in a man's life when home is no longer home. Pittsburgh, for Elliott Rollins, had always been home. Until now. Now, home was wherever he lay his head at night. Pittsburgh was as foreign to him as any other town he found himself in at any given time on any given day. Deja vu moments became the norm; a faint recollection as he rounded a corner and noticed a diner that looked slightly familiar, or walking past a person who he recognized, but couldn't figure out the connection. When their eyes met, there was that slight moment of recognition before both passed, neglecting even the slightest nod of acknowledgment but pondering for the next few minutes just where they had met before. That was then. "Elliott?" This is now. He paused. His eyes left the pavement and met the bright green eyes of a female much shorter than him. The straw blond hair, the defined cheekbones, and slender jawline reminded him more of a painting he had seen, not a person he had known for the majority of his youth. This young woman, however, was no painting. "Yes?" A look of confusion donned both their faces, but for two entirely separate reasons. He was unsure of how she knew him; she was unsure of how he didn't know her. She stood in front of him, chest-high, a faint smirk creeping across her face. "How have you been?" She had a habit of blinking too much. "Fine," he stated bluntly, still slightly flabbergasted. "You know who I am, right?" she asked, knowing full-well what the answer is. "I know that I should," he replied. She smiled, nodding. "It's been awhile." The moment in the conversation where everything becomes awkward and neither knows how to progress it? This would be that moment. "So," he said, "and I feel like an asshole for asking, but who are you?" He couldn't feel more uncomfortable. "Hannah," she said, extending her hand. He shook it, his eyes slightly wider than normal. "Hannah Rose," he said, his voice trailing off. .flashback. The two youths had been trapped for days. By this time, Elliott had known every intricacy of the tile ceiling above them. Every divot in the cracked tiles made faces that mocked him as he searched for them. He noticed the stain in the back left corner of the room; a water-damaged tile that bent and swelled inwards, threatening to release its collection of fluid on to the floor below. "We have to get out of here," Hannah Rose whispered in his ear. He put a hand over her mouth. "Quiet," he said, more hushed than her higher-pitched voice had allowed. "You'll wake the guards." He sat up, his eyes moving towards the steel metal door that blocked them from the outside world. "How do we get past the door?" he asked. "Just try," she responded, enthusiasm dripping from every pore. He nodded. She had always been his strength, and this time was no different. The two children stood from the concrete ground that had been their bed for the past three days and tip-toed their way to the mass of metal that caged them. As they got closer, the door began to dwarf them, their small stature no competition for the hulking steel that stood before them. Elliott put one hand on the latch, but paused. "Try," she whispered again, affirming him. And try he did. Much to his surprise, the door offered little resistance. He pulled, working the rust from the hinges, trying to remain quiet for fear of getting caught. It cackled at them, but allowed them to pass. Elliott and Hannah Rose pushed their heads through the frame of the door and around the corner, hoping to avoid their captives. She nudged him, then pointed out in to the distance. The little light of the dungeon was cast upon a single man, working rusty pipes with a wrench. A sword was donned at his hip. Elliott nodded, and they rounded the corner quickly, pushing their bodies against the wall. "We can't do it," Elliott said in a whisper. "We're going to get caught." She offered no response to his obvious pessimism. Instead, she pointed past him. To their right was a concrete stairwell leading to another steel door at the top. "We need the key," he said. She mummed him, then pushed forward. They creep up the stairwell, their small feet slapping against the cold concrete stairs. They held hands as they ascended, and when they finally reached the top step they paused, neither sure what to do. Fortunately (or unfortunately) the decision was made for them. The door swung quickly open, and there standing before them was the most hideous of creatures. The guard was short in stature, no taller than them, but plump and covered in patches of thick fur. Scars adorned both arms; battle wounds that healed incorrectly, leaving jagged etchings across the pale flesh. "What are you kids doing?" the guard grunted. Hannah Rose and Elliott stared back, their eyes wide. "You know I told you not to play in the basement," the voice said, adopting a more feminine tone. "You'll catch cold." Elliott's mother looked down at the two of them, silhouetted by sunlight behind her and a look of disapproval on her face. .flashforward. "Where'd you go?" Hannah Rose asked. "Huh?" Elliott grunted, startled to realize he was again standing on pavement outside of a convenience store. She looked at him with a befuddled grin, realizing that the man standing before him was still the same boy she used to play pretend with years before. He was constantly lost in the hedge maze of his own mind. "When you left," she retorted. "You know? Here? Where'd you go?" "Oh," he said. But that was all he said. "Your dad said something about California or Louisiana," she continued. "He wasn't certain which." "It's easy to confuse the two," he said, with a slightly dry smirk. "Well which was it?" she asked. "Neither," he responded, neglecting to give her a legitimate answer. "Oh," she said. Silence filled the void. "Do you want to get some coffee?" she asked after a few moments. .flashback. He dropped to his knees at the grave of his mother. Tragedy had followed him wherever he went. It was pouring freezing rain in Pittsburgh, but that didn't stop Elliott from mourning. He traced the headstone with his finger tips, running his index finger through the grooves of the name embedded into the stone. His tears mixed with the rain to a point where it was impossible to differentiate the two. His body shook, but he wasn't sure if it was from the near-freezing temperatures or the emotional shock his body took every time he had entered this cemetery. In the eleven years since his mother had passed away, he had only visited a handful of times. Five, to be exact. It was never meant to be disrespectful (although that's how his family viewed it) but it was just too difficult to bring himself here. Now that he was here again, that difficulty was confirmed. "She loved you, you know?" a voice said behind Elliott. He looked over his shoulder, and spotted a man standing a few feet behind him, an umbrella held over his head. The man, Elliott's stepfather David, crouched over Elliott and held the umbrella over both of them. David is an older man, in his fifties. He had never looked his age, but since Elliott had last seen him it appeared that father time was finally catching up to him. His eyes sagged, his skin wrinkled, and his hands shook. "You were everything to her," David said. Elliott nodded. The two sat in silence, simply staring at the slab of marble. "Why did you go?" David asked. "I didn't have a choice," Elliott said somberly. "Of course you did," David responded. "You could have stayed here. With me, or your father. You still have a family, you know?" He sighed. "We needed you here." "And I needed to not be here," Elliott retorted, his eyes never leaving the grave. "Did it work?" David asked. "Running away? Did that really solve anything?" "More than you know," Elliott said softly. David had been more of a father to him than his own father, and was one of the few people Elliott felt comfortable actually talking to. Now, silence filled the voids, neither sure what to say to the other. "You should have been here," David said at last. "He would have wanted you to be here." "Well I wasn't," Elliott said coldly. David just shook his head. "And now you have to bury your father after not seeing him for ten years." Elliott didn't know what to say to this. He didn't even know how to feel, or if he felt anything at all. "Come on," David said, standing. "We'll be late." Elliott took a moment, one last glimpse at his mother for he didn't know how long, and then stood at the side of his stepfather David. The two took another moment before turning away and walking towards the car. "You know, you're a hard nut to crack," David said with a slight smile as the two made their way to parking lot. "I know," Elliott said, returning the smile. .flashforward. "I went to Chicago, L.A., New York, Denver, Cleveland..." Elliott and Hannah Rose sat in a local diner. It was surprisingly busy for a Wednesday afternoon, and the commotion didn't offer the best environment for catching up. Nevertheless, that's what the two continued to do over a cup of coffee and a pack of cigarettes. "You're well-traveled," Hannah said, taking a drag from her cigarette. "I've traveled," he responded, taking a sip from his cup. "I wouldn't say I've traveled well, though." "What'd you do?" Hannah Rose said, her honest interest catching Elliott slightly off-guard. "I beat people up for money," Elliott answered, a wry smirk on his face. "Good times." She returned the grin, but wasn't sure whether or not to believe him. "Seriously?" "Well," he said, "yeah, kind of. Remember how I always said I wanted to wrestle?" "I do," she said, snubbing her cigarette out in an ashtray and then immediately grabbing another one. "Well, I did," he stated. "Do." She lit her cigarette. "I didn't think you were serious about it." "I was," he affirmed. "Still am. I actually have to catch the bus tomorrow to go to a match." "Then what are you doing here?" she asked. He was silent for awhile. "I buried my dad yesterday." Her eyes widened, and then swelled slightly. She had been as close to Elliott's family as he was, and the news hit her like a ton of bricks. "I had no idea," she said. "Neither did I," Elliott responded. "I found out a couple of days ago. I would have asked you to come to the funeral, but I didn't know if you were even around. I figured you'd be out of this place by now. I thought that was the goal." She nodded, wiping a tear from her eye discretely. "It was. Goal's change constantly, though." "It's never too late," he said. "Never," she said, smiling. They were both silent for a moment. "You should come with me," he said, showing the most enthusiasm he had displayed in quite some time. It was a pipe dream, for sure, but for some reason he got excited about the idea. He pushed himself against the table. "Come to New Orleans with me. It'll be fun, and it'll finally give you the chance to get out of here." She smiled. "I can't. I wish I could, but..." "Why not?" he asked. "Like I said, goal's change," she repeated. "And although it wasn't the plan, I have obligations here now. I can't just pack up and leave." Her pause only helped to add emphasis to the following statement. "Some of us have reasons to stay." He sighed, pushing himself back from the table, nodding solemnly. "I see." "Some time, though," she began. "Some time I'll come see you." "Well at least promise me one thing," he requested. "Anything," she responded. "Promise you'll watch the match." "Is it on TV?" she asked. "Pay-per-view," he responded, a faint smile on his face. "Wow," was all she could say. "That's a big deal." "It's an extremely big deal," he said, and although he realized this, he couldn't help but concentrate on other matters at hand. In fact, this was the first time he really thought about the match since coming back to Pittsburgh. "I promise," she said with a smile. He returned it. .flashback. "Where'd you go?" 'Huh?' He was in Dr. Thapes' office again. The room was stuffy. He couldn't breathe. The environment suffocated him to the point of him clutching at his collar, pulling it from his neck and fanning it for air. No matter how many times he entered those doors, he could never get comfortable. "What do you mean?" he asked. "You drifted off," Dr. Thapes responded. "Where'd you go?" "Nowhere," he said. "Just thinking." "Feel free to do that aloud," she stated. "It might help." Nothing had to this point. "I have to go bury my dad," he said. "I leave for Pittsburgh tomorrow." She nodded. "Why didn't you tell me before?" "I just found out." "I see," she said, pushing her glasses up on the bridge of her nose. "And how does that --" "Please don't," he interjected. "Don't ask that question." "You pay me to ask it," she confirmed. "And stop reminding me that I pay you for our session," he continued. "It makes you sound like a hooker. I know that I pay for this, I get the statements every month. Just..." "What is it?" she asked. "Just be my friend," he said meekly. It sounded pathetic to him. "Even if you have to pretend. Drop the professionalism and just talk to me like individuals." "I can't do that," she said. "Why not?" he inquired. She had never really thought about it before. "I guess..." she started, and then paused to really give it thought. "I guess if I allow it to become personal, then I bring those issues home with me. If I keep it formal, then I don't carry the weight of all of my patients on my shoulders when I leave this room." "That's not human," he replied. "Maybe not," she said back. "But if I didn't do that, it would kill me." .flashforward. As he sat on the bus to New Orleans, his mind drifted back to his session with Dr. Thapes. She did her best to separate her professional and personal life. Unfortunately, for Elliott, he couldn't differentiate between the two. He carried the baggage of his real life in to the ring, and vice versa. He knew he shouldn't, but somehow couldn't draw the line. Now, a few days after burying his father, he was asked to enter a ring against seven other people and compete in front of thousands of screaming fans. Somehow, his mind couldn't concentrate on the task at hand when there was so much else going on around him, but he had to figure out a way to make it work. If he didn't do that, it would kill him.
