The Return to Arms Chapter: - Episode II: Dawning

3 October 2007
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: Today Seymour Almasy: Advance The Return to Arms Chapter Episode II: Dawning Two weeks on the road with FUSE, and he already felt himself getting back into shape. His match with Itachi had been tougher than most expected, but Seymour was a realist. First match back…after a long layoff, if he was going to be caught off guard one on one, it would be then and there. “Survived,” he mused, wryly, seated in the backseat of a hearse, of all things. Mr. Mohammad from the Dignity Cemetery had called, and that meant that Seymour dropped everything to return back to New York. Among the perks of Almasy’s deal was that he could more or less come and go when he needed to, about the only provision he had insisted upon. Everything else had been all Smitty’s doing. “Least he’s booking me like one of the guys,” Seymour acknowledged, shaking his head. Some promoters would have booked Seymour against their top talent right off the bat. It had happened to him in Global Wrestling, and resulted in admittedly poor performances by a Seymour Almasy not adjusted to the landscape. In FUSE, to compete with the top echelon, one had to learn how to deal with the Highwaymen. With the Affliction. With the Cowboys From Hell. With Mayson Colby. Seymour admittedly wasn’t there yet, but he was scouting more and more by the week. Before he could get there though, he had presumably more weeks like the one upcoming, pitting him against three fellow newcomers in a Fatal Fourway match. In truth, it wasn’t his favorite thing on Earth. “I hate fourways,” he admitted to himself quietly as the hearse sped on down the Long Island Expressway. “Total and utter crapshoot, especially for me.” His style of offense wasn’t predicated for matches with multiple competitors. Hard enough to fly against one opponent accurately, let alone when there might be two others lurking to spoil your plans. Skill mattered, of course, but luck was a huge part in such contests. Which was part of why he wasn’t especially concerned about not being in the gym as he headed towards the supposed burial site of his wife to hear whatever news Mohammad had cobbled together from his staff. If it was to be a crapshoot, he would roll the dice and see what number came up. “We’re almost there,” the driver called, back to the man riding in his back seat. “Mr. Mohammad has found the employee who buried the casket, and after a bit of persuasion, he is willing to talk.” “Persuasion,” the Final Fantasy asked dubiously, the image of an employee being worked over in a back room slowly etching itself in his mind. “Not as sinister as I made it sound,” came the reply. “Nameed just talked to him a bit. The guy’s been a wreck inside for a year and a half, apparently, holding onto this thing. He’s happy to finally get it off his chest.” That, Seymour knew, meant that he was going to get somewhere. He had inquiries in at the bank he and Laura used, as well as the hospital she had been taken to after shooting herself. Those things, he had been told, took time. This would provide more immediate answers, and in as confusing and elaborate of a story as this seemed to be, any answers would be of utmost importance. The hearse stopped in the parking lot of the cemetery, and Almasy was out the door almost before the wheels ceased moving, breaking into a dead run towards two figures about five-hundred yards away. One he easily recognized as Nameed Mohammad. The other, Seymour presumed, was the man responsible for the burial of an empty casket. Of his ex-wife and best friend’s empty casket. To Seymour’s credit, he kept his voice down even as he stared down the unknown man, an icy touch in his gaze. “You’re the guy who buried the coffin,” Seymour asked, the question almost an accusation. The other man gave a slightly frightened nod, and Seymour’s reaction was immediate. “Talk.” “She offered me thirty grand,” the employee stammered out, clear fear now etched in his eyes. “Thirty grand to bury the casket, empty. She paid me in cash. Listen, I had to do it. I’ve got a wife and three kids, I make fifty grand a year…” “Did she tell you anything else?” Clearly, Seymour didn’t much care about the nature of the financial transaction. At least, not as much as he cared about the rest. “Not really,” was the employee’s still-nervous reply. “It wasn’t much of a conversation. She asked me to do it, showed me the cash, and I nodded my head. Only ever asked her one question.” “And that question was,” Seymour asked, a sick feeling starting to spread in the pit of his stomach. “Why you want me to do this,” he answered, as Seymour had expected. “And her answer,” Almasy choked out, in a low, hoarse whisper. The employee paused for a moment, eyes closing as if in remembrance. “She looked at me for a minute. Didn’t say nothing,” he began. “But her eyes…never seen eyes like them. They were sad. Worried. Scared. Confused.” Seymour almost swallowed his tongue, looking at the other man with terrified interest, waiting for him to continue. “And then she left. Didn’t say a damn thing to me. “ A soft whimper escaped Almasy’s throat, as he racked his mind for anything else he could ask the man. Playing detective was not his strong suit; he fancied himself a Cloud Strife rather than a Phoenix Wright. “Did she look hurt,” Seymour asked, desperately grasping at straws. She had, after all, been in the hospital. This had been confirmed to him. He’d gone there with her. Ridden in the ambulance. Spoken to the doctors. “Couldn’t tell ya,” the employee replied. “She wasn’t limpin’ or nothing like that. Arms and legs looked fine. Head and neck fine. She was wearing a pink sundress, so it was sort of hard to tell.” “Pink sundress,” he repeated, looking at the man, before pealing away to pace a rut in the cemetery grounds. That made…no immediate sense. She had been wearing her wedding dress when she shot herself in the chest, her attempt to get Seymour to take her back having gone horribly and tragically wrong. She had been taken to the hospital in it. There were no other clothes for her to change into save maybe a hospital gown. So she hadn’t gone straight to the cemetery from the hospital. She had stopped somewhere, either to buy a sundress or pick up one of the several she owned. In the latter case, she could have only gone one place. “Home,” he whispered, shaking his head, and swallowing hard. She had to have gone home. Not to his house, or the apartment. No. To her parents house. They, after all, lived on Long Island. And if someone was going to fake their own death, and needed help…who ELSE would they logically go to if not their parents? Seymour Almasy sighed, a sigh from the tips of his toes up through the rest of his body. He had the hospital and the bank to deal with, but there was a more important conversation that had to be undertaken, first. A conversation that he’d put off for a year and a half. As he stood there, eyes cast to the ground in the middle of a cemetery, it was pretty easy for him to understand why. For the first time since he’d realized that his wife was in all probability alive, he had to acknowledge the bitter fact that there was a legitimate chance that Laura Marie Winters simply didn’t want to be found. =============================== So, the truth comes out, does it? I don’t know. I haven’t spoken to Mr. and Mrs. Winters since Laura died. There was no funeral for us to plan, because of Laura’s wishes to be buried without an audience. They moved from Long Island pretty soon after it happened. Maybe Laura’s living with them, and she just doesn’t want anything to do with me anymore. At the same time, though…that theory doesn’t make as much sense as it should. They would have contacted me. They would have told me something. They would have told SOMEONE something. Large chunks of the world right now believe Laura’s dead. Only people in on whatever plan Laura’s concocted know otherwise, them and myself, the employee, the cemetery director, and maybe some others. She’s disappeared from the face of the Earth. I almost HOPE that her parents know and simply aren’t telling me, because that would make logical sense. But if even Mr. and Mrs. Winters don’t know where she is…that is far more worrying. It means that there’s no logic to what she’s done. No path I can trail. It means that I could chase down every lead I can and come up empty. It means that Laura’s out there somewhere in the world and I will never see her again. Compared to that thought, how can I fight? How can I compete? How can I win? The fact is, it’s what I’ve always done. What I’ve always had to do. I go out and fight when the world collapses around me. The sport is my one constant. And as much as I hate to admit it, I need it now as much as ever.
