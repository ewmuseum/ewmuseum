Date: Today 2.0 - Previously on 'Duncan Drake is a Douchebag' and Five Other Clichés When we last checked in with the Hellion and Friends World Tour, it was all 'hey, look, fancy jet plane!' and 'man, that agent guy is, like, 83% New York dickface.' Then we learned that MacKenzie Malone's family was all poor and stuff. GASP! Then came Uproar, and the only way his match ended with Drake leveling Tsun for the three was in the peyote-inspired fever dreams of his after show party. In truth, it went as well (by which we mean 'as badly') as you might have guessed. Duncan looked overmatched at times and the truth is that, if not for the fact he has a bucket, he'd likely have suffered a terrible defeat. * * * * * 2.1 - An Egg Salad Sandwich, Part One (or, 'The Court of the Drake: Barton Marquis Franklin') The truth is, gentle readers, that, no matter how they might argue otherwise, women do not find the sight of sweaty men in any way attractive. They might use words like 'glistening' and they might like to imagine their men sweaty from a workout. But they do not, as a rule, actually wish to come home to the love of their life in an actual state of sweatiness. Sweat is icky; it smells, it's sticky, and it produces stains that are notoriously unbecoming. So, yes, the women of America want their men to be physically fit; they just don't usually want to bear witness to the process. (Yeah, yeah, they're horrible hypocrites -- life's tough, wear a cup.) Case in point: Duncan Drake at the gym. Now, Mr. Drake was a lot of things -- he was arrogant and scheming, he wasn't altogether bright, he was flashy, he was the product of good genes but bad parenting skills, and he was... well, actually, this list could go on for quite some time, but it could probably be boiled down to 'he's from Hollywood.' But with his spiky 'cool guy' haircut, his cockeyed smile, his soulful-when-he-wants-them-to-be brown eyes, and his clotheshorse sense of style, Drake was actually rather good-looking in almost any situation. Being repeatedly slammed to the mat by a large black man was... not one of those situations. In powder blue running shorts and a 'Property of...' t-shirt that gave new meaning to the word 'sweatstain,' Drake lied on his back in a weathered blue boxing ring. Standing above him was a black man with a face like a mountain crag, muscles like boulders and hair the gray of a threatening thunderhead. This man was Barton Marquis Franklin, and, yeah, he was aware of all the 'BMF' jokes you're already thinking of. Given that this was the BMFGym (hell, the name was the whole reason Drake was lying in agony here), he clearly supported the humor wholeheartedly. His friends called him 'Key' and he'd been hired by Duncan's parents -- and paid an obscene amount of money -- to make sure their son didn't drop dead after deciding wrestling would be neat-o to try. 'Ow, ow, ow, ow, ow,' Duncan said, coming slowly to his feet. 'Isn't there some kind of... some kinda magic needle that you can jam in my buttockal region to give me super strength? Or, since, y'know, I'm against the whole thought of needles, something that Mac can sensuously rub upon me that'll give me Cyrus Raynes' muscles before Pearl Harbor Day? We can both pretend I didn't know what it was.' The massive, barrel-like black man just snorted, like a bull. 'Ya think this shit's funny, don't ya?' Drake wiped off his brow with the back of one taped-fist. 'I mean, kinda.' 'Ya ain't gonna think it's funny when Timo Bolam--' Drake snickered; a horrible chittering sound like a hundred squirrels all laughing at once. Or doing something else at once. Think of the laughter. Think only of the laughter. 'Listen, kid. I know ya don't take this shit seriously. The other guys in that ring? They know ya don't take this shit seriously. Hell, my sweet Granny May knows ya don't take this shit seriously, an' she's blind in one eye an' mostly deaf.' 'I know; I'm sorry,' Drake shrugged. 'It's just... I get tired of hearing it. 'Duncan, you're gonna get yours!' 'You're just a worthless Hollywood layabout!' 'Random dude'll show you.' I don't need you to say the same thing. I need you to show me what the hell to expect in one of these royal battle things.' 'It's called a battle royale.' 'Is that like a royale with cheese? I saw Pulp Fiction. Know what they call a Quarter Pounder with Cheese in France?' 'No,' Key said, cutting off the quote-fest sure to follow. 'You're lucky, since there's only gonna be seven other guys in the ring with ya at once. There are folk -- yers truly among 'em -- who'll call the battle royale the most dangerous match there is for a wrestler.' 'No way. I've seen, like, cage matches and barbed wire matches. And just a couple weeks ago, I saw Aimz, like, casually kill a guy in the ring.' He rolled forward, and then laid there on the ground like a drowned Rat, tongue lolling. 'Just, wham, and he's dead.' The BMF shook his head, once. 'Ain't about the steel cages or the barbed wire, Duncan. It's about the accidents that can happen when ya got sixteen arms and sixteen legs flailing around, trying to muscle people out of the ring. Ya can make allowances when ya know there's going to be a cage over the damn ring or the ropes are going to be barbed up. What ya can't make allowances for is where the guy next to ya is gonna stand.' In the midst of getting to his feet, the Hellion just sort of... stopped. He was still on one knee, staring blankly at the colossal trainer. 'Wait, so the big key to victory you've got is, 'don't get stepped on and you'll win'? C'mon, man! You're supposed to be training me on how to win matches, not how to avoid getting my hand broken because Samoans are a big dumb klutzes.' Key nodded to himself, stepping back into a corner of the ring. He took a long squirt from his sports bottle. 'Let me ask ya something, Mr. Bigshot,' he said pensively. 'Yeah, that haircut totally makes your face look fat,' Drake responded, as if by rote. 'Nah, I'm serious. You wanna win this so bad it hurts inside? 'This particular match? Eh, sure.' 'Then you're done right now. Don't even bother going to New Orleans.' 'But I like New Orleans. You don't even wanna know how many beads I'm bringing.' 'It ain't even Mardi Gras.' 'So? Like drunk college girls care?' 'Then stay the fuck on Bourbon Street. I doubt Itachi or Bolamba woulda answered my question 'Eh, sure.'' Drake stepped forward, brushing his hands across his shorts. 'Look, I wanna win, okay? I'm a Drake; I wanna win everything. Also, I wanna shake that Fontaine guy's hand and learn his secrets to pickin' up random bimbettes, an', hey, I wanna get the chance to hit Mayson Jar's kid in the face with a chair too, 'cause kids suck. But... okay, it's like this. There are, like, three fancy belts a guy can hold in this place, right?' Key was not going to play along with Duncan Drake's Hyperbole Theater. He simply stood there, glaring down his recalcitrant pupil. 'Take the Universal Title an' you're all, 'Rar! I'm the king of everything I survey! I can also, apparently, heal from career-threatening injuries in, like, an hour due to the belt's magical restorative powers.' 'You do realize Sage was fakin' all that bullshit, right?' Okay, Key would play a long a little -- when said recalcitrant pupil was that dumb, there were things that needed to be said. But people who knew Duncan also knew enough to shut up. He wasn't listening; he wasn't really interested in listening. He was On A Roll. 'If you have your regulation cup of coffee with the Gateway belt,' Drake continued. 'It says you're edgy and cool and, judging from the last two folk who've held it, it also makes you turn into kind of a hosebeast overnight. No one really knows what happens if you hold onto it a long time, because it's like holding onto the wind, I'm told.' BMF and Duncan locked up, the untrained celebutante shoved quickly into a corner. The Hellion raised his hands, but let's be honest here, he wasn't really even wrestling Key anymore. He was wrestling the truth, an opponent of Drake's whose ass he routinely kicked. 'All the Mountain Dew belt says is, 'Ah sho' can wrassle purty.' Which we both know... ain't really happenin' with me. Now, maybe... maybe of the eight of us were gonna be in that ring to determine who got to jump on what was left of the Crispy-Devil war, it'd be worth it. But Palestine and Totally Jason -- or both, who knows -- gets that shot. So what you've got is eight guys thrown together for the right to be third in line at the third most important belt outta, y'know, three.' Drake climbed up to sit on the top turnbuckle. 'If this wasn't my pay-per-view coming-out party, I'd say they coulda held one of your 'dangerous' battle royals for an egg salad sandwich an' it'd have been just as prestigious.' Key scowled; Drake leapt. This went better for the former, as the BMF caught the Hellion easily and spiked him to the canvas in a hard body slam. 'Got quite a mouth on you, kid. You're lucky I ain't in that ring -- I'd slap the shit right outta ya.' 'What! I like egg salad sandwiches. Put a little bit of pepper on there... There's this place in the Hills. We should totally go.' BMF shook his head, mostly to himself, stepping through the ringropes. The sweaty, bruised-up B-lister rolled over on the canvas, shouting after him. 'Does this mean 'no' on the you hooking me up with steroids thing?' * * * * * 2.2 -- Deanna Everett and the Park City Ski Cabin It was the story of a lifetime -- sex, drugs, violence, it had everything, and she'd have it or she'd die trying. When Deanna Everett was a little girl, she hated phrases like 'intrepid girl reporter.' To her, Lois Lane wasn't a role model; she was a stick-figure caricature with the lines fading out. Girl reporters like her were everything women reporters shouldn't be, but everything they had to be to find success. They were cute, they were sassy, and they looked just smart enough to give the impression that they'd be wildcats if only they found the right man to get them to let their hair down. Never smarter than the boys. The trouble was, while the women she watched told empty stories about old ladies' bridge clubs, the tough, hard-nosed reporting was being done by others, who didn't give a shit about the latest debutante ball or high society weddings or the goddamn weather. If these reporters happened to wear their genitalia on the outside, Deanna would only claim it's because the female reporters she watched actually were pussies. At least, that's what Deanna told herself while she was illegally breaking into residences to get salacious dirt on semi-celebrities. She wanted a real story, though. She wanted something with teeth, something that would make people remember her as more than the pretty face who read the entertainment news. It's not like Deanna didn't look the part of the vapid entertainment reporter. Her dishwater blond hair was always in a neat ponytail down her back, her wardrobe managed to say she had money and taste without being full of thousand-dollar designer names. Several painful, expensive dental procedures had given her a dazzling smile, and while she'd never been to a plastic surgeon, she thought long and hard about it. She had a friend once (okay, it was more than that, but it was nothing serious, just some mind-blowing sex and a whole lot of shoptalk) who'd won a Pulitzer Prize. She didn't know what the story was that he'd won it for; he'd surely told her what it was, but, really, once she got a good look at the framed bronze medal, her ears stopped working, and she imagined herself giving an acceptance speech for the award. All the people she'd thank -- and all the people she'd remember not to thank -- would have filled a list as long as her arm. Deanna crept through the foyer of the loft, running a glove across the table beside the door. It was expensive shit, she'd give them that; it's not often that a Park City ski retreat sees the hand-carved teak. She moved slowly to the living room, one hand holding her digital camera to her not-so-ample bosom (hey, there was a reason she'd thought about those trips to the plastic surgeon). In the movies, when the detectives are looking through the villain's hideout, one will often turn to the other and ask, 'What are we looking for?' The other detective, usually the more experienced of the pair, will answer the same. 'We'll know it when we see it.' Deanna liked to think she was in the latter category, but she was more often than not in the former. Like tonight, for instance, she knew this two-story loft belonged to Sebastian Hart, who was some loudmouth British git who had his fingers all over the Drake family pie. She'd never met Hart personally -- from what she could tell, few people actually had. What was odd about the loft wasn't that it was hardly furnished -- many ski bunnies had basically empty cabins north of Salt Lake City for use in the wintertime -- but, rather, that it seemed like no one had ever lived there. Other than the expensive furniture, there were no mementos, no pictures, and no keepsakes. There was no stray laundry, no artwork, nothing that indicated that anyone had ever stepped foot in this place, even once. The floors had been mopped recently (Deanna wrinkled her nose at the scent of ammonia), the surfaces were all dust-free, and the closets were empty. She made her way quietly to the kitchen, a flashlight providing the only light in the loft. Outside, the season's first snowfall dusted the ground. With a little luck, it would be gone by morning; if it got worse, she'd leave footprints as she left. As she approached, the stainless steel refrigerator loomed large in the kitchen, a single 100-watt bulb lit overhead. A white piece of paper, torn in a jagged line from a yellow memo pad, was stuck to the fridge door, fastened there by a Minnie Mouse magnet. Deanna tiptoed in that direction, trying desperately to be silent. The paper was the only ornamentation she'd come across that didn't speak of vapid wealth, and she meant to at least have a picture of it. As she moved, her cellphone rang -- if we said her ringtone was a clip from Destiny's Child's 'Independent Woman,' would it really be a surprise? The tone nearly gave her a coronary right there, and she snapped it to her ear rapidly, racing across the floor to snatch the note. 'Wait, wait, honey,' she whispered into the receiver, turning away from the appliance. 'Start over.' There was a long, long pause. 'Duncan Drake is wrestling? On pay-per-view. In a real match? Someone is paying that punk-ass kid to fight someone and they don't think it's a joke?' She nodded. 'Of course I want a press pass! Tell them... tell them I'm doing a story on wrestling and celebrity and its affect on our youth. Or something.' She finally looked down at the note. 'I'll call you back. I'm in the middle of something.' She paused. 'No, it's not illegal.' Setting the note on the kitchen counter, Deanna lifted her camera, taking three successive snaps of the quickly scrawled message. Then she reattached it to the quaint Minnie-head magnet and headed for the door. 'GONE FISHING BACK AT 8,' the note read. But it was the ending that had intrigued this intrepid girl reporter. In the same handwriting as the bolder, carefully written first sentence, this was written in smaller letters. 'Sorry. Know you thought this was over.' The note went unsigned. Deanna made extra careful to lock the loft behind her. A lazy drift of cigarette ash drifted down from the upstairs balcony. * * * * * 2.3 - An Egg Salad Sandwich, Part Two (Or, 'We Doubt This Is The Place Duncan Meant') MacKenzie Malone slammed her phone shut with a long sigh. Hospitals got such shit cell signal. Ruffling a hand through her pitch-dark hair, she picked up her beaten turquoise tray and carried it quietly to the checkout stand. Banner Good Samaritan Hospital in downtown Phoenix was known for a number of things. It was one of the preeminent cancer treatment centers in the southwestern United States, its staff consisted of some of the most renowned surgeons in the country, and its facilities were top of the line in many, many regards. Surprisingly, MacKenzie considered as she set the tray, with her egg salad sandwich on rye toast and fries sealed inside Styrofoam, on the counter, these impressive amenities included a cafeteria that would be the envy of any all-you-can-eat buffet in America. She snagged a bottle of water from the cooler next to the register. 'That'll be $7.83,' the clerk, a friendly-looking twenty-something tow-headed boy said. MacKenzie nodded, reaching into her Coach bag for her wallet. The clerk paused, and then snapped his fingers; she winced at the attention. 'Say, you're that wrestling girl!' She shook her head quietly as she handed over the American Express Gold Card. 'I-I think you've got me confused with...' 'No, you're with that Drake guy.' The clerk swiped the card without looking. MacKenzie tapped her foot impatiently, waiting for the sale to be processed and hoping that he wouldn't ask her anything else. That hope was in vain. 'My roommate gets Showtime. We watch all the shows.' 'Ah,' she responded flatly, shooting him a beseeching look, partially hidden by her fly-eye Christian Dior sunglasses. A beat passed in silence. She hoped he wasn't going to say anything, but figured he was just deciding which question to ask first. She was right. 'Clinton Sage sure is an ass, huh?' the clerk asked. 'Is he like that all the time, or is it that just on the show?' 'We've, uh, we've never really spoken,' MacKenzie said awkwardly. The credit card machine was redialing. 'He seems kind of in--' 'Oh, I get it. You can't tell me, because of the whole... business thing. How about Jon Rhine, though? You can talk about guys like him, right? He seems kind of awesome, and my girlfriend has, like, this huge crush on him.' 'We... y'know, we don't talk, either, really. Duncan and I are -- ' 'Okay, okay. Well, I'm sure you know Aimz. You girls are tight in the business, right? It must do you proud to see a lady with such an import--' The machine printed her receipt. MacKenzie leaned in. 'Look,' she said, her voice a barely controlled hiss. 'I could give a shit about Aimz and her title, or her stupid-ass husband or her problems or your questions or whatever insipid feminist rah-rah and/or lesbian ooh-yeah bond you think we have. Because we don't. She's a self-important dyke in denial, okay? And you... you should consider me no more than a hungry customer with a family member upstairs. I'd like it very much if you gave me my goddamn gold card back so I can have a stupid sandwich before I have to board a fucking airplane. Are we clear on this?' The clerk raised his hands. 'Hey, lady, I was just asking...' 'I'm sorry.' Her words were dismissive now. 'The only valid answers are 'Yes' and 'No', hon. One of them virtually guarantees you will be on the wrong end of some egg salad. So which is it...' She glanced at his nametag. '...Mike?' Robotically, the sales clerk extended Mac's credit card and the sales receipt; his face was crimson, though whether it was shock, embarrassment or rage, she couldn't tell. The dark-haired woman scrawled her autograph across the dotted line at the bottom, snatching up her tray with enough force to knock over the bottle of water and stalking across the cafeteria floor. Part of her -- the part not beaten to shit just by sheer proximity to someone like Duncan Drake and the part that feared and anticipated the results of tests being run upstairs right now -- wanted to turn around, apologize, and make amends. But she didn't; she was exhausted, and, moreover, she knew that MacKenzie Fucking Malone didn't beg someone's pardon. Instead, she took out her frustrations by throwing all her one hundred and twenty pounds into a cheap plastic chair. As MacKenzie cracked open the white box of delicious eggy goodness, a manilla envelope slapped down on the Formica tabletop. Behind the wide lenses of those five hundred dollar sunglasses, Mac looked up at the rather nondescript man who was standing over her table. She took in his plain, sandy brown hair, his faded blue shirt, his tan slacks, and his tasseled penny loafers. She stared the tattoo (half kanji, half steel chain) that crawled down from the back of his neck, around his throat and down under the collar of that shirt. Suffice it to say, the clothes and the tattoo seemed at war with each other. 'Can I help you?' MacKenzie asked evenly. She did not look down at the envelope. 'Probably,' The Tattooed Man said, his voice soft, his tone matter-of-fact. He folded his hands at his waist. 'Read the envelope, decide if you can then. We'll be in touch.' He tipped an imaginary cap, which somehow seemed out-of-place for someone with such an imposing mark. As he left, it wasn't so much that he disappeared, as it was that the man simply merged with the sparse lunchtime crowd. His parting words drifted behind him. 'By the by, I preferred you as a blonde.' MacKenzie's eyes widened; her left hand flew to her trademark black locks, while the right fell atop the envelope. The envelope was quickly torn open. Her egg salad sandwich went uneaten.
