The Father

19 April 2010
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 4/19/10 June 11, 1993 Jefferson County, Mississippi   The rain had followed the Montgomery Queen Circus from Texas through Louisiana, making them all hope beyond hope that they’d catch some good weather before they jumped on to the next county. They were going to live and die on how the weekend show did at the Jefferson County Fairgrounds, which meant that Freddie Joe Kingsley had to play his part in burning the lot. But if he was going to make his nut, he was going to do it like he always did; with honesty and with his hands. While the rest of the carnies were busy gaffing their flats and overcharging for food, he decided to use the break in the rain to take his nine-year-old son, Terrence, fishing in one of Fayette’s ponds. His wife would do just fine by reading fortunes and sowing makeshift knickknacks that she said were imbued with voodoo powers. He didn’t believe in any of that. He was the only one in the troupe that didn’t. He wasn’t a large man, but he was stout with broad shoulders and a chin chiseled out of granite. He and his son shared some of the same physical features; unkempt mops of dirty brown hair, dark green eyes, and banana yellow teeth. But unlike his son, his skin was rough and tan and dotted with pockmarks. Wrinkles had started to stretch underneath his eyes. Gray dusted his temples. But his hands, covered in oil thanks to working on the side as a roustabout, were still as soft as the day he was born. It was after lunch when they heard some of the locals chatting about the Confederate ghosts around the abandoned town just down the way. With nothing but carp eating through their bait and an explorer’s spirit in their blood, the two decided it would be worth taking a look and asked for directions. When Mississippi was just a territory, Rodney was a mere three votes away from becoming the capital. It was a cultural hotbed, a noteworthy river town along the Mississippi known for its high literacy rate as well as cottonseed development. The first man to connect the cotton gin to steam power had lived in the city. Former Presidents Zachary Taylor and Jefferson Davis owned land around the area. But the Mississippi River, as it was prone to do, changed course. In 1930, Rodney was abolished as a city, and by 1966, it was deserted because of a natural disaster. At least that’s what most of the folks outside the area wanted to believe. Hunting shacks dotted the area around Rodney, deer hide and snakeskin strung along their ramshackle edifices so they could bake in the hot sun. Thankfully, Freddie had a habit of keeping his knife sharp. After all, it had been serving him well since the Vietnam War. He pulled his truck just past the Old Presbyterian Church, shifting to park so the two of them could take a look. The brick structure stood nearly two stories, six openings stretched in rows of three across its fa�ade; there were white doors to the left and right, the middle opening on the ground level and those across the top filled in with sea green tinted windows. A cannonball, shot from the USS Rattler during the Civil War, was embedded in its upper center. On the top of the church was a bell tower encased in ornate white columns, its windows shuttered, the dome over its top corroded green. They tried the doors but none of them opened. All they could do was look inside, look at the pews and the flower pots and brass candle holders that had been locked in stasis for God knows how long. They walked behind the church, past the red maples and mayhaw trees before pushing on through to the bog that was nestled behind the forest. Freddie ambled behind his son, cutting through the swamp’s thickets and spider webs with his combat knife as he watched his boy dart between the patches of dry ground and the mud holes. The mosquitoes were thick, his arms swelling up thanks to their bites. It was far too humid to roll down his flannel sleeves. His snuff was choking its way down his throat. "Shit!" Terrence yelped as he tripped on some underbrush and fell into a pool of murky water that had been camouflaged by fallen leaves and white flower petals. His father shook his head, spitting a rope of tobacco as he sheathed his knife to his boot and caught up to his son to pull him out. Drying him off with his hands, he nudged him, playfully patting his cheeks and leaving behind a trail of oily fingerprints. When he looked up, he saw the most peculiar of sights; an upright grand piano jutting out of the trunk of a mighty magnolia tree, the pair seemingly fused together. Terrence looked around, finding a dry strip to dance across before hopping onto the tree’s gnarled roots. He swung around, his hands gripping against the molten bark as he shimmied along to the piano, stepping into the basin before running his fingers across the caked ivories. The loud pitched drone was off key, but from the look of the piano’s body, it was a miracle it worked at all. The cabinet which housed its strings had long begun rotting away, the wood sinking with each press of his fingertips, each depression causing light to glint inside. There was something resting on top of the catgut. He reached inside the sliver mush, fishing around before he felt something soggy. It nearly slipped out of his grasp as he tried to pull it out. His father swabbed the snuff out of his bottom lip, looking over his son’s shoulder as he clasped his arms. "What you got there?" "Dunno," he said as he turned around and handed the leather over to his father. Freddie combed his fingers along the book’s seams, grazing his tips along its pressed dips. The ink on the cover had eroded away. When he opened the cover, he saw it was a Bible. King James edition. The year it was printed was lost thanks to water damage. Most of its pages had rotted away. He handed it back to his son, stepping around the edge until he heard a dull hiss. A water moccasin launched out of the brush, nearly catching him on the calf of his jeans. He ripped his knife from his sheath. The moccasin coiled. He threw the blade. It caught around its body and pinned it to the ground. He pulled it out of the pit viper and grabbed around its body before lopping its head off. "Let me see that bible," his father said as he squeezed the scales downward, draining the body of blood. After it was tossed over, he opened it up and folded the snake inside the pages before putting it in his jean pocket. "Had enough sight seeing?" he asked as he wiped his hands on his thighs. "Got to get going. Showtime’s in a few hours." Terrence had never seen a snake die before, but it didn’t shock him. He just wanted to know why the body flailed around after his father cut the damn thing’s head off.         The big top was filled to capacity, cigarette smoke filtering through the opening at the apex. The sun had gone down hours ago, and while the Midway was still buzzing with stragglers, the main event was where everyone wanted to be. The rows of seats formed in long lines from north to south, east to west, all held behind a square barricade with a wrestling ring in the center. Terrence and his mother were part of the standing room only crowd near the entrance, his two friends also nearby but sitting in chairs they’d scrounged up from the boneyard. As the clowns busied themselves making balloon animals for the children wrapped around the edges of the guardrail, he looked over at the five locals who’d paid a yard thinking they’d be the one who beat his dad. Nobody had ever collected the strongman bounty in the 90 year history of the Montgomery Queen Circus. The marks were nondescript save the tall man with the handlebar mustache and balding head of black hair who hung at the back of the line. He was busy sweating sheets as he shadowboxed, his gut so large that his wife-beater folded up to his chest due to the stress. It didn’t take much to get him huffing and wheezing. "Oh man, a ghost town?" Tyler Meester said, drawing Terrence’s attention away from the larrys. He was a few months younger, but he had an awful habit of looking up to him. His sandy blond hair was shaved high and tight, making his fat cheeks all the more pronounced. His eyes were hazel. His fingers always seemed to be sticky with cotton candy. "Yeah," Terrence spoke softly, trying his best to keep his voice so his mother couldn’t hear him. "Was really cool. Swamps and all that." Mikey Meester, seated between the two, shook his head before kicking at the bottom of the chair in front of him. "Ain’t no ghost town if people is around there." That was his way, always being the killjoy. He looked an awful lot like his brother save the fact that he was rail thin and kept his hair painfully long. "Dude, shut up Mikey," Tyler groaned. "He’s right," Terrence started, cupping his hand over his mouth. "That’s why we got to watch our step. It ain’t too far away, but they might damn well try to—OW!" His mother cuffed him upside the head. "Do you want to eat a bar of soap, young man?" "No, ma." "Then watch your tongue." Juliana Kingsley’s hands were busy scrubbing the pulp from the moccasin’s scales. She was a different kind of woman than the rest of the troupe, having not been born into this or joining up because she was running from her past. She was a natural southern belle. A rare white mambo. Just below average height, her figure bowed in a dainty hourglass, her skin soft and glowing porcelain save faint tinges of red on her candy apple cheeks which were framed by her mane of curled auburn hair. Her fluffed white top hugged her thanks to her black leather corset, a flowing blood red skirt falling down from her hips. Her feet were bare. She never needed make-up. Rubbing the back of his head, Terrence’s gaze lingered away. He wasn’t sure how much she’d heard, but he wasn’t about to take any more risks. "We’ll talk later," he told the two as the house lights faded and the crowd silenced. A spotlight cut through the smoke and shined down in the center of the ring, drenching the form of a woman who appeared out of thin air, one hand pinching the brim of her peacock feathered top hat and the other grasping the bend of a long oak cane. Raven black hair flowed to her shoulders. Her ruby sequin vest shimmered through the second hand smoke. When she brought her head up, the crowd gasped. Her face was that of a child model who had the misfortune of being mangled by a serrated blade, black lipstick licking along her Glasgow smile scars. While Francis Montgomery was the young heir to the last great circus of the South, she was more infamous as The Big Top Black Dahlia. 'Welcome ladies and gentlemen, boys and girls, but hopefully not children of all ages,' she bellowed with practiced bravado, chewing on her vowels. "I’m Francis Montgomery, the ringmistress of this twisted circus. It’s my solemn pledge that any person who can take down my strongman mano-a-mano will earn themselves five-thousand dollars!" The spotlight redirected its gaze towards the tapered canvas door to the northeast as a gramophone played vaudeville famed singer Billy Murry’s rendition of Over There. Terrence made a beeline for the guardrail when the flap exploded open, trying his best to get a view of his old man before everyone else robbed him of the chance. Freddie Joe Kingsley ambled out in his Stars and Bars singlet, his fists pumping skyward before he combed along the guardrail to slap high fives with all those around him. They loved him for his shooting ability, loved him because he’d earned a Purple Heart in Vietnam, and loved him because he beat back the Yankees in National Guard Armories from the Carolinas all the way to Texas. They were the ones who gave him the nickname ‘The Pride of the South’. He snatched an apple tossed his way by the ringmistress as he entered the ring, turning around towards the five men and grinning as he tightened his fist. The apple exploded under the pressure. "To beat him, you must pin him,' Francis shouted as she exited the ring. 'You have five minutes.' "Remember the plan," the balding man belched to his counterparts, but none of them paid attention to his drunken ramblings. The house lights came to life, and the first man in line stepped up, only to get planted to the canvas with a high waistlock takeover. The crowd roared as Freddie swung around and grabbed him with a front chancery. "Just remember," Francis licked her lips and chuckled, "you were the one that paid a yard to get in there." Terrence tried to peek around the corner to get a better look, but flailing arms were making it difficult. His father dominated, slamming the first larry into the canvas and keeping him grounded for the full five minutes until the bell rang, making sure to shake the man’s hand in a show of sportsmanship after he let him up. That was his old man, always being respectful to others. The second contestant didn’t have much of a chance, but at least he took his time and tried to sprawl away from Freddie’s takedown attempts. Once he grounded him, there was no way he was going to let up. In the final minute, the balding man reached into the crowd and pushed a person away to grab their wooden chair. He flipped it closed and made a dash for the ring. He slid under the bottom rope. He stumbled to a stand. Freddie had his man pinned down. Had him down for the count. He was defenseless. The seat of the wooden chair splintered as it cut through his mop of hair and broke open his brainpan. The crowd booed. The balding man kicked him over and went for the pin. "Hey, Rube! Rube!" Francis roared in the microphone, wildly motioning to the back for help. Terrence was in shock. Nobody ever hurt his dad. Freddie didn’t need help. He grabbed onto the man’s throat and squeezed as hard as he could, strangling him as his fat rested against his shoulders. He was going to choke the life out of him. Was going to kill him. He brushed his shoulders out from underneath the bastard’s girth. Clubbing blows came down from his knuckles. The man tried to tap, tried to beg for mercy, tried to say that nobody said it was against the rules, but Freddie wasn’t home. He kept throwing hammerfists until the man went limp. Francis entered the ring and tried to restrain him. He shoved her aside and began stomping on the back of the man’s head. The crowd was oddly still. Police officers jumped the guardrail and tried to get in the ring. "Let’s go." Juliana’s hand, covered in snake pulp and blood, yanked at her son’s wrist. He wrenched away from her grasp. He was horrified but he kept his eyes on the ring. His father was beating a man to death and he deserved it but it didn’t make it right. Or maybe it did. The police officers couldn’t contain Freddie. He started wildly swinging at them too. That’s when the crowd turned on him. "Let’s go!" his mother shouted as she peeled him away, dragging him out of the tent before the scene got any worse. But Terrence wanted to stay. Not because he felt joy in seeing that bastard get beaten within an inch of his life but because, for some odd reason, he wanted to be strong enough to take on everyone. To be able to do that to someone else. To be just like his dad.         For the first time since setting up camp, the genny was shut down. Stragglers and green help on the Midway had to use their lighters to find their way back to the boneyard. Word of the strongman show spread like wildfire through the fairgrounds. The troupe made sure to close up shop because there was no point in giving upset locals a reason to start a riot, like damn near happened under the big top after Freddie started swinging at the police officers who were trying to break up the mess. Juliana could feel the odd vibrations through the rolling fog. The winds were picking up, which meant the worst was yet to come. If the bastard who started this mess didn’t pull through that evening, it might’ve—it was the snake. Snakes symbolized unity between this world and the one beyond. And her husband had sheered its head off. She tried to wear a brave face as she dragged her son back to their trailer, but it was hard. And as she neared her steps, it became even harder. The man propped against her makeshift stoop was burly with broad shoulders and a shaved head covered in a sheen of sweat. He could have been a stand-in for Santa Claus. "Sorry to see you, ma’am." Her heart raced as she stopped, letting go of her son’s wrist. "Wyatt," she started, "sorry to see you as well." "Under these circumstances at least," he extended his hand. "Don’t expect I’d be able to have a word with your husband after that bullshit he pulled." She wanted to defend her husband, but she knew better than to launch into a tangent. Sugar went further than bile ever did with Wyatt Conyers. "You know it wasn’t his—" "That ain’t up to me to say." "Wyatt..." He spit, his gaze lingering onto Terrence. An odd discomfort washed over the boy as the Sheriff began rubbing his hands together. Suddenly, behind them, Freddie staggered and clasped his hands on his wife. His awkward giggles were drenched in a fountain of blood spilling onto his singlet and covering his arms. It wasn’t all his. He planted weak, drunken kisses along the neck of his wife, causing her to brace against his shoulders to push him away. "Joe…" He kept kissing, his trembling lips leaving smeared ruby blemishes along the nape of her neck. "Joe!" He bolted upright, looking forward toward a familiar face. "Oh shit..." "Freddie. What the fuck were you doing?" He sighed, looked down to his son. He tossed aside tufts of his hair before he kneeled down, digging into his pocket and pulling out a few crinkled bills. "Hey?" he asked as he put them in his son’s hand. "Why don’t you go get yourself something? From Aldo’s. My treat." Terrence walked away a few dollars richer, but not before he caught a few glimpses of what was going on between the adults. He watched his father hand the Sheriff a few bills of his own before the two walked away into the dark. The crook of the Sheriff’s arm was around the back of his father’s neck.         "What the hell happened?" Tyler asked as he tongued one of Aldo’s shaved ice treats, the concentrate leaking through the bottom of the cone. Terrence shrugged his shoulders as he rested against the boneyard’s picnic table. "You saw what happened." "Is he going to jail?" "I don’t know. Wyatt likes us." He wasn’t sure if he believed the statement. Sure, he always seemed nice enough, but that was mostly because Conyers was a sharpie. But he’d never seen his father give someone patch money before. That’s how he knew his old man was in trouble. "But he swung at them officers. Might’ve killed that guy." "Shut up Mikey," Tyler glared at his brother, flicking slush at his face. "Quit it!" He wiped his hands on his jeans, looking toward Terrence before taking another lick. "Misses Montgomery told ma that we’re gonna get an early jump. Said things aren’t safe around here no more with what happened. We gonna miss seeing that ghost town." "No we ain’t," Terrence started. "Don’t have to, at least. I know how to get there. You still got your flashlight, Mikey?" "Yeah." "Then we can go. I know how to get there." "You sure?" "Shut up, Mikey." Terrence nodded his head. "Yeah, I can get us there. Might catch a ghost too. Didn’t see none of them around when we went earlier but folks said some soldiers hang around the church. All we got to do is leave when everyone falls asleep." Tyler pushed further. "What time?" Terrence thought about it for a moment, scratching his head. "One. Meet by the flying jenny?" "You sure?" "Yeah I’m sure," he told them as he picked himself up. They had about three hours before they made their way over. "Meet you guys in a few."     June 12, 1993     The second Terrence woke up and saw the daybreak through his blinds, he knew he’d messed up. He scrambled out of bed, fighting to get his shoes on before tiptoeing out of his small bedroom. His folks bedroom was on the other side of the trailer, and while his mother slept like a rock, his father had a strange way of hearing every little thing no matter the time. Before he opened the door, he noticed light filtering from the kitchen underneath his door. He pressed his ear against it. Someone was sobbing on the other side. He opened the door, trying his best to creep along the laminate. It was his father sitting at their makeshift kitchen table, one hand nursing a cup of coffee while the other held open the pages of the Bible. The second he touched down, his father’s gaze shot in his direction, and he aggressive scraped his forearm across his eyelids. "Hey," he stammered as he stood up, his stumbling steps bringing him close to his son. "What’s got you up so early?" He was still dressed in his singlet from the night before. He didn’t bother to kneel down. Terrence looked up towards his father. "What time is it?" There was an odd stillness as his father tightened his posture. Soft rain pattered along the roof, the hole in the ceiling dripping water. "Don’t know." It was as if all the energy had been driven from him as he slinked back to his seat, motioning for his son to follow over. When he sat down, his father pushed forward a paper plate that had been near the edge of the table holding nuggets of white meat. Blotches of grease pooled underneath each morsel. "Try some," his father snorted, his eyes gazing down at the Bible. Its pages were caked with blood. "What is it?" "If I tell you, you won’t try it." Terrence grabbed a soggy piece, gingerly putting it in his mouth. It chewed like a piece of rubber, tasted like a piece of chicken. His father rapped his fingers against the leather of his Bible. "I didn’t know a lot about snakes, but I reckon I learned a lot about them through this." He didn’t ask questions, just kept chewing what was in his mouth until he swallowed, nearly gagging as it slithered down his throat. "Mind if I tell you a—" his father was interrupted, a loud banging on the door causing him to freeze up and reclining into his chair. Terrence got up and reached for the door. "Don’t get—" When he opened up, Francis’s paint smeared face was there to greet him. She had yet to change her clothes. "Meeting. In the boneyard." She looked over his shoulder. "Freddie...if you can bring—" "I’ll get her up," his voice cracked. "Any reason I—" Her voice was uncharacteristically somber. "Just meet in the boneyard." He lingered near the door after he closed it, watching his father blankly stare towards the bedroom where his wife was sleeping. His bottom lip was quivering as he picked himself up out of his chair and dragged his feet to the end of the trailer.         Francis was a friend to everyone, but the truth was she wasn’t a pragmatist, and it was starting to run her business into the red. Even though she’d changed the troupe’s name, most of the signs still called the place the Montgomery Brother’s Circus. Her father would have made sure everyone was settled down the night before, would of assessed the situation from all available angles. He was also the one that let her get sliced up in first place, or at least that’s what folks close to the situation told her. In truth, she hadn’t the faintest clue of who did it because she was too busy trying to hide the scars. Unlike her father, Augustus, she wasn’t a born leader, which made it all the more odd that she was the one who went across the trailers and the tents to make sure everyone gathered up as soon as possible. What they found at the center of the miserable and muddy boneyard wasn’t just her, but also the Sheriff. Most of the carnies rolled their eyes as Leah Meester, sobbing and decked out in the trashiest negligee money could buy, joined them in the middle. It wasn’t as though this was the first time the biggest lizard on the lot had caused a scene, but they never had to get up early for her lovegame threats before. The limp, tanned skin of her arms flapped as she tried her best to shakily chainsmoke through the rain. She looked like she’d broken out of a cancer ward, her hair a big poof of black curls that couldn’t be real. Francis boosted onto the closest picnic table, motioning for the murmuring to silence. "Bad news everyone," she started, but her throat kept catching as she tried to continue. "I...uh—" "It was reported earlier this morning that we have a few people missing," Wyatt shouted. "A few boys. Tyler and Mike Meester. Their mother didn’t find them in their beds when she woke up today, so we’re wondering if anybody here’s seen them." Suddenly, the troupe started looking back and forth at each other, a maelstrom of questions hitting everyone from all sides until, finally, Conyers whistled for them to stop. "Does anybody here know where they might be? Where they usually hang around?" A few looks shot to the back of the crowd, looking for the Kingsley family. Terrence stepped forward, but his father corralled him, shoving him back behind himself before he turned around. "Don’t say anything," he hoarsely whispered. But his son didn’t understand. His father’s grasp was harsh. Strong. It felt like sandpaper. "But dad—" "You heard me." His father refused to make eye contact. "Dad—" "Terrence!" his grip seized hold of his son’s shoulder, causing him to wince. He tried to fight the hold, but as the pressure ramped up, he started to cry. Juliana grabbed at her husband’s wrist, trying her best to wrench his grip free. When he let go, her eyes burrowed through his head. He looked away. It was too intense a glare to return. "Joe, what’s gotten into you?" "Just—" "No," she tried to keep herself hushed. "Don’t." She looked down at her son, brushing away his tears before faking a smile. "I know where they are." "Terrence, don’t." "Joe," she glared at her husband, cutting him off so her son could continue. "We were gonna go to that ghost town. Last night. I was gonna take them but I didn’t wake up and we were gonna just take a look because they wanted to see it." He wasn’t sure if he was in trouble, but his stomach fluttered about all the same. "Sheriff," she yelled, waving her hand from the back of the crowd. Conyers looked toward her, pointing her out. "Yeah?" "My son," she started, taking her son’s hand into her own before she walked to the center of the boneyard. "He’s best friends with those boys. He might know where they are." "Oh? What say you, boy?" The looks from the crowd were intense. "Me and the guys were going to go to Rodney last night but I didn’t wake up. I was gonna show them the way." "Is that so? Alright." Conyers nodded then turned to the crowd. "Anybody here who wants to help in the search, get with me. We’re going to go through and find them." Francis followed up. "I said to some of you that we would be jumping on to Demopolis as soon as possible because of what happened last night, but since we don’t have any answers, I..." she began to stutter, but she stomped her foot and caught herself, "we’re gonna stay here. Those boys are family, and I’ll be damned if we jump to the next town leaving family behind. That is all." The crowd dissipated, most of the male members of the troupe gathering around Conyers. "I’m sure they’re around," Wyatt reassured Terrence, his gentle touch brushing across his mop of hair. He turned around, doing the same to Leah. "It’s okay. I’m the law in this county, and every single resource I got is going to be used to find your boys." She wiped away her tears, nodded her head. "Thanks. Means an awful lot." "Don’t mention it." Juliana looked to the back of the crowd. Her husband was nowhere to be seen.     June 15, 1993     Freddie Kingsley joined the search party, not for the search, but because of it. In the nights that followed that chair shot to the back of his head and the disappearance of the Meester boys, his dreams had become all the more pressing, all the more certain. The shrill voices of angels teased his sanity. It might have been a concussion. He knew it was God. Every moment he closed his eyes, he could see it; a wall of fire enveloping everything, the flames licking across cities and all the way through the peaks and valleys of the United States, through places he’d been and places he never had a hope of seeing. And in its wake, the cacophony of droning cries ceased. An entire planet, burned to cinders, locked in atrophy. There wasn’t a time or date ascribed, but it was soon. He knew it was soon. One place, with a beautiful and startling magnolia tree in full bloom, was safe. Siren. Tranquil. His son had learned the piano and was playing it at the foot of the Tree of Life. He knew where it was. It was there that he saw himself, dressed in a white robe, his hands combing the waters made eternal through absolution. His family had grown; not only were his son and wife there, but other children, a mixture of all races, a mixture of young and old, a mixture that looked towards him and only him as their spiritual guide. He had saved them from the end. He took the Bible with him everywhere he went, nestling it in his back jean pocket as he broke away from the search party and retraced his footsteps. He had to make sure. The skin of his brainpan had swelled around his infected stitches. His hair was greasy. Head rushes were all too common. His eyes were puffed out and his skin was pimpled from mosquito bites. Moses had spent a lifetime searching for Israel, but Freddie had already stumbled upon his Holy Land. His Jordan was the Mississippi. All roads in this life lead to Rodney. That’s what the Lord told him over and over and over until the statement became mechanical, until he found himself whispering the statement in moments of tedium. The space was drab, dirty, the rumbling of insect swarms and the chirps of lizards polluting the grandeur. The petals of magnolia fell to the ground from gentle breezes. It was the most beautiful thing he’d ever seen in his life. It was here where he buried his sins.
