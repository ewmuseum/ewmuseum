Den of Snakes, Episode 5; Consequences

Date: 5/16 - 5/20 September 15, 2009 Ernie Spisak’s goon squad stoically drove the station wagon down Rodney Road, their boss shifting about in the backseat as he belched Neo-Nazi rhetoric between gulps from his flask. He was on about his new Brotherhood, on about showing no mercy and smoking the squatters out of his county so he could put their heads on pikes. Whiskey and gum rot fogged the windows. Jethro scrubbed the haze on the window away with his forearm. It was too dark to see much of anything, but for some reason, he found comfort in that. Less to focus on. His mind was already too busy trying to think of anything except what he’d done. Donovan was still out in the forest behind Griswold’s Way, still slumped face first and lashed against the tree. The poor bastard was probably dead, probably ended up choking on his own tongue after they’d stopped keeping of track extension cord strikes. It was a brutal way to go, to be flogged to death, but it was probably better than living on with his hide sheared off. There wasn’t any way out of this, and even if there was, he was too damn scared to think straight. He’d made his bed the second he got in the truck with Clarence Watkins, and even though it was done out of fear, he was now stuck with Jefferson County’s Boogeyman. It might’ve been Spisak’s orders, but it was his arms, his hands, his wrists that brought the cord across Donovan’s back until his skin peeled away. He glanced away from the window, watching Spisak as the son of a bitch wound the extension cord around his knuckles. He fiddled with the plug, picking away ribbons of back flesh from the bent metal prongs before licking them into his maw. "Tastes like jerky." He wanted to throw up. "We left him out there." Waves of vapor smoldered from Spisak’s tattooed and shaved skull. He craned to look Jethro in the eye. "So?" "What if they find him?" "You ask too many fuckin’ questions," Spisak chuckled, his hand slithering up Jethro’s thigh. The psychopath’s fingertips left a trail of maggot guts and mush slivers on his jeans. His glare was intense. "You don’t think I know he ain’t out there? He probably drove there. When the car ain’t been moved in a few days, they’ll start looking. It’s there for the message." "What—" But he was quickly cut off. "I want Kingsley’s number." He gulped hard, breaking eye contact before he reached down to pry the hand away from his leg. "He don’t have one." The barrel of a pistol was pressed into his neck. It froze him. Spisak’s wrist said that ‘The South Will Rise Again’. "Don’t be thick with me. He’s got something. A land line. A prepaid. But I know he’s got something." He cocked the gun. "This goes one of two ways; either I put a hole through your damn neck or you give me the goddamn number. Choice is up to you." The goons in the front seat were still wearing their ski masks. The one in the passenger seat looked back and him and laughed. Frantically, Jethro reached into his jean pocket, ripping his prepaid phone out before handing it over. The gun was cutting a circle into his flesh. "I left him for you to take care of. Later today. You’re gonna go back there. And if he’s dead? You’re gonna bury him. And if he ain’t? You’re gonna bury him. We clear?" "You don’t think I’m loyal?" Jethro stammered, his muscled form quivering as he drew his arms across his chest. "Quite frankly?" Spisak started, holstering the gun in the crotch of his pants. His bony fingers combed across his cheeks. "I could give a damn if you was." Autumn beat the daybreak, moseying into the kitchen before sifting through the fridge for a carton of eggs. The bacon had been left thawing in the sink the night before. The major problem, though, was that the big frying pan was still dirty and sitting on the counter with charred chicken breast having hardened on the teflon. She sighed, looking back to the bedroom as she drew the spaghetti straps of her tank top back over her shoulders. Her face was covered in a tangled mess of amber, forcing her to sweep it away as she took the bacon out and turned the hot water on to fill the sink. After squirting in some dish soap, she combed the crust of sleep from the corners of her eyes and grabbed a brillo pad and went about throwing her shoulder into the scrubs. Don’t forget the almonds she reminded herself as the water tinged brown. Only got the salted kind. Shouldn’t matter. Hopefully. It wasn’t like Terrence was the pickiest eater. Nor was he picky about the company he kept. But that was going to end soon. He’d promised her that. Promised to get them away from Jefferson County. Promised to get them away from the life he led. Promised to get away from Royce Valley and Jethro Tolliver and Donovan Mackey and the Crowley boys. There might not be a white picket fence out in the country with their names on it, but there didn’t need to be. Anywhere was better than here. Anywhere else, nobody would know who they were. Anywhere else, she didn’t have to worry about the cops taking her boyfriend back to Parchman Farm. Anywhere else, she didn’t have to worry about the Kingslian Militia breaking down her door in the dead of night to execute someone she loved. He’d promised it all before, but his tears from the previous night showed his desperation. Now, it was just a matter of time before they severed their ties to the only place they’d ever called home. It didn’t take long to dry the pan and start frying the bacon. The wharblers outside the kitchen window had a habit of chirping a bit too loudly for Terrence’s taste, but she didn’t mind. She picked up one of her packs of bird seed off her work bench before going to her front porch to feed them. The dew on the steps soaked her bare feet. As the aroma of sizzling pork filled the trailer, Terrence began shifting in bed. He didn’t open his eyes; if he did, he wouldn’t be able to get back to sleep. The brackets of the front door whined as she eased it shut. She always cooked bacon and eggs together because the grease would make it so the whites didn’t stick to the pan. It was a trick she learned from Tammy Lynn, back when they doubled up as short order cooks running the graveyard shift at Edgar’s before the Kingslian faith got in the way. Cracking four shells on the counter, she carefully pried them open with her fingers, resting the tops inside the bottoms before tossing them in the trash. They crackled loudly as she poured them in. Neither of them liked soft yolk, so she took her spatula and cracked the yellows before gently swabbing them into the already solidifying whites. A few dashes of salt and pepper followed, and then she turned the bacon over. It was already starting to crinkle. A jug of Milo’s Sweet Tea always worked as a perfect compliment, and it was even better if it was ice cold. She took hers with a slice of lemon. Terrence took his plain bitter. The smells weren’t what bolted him upright; it was the ringing of his phone on the nightstand. His hand slapped around the wood, causing the damn thing to skitter across the polish before he finally managed a hold of it. The fuck he thought as he looked at the number. The zip code was different than any he’d seen around the Delta. He pressed the receiver, bringing it up to his ear. "Yeah?" The voice was distant. Unrecognizable. The name, however, was all too familiar. It was Clarence Watkins. How’d the slimy bastard get a hold of his number? Had Royce been that pissed off with him that he’d sell him out to people neither of them trusted? Watkins asked if it was alright if they two of them met out at the dilapidated barn out on Hard Times Road, just before Jefferson became Adams County on US 61. Asked if they could do it alone. "Around ten work for you?" he asked tepidly. Watkins agreed. The line went dead after that. He got off the bed, scooping his jeans and shirt from the carpet and putting them on before lacing up his work boots. He might’ve been groggy, but he was pissed. Pissed at Royce. And he was going to make that bitter old fuck know before he went out to deal with the bullshit errand boy the cartel sent their way. Autumn stopped him; her touch on his arm was calming, her tired smile nearly making him forget just what he was about to do. She never needed make-up. "Got to go. Sorry." His beard tickled her cheek as he gave her a kiss. Then, reluctantly, he shirked out the door. Her smile wasn’t indicative of how she felt, but it lingered as she turned her attention back to the burner. She didn’t get a chance to say anything. Didn’t even get a chance to ask about the almonds. Hidden in the swamps near News Lake, the Devil shotgunned whiskey as he watched the reluctant from the far corner of his tarpaper shack. Clarence’s usually impeccable suit was ruffled and untucked, his graying ponytail slick with grease. He put his cell back in his pocket, then pushed Jethro’s prepaid across the picnic table. He caught it as it skipped across the knots, choosing to focus on the small piece of plastic instead of looking at anyone else. Watkins rapped his knuckles on the table top and stood up, swinging his single breasted jacket over his shoulders before sidestepping the dark blotches littering the floor on his way out the door. The goons followed closely behind, their shotguns cocked and hidden underneath the flaps of their hate-speak laden jackets. "Better get a move on," Spisak said as he scrubbed his rusty pocket knife over his greening teeth. "The hounds ‘round here’re pretty good at sniffin’ out nigger blood." Royce Valley was a mountain around these parts; his face was laced indiscriminate scars and pockmarks thanks to cystic acne. His bare chest was about the size of a barrel. He looked like a leathery relic, his tattoos (including the fallen angel across his front) long since having blurred into his sun-beaten complexion. But his mane of hair, as well as his prominent horseshoe mustache, was still as black as they’d even been. Eight o’clock wake up calls threatened to make them gray. He rolled over in his bed, grabbing his pillow as the loud knock on his door continued without pause. It didn’t help. At least he didn’t have far to go; his legs jutted off the mattress because he was too damn tall for it. He gingerly limped to the door, rubbing his eyes with his forearm as he opened it up. It was Terrence and he had his Stars and Bars bandanna on and he looked pissed. He didn’t even ask if it was okay to come inside, just pushed on through. "You mind explainin’ to me—" Valley groggily cut him off. "Woahwoahwoah—" "Shut your fuckin’ mouth! I got a call, Royce. Six, maybe seven this morning, yeah? From fucking Clarence fuckin’ Watkins." Royce arched his eyebrow. "He shouldn’t have your—" "Yeah, he’s got it. He’s got my number to my prepaid. What the fuck, Royce?" "Calm down," he said as he slumped into his easy chair, taking a deep exhale as his fingers tapped the coffee table. "It’s just a call." That answer wasn’t the right one. He knew it. He just didn’t care. "Just a call? Just a fuckin’ call? It ain’t just a call! He wants to meet me out in the middle of nowhere." "Alone?" "Yeah! You gonna explain yourself?" He pushed himself up off the chair. "Explain myself? I got nothin’ to explain." "He’s your contact. You’re the reason he’s here." "I ain’t the reason for shit. Times is changing, and I reckoned it was time we changed with them." For a moment, there was a pause. "So you gave him my number." "I won’t trust that man to take a shit." "I wanted out of the deal. You’ve been the one pushin’ for it." "We’ve all been pushin’ for it." His voice was rising with Terrence’s, and suddenly, mere inches were all that separated them. "At least those of us who do the cookin’, which I don’t see you ever volunteering your time for because you got...well shit...I dunno what you be doing." "That ain’t your concern." "I’d say it is. I ain’t the one promising to run away. It is a goddamn concern! A valid one!" Valley balled his fists. Kingsley did the same. "Ever since you got strung up by your old man, you got a conscience." Spit wildly flew from his mouth. "And now you’re looking for someone else to blame?" "Fuck you—" "No, fuck you Terrence! This ain’t you. This ain’t the fuckin’ kid who did hard time for the club. This ain’t the kid who I took under my wing when Opal and them guys wanted to piss on you." Each fist he pounded into his chest sounded like a thunderclap. "You got this county out of respect because you earned—" "You gave it to me because you’re pissin’ your pants about going back to prison! Don’t act like you gave me this out of the kindness of your heart, Royce. I know you. You’re the same gutter ass coward that ran to Bakersfield when times got rough. Don’t spin your tires about loyalty because you don’t got none, and this bullshit with Watkins is proof." "Pick up your britches, boy. You keep talkin’ like that to me and I’ll put a bullet between your fuckin’ eyes without even blinking." Kingsley stepped back, swallowing hard before nodding his head. He licked his lips in disgust. "So you did do it." "If I did, there ain’t shit you can do about it." He coiled up, ready to strike. But Royce wasn’t giving an inch. They stared through each other for a few moments, lips teetering toward the next bitter heart punch. Kingsley shook his head, spitting a thick wad on the floor before he slammed the door damn near off its hinges. Royce slinked away, strangely exhausted as he sat down in his chair. Anger flushed his cheeks. It continued into subtle twitches head, then full blown nods of his head. His fingernails tore into the heels of his hands. When that didn’t satisfy him, he brought his fists across the coffee table like a hammer. Its legs gave. The top splintered to the floor. Cigarette ash and tobacco spit spilled onto the carpet. Jethro’s foot hovered over the accelerator of his pick-up, his eyes trained on the only car in the lot of Griswold’s Way. Ernie was right; Donovan’s Ford Pinto was still there, its windows covered in a sheen of dew. Donovan was infamous for using bottles of Jack as a chaser for pitchers of cheap beer. He never drove himself home afterwards. Nobody would think anything about his car being there, at least for a few days anyway. He knew his way around the forest behind the bar. Hell, he’d been off-roading back there since he was fifteen. His shovel rode shotgun. Its rusted spade rested against the back window. If it could tell a story, it could spin a yarn about the bogs of Jefferson County. Could also tell you where the bodies were. Thankfully, like its owner, it couldn’t remember which ones had the misfortune of being alive during the process. He was on autopilot, the cabin of the truck filled with frosted air that made gooseflesh of his tightly wound muscles. He had to be safe. The offshoots from Rodney Road were littered with potholes, some so deep that even his four-wheel drive couldn’t make it through without getting stuck. Not that that mattered much. It wasn’t like he was going to move the body. Once deep enough into the woods, he shifted into park and cut the engine. He wanted to get out. Wanted to put on that stone cold veneer and give Donovan the goddamn courtesy he deserved. But he was locked in place. His fingers trembled as he swabbed the snuff from his bottom lip. Every movement was ginger, slow. He couldn’t feel anything and it frightened him. Reaching into the glove compartment, he pulled out his pistol. Just in case. That’s also why he had the potato in his pocket. Just in case. Swallowing his heart, he grabbed the shovel and made his way through the forest. Terrence’s Thunderbird kicked up dust as it speed down Hard Times Road, his eyes darting about as he looked for the barn Watkins said for them to meet at. It was just before ten, and while his head was in the gutter, he tried to keep his wits about him once he found the property. Turning left, the kicked-up stones scraped the Thunderbird’s undercarriage. His hand was lingered near the pistol in his waistband. The property looked ominous enough; it was a vacant stretch of land, thick with tallgrass and sandwiched between forests of magnolia. The farmhouse that was just to the north was burned to its brick foundation. A white pick-up was parked near the dilapidated barn, its roof sinking inward and its walls threatening to collapse at any moment. Its red asbestos planks had long since begun to chip. It was the kind of place where serial killers would find comfort staying. Watkins was here early, and everything inside Terrence screamed danger. He swung his car around into the thicket, backing up so he could keep his eyes on the barn and make a quick getaway if necessary. It might’ve been a false thought, but it was better to be prepared. Just in case. As he got out of his Thunderbird, the cicadas in the grass began hissing. Dusting his hands along the flaps of his leather jacket, Kingsley felt naked as he slowly walked to the barn, his hand never far away for the quick draw. It was the first time he’d ever seen Clarence Watkins, but even so, he thought the man was a tad overdressed for rural crime. He and Valley went way back, coming from a time where they could cook in California’s state parks and hide their meth in the crank shaft of their Harley’s without local law enforcement giving a damn as long as they kept it out of their county. Those days were dead. Anyone who wanted to push seriously now had to start thinking bigger picture, which meant dealing with the Super Labs and the cartels south of the border. The man was a mess of wrinkles and gray, the kind of person who would scream government agent if not for his ponytail and the fact that Terrence knew the truth. He wore a gaping smile. His eyes were bloodshot. "I take it you’re Kingsley." "I’d reckon you’re right." He motioned him over to the side of the barn, deep into the cover of shade. "Been in Mexico for a few years, but I never got used to the heat. Come on. The shade’s a bit nicer to talk in." Terrence, however, stayed ramrod straight and in place. "Rather not, friend." Watkins brushed it off, reaching into his breast pocket to pull out a cigarette. "Suit yourself." As he lit up, Kingsley started to speak. "If you don’t mind me asking, how’d you get my number?" "Would you buy it if I said I just took a lucky guess?" "Reckon I wouldn’t." Watkins took a long, hard drag before blowing the smoke out his nostrils. "Mutual friend. Nothing to worry about, really. Just figured, since I’m around, that I could put a face to what you’ve been dusting your spurs about." "Royce is the one who knows you. I don’t know you from shit in a cow pie." He chuckled at that. "I don’t get it." Another drag and he stepped from the shade, his eyes squinting through the sunlight. "Just a few minutes is all I ask. I got to get an answer from the source or my bosses will be none too pleased. You understand, right?" "I don’t, but I do." "They don’t usually do this," Watkins smirked as he ambled closer, "either people want in or we push ‘em out. They don’t know the lay of the land, though. You do. You got prime real estate here." "You do know that this county is poor as shit, right?" Terrence was starting to relax. He folded his arms across his chest, spitting off into the tallgrass while maintaining eye contact. "Prime real estate don’t consider trailers to be a positive attraction. You want good real estate, I suggest Adams County. They got Natchez. They got apartments. They got a hospital worth a damn." Clarence laughed again, this time with more vigor. "Tensas is a poor county too. Not a lot of people living between here and there, but lots of forests and swamps and places lawmen aren’t privy to travel because they’re usually underfunded." "You don’t know much about here, do you, sir?" "Nope. Can’t I say I do." Why the fuck does this got to be the only place around here with hills Jethro sweated as he stomped his way across the underbrush thickets of the forest. The magnolia trees were beginning to molt, and as much as he blanked out what had happened the night before, he found it strangely ease to remember just where the hell they’d been. He saw him as he rounded the hill, and while he’d wrestled with what he had to do, he hadn’t prepared himself for what he’d find. The shade mercifully shielded the details in the distance, but it couldn’t spare him for long. Donovan’s body was still lashed to a tree alright, his body slumping against its trunk. Flies were flitting around the swollen cuts on his shoulders. The rest of his back was absolutely ravaged. It was as if they’d taken a serrated blade to it, slicing through the flesh and spreading it out so they could show that even black men were pink inside. Pus slicked his wounds. He didn’t want to look at his face, figured it was better not to. But as he shuffled closer, the body came alive and spit out withered moans. Donovan didn’t have enough strength to move. FuckfuckfuckfuckfuckFUCK Jethro was panting. Donovan’s sickly shimmies against the bark rattled his scabs loose. This was an execution. A mercy kill. Something out of pity. Donovan would be better off dead. Better off living without the reminders across his back or the pain he’d suffered. But, most driving of all for Jethro, was the fear of what Ernie Spisak would do if he let him live. The potato was an urban legend and he didn’t know if it would work as a silencer. He stabbed the barrel of his pistol into its end. Tears stung his eyes. Ropes of vomit splayed from his lips. He pushed the spud against the poor bastard’s brainpan. Everything froze. "America wants to outsource everything, not just its car manufacturers and call centers. That’s where we come in. We’re the kicks they’ve made it possible to only get from someplace else." Terrence wasn’t buying it. "We manage fine just by ourselves." "Oh, you do?" Clarence’s smirk was far too annoying. "How much longer you think you can go around to drug stores with fake IDs just to get ephedrine? Or what about the battery acid, huh?" "We’re managin’—" "If you’re the one doing it, sure. But you can’t make piss for grams once these faggot Democrats get their way." It was getting harder to make product, with the drug companies always switching up their pill recipes to make it more difficult to get raw ephedrine. "I ain’t asking you to cook. Hell, I ain’t even asking you to sell. Cartel’s got penetration into Texas, some of Louisiana, gotten into the West...but we got to cut through to—" "Florida?" "East coast." It was Terrence’s turn to chuckle. It was one thing to cook the shit themselves. It was another to deal with dirty fuckers who kept fingers as souvenirs. To those kind of folk, having humanity would be a liability. "I don’t think so." Watkins tossed his cigarette away as he looked back toward the barn. "You got plenty of places here. Places like this. Better they go to use, you know?" "I don’t think so, Clarence, and I appreciate your offer." He gave a curt nod and sharpened his tone. "I implore you to leave me alone, now. You want to make a play, look at Adams. Lots more people there you can work with. I’ll pay you for the inconvenience of coming out this way, just as a measure of good faith." "That’s a shame," Watkins chewed on his bottom lip, running his thumbs along his tie. "It’s the way it is." Taking a step back, he paused, looking back to Kingsley. His glare was stoic. "Mind if we step into the barn? I think if you saw the kind of product we got, you’d strongly reconsider." He moseyed to the crumbling building, opening the door before motioning him to come over with his head. "At least you can pay me in here. Come on. Just in here." Terrence swallowed hard, looking forward as that damn smile slithered across his face. That’s when knew. If he stepped inside that barn, he’d never been seen again. His hand drew closer to his pistol as he started to backpedal, his eyes remaining focused forward as a queer rush of adrenaline shot through him. "I’d...I’d rather not." "You said you’d pay me." But he didn’t hear it. As Watkins continued trying to wave him over, he stepped around to the driver’s side of his Thunderbird, swinging the door open before darting into his seat. Clarence started to walk towards him, but by the time he’d gotten close, Terrence’s foot was lead against the gas. The pistol was in his hand the entire time. He couldn’t do it. He couldn’t fucking do it, Spisak be damned. Donovan was so out of it that Jethro was sure he didn’t know what was happening. He wouldn’t remember the gun to his head, nor would he remember the details about the night before. Even if he did, when faced with the burden of putting him down, he simply couldn’t do it. If anyone had seen him trying to brace the wounded, they’d laugh; Donovan towered over him, but then again, almost everyone else did too. His throat was raw, the bile burning along with the swigs of whiskey from his flask. Maybe that was what was numbing his mind as he drove down Fayette’s Main Street. He’d haphazardly covered Donovan up with a blanket and made sure he was below anyone’s sight. The musk of piss and shit did him few favors. The poor guy was murmuring and moaning. None of it made sense. The hard part came when he neared Fayette County Hospital. It wasn’t a big campus, the brick structure damn near blending into Main Street like any other building. It wasn’t safe to take him in now, and once the emotional wellspring of the situation wore off, there’d be questions from the police about how Donovan had arrived. While he might not remember anything, the hospital staff would, and if they didn’t, the sheets he’d be forced to sign would point in his direction. It wasn’t like he was a stranger to those people. He kept an eye on the traffic near the entrance, waiting for the right time when nobody was around. It didn’t take long. He opened the passenger’s side door, and without hesitation, shoved Donovan out of the truck to the doorstep of the hospital. It was better this way; the less questions the better. And with that out of the way, he sucked down whatever was left in his flask before he got to News Lake. It was time to figure what he was going to tell the Boogeyman. He didn’t remember how he got back to the Stonewall Motel, but Terrence Kingsley was still quivering with adrenaline. Sitting in the parking lot for a few minutes, he slapped himself across the face to try and calm himself down. Whatever had just happened, it was clear; they were all in danger. Clarence Watkins was just one man, but what he represented made a habit out of mauling police task forces. They had unlimited resources. And like all vampires, once invited in, they come and go as they please. When he’d taken the county, it had been handed to him because there was a vacuum in power. It was a small spat of land, dirt poor and without the infrastructure necessary to deal with them. The most they had to worry about were speeding tickets. And while he’d ruled it, it wasn’t as though he’d constructed some elaborate empire. He had five men. Ragtag bumpkins to the bone. They’d hustled country folk who knew no better and pushed meth to those who were too lazy to make their own. It was an easy life. He got out of the Thunderbird and knocked on Royce’s door. It wasn’t the time for pounding. It was time to swallow his pride and apologize. The door was eased open, and to his shock, Royce stepped aside and let him in without hesitation. Bobby Crowley Junior was sitting on the floor, his Kool-Aid stained upper lip curled outward as he watched the television. He looked like a five-year-old trapped inside a sixteen-year-old’s body. "We need to talk." "Yeah we do," Kingsley started, his fingers nervously peeling away his bandanna before running through his hair. "I’m sorry about earlier. Things have been going on in my head, you know?" "Bobby," Royce patted the young man on the back. For all his personal faults, he seemed to have a world of patience in dealing with the mentally challenged Crowley boy, no thanks to his brother always leaving him at his doorstep to play babysitter. "Need you to go in the bathroom for a bit. Me and Terrence have to talk." He whined through the nudges to stand up. "But I don’t need to go." "I’ll take you out for some ice cream later, okay?" Terrence said as he faked a smile. "Just...we need to talk. Okay?" Bobby nodded his head, scampering through the kitchen and shutting the door behind him. Now alone, Terrence slumped onto the couch, looking down at the fractured frame of the coffee table. "Something happen—" "It’s nothing," Royce said as he slowly sat in his easy chair. "I need to talk to you—" "Royce, it’s Watkins." "I didn’t give him your number." "It don’t matter if you did or not—" "I didn’t—" "Look," Kingsley was quick to cut him off. "This is bigger than that right now." "Yeah," Royce murmured, his hands clasped together and his elbows resting on his creaky knees. Terrence ripped a pack of cigarettes from his pocket, pulling one out and biting off the filter before lighting it up. He was slouched over, just like his fellow man. He was starting to calm down as he sucked down the nicotine. "Watkins is making a move. I don’t know what he has, I don’t know how many people are around, but he’s not here to beg for a deal anymore. He’s gonna make it with or without us." "What the hell happened?" "Nothing," he tried to seem reassuring through drags, but it wasn’t much use. "He took me out to the country and I told him no. Then, he wanted me to go into the barn he’d been shacked up in. Now, I don’t know if the thing was rigged to blow or if he’s got fifteen guys with lead trigger fingers, but there’s more than just him now or else he wouldn’t be willing to do something like that." "That sounds like him alright," Valley coughed. "He’s always been a pushover who pumps out his chest when the chips are in his favor." "What are we gonna do?" "You’re not gonna do anything." "Don’t give me that shit right now. It’s not the time." "Sorry. You’re right." No immediate course of action was clear, and their silence said as much. Maybe they needed more heads in the room. "Let’s get Benny and Donovan and Jethro in on this." That caused Royce to stand up. He walked around the edge of the motel room, his fingers flicking the blinds down. "Benny was supposed to be here to help me cook today, but he ain’t been around. Bobby ain’t seen him in over a day. Jethro’s prepaid goes straight to voicemail. Donovan—" Anger started to well inside Kingsley as he stood up. "What the fuck is wrong—" Royce shot his head back toward him. His voice was gravel. "Donovan’s in the hospital." His raw emotion dissipated quickly. "What?" "He’s in the hospital. Was left at the door." Valley pursed his lips as he turned off the television. "When they took him to ER, he was in shock. But that’s not the worst part. They said his back was nearly sheared to the bone. It’s mess. There’s infections. There’s cuts that need stitches. Some of his ribs have been broken." He needed a moment to steady himself, not out of emotion, but out of disgust. "They don’t know if he’s going to make it. His back, Terrence...chunks of it are missing. They hazard a guess that it was something like a rope, but stronger." His eyes were on the floor. "Something like...an extension cord. Sound like anybody we know?" Terrence knew the truth the more graphic the description got, but he was frozen, unable to respond. It was a calling card. But that son-of-a-bitch was gone, sent up to Parchman Farm five years ago. Clarence Watkins could’ve had a legion behind him and it wouldn’t have changed reality; with the Boogeyman stalking around the edges of Jefferson County, he was nothing but small potatoes. He hadn’t done it in years, but suddenly, Terrence Kingsley felt compelled to pray. Spisak didn’t have to know how Donovan went missing, just that he was missing. Maybe it was some folks going for a jog through the forest? Maybe it was some hunting dogs? But it wasn’t him. Spisak didn’t have know it was him. Or he could tell him that he buried him. That might buy enough time to get out of town, and even though he had shit for shoestrings in his pocket, he could always hitch a ride somewhere. Anywhere. Anywhere but here. Jethro could hear Spisak stomping around inside the shack. His mind went blank. He went to take a swig from his flask, but it was empty. He tossed it away and walked inside, losing his breath somewhere in between. The Devil hissed as his bony fingers combed across the table, his eyes burning and the steam rising from the spider web tendrils tattooed to his skull. "Where’d you put ‘em?" "I—" "Where’d you put him, Jethro." "He wasn’t there, Ernie." "Wha?" "Some joggers...the police were—" Spisak didn’t hesitate. He drew his gun. He squeezed the trigger. The gunshot ripped through Jethro’s right knee, shattering the kneecap and dropping him on the floor.
