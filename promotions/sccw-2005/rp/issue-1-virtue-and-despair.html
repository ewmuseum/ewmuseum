Issue 1: Virtue and Despair

undated
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: Varied Years ago there was a general, and through the strength of his army he ruled over the countryside, keeping the people safe under his hand. His soldiers were small in number, but fiercely loyal. Any order he gave his men was carried out without a second thought. But, as protective as he was of his land, he was even more protective of his only daughter. When she told him of her love for a blacksmith he was hesitant, until he learned of the master's skill at his craft. He was a weapon smith; a master steel shaper. 'Make me a weapon,' he said, 'and you will have my daughter's hand in marriage.' The young man was all too eager to set about his task, immediately retreating back to his shop to begin preparations. There he toiled over the formula, blending the right metals to forge an alloy that would mirror his love. It was designed in her honor, it would flow through the air with the grace of the wind, but its resolve would not waver, no matter the obstacle. Late into the night did he toil, testing and retesting until at last he had a composite that would fit his needs. In the first light of the morning, the young man stepped to his forge and began smelting his ores. The metal was heated and reheated, folded and refolded, with every pass removing any imperfections he discovered. Small holes were bored into the blade to reduce the resistance of the air. Lastly, so that her father might remember his daughter, the blade master took from her a lock of hair and wove it into the hilt. Now his child would be forever with him in body and in spirit, whether in battle or at rest. Thus was born the Maiden's Virtue. The young smith delivered the weapon on a cold winter morning, when the leaves had fallen and the frost had covered the earth. He knelt before his lord as he unwrapped his masterpiece, smiling as he offered the product of months hard work. The general took the weapon gently in his hand, noting immediately how light it felt. The wind rushed through the pores in the blade as it danced through the air, filling the room with a kind of light song. 'Does it please you?' the young smith asked, eager for his answer. The general smiled as he brought the sword to his side. Truly he was pleased. It was a weapon like no other he had a seen, a true masterwork of craftsmanship. But, he thought, if the smith could make one such weapon, then what would stop him from forging a rival. The existence of such a sword could rival his rule, perhaps even depose him. His fingers tightened around the hilt and he worked to push the thought of his daughter from his mind. The blade shrieked as it whipped through the air, striking down the young blade master in one stroke. When the news of her love's death reached her ears, the general's daughter became secluded and withdrawn. Rarely did she speak, choosing instead to spend much of her time wandering the path that led to her love's shop. Every day she would rap lightly against the door, hoping against hope that he would answer and take her in his arms, but when no answer came she would lower her head and began the slow walk back to her home. She crept silently into her father's room on the night she learned the truth. Her father had told her that robbers had attacked her love in his sleep, using one of his own creations against him. He had told her that the men were chased into the woods before they were rounded up by his army and defeated. Her love had been given a hero's burial, and would forever be remembered for his service to the land. The anger raged inside her as she reached for his weapon -- the weapon that her love had crafted. He fingers trembled at the touch, but her resolve held. She brought both hands together, raising the blade slowly up. Her father's eyes slowly opened, but his cries were muffled, drowned out by the scream of the blade as it tore through the air and took his head. The blade was born as a symbol of love, but as she held it now in her hands all she could feel was hatred. It had taken her love from her, and she had taken her father with it. As the approaching footsteps grew louder, she turned the sword in her hands, and as the tears fell from her eyes she gave into to the siren song of the blade and pierced her heart. The Maiden's Virtue reborn as became the Maiden's Despair. * * * * * * "Dad, where are we going?" the voice from the backseat asked; his question was shy, his voice was small. He could tell that his father was upset from the way that his hands held the steering wheel and from the words that slipped from his lips every few moments. And while he didn’t want to upset him further, he was curious after all, and that curiosity had to be satisfied. His father looked into the rearview mirror and summoned all his courage into a smile. "Just over the bridge, champ. Just over the bridge and then we’ll find someplace new to live." "I’m scared." The man smiled again into the rearview mirror, now with a lump forming in his throat. Glancing to his wife in the passenger seat, he could tell that she felt the same. They were both scared, as was half the city. The traffic was a clear indication of that. People had left before, but never had there been an exodus quite like this. "It’ll be okay," he reassured his son. He hoped it wasn’t a lie. It seemed to be enough to sate him, at least for the time being, and the boy went back to leaning against the restraints of his seatbelt for a better view out the window. There were a lot of cars, he thought, and an awful lot of people. He wondered if something important was happening on the other side of the bridge. "Dad?" he asked again. "Why are we leaving?" There it was, the hard question. The man thought for a moment on how to answer, even looking to his wife for support. She pursed her lips, tears welling in her eyes, and turned away. The boy was too young to understand what went on in the city they sought to flee. Every now and again he would sit in front of the television during the evening news and point. "Superhero!" he would say, gesturing to the costumed crusader in the corner of his screen. But his father never had the heart to tell him why they were on the news. This city stole the hearts of good men. It corrupted their virtue, it broke their conviction, and it led them to their end. For every good man who died, for every man in a colorful costume who graced his television, the man knew that there were at least a half dozen of a different ilk who tried to put them there. His son knew heroes and villains only through comic books and cartoons, and for now at least that innocence would be preserved. "We have to," his father answered. He wasn’t aware of it, but his grip on the wheel had tightened. The color had drained from his knuckles, and he could no longer feel the tips of his fingers. "Oh." A flash in the distance caught the boy’s attention, and he gripped the handle on the door to roll down his window. "Whoa," he whispered as the flash got closer. "Baby, what are you looking at?" his mothered asked him, but whatever it was already had him mesmerized. He was working his way to his knees, fighting against the seatbelt that tried to keep him in place, all so he could get a better look. "Baby?" His father cursed again under his breath, and slammed the palm of his hand into the horn. Traffic didn’t move. The boy pulled his seatbelt looser and knelt at the window. He pressed his tiny hands against the glass and poked his head through the opening. A third flash appeared in the sky, this one much, much closer. "Cool!" A burning sphere shot through the sky just beyond the bridge, whizzing through the air and leaving a trail of smoke behind it. He watched it fly out of sight. "Baby, get back in the car!" his mother shouted from the front seat, but it was too late. The bridge shook as the fourth missile found its target, detonating against a concrete pillar. * * * * * * The support crumbled under the blast, and the bridge buckled. The air was filled with screams and panicked cries from both outside the car and within. Instinctively the boy covered his head and got as low as the restraints of the car would allow him, just like he’d practiced in school. A second blast rocked the bridge and the car lurched forward. His mother gasped as the force of the seatbelt drove the air from her lungs. All around them the world collapsed. The bridge, with its foundation compromised and unable to sustain the weight of its load, bent in on itself. The steel supports groaned beneath them as the car lurched forward again, slamming into the back of a station wagon. The boy pressed his hands to his ears, trying to drown out the screams. 'Baby, it's going to be okay,' he heard his mother say. She was sobbing, unable to control herself. He wanted to believe her, he wanted it so badly, but he knew. 'It's going to be okay, baby. I promise.' It was a lie. The ground beneath them cracked and moaned as a large chunk of asphalt broke away from the supports and plunged into the icy depths below, taking with it a few cars and a dozen poor souls. The boy watched as the driver of the car next to theirs kicked open his door and tried to run for safety, but a second tremor knocked him off balance. The boy watched in horror as the man tumbled towards the split, but he did not fall. A piece of rebar, jagged and bent, found a home in his chest and stopped his momentum. Terrified, the boy lost control of his bladder. 'Daddy! Daddy!' he cried. He reached out his arms towards the front seat. 'It's alright, it's alright!' His father turned, but as his eyes moved to his son they caught sight of a delivery truck that had lost control, careening towards them and unable to stop. 'Hold on! Just hold on!' He reached for the emergency lever, desperate to keep his foot on the brakes, but it was no use. The shock of the impact jarred him, his foot came up, and the car began to roll. By the time he regained control it was too late. The car was already over the edge and falling. The car hit the water below, and the boy's world fell into silence. He struggled against the seatbelt as the car sank deeper and deeper into the crushing black. His first thought was to flee, to escape through the open window and swim to the surface, but in the front seat neither his mother or father were moving. He tried to shake them awake, to disengage the latch on their seatbelts, but with every passing second the car sank further and further. His eyes stung, his lungs burned, and the water was becoming ever colder. The boy pushed himself through the window and frantically kicked his legs. As he neared the surface he glanced down into the dark and watched as a thousand hands, gaunt and decayed, drag his parents down into a watery prison. He kicked harder when some of the hands released the car and slowly rose to meet him. He reached for the surface, now only feet away, and watched in horror as it turned to glass before his eyes, held firmly in place by a thousand hands of stone. Water filled his lungs as he tried to scream, suffocating him, drowning him. With his energy spent and his will broken he surrendered to the current. He offered no resistance to the hand that took him, pulling him from the sea and into the air. He gasped for air as hands pressed against his chest and drove the water from his lungs. In between coughs and panicked breaths he opened his eyes and looked into an unfamiliar face. The Cosmonaut had only arrived in the city a few weeks ago, but already was making a name for himself as a force for justice. He was young, barely twenty years old, with a mop of blonde hair and kind eyes. The boy looked up at this man, who only moments ago had saved his life, and for a moment felt at peace. Everything would be okay. 'You are safe, comrade,' The Cosmonaut said in a voice the boy found strange. He had heard voices like it before, but only on television, and even then only when the news spoke of something called 'The Reds'. Stranger than his voice was his outfit, silver and sparkling, with what the boy believed was a jetpack strapped to his back. His parents had always told him that jetpacks didn't exist. It was then that he remembered them, having forgotten in the chaos surrounding his fight for life that his mother and father were still trapped in the car and sinking faster with each passing second. 'My mom and dad,' he pleaded as tears burst from his eyes. The Cosmonaut's smile vanished, and with a shrug of his shoulders let his pack fall to the deck of the ship. 'Do not vorry,' he said, patting the boy on the head as he stepped to the edge. 'I vill get dem.' He steadied himself as the boat rocked and prepared to dive. 'You're in mah way, boy,' hissed a voice from behind him. The young boy had never seen this new figure before either, but he also spoke funny. A different kind of funny. To the child he sounded like the cartoons he watched with his father, like Foghorn Leghorn, only deeper, like he'd been chewing gravel his entire life. A lit cigarette hung between curled lips, which he parted in a grin to reveal a wall of yellow teeth. The Kingsnake was new to these parts as well. Many chose not to believe the tales of cruelty they'd heard. After all, no man could be so low, so depraved, to commit the acts that had been associated with his name. They were wrong. 'Please, stand aside. There is vork to be done.' The Kingsnake exhaled a cloud of smoke with his laugh. 'This here's a reckonin', boy, and I'm on the path of the righteous. I got a whole flock behind me. So when the shepherd tells ya' ta' get out his way, son...' he pulled the cigarette from his lips and tossed it casually over the side of the boat. 'Ya' do it.' The Cosmonaut tried to ignore him as he turned his attention back to the water and prepared again to dive. 'I said you're in mah way.' The Kingsnake's hand flew to his side and into the leather sack that hung there. From it he drew a length of barbed wire, which cut the air and wrapped itself around the Cosmonaut's neck. The boy covered his eyes, but the image of the blood starting to fall had already burned itself into his mind. He held them shut as the Cosmonaut struggled against the biting tether that slowly drained his life. They stayed closed as blood poured from his savior's neck and pooled with the water on the deck of the ship. The Kingsnake removed the hellish noose from the Cosmonaut and shoved him over the side of the ship, where a thousand morbid hands would drag him to his end. 'Pity,' he said, lighting another cigarette. * * * * * * 'I thought I told you to leave me alone; to stay away from this part of town.' The Crooked Man looked down upon his victim, draping him in the dark of his shadow. The young man wiped the streak of blood away from the corner of his mouth, and then spat some onto the floor. The Crooked Man simply sighed. 'Alright, pick him up,' he said. Two men flanked the young man, each one taking him by an arm and hoisting him to his feet. The Crooked Man nodded his approval and hopped down from the crate on which he stood, straightening his tie and adjusting his fedora as he approached his captive. 'This is...' he paused, glancing up at the intruder. Eight inches worth of height separated them, and this only made him all the more annoyed. 'This isn't going to work.' A nod to his henchmen signaled them to lean a bit on their captive, bringing him eye-to-eye with their employer. 'Much better.' 'Look,' he continued. "I’m a busy man. This little empire doesn’t just run itself, you know. But I’m starting to get a little tired of these meetings, so what say you and I make this the last one, huh? After all – what’s that expression again?" "Motherfuckers got to be told, sir," the smaller of the henchmen said. "Motherfuckers got to be told," the Crooked Man repeated. The young man closed his eyes and took a deep breath, steeling himself for the inevitable. He’d faced death before, on the bridge and on the boat, but it never felt this close; it never felt this real. The Crooked Man chuckled. "What, you think I’m going to kill you?" He raised his brow and glanced from one thug to the next, always smiling. "No, we’re not going to do that. I like this suit, you see. Just had it cleaned, and blood just, well, you can never get it out, no matter how hard you try." "And he’s tried." "Shut up." "Sorry, sir." "Just throw the kid out already." The hands on his arms tightened. Fingers dug into his flesh. There would be bruises to tend to, and wounds to let heal, but at least he’d be alive. The thought offered him a little comfort as he was thrown from the edge of a loading dock onto the hard asphalt below. * * * * * * He staggered to the water’s edge, drained and weary from his encounter with the Crooked Man. The young man leaned against a broken pillar. It had once supported a bridge, it would now support him. His eyes wandered to the water. Twenty years ago he had lost his family there, and in the moments where his own life was saved he watched in horror as his savior was executed in front of him. In the years that followed those events the power and influence of the Kingsnake had grown. He now made his home in the swamps on the outskirts of town, a place where even the foolish feared to tread. Rumors abounded of a dark magic, a kind of voodoo, that hung in the air over the bog, bathing it in a perpetual haze. Slowly the young man leaned down to the water, scooping some in his hand to wash away the caked blood from his face. But as his hand disturbed the surface he watched a thousand fingers drift in the ripples, and jerked his palm back. Behind him the city was silent, enjoying a rare moment of serenity. There had been heroes here once. When he was younger they would show pictures on the news, and he would delight in listening to the broadcasters tell stories of their greatness. He wasn’t sure exactly when it happened, but one day that all seemed to change. Villains had rose up to challenge those who would protect the city, growing more hostile and violent with each passing day. Soon the news stopped reporting on the heroes: there were none left. The Lionheart had protected the city under his massive paw, but problems within his own pride had drawn his attention elsewhere. The Hydra had recently defected, having chosen to forsake the life she led in favor of something better. She was powerful, no doubt, but had yet to truly prove herself. The few honorable men and women left had yet to trust her. The city had taken his parents, his savior, his pride, even a means to escape. But it needed a champion – someone to take up its flag and return it to its former glory. It needed someone to ensure that no one else ever had to suffer what he had. He wanted to cry out, to shout at the heavens and curse the world, but as he opened his mouth a wave of fatigue swept over him, and he collapsed to the ground. Another piece among the broken. * * * * * * In a tower at the heart of the city he stood, surveying his realm. The night was warm, humid after a midday rain, and a few stars peeked through the parting clouds. They called him a warlord, they called him a tyrant, and for good reason: this was his city. He had the control, he had the power, and no matter how many other villains rose through the ranks, any who wished to rule knew that it was him they would need to dethrone. He popped a few pills into his mouth, washing them down with the last drops from a vodka bottle. The raven-haired woman sashayed up behind him and wrapped an arm around his waist. Her fingers traced the contours of a scar than ran the length of his chest, one of many paths on the map of depravity that covered his body. His own hand moved as well, falling to the hilt of the blade that hung at his waist.
