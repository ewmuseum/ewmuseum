Photographic Memoirs.

25 March 2008

Date: 3/25/08 .memoirs. His eyes burned. They say that blinking is an unconscious act, and for the most part it is. That is, unless your eyes are transfixed on something that sustains your interest, like a beautiful sunset or a beautiful woman. Then, blinking becomes secondary, and it isn’t until your vision begins to go blurry and they start to sting that you realize you haven’t blinked in minutes. Let’s just say Elliott Rollins is attentive. The box had settled itself at his front door around 2:30 in the afternoon. Elliott had estimated that was when it had first arrived, as neither he nor Hannah were home at that time. When they did arrive, it was waiting for them on the doormat outside. It was a cardboard box, about a foot long and half a foot wide, and had no markings of significance on it. The letter "R" was scrawled illegibly at the corner with marker, but outside of that, there was no clue as to the origins of the box, or who had delivered it. When Elliott took the box in to the small apartment he and Hannah now shared, he opened it delicately. He wasn’t sure if it was from a fan or an enemy, so he slit in to it with a utility knife with a certain gracefulness that he lacked opening the few gifts he received at Christmas, or a letter he received from bill collectors. He wasn’t sure if it was a bomb or a kitten, neither of which he wanted to take a knife to, but when he finally got the wrapping tape peeled from it, he realized its contents were also something better left untouched by a sharp blade. They were photographs. It took him several moments to recognize the people in them. Some were dated back as far as 1960, and most were worn and faded to the point of complete ambiguity. The few that he could see clearly, where the photographers eye was focused, the subject steady, and time had not completely tarnished them, he stared at for minutes. He examined them thoroughly, seeking any hints as to who the people in the pictures were. Then, after a few minutes of inspection, he felt a ping of recognition; the dark black hair of a child hiding behind a sofa in one picture, the raven hair of his mother who smiled as she clung to him in the next, and distant relatives that now only existed in these pictures. They were the brief legacy these people shared; the only evidence of their existence. It was Elliott Rollins’ history. His past; past that he had either shunned or forgotten, all collected on celluloid negatives that, when turned positive, brought back a feeling of nostalgia in the viewer, even if they were Polaroid’s taken mere minutes ago. As his eyes scanned them, more rapidly and feverishly, waves of reminiscence hit him. Hannah clung at his shoulder, watching his face as he remembered. That was his aunt’s living room, that was his grandmother’s dining room, and that was his ninth grade homecoming date -- what was her name? -- there were countless smiling faces to study. Every picture took him back to a particular moment in time, and it wasn’t until his eyes were completely exhausted that he shut them, sinking back on his hunches. The box had never made it past the living room floor. "What are these?" Hannah asked quietly. A slight smile hit his face. "Memoirs." He picked up one of the photographs; it was of him, his mother and father, their parents, and his great uncle. "It’s weird to think," he said, as his eyes continued to scan it, "I’m the only one still living in this picture." It didn’t really sadden him as he said it. He didn’t feel much of anything, really. .all in. His tongue burned. "Fuck, that’s hot," Elliott nearly squealed as he put the coffee down on the table. His mind must have been wandering, as he hadn’t noticed that the waitress had refreshed his half-empty cup just a few seconds ago. William McDormant sat across from him, still looking hung over from the Saint Patrick’s Day show. His eyes were hollow and dark, and the skin on his face drooped. He, at the moment, was not the image of perfection that he tried to hard to maintain. "Brilliant," McDormant muttered to the tabletop. "Call of the match, the old boy’s injured." "You’re losing some of your wit, Billy," Elliott said with a wry smirk. McDormant hated it when anyone called him ‘Billy,’ especially so when it was the man sitting across from him. "And you’re losing some of your luster," McDormant retorted. "What the fuck was the deal last week? You missed three promotional events, and not to mention you were nowhere to be found when you were supposed to do that scheduled interview. We’re going in to one of the most vaunted pay-per-views in the history of this place, and you’re fucking up left and right." Elliott cocked an eyebrow. "This place is like, a month old." "Just because the name changed doesn’t mean it’s lost its history," McDormant corrected him. "You’re quickly becoming one of the finest wrestlers to step foot in FUSE, SCCW, or otherwise, but you seem to be forgetting the fact that it’s not just about what you do in the ring. This is sports entertainment, and that means going out there and wowing the fans with your backstage antics and in-ring shoots. Wrestling fans have short attention spans, and the second you take yourself out of the spotlight is just more work we have to do to get you back in to it." "Yes master," Elliott said dryly, not even paying attention to his manager. McDormant’s upper lip curled. "Can I ask you something?" "Sure," Elliott shrugged. "Do you even want to do this anymore?" McDormant said. Elliott’s eyes lifted their gaze from the table and met William’s. "Seriously," Slick continued, "do you even give a shit about this sport? I know you did, I’m not questioning that, but as of late you just seem like you’re going through the motions. You busted ass to get to where you are, but now… you’ve just gotten fucking lazy." Elliott’s lips wrapped in to a snarl. "Of course I do." "Then fucking act like it," McDormant snapped. "You’re the LiveWire champion. You’re one victory away from getting a shot at the Gateway championship, and the mere fact that we even have to have this conversation just shows me that you’re not on your game, and you absolutely need to be if you plan on walking away this week with a win." "You don’t know what you’re talking about," Elliott said defensively. McDormant scoffed. "You really think you’re giving it your all out there?" "Every week," Rollins affirmed. "Do you even know who you’re facing at Snake Eyes?" McDormant asked. Rollins was silent for a moment. "The card’s out?" McDormant sighed. He had known there were issues, but he didn’t realize just how far they had reached. "The card’s been out for a few days, and thank you for proving my point. So, just a little word of advice: pay the fuck attention. You think this shit isn’t important, that all you have to do is go out there and bust skulls and be super-hulky badass Elliott Rollins, but there’s more to it than that. You need to strategize, you need to game plan, you need to know their weaknesses before you get in there, or that shiny strap you covet so much will be around some other fuckers waist, and it won’t look quite as good there." "Who am I facing then?" Rollins asked with moderate disinterest. "Vassago and The True Prophet," McDormant confirmed. Rollins cooled his coffee a little. "Never heard of them," Elliott shrugged. McDormant stared at him, but his focus was on the coffee. "You’re unbelievable, you know that? Listen to me when I say this: you are one victory away from a Gateway Title shot. You win, you’re in, and I guarantee you there is no way that Pierre will beat you. If you make it past Snake Eyes, you will very likely be the next Gateway Champion, and you’re sitting here like you could give a shit less." Rollins scoffed in to his coffee. "Okay, so you’re saying there’s no way Pierre can beat me, but you’re making it out like Prophet and Vassago are my mortal enemies and that I don’t stand a chance in Hell against them unless I know their mother’s maiden name and the name of their childhood pet. Which one is it?" "Fuck you," McDormant snarled. "That’s which one it is. I’m done wasting my time." McDormant stood to leave. Rollins just sighed. It was like dealing with a woman; a woman that didn’t put out or even have a vagina for that matter, but was equally overdramatic and emotionally distressed at any sign of dissent. "Fine," Elliott said, simply so his manager would stop his hissyfit, "tell me a little bit about them." In reality, Rollins’ confidence was growing more and more by the week, and if he had been facing Jonathan Rhine at Snake Eyes, he would have the same plan of preparation for him as well. At the moment, Elliott felt untouchable, and it would take the concentrated effort every man on the roster to stop him from making it to the top. .countryside. His eyes burned. He had been staring out the window for hours. The countryside passed like an impressionist painting, blurred and difficult to comprehend. The 98 Silver Meteor had taken him from Orlando to Washington D.C., where he had a few hours of layover time to visit the capitol. It amazed him as he walked to see that the surrounding areas of the central gathering spot for the United States government was, for the most part, rundown. Even the people seemed worn out. From there, he took the 29 Capitol Limited to Union Station in Chicago. He walked around downtown Chicago, ate at a nice bistro that garnered uncomfortable looks from fashionable folks, before he was back on the 3 Southwest Chief to Los Angeles. To him, it had seemed like a roundabout way to get to his final destination, which was San Diego. Snake Eyes. The trip, in total, took him a little over four days. It took him four days to travel from Orlando to San Diego, with few stops in between. He had sat or slept for over 96 hours straight, and was offered few breaks to step out of the train and get a cigarette before being carted back on and herded to the next station. He was afforded little amenities in the sleeper cart, and had finished what remained of Atlas Shrugged before they made it too far out of Chicago. The batteries for the portable DVD player had run out only about five hours after leaving Orlando, and they had forgotten the charger. He was starting to get cabin fever, and his eyes were constantly seeking for a way out -- that window looked tempting -- but he couldn’t argue that it had been an enlightening experience. He had written, which he hadn’t done in years, personal things, but was too stubborn to call it a diary. He also got to spend quality time with Hannah Rose, something that wasn’t an abundance at this point in his career. When the train pulled in to the San Diego station, he was lucid. He couldn’t comprehend that this was his stop; that the ride was over, and he was freed from the confines of the makeshift apartment on the Amtrak train he called home for the past four days. When he stepped outside, the light hit him, staggering him back as his eyes struggled to adjust. Then there was the fucking taxi. He groaned quietly as he shifted uncomfortably in the back seat, watching as San Diego blurred past him. They had to rush to the arena for a press conference before they would hurry back to the apartment, shower, and then prepare for a night of meet and greets and publicity parties. Snake Eyes would be the first pay-per-view in SCCW, and it was important for the transition between brands that all eyes were on the show. If that wasn’t enough pressure, Elliott himself felt exceptionally stressed to make the night memorable, as he was one of the mainstream stars on the card. The night would be memorable, for sure.
