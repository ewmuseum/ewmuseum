Date: 1/12/08 .who gives a shit?. "What?" Elliott Rollins looked on, flabbergasted. Slick Willy's jaw was shut, and his eyes were empty as he looked at Rollins from across the diner table. Had that table not been there to separate the two, Rollins would have assuredly been throttling his current manager, possibly soon-to-be-ex-manager. "So your mom died," he said, as insensitively as the previous question he posed. "Everyone's mom dies. My mom died when I was three. You've really spent ten years pining over this?" McDormant stirred his coffee. Rollins had yet to blink. "What?" McDormant inquired, taking a sip. "You do realize how much it took me to actually tell you this?" Rollins asked, convinced that his manager had a vacant hole in his chest somewhere. "You realize you're only the second person I told?" "That your mom died?" McDormant said, shrugging his shoulders flippantly. "Is it a secret?" "No, it's just..." Rollins' voice trailed off. "It's difficult to talk about." "You're fucking weird, dude," McDormant replied, downing the last sip of his coffee and motioning to the waitress for more. "I mean, and when I say that, don't think I haven't had my share of encounters with weird people." "What's so weird about that?" Rollins asked. "Everything," McDormant said with a smirk. "You're convinced that you killed your mother by praying to a God you're not even sure is there, and then you've been beating yourself up over it for the better part of a decade for it? That's not normal behavior." "Blaming yourself is a common step in the grieving process," Rollins said, sounding like he was reading from a text book, "and it can be the hardest one to get past. Some people never do." McDormant scoffed. "Listen to you. You're really buying in to what that woman's saying, aren't you?" Rollins had no reply. "Look, I set several small house fires in my fathers mansion, causing him to have a heart attack and die in my arms." Rollins looked at him sympathetically. "See?" McDormant said. "Fucking easy." Rollins suddenly felt a slight ping of compassion for him. "And you're okay?" "Yeah, I'm fine," McDormant shrugged. "How long did it take you to get over it?" "I don't know," McDormant said, nodding to the waitress as she filled his cup. "Six months or something?" Rollins was constantly astounded by the man sitting across from him, but for entirely the wrong reasons. "You and I are entirely different people." McDormant nodded as that trademark smirk creeped to his lips. "That's very true." .And how did that make you feel?. He wasn't sure how he felt then. Right now, however, he felt like strangling her. Elliott Rollins sat next to Margaret Thapes on that trademark couch, his eyes void of any emotion. They say that the eyes are the gateway to the soul, so either Elliott Rollins has no soul to speak of, or he has one Hell of a poker face as his eyes read absolutely nothing. Behind those eyes, though, a man was fighting every urge to leap across the couch and wrap his hands around the leathered skin that housed this woman's breathing passages. "What the fuck?" It wasn't elegant, but it was the most he could muster. She looked on as if she had asked him what his favorite color was. She was oblivious to his intentions, and if he was a man of lesser self-restraint, he would have made it well-known what he currently wanted to do. She was on the verge of losing him, and she was none the wiser. "Well?" Twist the knife. "Well what?" he hissed. "How did you feel when she died?" It wasn't meant to be cruel, although one could interpret it as such. Elliott just shook his head, trying to keep himself composed, only this time for much different reasons. "How the fuck do you think I felt?" he snapped. So much for keeping himself composed. "Have you ever lost anyone, doctor?" he continued. "Have you ever felt anything at all?" She blinked, unsure of what had happened to cause such a riff between the two of them. "Are you even human?" he prodded. "Because I'm really beginning to question how you can stomach sitting there, day in and day out, offering advice to people who's pain you can't relate to, not even in the slightest." She was silent, as was he. This could either get much better, or much worse. .remake. Elliott Rollins and William McDormant entered the office nervously. Elliott was nervous because he wasn't sure what to expect. William was nervous because he had based his entire managerial career around a man who, for better or worse, had little or no personality to speak of. "William!" a man shouted from behind a desk. As they furthered in to the office, Rollins' eyes scanned the room, which he tended to do. What he found was alabaster décor and expensive furniture lined the walls of the rather expansive office. The rotund man leaped from behind his desk as fast as his body would allow it. He was dressed in a fine Italian suit which fit his body a little too snuggly, but still made him look far more debonair than the sweats he would be found in lounging around the house. This job, as it were, was all about presentation. "Bryant," McDormant said with a smirk. William McDormant and Bryant Sharpe had known each other for quite some time, but throughout the past couple of years, had lost touch with one another. "Glad to see you're still fat," McDormant said as he approached. "Glad to see you're still an asshole," Sharpe responded, extending an arm to his former partner in crime. They shook hands, neither sure how long it had been since they'd seen each other last so neither mentioned it. Instead, Sharpe turned his attention to Rollins. "So this is him?" Sharpe asked, wondering how he would make something out of nothing. "He's a looker." Sharpe smirked as he invited the two of them to sit down. "You're doing well for yourself," McDormant said as he took a seat. Sharpe nodded, returning to the leather chair behind his desk which looked far more comfortable than the high-rise wooden chairs McDormant and Elliott were now sitting on. "You're not," Sharpe said, his eyes shifting back to Rollins. McDormant leaned towards Rollins. "Don't worry about this tubby piece of shit," he whispered, "he thinks he's better than he is." McDormant smiled the friendliest smile he could fake as he leaned back in his chair. "That's why we're here," he said. "We need you to work that wonderful magic of yours." Sharpe nodded again. "You said." "Stand," he said to Elliott, more of a demand than a request. He did so, standing up in his black band tee shirt and more-than-slightly torn jeans, his black hair tied tightly back. Sharpe gave him the once-over, sizing up our hero in seconds. "This is going to take a lot of work." .theory of the superman. When Elliott turned fourteen, his aunt gave him a copy of Friedrich Nietzsche's 'Thus Spake Zarathustra' for his birthday. His mother worried that the book would be too difficult for a mind as young as his to comprehend. His mother also feared the influence a man who claimed God was dead would have on her son. The only real influence it had on him was that he read constantly, about philosophy and life and death and religion, and instead of it guiding him, it only confused him more. One thing that stuck out in his mind as he sat in Dr. Thapes' office, though, was Nietzsche's theory of eternal recurrence. The theory that, as some scholars argued, life repeated itself over and over, the exact same way it had before, for all eternity. Maybe that's why nothing surprised him anymore. And maybe that's why he got a recurring sense of deja vu as he looked at Dr. Thapes, and she looked at him, and neither of them was sure what to say to the other. It wasn't the first time, and it certainly wasn't the last time. Elliott stood. He began to pace around the room, trapped by four walls and a clusterfuck of thoughts bouncing around in his brain. "Elliott?" Dr. Thapes spoke softly. So softly, in fact, that his lack of response made her think he hadn't heard her. He didn't, but not from the level of volume, but because his mind was elsewhere. "Elliott, we need to continue this," she said, much louder than before. This only irritated him more. "Please sit down." "Why?" he asked, continuing his pace around the room. "I have something I want to tell you," she said, her voice returning to a normal level. He paused and looked at her. She never had anything to tell him, only things to ask him, and this made him curious. He moved towards the couch where she sat, taking his place next to her. She folded her hands on her lap, her eyes already looking soggy behind her large-framed glasses. "You spoke to me about loss," she said, "and asked if I ever felt it." He nodded. "Trust me when I say that I have," she continued. "And how did that make you feel?" he asked, still bitter from earlier. She flinched, finally realizing once she was asked it how awful that question was. She paused for a moment. "I had a son," she said somberly. "His name was Ethan." Elliott had seen the room many times before. Never once had he noticed a picture of her family. She didn't have any pictures at all, actually, for the exception of some Ansel Adams photographs on the far wall. "He was five years old," she continued. Elliott realized that her speaking in the past tense was never a good thing. "I was late to work one day, and I had to take him to the babysitter so we got in the car and took off. I wasn't driving reckless, per say, just..." She considered her wordage here. "Just faster than I should have been." He could see where this was going. Her voice trailing off, and her eyes did the same, falling to a distant corner of the room. If he could hug her, he would have. She looked like she needed it as she held back tears. "You want to talk about blaming yourself? You prayed, I murdered." That last sentence didn't ease its way in, but instead struck him in the face. "So, you can continue to beat yourself up over it," she pressed on, recomposing herself quickly, "or you can find a way to get over it. Everyone dies, Elliott. It's a fact of life. An unfortunate one, but it's the only thing we're guaranteed in it. You can look at it as a curse if you want, or you can look at it as a blessing." Her eyes returned to his. "It's up to you." "How is that a blessing?" he asked. "At least we're guaranteed one thing in this shit life we live," she said bluntly. Nietzsche had spoken of many things, which is why it is no surprise that he had spoken of death as well. He said, "In your dying shall your spirit and your virtue still shine like an evening after-glow around the earth: otherwise your dying hath been unsatisfactory." Finally, Elliott Rollins thought he understood what that meant.
