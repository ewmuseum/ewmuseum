Date: 1/27/2008 'She draws the costume correct In thick black and red eyeliner Yeah, she's too young for the club But the guys at the door don't mind her The boys are all lining up For the chance to wine and dine her She thinks she's working the scene But she's caught up in a web full of spiders.' - Strata, 'Cocaine (We Are All Going to Hell),' Strata Presents the End of the World. 6.1 - The Present: Warming Up in the Bullpen It was the get of the lifetime, and it belonged to him. Needless to say, 'Hollerin'' Hank Hendricks should have been a happy man. Most people considered Hendricks a dinosaur, a product of a bygone era, when wrestlers were real men and it was more about the joys of physical competition, not about who was the bigger asshole, or which one could turn his body 630° before landing on his opponent's face. For the most part, his column was treated as the laughable opinions of a man who no longer understood the sport about which he was writing. In short, he was the Andy Rooney of wrestling journalists, little more than an eccentric throwback to the days of yesteryear. At least, he was until FUSE's advertising for their Super Bowl Weekend show on ESPN claimed, 'We are FUSE; We Are Athletes.' Hank's editors thought it was a great time to throw him out there; since the federation was making such grand promises, they'd see how well Smitty T. Duluth would stick to them. Given whom they sent for the interview, the answer to that question was, 'Not very fucking well.' When Duncan Drake arrived at Hank Hendricks' offices, Hollerin' Hank was... well, he was hollerin' mad. Drake was everything he railed against in his column, everything he'd grown to hate about his beloved sport. He was an arrogant, untalented punk, more interested in 'getting over' with his mouth rather than by any kind of athletic ability. It was ridiculous; moreover, it was an insult. He told his editors to go fuck themselves, that he wouldn't do it. He told them they were lucky he didn't go out there and smack the Hollywood Hellion and his damned wench in the rear end with his office door. His editor stopped him right there. 'MacKenzie Malone is with him?' his editor asked. 'Yeah. They're all fuckin'... fuckin' kissy on my goddamned couch. It's disgusting.' 'Hank. No one's talked to that girl since Smitty fired her.' Pro Wrestling Online News wasn't a very popular site, not in the digital age, where, on a good day, there were upwards of three-dozen competitors sharing the same bullet points of news about the PTC and the Big Two. 'You're doing this interview.' 'What? I just told you that guy is -- ' 'A goldmine of ratings. Listen, I respect your bullshit old school deal. Really, I do. I'm sure you and Dave Gibson have tea and crumpets and bitch about how great it used to be when guys wrestled two out of three falls in the snow, up hill both ways. But this is Two Thousand and Fucking Eight, and you'll do this interview with those two assholes and you'll be a broadcast journalist for once in your life, or else you'll be on a street corner this time tomorrow with a sign that says, 'Will Rant About the Greatness of the Spinning Toehold for Food.' Make something out of yourself, Hank. This is your shot.' Out of that whole rant, all Hollerin' Hank Hendricks heard was, 'This is your shot.' He didn't hear very well anymore. (End Transcript.) * * * * * 6.2 - The Future: There's Nothing Good on TV Anymore The woman with the grocery bags fumbled with her keys, bumping the brown paper sacks up her hip. Her keychain (a bulky thing which had originally started with a glittering, stylized 'M' and kind of exploded from there) tumbled from her grip and to the concrete stoop. 'Hell,' she whispered to herself. The woman was dog-tired; she couldn't remember the last time she'd gotten to sleep in her own damn bed. This new job was beating her into warm past, and she had to remember that she was doing everything she was doing for her girls. The woman tried several times to bend down to pick the keys up, but the two overloaded paper bags kept getting in the way and, finally, with a sigh, she just rang the damned doorbell. The man who answered was tall, broad-shouldered, and good-natured, with long, flame-red hair and deep, soulful blue eyes. Her daughters called the man 'Uncle D,' and he was one of the woman's oldest friends. 'Hey!' Uncle D said. 'You're, you're home. I didn't think you'd be back from your trip until later.' He accepted the heavier of the two bags without comment. 'I got on a standby flight out of O'Hare,' she said, ruffling a hand through her reddish blonde hair as she stepped past the larger man into the house. It was a small, colonial-style house, nestled into the suburbs outside Rochester, sky-blue and smaller than the woman would have liked, but sometimes, beggars really can't be choosers, and sometimes a mother makes sacrifices for her children. The woman made her way down the hallway, stopping to smile at her twin daughters, who (unsurprisingly) were holed up in front of the television set (where some commercial or other was playing). Lil was curled up on the couch with a book, glasses slipping down her nose, while Dee was planted just a couple feet from their flat-screen television, feet waggling in the air. Uncle D was settling back into his post beside the entertainment center, rubbing an old war wound of a knee injury. So, basically, everyone was in his or her usual spot, and that brought a smile to the woman's face. 'Hiya, girls!' she said, shifting her burden. 'Whatcha watchin'?' 'Nothing,' Lil mumbled. 'Just some TV,' Dee said distractedly. Later, she'd remember these words and know they were lying to her, know they were doing something they weren't supposed to. Right then, however, the blonde was soul-drenchingly tired, so she missed it as she headed into the kitchen to unpack her groceries. Her iPhone rang almost as soon as she set the bag down. 'Christ, what do they want now?' she asked the air, picking up the call as she shoved a box of Hamburger Helper into the cabinet. 'Hello?' she asked. 'No, I just got back into town. I haven't even unpacked yet -- look, don't you have someone else who can take this one?' She pulled a collector's edition Little Mermaid cup out of the cabinet, grabbing the pitcher of iced tea from the fridge as she shifted her grip on the phone. 'That's fine,' she continued. 'No, I'm not saying I can't do it, it's just that... I don't know. I haven't even been home with the girls for more than a day or two in almost a month. I just want to spend some -- ' That was when she heard it from the other room, a sound she hadn't heard in almost four years, but there are some things you just don't forget. The wet smack of something metal hitting something fleshy was one of those things. 'Holy shit awesome!' Dee shouted excitedly. 'Mom! Momma, come see!' Lil called at the same time. 'Dee! Language!' Uncle D barked. His voice lost its edge, but he clearly directed his next sentence to the kitchen. 'You're... you're going to want to see this.' 'I'll have to call you back,' she told her caller. She hung up before her boss could answer. The woman rushed out from the kitchen into the den, stopping short at the sight of a man she certainly did not expect to see on her television set, standing over a man she considered to be one of her closer friends. A play-by-play announcer she did not yet know the name of -- and his color man, who she, unfortunately, did -- were in full-on hyperbole mode. 'Damn him! Damn them both! Damn them for liars!' the play-by-play man shouted. 'This is greater than the night I ate my weight in butterscotch pudding!' Eugene Ware responded. '...fuck me sideways,' the woman said at length. 'Mom!' Lil scolded her. 'Language!' * * * * * 6.3 - The Present, The Future: Acceptance (A Montage) (Editor's Note: 'To show us the passage of time, we're gonna need a montage! Consider this a momentary parting of the veil between then and now, a taste of what's to come, because this story, while not the whole truth, is still true. It is immutable and it is unchangeable. This train is already on the tracks and destiny has plotted its course. 'You're doing the right thing.' They'd assured the Shooter that, back when the lot of them had sat around the fucking kitchen table to concoct this plan. It seemed like ancient history now; it seemed like crazy talk when they were having Danishes a couple of years ago. Certainly, when the Shooter had first been introduced to the business... well, they didn't mention this kind of stuff in the job interview. The Shooter was lying flat on a rooftop behind a KAC SR25 sniper's rifle, the same kind of gun the U.S. military used. The gun was massive, with a free-floating, center-fire barrel just a hair over two feet in length. It fired NATO-issue 7.62x51mm rounds, and while the Shooter did not hand-load these rounds, they had been present when they were loaded, and the man who did so had extensive experience. A single shot from this rifle would cut a hole through a car door or carve the top of a person's head off. The gun was an effective, if final, solution to the problems the Shooter now faced. 'You're doing the right thing, you know.' Other people had told the Shooter that much more recently -- people whom the Shooter knew were liars. But the deal had been made anyway; the contract had been signed. People like these... they didn't really 'negotiate' so much as they picked what they wanted and you agreed or you got run the fuck over. Now, since agreeing to terms there had never really been a choice in, the Shooter had seen, up close and personal, the kinds of things these people did every day, like it was just another fucking day at the office. Lives had been ruined -- hell, lives had been lost -- at their insane whim No. No. The Shooter wouldn't let it stand. Couldn't let it stand. The cool breeze wafted in off the water, tickling the shooters, features where they weren't covered by a black mask. The gun -- the Shooter had come to think of it as The Gun -- was matte-black, with only the wider end of the integral sound and flash suppressor visible, flush against the edge of the rooftop. 'We doing the right thing, you think?' It had become a popular topic of conversation between them as, like an anchor, they sunk to lower and lower depths in the rabbit hole. They could never have dreamt, could never have imagined, how deep it went. Had they sacrificed too much? Was it all worth it? All the lies they'd told, all the people they'd hurt, and all the livelihoods they brought crashing down around them -- just for the chance to succeed where everyone else had always failed? The forces they were arrayed against didn't fight the same way as their opponents did. It was like trying to beat a grandmaster at chess when you were playing checkers; it just didn't work. For every setback they suffered, the enemy would take a piece of their opponents' life with them; it was a scorched earth tactic the enemy knew would result in only the most Pyrrhic of victories. Around that dining room table, eating pastries and drinking hot cocoa, they had decided to stop playing with one hand tied behind their backs. They'd known their plan would call for some sacrifices to be made, and they made them. But none of them, not one of them, imagined this moment, with the Shooter lying on the rooftop with a rifle pointed at an office building. They couldn't have; it was inconceivable, even back then. Now was a different story. The Shooter had been lying on this rooftop since before dawn, for almost four hours, immobile, waiting for the right moment. A dark blanket lied over the Shooter's body; every once in a while, it pulsed with electricity to stimulate the muscles along the back and thighs to prevent cramping. An ergonomic stock, fitted exactly to the Shooter's measurements, rested against the gunman's shoulder, with a soft pad to protect the cheekbone from the violence of recoil. The Shooter's right hand eased around the customized grip of the gun, molded to fit this hand and this hand alone. The other hand lifted to the 8x10mm telescopic sight, carefully adjusting for range and focus as the Shooter slid their head behind the eyepiece. The Shooter hardly moved the weapon to sight it; the scope did most of the work, pulling the barrel through the conference room that lied 319 yards away. The Shooter only had one target in mind. Seated at the head of a long, glass table, back to the floor-to-ceiling window, was a figure that had gone by many, many names. In December of 2007 and January of 2008, fans of the FUSE Wrestling federation used the name 'Monet,' but that was a lie. On this unseasonably cool evening in a few months, the figure went by a different name entirely, a name that was also a lie, but for a different reason. The Shooter breathed out in a long rush, then in through the nose. This is it. Fingers flexed in along the grip. The chair in the conference room turned towards the window. I'm doing the right thing. Monet stood in the shadows within the room, but through the scope, the Shooter saw everything, as clear day. The Shooter remembered all the lessons learned in the lead-up to this moment. The Shooter remembered everything they were taught about firing this gun. Breathe out, and then in. Out, and in. Out. In. And squeeze.
