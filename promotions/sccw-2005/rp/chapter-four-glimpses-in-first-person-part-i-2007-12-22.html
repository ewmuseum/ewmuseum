Chapter Four: Glimpses in First-Person, Part I

22 December 2007
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 12.22.07 4.1 - The Orchid: Belief They say the best way to kill a snake is to cut off its head. When the Quietus came, people became like rats scurrying from a sinking ship. They all had their reasons -- people always seem to have the soundest excuse for taking the easy way out. Some of them wanted to go into business for themselves, and I had no doubt they would be very successful. My master's lessons would get one quite far out there in the 'real' world. Some of them were exhausted or wounded or simply wanted nothing to do with the world we resided in, and I had no doubt they needed their rest. My master's lessons would often be quite hard on one's body, mind, or morals. Some of them feared an attack from the enemies we'd made, now that we were weak and alone. This I doubt, because my master's lesson drove away fear, because you use fear, you do not let it use you. I did not turn my face, however. I did not stumble, I did not falter, and I did not stop. I am the Orchid, and I would not stop believing. They called me crazy, and maybe I was. They thought my quest for restoration was a fool's errand, and, at its start, I could not have disagreed with them. I was alone, I was without funding, without backing, and without the greatest love I would ever know -- without any support at all. But I still fought on. For I am the Orchid; I could not stop believing. They left me out there to die on the vine. They had made me a scapegoat for their own misdeeds -- bait for foes I didn't even know existed. The cold and the cruel among them (and that was saying something indeed) made wagers on how long I would last and how long it would be before they would gather for my funeral. I'm told not a single bettor would wager past six months. But did not falter. I could not. As I am the Orchid, I refused to stop believing. Perverse curiosity and sick anticipation would lead them to watch me struggle. They watched me fight, they watched me search, they watched me learn and grow. But, most of all, they saw me harden, because youth is a weakness I could ill afford. They would watch for nearly two years, as that is how long it took my search to bear fruit. They say villains tried to stand in my way, and they were right. They say some of these villains joined my cause, as no one else would listen, and they were right. They say I became a villain myself, letting my quest slide from reclamation to annihilation. In this, they were wrong. Fully, ultimately, finally wrong. Because I am the Orchid, and I did not stop believing. It took one year, ten months, eighteen days, sixteen hours, and forty-seven minutes (I didn't keep track of the seconds, I'm sorry) before we found anything, and what we found... it was pretty much shit, honestly. But something was better than nothing, and because it meant that my master's defeat -- it meant the Quietus itself -- was not complete. They say the best way to kill a snake is to chop off its head. But you had damn well make sure no one manages to sew that son-of-a-bitch back on before the serpent is well and truly dead. * * * * * 4.2 - Maxwell Drake: A Dark and Dirty Hole The first duty of a prisoner is to escape. But I still screamed myself hoarse for the first twenty minutes after I woke up from whatever that... woman... gave me. I decried the outrage, I hollered about who I was and what I would do to them, I called for my sons and for Gregory and for the police and for help, I shouted for my captors to ANSWER ME! I begged for release. I bellowed for some kind of retort. I was provided with nothing. I was left alone, strapped to a rickety old wooden chair in some kind of wine cellar The room smelled of mold and sweet and sour grapes, with rows of wooden casks on racks to either side of me. I believe there was a cloudy white window behind me, but I was bound such that I could not turn my head to see. All I could see were the concrete steps before me, stairs that led to a rusty steel door whose paint had long since flaked away to the point of not being recognizable. Some kind of locking mechanism secured the passage, two conjoined steel bars nesting in cradles bolted in the wall. A long white fluorescent bulb hid, dark, behind a cage over the door -- I was fairly sure the light would go on when the bolt was thrown. 'What do you want with me?' I demanded of... of the door, really, since it was my only point of reference. 'You can't just keep me here! Who are you people?' The door did not deign to answer. I strained against the bindings. I bucked against the chair. I may not be young any longer, but when I was, my brawn matched my brain and my bite equaled my bark. I figured leather straps and an old chair would give before a man's skin, bones, and muscle. All my temerity earned me was an unfortunate overbalancing of the chair, sending me tumbling face-first to the damp concrete. My cheek and left eye against the rough ground, a shallow abrasion drawing blood from beneath my ear, I will admit that a series of choking sobs took control of me. I am not too proud to do so. 'Why won't you answer me?' I beseeched, plaintively. 'What have I done to deserve this?' The bolt was thrown in answer; the dull white bulb above the door flickered on, but the light beyond it was brighter still, and I found myself squinting. I made out a gold-filigreed mirror and a finely carved chair with a red velvet cushion before the two men's silhouettes swallowed the light. I did not see much of the first man, except to say that he was large -- tall, yes, but wider still, built like an offensive lineman and smelling of strong tobacco. I never got a good look at him, even when he dead-lifted the chair (with me still in it). All I saw were urban-print camouflage cargo pants and a black wifebeater. I did not miss more details of the first man because I was not trying to look, but rather because I was more interested in the other man, the one who limped down the steps in the big man's wake. This man was wire-thin, dark brown hair slicked back away from scowling features. In pleated black slacks, a black turtleneck sweater and black shoes, he was like another shadow in the room. Except for his cane, a stone club of a thing that thunked heavily on the concrete with each stride. The cane's head was shaped like a hawk, its glittering red eyes boring into me hungrily, every bit the predator the man was. I glared right the fuck back. My name is Maxwell Emerson Drake II. A cripple with a pretty walking stick would not bow me. 'Who are you people?' I demanded, keeping my voice as hard and even as I could manage. 'What do you want with me?' 'My name is Mister Hawke,' the man responded, his own voice a barely controlled invective of a whisper. 'You can call me Mr. Hawke. And you and I... we are here because of Duncan Davidson Drake.' 'Duncan? What's...?' I was prepared for a lot of answers to my question. The woman's words aside, that was not one of them. 'What do you want with my son?' 'He's stupid,' Mr. Hawke said, tugging up the cuffs of his pants as he settled uncomfortably into a crouch. 'And I'm afraid the case is terminal.' Both of the mans hands had a deathgrip upon that beautiful hawk's head. I wanted to push him over. 'But we'll have plenty of time to talk about that, Maxwell. Tell me -- do you find hot women arousing?' I stared blankly at the man. He smiled passively at me. The first duty of a prisoner is to escape. The second duty of a prisoner is to figure out what the hell is going on. * * * * * 4.3 - Duncan Drake: The Afternoon Drive Time I pressed the number two on my phone and hit dial. Mac picked up before the second ring finished. 'So I was thinking,' I said by way of introduction. 'Oh, God, Duncan,' she responded; I could hear her tucking the phone under her chin. 'You're driving! Don't you remember the last time you were on the I10 and you 'got to thinking'? People almost died.' 'Hey, that was serious stuff.' 'I know! The fire and rescue teams told me they could smell the fumes before they even reached the wreck.' 'Can I finish my own set-up here, please? It's my dime.' Mac let out one of those long-suffering sigh things. My grin widened. 'So I'm thinking of swinging by, I dunno, Babies 'R' Us or something. Getting a car seat.' MacKenzie didn't say anything for a long moment. I changed lanes while I waited for her to figure out how her brain worked again. I might've signaled; I'm not sure. 'Duncan,' she said finally, quietly. I could imagine her there, with her cute little hand in that dark hair, trying to figure out how to break it to me. 'I'm not -- we haven't even talked about this. Hell, you're coming over to my apartment for the first time tonight. We haven't even... how can you be sure you want to be a dad?' 'What?' People think I'm dumb, and that's fine. It gets me places in life -- places that Dad's smarts don't get him, places that I wouldn't get if they didn't underestimate me a little. 'No, I meant for the trophy-thing they give to the Lion Cup winner. 'Cause I am so driving around with that bitch, and you can't be too careful.' 'Sweets, the tournament is Sunday. You haven't even won the thing yet.' 'A-ha!' I thrust a finger in the air. I live in Los Angeles; if a guy pointing at things while he talks is the strangest thing you see on the freeway, it's been a slow day. 'What's the keyword in that sentence?' 'Uhm. 'Haven't' or maybe 'won'.' I blew my own horn -- literally. The guy in that Bug I whipped by must have jumped a foot. 'I'm sorry, Alex! We were looking for 'yet'! Tell her what she's won!' I deepened my voice; think the announcer-guy from Saturday Night Live. 'She's won an all-expenses-paid night of dinner and dancing with the next first-ever FUSE Wrestling Young Lion! They'll enjoy a romantic seafood dinner, walk barefoot on the beach, and then get it on till the break of dawn!' MacKenzie laughed, and I'm glad, because if no one laughed at my jokes, I'd be sort of a sad panda. 'I'm telling you. Every time you declare that you've already won this tournament, I get nervous. I think you're jinxing yourself!' 'Eh,' I said, waving a hand through the air. 'It's no biggie. I don't believe in karma. You'll be ready when I get there?' 'That depends on how long it'll take you to get here, doesn't it?' 'Eh, I'm on the freeway, it's six o'clock. Technically, I've got, like, fifteen miles.' 'So, an hour.' 'Probably, yeah. I can't believe it took you this long to let me visit your apartment.' 'You've got so much more room than I do. I live in a cramped one-bedroom, and you have the biggest house I have ever stepped foot in. I can't believe you want to come all the way down to Glendale.' 'Eh.'
