Part Twenty-Two: Beware The Light At The End Of The Tunnel...

16 August 2009
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 8/16/09 It is four days ago. From the beginning, what you should know is this: you can hit the back button on your browser at any time. You don’t have to do this. What you should know is this: this does not end well. No matter how this turns out, even if good does triumph over evil or whatever, you still won’t feel any better. It’s all just a waste. Welcome to the shit. From up high it looked like just another car…and of course, it was. As we glided in closer, more details became apparent. It sped down Olympia Avenue, just another anonymous vehicle in the Los Angeles night. The color was taupe. Getting closer still, it was a Pontiac Grand Prix from early in the millennium. A rental car most likely. Even closer yet, we could hear music. It was a song by the Violent Femmes called "Country Death Song". The lyrics had a squealing quality about them that many would describe as unsettling. As we approached the passenger side door, we could make out the words coming from the sound system of the vehicle. #Come little daughter, I will carry the lanterns. We’ll go out tonight, we’ll go to the caverns. We’ll go out tonight, we’ll go to the caves. Kiss your mother goodnight and remember that G-d saves. Kiss your mother goodnight and remember that G-d saves.# We were inside the car, just a fly on the wall. Sitting in the passenger seat was Miles Cavanaugh. He was dressed in a plain black t-shirt and jeans. He flicked a strand of blond hair out of his eyes and stared off into the skyline. Subconsciously he tapped his finger on the dashboard to the beat of the song. In the driver’s seat, Jeb Stewart was dressed in an old FUSE shirt. He looked rigid and nervous. "So," Miles asked, "do you not believe in hell…or do you just think the climate will agree with you?" In the backseat, the River Rat grinned like a wolf. #I led her to a hole, a deep black well. I said make a wish, make sure and not tell and Close your eyes, dear and count to seven You know your papa loves you, good children go to heaven You know your papa loves you, good children go to heaven# "People are shitty, Miles," Lane started, "whether they ever show their true self or not. You, me, Rhine, everyone…but if everyone’s shitty, I think that means that everyone is good. Right?" "I don’t follow," Miles replied, still staring at the skyline. The vastness and absurdity of it was turning his stomach. "Terms like good, bad…these are all relative. And if no one is really good," Lane proclaimed gleefully, patting Jeb on the shoulder, "then certainly by comparison no one can really be all that bad. So good news, boys; we’re going to heaven after all. " "Dude," Miles said, actually turning around to face The Warlord, "that’s fucked up." Stevens seemed to ignore that. His eyes also wandered out the window, settling on a road sign. There was a zen like calm about him as he spoke. "We’re almost there." #I gave her a push, I gave her a shove. I pushed with all my might, I pushed with all my love. I threw my child into a bottomless pit. She was screaming as she fell but I never heard her hit. She was screaming as she fell but I never heard her hit.# Mercifully, we move elsewhere in time. It is ten days ago. "Just hold your breath for a minute…now breathe normally." Alanna Marshall didn’t like hospitals. The only thing that kept her from outright hating them was that not all of the memories she had of hospitals were bad. But at a ratio of roughly 98 to 2, she felt reasonably confident that "strong dislike" wasn’t too strong a description for how she felt about hospitals. She had nothing against the doctors and nurses. They were simply doing their jobs to varying degrees of competency. Alanna winced inwardly as she recalled the last time she’d had to have blood drawn. The nurse had not had what you would call a delicate touch with a needle and the resulting bruise had taken nearly a week to disappear. Hospital rooms were never pleasant, Alanna thought. Beds you could never get comfortable in, lumpy pillows, air conditioning that never actually seemed to cool no matter how low it was set. Walls painted in dull, flat colors. Food that never managed to taste of anything. And why did not one of the rooms that she’d ever been in have a shower in it?! Then there was the testing. Getting poked and prodded and stuck and injected and hooked to IVs…it had gotten to the point where Alanna could just zone out whenever anyone approached her with a needle, blood pressure cuff or thermometer (although she still had no idea why anyone would need to draw blood at three o’clock in the morning). She could also live without moments like these. Moments where concerned hospital staff kept telling you to "breathe normally" when you were in the most abnormal position of your life; lying on a cold metal bench, covered by a thin blanket with your pants around your ankles. Moments where you had to keep your eyes closed at all times so as not to face the reality that moving your head just two inches in any direction would mean you smacking against unyielding metal. Moments where you thank G-d that you’re not claustrophobic…and thinking that, if you had been, they’d be peeling you off the top of the machine right now. And let’s not mention the… "Now, we’re going to inject the contrast. You may feel a slight burning sensation, that’s completely normal." Ah yes, the iodine. Slight burning sensation, my ass, Alanna thought. She had to suppress the urge to vomit as the familiar warm, oily sensation spread through her body. Every single time, Alanna thought, it’s like I’m getting injected with that black oil from the X-Files. I half expect my eyes to change color. "Just hold your breath for a moment…now breathe normally." The routine from this point forward was always the same: a few more moments of discomfort followed by the bench slowly being removed from the machine and her having to quickly do up her pants underneath the blanket. At least they’d done her the courtesy of having a female tech this time. "Are you all right, Mrs. Marshall?" Alanna smiled weakly at the technician. She was just doing her job, Alanna reminded herself. It wasn’t the tech’s fault that she wasn’t in the mood to deal with any of this today. "I’m fine, thanks. Just a little tired." "Are you sure you’re okay to drive?" Alanna waved her hand dismissively. "Don’t worry about it. I had my husband drop me off, he should be here to pick me up any minute now. How long will it be till I get the results?" "Well," said the tech, "Dr. Rasmussen should have the results within 48 hours, if not sooner. Once he’s got the results back, he’ll contact you to schedule a follow up appointment." Alanna nodded, blankly processing the information. "Thanks." "Do you have everything, Mrs. Marshall?" "Yeah, I think I’m good." "All right, then. Have a nice day." Thanks ever so much for that, Alanna thought, as she slowly made her way down the dully painted corridor towards the exit doors. It was amazing how much of this they never actually told you about, she thought. Not the whole thing with the drugs and the treatments and the tests and the prospect of surgery…that part they were all too eager to talk about. No, what they neglect to mention is just how utterly exhausting the whole process is. How, sometimes, just waking up in the morning was enough to make her want to bawl her eyes out. How tiring it could be to live feeling like you had a sword hanging over your head all the time. As she stepped out into the midday sun, all Alanna Marshall really wanted to do was sleep. She smiled slightly as the red SUV drove into view, coming to a slow stop next to her. Right on time, as always. G-d, Lance had been such a blessing through all of this. Always ready to do what was needed, always eager to do what she asked, always by her side when she needed it. She couldn’t imagine how she would have handled all of this without him…and was glad she didn’t have to. The SUV’s engine came to a halt as Lance Marshall bounded from the driver’s side, striding over to the passenger side door. He smiled deeply at his wife, giving her a small kiss on the cheek. Alanna responded by hugging him deeply, taking the moment to absorb everything she could of the man she loved. "Bad day?" Alanna just nodded, her eyes watering. Lance didn’t respond with words. Instead, he gently broke his wife’s embrace before kissing her again…this time deeper and more passionately. He then opened the passenger side door, gently helping his wife into the seat before closing the door behind her. A brief moment later and he was in the seat beside her, starting the SUV’s engine and sending the couple on their way. A few more moments and they were back in the eternal parking lot that is Los Angeles traffic. Lance looked over at his wife, who stared out of the window. "You okay?" Alanna shook her head. "I don’t know what the hell’s going on. I guess I’m just tired and emotional. I’m sorry." "Hey," said Lance, "don’t apologize. You’ve got every right to be tired and emotional. Dealing with this would do a number on anybody." Alanna turned her head and smiled at her husband, meeting his free hand with hers across the center console. "How do you always know just the right thing to say?" "Years of practice," Lance smiled. Alanna laughed, softly. "I just hope today’s results are good. Well, as good as they can be in this situation." "I have faith," Lance replied, "that everything’s gonna be fine. You’ll see. The next time we see Doctor Rasmussen, we’ll see that everything’s going according to schedule." It is three days ago. "What do you mean, it’s gone?" In his particular specialty, David Rasmussen was often the bearer of bad news. Breaking it to people that they had contracted a terminal illness, informing them of how little time they had left to live, making recommendations for counseling and support services…it was a job that could wear away at your soul if you let it. He’d seen a number of his coworkers in oncology suddenly quit the hospital or leave medicine all together, unable to deal with all the crappy parts of the job any longer. Sometimes, although he’d never admit it, he wondered how close he was to that point himself. This is why he loved moments like these. Normally. "As you can see from the scan, Mrs. Marshall, the course of treatment we prescribed has been far more effective than we could have possibly imagined. " "Here," he said, pointing a pudgy finger at one of the two films on his desk "is the original scan. Notice how the mass is clearly visible. " Doctor Rasmussen then pointed to the same area on the second film, positioned to the right of the first film. On this one, the mass was no longer visible. "We had anticipated that the chemotherapy would be able to shrink the tumor to such an extent that we would be able to remove it through surgery. It seems that it had an even more aggressive affect than originally planned and has eliminated the tumor all together." Alanna found herself unable to speak. Whatever she had been expecting, it certainly wasn’t this. It was Lance who followed up with "How is this possible?" "Well, Mr. Marshall," said Doctor Rasmussen, "despite all that we now about cancer, it is still a very unpredictable disease. The treatments we prescribe are typically the best course of action but we can never tell if they’ll work as well as we expect…or, such as in your wife’s case, even better." "So this means I’m cured?" Alanna squeaked. "Well," said Doctor Rasmussen, running a hand through his graying hair, "we will want to do follow up testing every six months or so, just to keep an eye on things and make sure nothing new develops. But, barring any complications…yes, I would say these latest results indicate that you’re in full remission." Lance bounded out of his seat, grabbing the doctor’s hand and shaking it vigorously. "Thank you so much, Doctor. You have no idea how much this means." Alanna, close to tears, now joined her husband. "Yes, thank you. This is amazing." "I’m glad I could be the bearer of good news," smiled Doctor Rasmussen. "Now, go out and celebrate. You deserve it." "We will," boomed Lance Marshall as he and Alanna headed out of the doctor’s office. "You can count on it!" It was only once the Marshalls had left his office that Doctor Rasmussen allowed himself a troubled sigh. The looks of joy on the Marshalls faces had almost been enough…but he’d managed to hold on, somehow. They’d believed him. He’d given them the news every couple in their situation wanted to hear…and it was the last thing he’d wanted to do. It is two and a half days ago. The trip to Irvine had been a bit of a bear but when Lance and Alanna had broken the news to Zach that Mommy was all better now and that they were all going to go out and celebrate, Zach’s answer had been immediate and determined. "Dave and Buster’s!" As it was obvious that no amount of persuading would change Zach’s mind, Dave and Buster’s it was. The family had decided to make an evening of it, starting with dinner (chicken fingers for Zach, salmon for Alanna and a sandwich for Lance that had been so big that it had made both Zach and Alanna’s eyes boggle) and ending with games. They had started with the video games. Alanna had bet Lance twenty dollars that he wouldn’t even try to get on the Dance Dance Revolution machine. She’d lost…but the five minute laughing fit at the result had been worth it, Lance’s grumpy face included. Lance had then managed to win Zach a teddy bear at the basketball shoot before they’d ended the night with a vigorous game of bowling. They were now making their way home again, the SUV speeding its way through the Los Angeles night. Zach was buckled up safely in the back, deep asleep, both hands clasped tightly to his brand new teddy bear. Alanna sat in the passenger seat, stealing glances at her husband and stifling the occasional giggle as her husband maneuvered his way down the freeway. Normally, Lance would be slightly grumpy at this, frowning and mock complaining that "it’s really not that funny." Tonight, though, he just smiled as he stared at the road ahead of him. "I’m glad to see you happy. It’s been too long and you deserve it." Alanna sighed. "I just…it feels like I dodged a bullet, you know? I’m not sure that I’ve processed it yet, it doesn’t feel quite real." Lance took his wife’s hand in his free hand. "Believe it, babe. I told you everything was gonna be fine and it is. From here on in," he boomed, "only good news!" "Shh," Alanna giggled, "you’ll wake up Zach!" The two of them spent the next few minutes giggling to themselves, smiling at their apparent good fortune. Alanna then stretched over the center console, planting a kiss on her husband’s cheek. "I love you, baby. Thanks for everything." "I love you, too," Lance smiled. It is four days ago. Driving through Northridge at night was a remarkably peaceful experience. When the sun went down, the rows of houses had a way of looking identical, providing an almost hypnotic effect. The neighborhood was not a violent one and there were hardly any noise complaints. It would have been a good place to raise children…but unfortunately for Doctor David Rasmussen, his wife had been unable to bear children. A recent demographic study revealed that the median of the average household income in this particular suburb was $60,000. Doctor Rasmussen had surpassed that figure by some measure but saw no reason to leave the neighborhood. He had been born here and imagined that this would also most likely be where his story would end. So, a few years back, he had simply built a bigger house. A house that he returned to on this night just like he had done the night before and the night before that. But this time…this time, something was different. "What the…" he trailed off. David parked on the street as there was already a car occupying his usual space in the driveway. He didn’t recognize the Grand Prix as belonging to anyone he knew. Judging by the exhaust spewing from its rear, the engine was on. And judging by the shadows inside, it was occupied. The oncologist approached the Grand Prix tentatively, his heart thumping. He couldn’t imagine that approaching the car was a good idea but he didn’t just want to call the police for no reason and he didn’t want to run inside the house like a scared little girl. There didn’t really seem to be an appealing option. As he neared the back seat of the driver’s side, he saw the window roll down. The smiling face of Lane Stevens greeted him. "What’s up, Doc?" "Can I help you?" Rasmussen asked in a low and helpless voice. "Yes, sir," Stevens said, a hideous smile growing on his face. "I believe you can." A few minutes later the Grand Prix drove away, having gained an extra passenger. We now return you to your regularly scheduled lives, already in progress.
