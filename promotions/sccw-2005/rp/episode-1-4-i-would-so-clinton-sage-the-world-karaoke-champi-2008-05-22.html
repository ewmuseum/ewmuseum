It has something to do with a confluence of lakes or something. The city's detractors will tell you that Chicago earned its nickname because its residents are all a bunch of blowhards. While some natives might be offended by the appellation, others open the bar that our shot opens, courtesy of the super-fancy Sony 1080i High Definition digital camcorder (say it with us!) of DEWM! 'At first, I was scared. I was petrified...' And the karaoke...can't forget the karaoke. It's a gibbous moon of a stage that serves as the centerpiece of the Blowhard, backed by a set of glittering, midnight blue curtains. A spindly man in a suit and tie clutches the simple mic stand in one hand, belting out the Gloria Gaynor classic. The shot swoops over the heads of bunch of bar patrons, down to a small table, off to the side, where four fine folks sit, sharing a cocktail and some laughs. They are utterly insignificant, not worthy of our time...and they totally have a camera, so we're stuck with them for now. We'll go left to right, because that's how you learned in first grade. Sitting with her back to the stage is a woman on the small side, dark brown hair done up in a pile on her head and thick-framed eyeglasses. Her name is Edith Raymond. Her sister's...gonna go with 'friend' sits to Edie's left. Contrary to Edie's businesslike (some would say 'severe') look, Krissy Jamison is dressed down tonight...by which we mean, 'Charlie's gonna have to fight the boys off with a stick'. Kids, in the entertainment industry, this is called 'the set-up.' In the interest of full disclosure, however, they also use that term in other fields. There is a rented office on the East Side of Chicago that is nestled into the sixth floor of one of those blocks of tinted glass that seem to sprout up everywhere like weeds. The office is listed on the building directory as 'Anderson Insurance,' though a good number of the other tenants weren't sure there was actually anyone practicing within until quite recently. The company never missed a rent payment, however, and when Mr. Anderson came to pick up the mail, he'd positively charmed the pants off the secretary pool of his neighbor. In the man's words, it was 'as useless as tits on bacon' for anyone to be in the office this late. (Admittedly, he used that phrase because it made his female companion giggle.) However, one lonely, late spring evening, the offices of Anderson Insurance were hopping. Mr. Anderson stood in front of the floor-to-ceiling window in his office, overlooking the subdivision nestled nearby. A Bluetooth headset was hooked over his left ear, but he wasn't listening to the conference call he was part of. A soft rapping (a gentle tapping) came to his door; Anderson knew who would be on the other side without looking. A tap of a button on his PDA put him on mute. 'You know you don't have to knock, dear,' Anderson said; as always, his smile colored his words. His visitor's grin matched his own, though hers was a little silkier. 'I was being polite.' Kirsten Pendleton had a dachshund named 'Bogart.' Bogart would...well, Kirsten wasn't really sure what he did during the day. (She liked to think he was plotting to take over the world...or at least the house...but the puppy was probably too dumb to manage that, given how long it had taken to housebreak him. The cat on the other hand...) Without fail, Bogart met Kirsten when she came into the studio apartment her tragically stereotypical waitress salary barely kept in her name. One afternoon in late January, Kirsten schlepped up the stairs, her purse hanging from one hand, a week's worth of mail (including more 'Past Due - Final Notices' than she cared to think of) cradled in the other. When she opened the door, Bogart leaped up onto the couch, tail a-waggle. 'Heya, Bogey...how was your day?' the soft-voiced girl asked with a small frown coloring her brow. The puppy yipped in response. 'Yeah, but you kind of have a kickass existence...all the food you want and a tennis ball to play with.' Apparently, all that the dog heard was, 'tennis ball to play with,' because Bogart was off the sofa in the proverbial blink of an eye, trotting merrily through the apartment in search of what was probably technically still a tennis ball. Pendleton set her purse down in the spot the puppy had just vacated, flouncing down into the couch cushions as if she hoped to sink right through them. The mail was scattered onto the coffee table beside the couch, and she set about ordering it the way she liked it: bills (way too many), junk, personal correspondence, magazines she wanted to keep, magazines she wanted to throw away, and magazines she would flip through to figure out if there's anything good. Kirsten got a lot of magazines...her friends once told her she was one of those girls who are impossible to shop for, so the easy answer becomes, 'Eh, she likes to 'insert something, where that something has a magazine devoted to it'! Get her a subscription.' She had two to Cook's Illustrated (but that's a kickass magazine). One piece of mail didn't immediately fit into any of the categories: an envelope that claimed to be from the Sierra Verde Agency with a return address as a post office box in Concord, New Hampshire. It was this she looked at first (her Entertainment Weekly would just have to wait). Blue eyes skimmed white paper, and she reached for her cordless telephone, punching in a series of numbers (she didn't think '425' was a New Hampshire area code). Bogart hopped up on the couch beside her, haggard green felt protruding from the end of his snout. Kirsten wrestled the ball from her puppy's mouth as a warm-voiced woman answered the line. 'Thank you for calling The Elisabeth Foundation, this is Trinity, how may I direct your call?' The dark-haired woman blinked. 'I...must have the wrong number. I was trying to reach the Sierra Verde Agency.' There was a short pause. 'Did you receive a letter in the mail?' 'I did.' Kirsten nodded, though only Bogart could see it. 'Whom is the letter signed by?' Kirsten squinted down at the paper. 'Ellen Henning?' 'One moment while I transfer you.' That was how it started. Later that night, a rather inebriated trio departs a bar called the Blowhard. Across the street, there's a ratty, tumbledown tenement apartment, and on the third floor is a room the landlord just rented this week to a nice dark-haired man and his new, redheaded bride. A high-speed telephoto lens snaps a half-dozen pictures in just a few seconds. A pay-as-you-go cellphone dials another (bought from two separate stores in the greater Chicago area). 'Party on, Wayne,' a man says into the phone. 'Party on, Garth,' a woman responds. When the landlord gets complaints that no one has emptied the mailbox in three weeks, he'll open the door.
