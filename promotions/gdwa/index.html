An all-women promotion that ran from 1996 to 2000, booking a weekly Tuesday Night Catfight and ten championships on the strength of a roleplay judged first and a written match strategy judged second. Eighty-two wrestlers, 51 shows and six annual award categories survive. Written by AI, 2026

Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

The Grand Dragon Wrestling Alliance opened on 21 April 1996 and booked women only. It came out of a Women's Division that had outlived two promotions inside two months — Dawg Pound Wrestling, whose first women's card ran that February in Cleveland, and Internet Championship Wrestling, which Dawg Pound became on 4 April in Tacoma and which folded almost at once amid conflict between its promoters. The division kept drawing under the defunct name until it was given a name of its own.

It ran a card a week called the Tuesday Night Catfight, a promo show called the Friday Night Tease, and four pay per views a year: Summer Supercard, Fall Moonsault, Founders' Day Tradition, and Dawg Pound Nights, named for the crowd section that had followed it out of Dawg Pound. Its application took original characters and said so in capitals. Its FAQ ran to ten sections and argued for a six-attribute character sheet, a contention list, contracts binding champions to defend, and a front office of seven named non-player characters to enforce them. Matches were booked rather than simulated: the fed head read a roleplay and a written match strategy for each wrestler and decided from both, weighting the roleplay first and saying outright why — a strategy benefits one person, and a storyline adds to the league.

Eight championships and thirty-four reigns survive on its own two lineage pages. Two belts were settled by Royal Rumble and three in tournament finals, one of them in the Fukuoka Dome. Sierra Browne became the first wrestler in the promotion to hold two of its titles at once, then three. Daisy Butterfly won the first Ironwoman crown at the first Fall Moonsault, carried the Internet Heavyweight title through a rename to Intercontinental, and in August 1998 unified it with the Western Heritage title to be crowned the only American Heritage Champion its ledger records. Two champions were stripped for breach of contract, ruled on from front offices the fed placed in Walnut Creek, California.

The ledger stops on that August night, and the site picks the story up eighteen months later. In January 2000 a scrolling banner announced the promotion back in action, with a tournament to crown a new Cruiserweight champion and a pay per view called Glory Days to crown a new and undisputed World Champion. The card it ran that month at the Key Arena in Seattle is the only one of its own that survives, and it survives because a fan wrote a workrate report on it and the promotion published the report on its own site: six matches, a heel turn out of an offered handshake, a fifteen-minute draw overturned to a disqualification and a stretcher, and a legends match that ended with a woman storming the barricade, pulling back her hood, and turning out to be Daisy Butterfly.

The last thing the promotion dated was 21 October 2000, three days out from crowning a brand new Western Heritage Champion on its first card out of Tacoma. What happened on that card is not in the surviving record, and neither is when the promotion stopped. Its own copyright line reads 1996 to 2000. The site stood untouched on free GeoCities space until GeoCities closed in 2009.

Storylines

Most of what survives of GDWA is a title ledger rather than a run of cards — the two Lineages pages narrate five years of championship history in their own sequence, date and building by date and building, and the fed's own Wrestler of the Month and Wrestler of the Year awards say, in its own words, which of those results it thought mattered most. Only one card survives whole enough to recap beat by beat. The arcs below are recapped as far as that record actually supports, and no invented beat fills what it doesn't.

The Key Arena card, 18 January 2000 Written by AI, 2026

The one show of its own the fed's site preserves, and it survives secondhand — a fan's workrate report, published on the fed's own site rather than the card script itself. The opener billed Andrea Chandler and Black Lotus, both former champions, against Rekka Sakura and Marissa Monet entering together as "The Grand Dragons." Monet spent the match's first minute settling an unrelated score, cracking a chair over announcer Spud McKenzie's head "as a message" to Paul Laurence Dunbar, whose commentary about her had gotten under her skin — a thread that ran the length of the card and paid off when she came to ringside during the fifth match to choke out Officer Order and scream at Dunbar again. Monet closed her own match with a moonsault off the top, clocked by the report at "six-foot, five-inch," for the pin.

A cruiserweight tournament round followed, Lida Yanagisawa over Princess Yamaguchi off a victory roll with the clock already expired, and then a four-way pitting Bloody Mary and Anais Exotica against Amanda Reese and Rachael Edwards — the match the report itself called the card's worst, but the one that planted two threads at once. Edwards kicked her own partner Reese in the back of the head to feed Mary the pin, and then joined Mary in stomping Reese out after the bell — an alliance of convenience across the two teams that left Exotica to powder out alone. Lady Tiger ran in to make the save for Reese, setting up the friendship the next match spent: Jen Grier and Lady Tiger, old friends forced into the ring together, wrestled a clean match until Grier offered a handshake and slapped it away instead — a heel turn finished off by stealing the win with an inside cradle and then attacking Charlotte La Mancha, Tiger's manager, when she objected.

Officer Order against "Diamond Girl" Heidi Leick ran to a fifteen-minute draw at the bell, and the fed overturned it. The fight had already spilled outside, where Andrea Chandler brawled with both Lady Tiger and Charlotte La Mancha while the match continued in the ring; Leick kept working past the bell with a post-match powerbomb, and Marissa Monet came down as if to help Order to her feet and instead choked her out — still working the Dunbar grudge from the opener — before Leick returned to finish Order off with a Fisherman's Buster onto a steel chair. The referees sent Order out on a stretcher and awarded her the match by disqualification rather than let the draw stand. The card closed on a Legends' Match, Zaranna against "Fear Factory" Lanny Manson, that the report itself complains was never given room to be one — Chandler pulled the referee off a two-count, then knocked Manson out with a chair for Zaranna's DDT and the pin, before beating down both women afterward. A fan stormed the barricade to make the save, pulled back her hood, and turned out to be Daisy Butterfly — gone from the ledger since defending her three unified titles the previous August, back in the flesh to close the night. What Chandler's own run into the new year amounted to, and what Butterfly's return led to, is not in the surviving record; this is the last night of GDWA the site preserves in any detail.

Sierra Browne's three belts Written by AI, 2026

The fed's own ledger calls out Sierra Browne by name, twice, as a title history in miniature. She won the Western Heritage Championship from Officer Order on 5 May 1997 while already Cruiserweight champion, becoming — the ledger's own words — "the first multi-titled champion in GDWA history." Four months later, on 10 September 1997, she beat Daisy Butterfly for the Internet Heavyweight title to become its first tri-titled champion, carrying all three belts into that November's Fall Moonsault, where she lost the Internet title in a three-way and was forced to vacate Western Heritage for holding two at once. Browne rebuilt the feat once: on 28 July 1998, in Pittsburgh, she won the Western Heritage title back from 'Jungle' Radhi Ananda while still Cruiserweight champion — a "two time multi-titled champion," the ledger says — and lost it the same night, same building, to 'La Femme Nikita' Nikita Marx in a "Throw in the Towel" match. She kept the Cruiserweight title itself a month longer, until it was vacated at that August's Founder's Day Tradition II with no successor named until the January 2000 tournament the Key Arena card ran a round of.

Daisy Butterfly's unification Written by AI, 2026

No wrestler on the ledger changed more championship history than Daisy Butterfly. She won the first Ironwoman crown at the first Fall Moonsault in September 1996, and over the following two years held the Internet Heavyweight title twice, carried it through its formal rename to the Intercontinental Championship at a card in Sendai in January 1998 without the reign itself ever breaking, and on 30 August 1998 beat 'Ultima' Sarah Summers for the Western Heritage title to unify the two into a championship the fed minted for the occasion: the American Heritage Championship, a belt its own ledger records exactly one reign of. Butterfly is also the fed's own 1997–98 Wrestler of the Year and, across 1996 and 1997, twice named to the Lanny Manson Fan Favorite Award — the 1997 vote's own note recording a second ballot four months later that named the same pair again. What became of her titles after that August night is the one open thread the site closes without answering: she is out of the record entirely until the Key Arena card sixteen months later, where she resurfaces from the crowd rather than from a title defense, and the fed's own handler's page of October 2000 announces a new Western Heritage Champion about to be crowned with no word of who held it, or lost it, in between.

Officer May Order and the injury angle Written by AI, 2026

The fed's own FAQ names its house style outright, and names its own wrestler doing it: "Many an angle has surrounded GDWA legend Officer May Order and the injuries she has accrued." Nothing else held says which injuries, or which cards staged them — the FAQ's own worked example earlier in the same document, a hypothetical match between "Officer May Order and Daisy Butterfly," reads as the fed head using its own two biggest stars to illustrate the rule ("the roleplay is favored over strategy") rather than as a report of a real result. What the surviving record does confirm is the durability the FAQ is describing: Order held the Western Heritage title twice and the World Heavyweight title once across 1996–98, was voted the Lanny Manson Fan Favorite three separate times, and was still drawing a diminished pop — the Scratching Post's own word for it — by January 2000, in a fifteen-minute draw the fed overturned rather than let stand. The injuries themselves are a named device with no surviving card to recap; the gap is left exactly that, not filled.
