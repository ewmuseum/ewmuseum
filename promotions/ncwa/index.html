Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

The Nationwide Championship Wrestling Association opened in 1992 as a simmed fantasy federation, its matches decided by a ruleset with its own point system for improving a wrestler over time and its own weekly newsletter keeping members informed. It crashed over the winter of 1996–97, when its operator fell seriously ill, and came back that April under the same roof.

“Flyin' Brian” announced the fed's own return on 6 June 1997, promising it would prove “even better this time around”; by July the operator was writing as a third persona, Brickhouse Brown, billed as the federation's own President and CEO and sharing the commentary desk on its broadcasts. Under his voice the NCWA converted from simmed to booked mid-run and ran five weekly series — Friday Night Slam, the two-hour Tuesday Night Livewire, Thursday Mid-day Maddness, Saturday Night Slam, and a monthly pay-per-view — alongside a near-constant recruitment drive for new talent and roleplayers.

Its own Spring Slamfest 97 supercard, staged at the Spectra Arena, put every one of its five championships up for grabs at once — the World, United States, Television, National and Tag Team titles — and opened with rival stables WAO, EE and the Brotherhood all in the building at once. The card mixed original characters with gimmicks built on real wrestlers' names and mannerisms, one of its own house rules candidly stated: “yes they are rip offs of real wrestlers but what the hell? They are my favorites.”

By August 1998 the promotion was broadcasting Livewire from the Douglas Memorial Colosseum in Greensboro, North Carolina, with a new pair of announcers at the desk. That month's show opened with “the Tricky Kid” Derek Craven, formerly of the hooded stable the New Breed, staging a beachside confession that he had lost himself chasing “the glitz and glamour of the wrestling industry,” then re-dedicating himself and his men to the group's own original, rougher identity on camera. The federation closed for good the following month, after seventeen months of the run that had followed its 1997 relaunch, ending in the way its own operator later described plainly: burnout.

Its own record does not end there. A Hotline Report broadcast in May 2004 staged a fictional epilogue over the promotion's own real closing: Brickhouse Brown's death in the summer of 1998, a court fight between his family and a rival company over the federation's ownership, and his son, Brickhouse Brown Jr., finally winning the rights and relaunching the NCWA with a fresh signing class in 2004. Whether that relaunch ever produced a card of its own is not established by anything else the promotion left behind.
