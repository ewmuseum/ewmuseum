Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

Real American Wrestling dated its own opening to September 1996 and, by the millennium, was already telling new recruits it was entering its fifth year with four out of five roster spots held by wrestlers who had stayed two and a half seasons or longer. A six-month layover in the back half of 1999 gave way to a relaunch that January, a shrunken twenty-man roster, and a house style built around two broadcasts: a weekly Midweek Massacre and a bigger, biweekly Weekend Warriors, punctuated by a pay-per-view roughly every two months.

Its own commissioner, an on-screen boss billed only by his surname, ran the promotion out from under a locker room that never let him keep the peace for long. A masked stable calling itself the Vic World Order treated the world title as its own private property, its leader needling the announce table between matches and threatening a returning rival with a signature finishing hold delivered from a hockey stick studded with nails and thumbtacks. A round-robin tournament that April settled a vacant championship in the Vic World Order's own house, only for the new champion to be jumped by the stable's muscle the moment the bell rang.

Its second pay-per-view of the year, staged on Independence Day itself, opened with a National Anthem sung by a credentialed paleontologist and closed on three men still swinging past the seventy-minute mark inside a steel cage built around a dumpster, a stray pair of snapping turtles among its contents. Real American Wrestling's own television title changed hands there in a match that ran the full length of the card's undercard build, and a masked interloper turned up mid-show wearing first one rival stable's shirt and then another's before laying out two men with a folding chair.

Its last surviving broadcast closes on a different kind of ambush: the lights cutting out four times in a row over a tag-team title match until four masked figures calling themselves the Circle stood at each corner of the ring, then tore through the champions before their own herald delivered a warning, in broken English, to a wrestler who had not yet returned to the promotion. The record ends there, mid-threat, the Circle's own business unfinished.
