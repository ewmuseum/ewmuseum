He looks directly into the camera...] SB: In the world of professional wrestling, many kinds of men come through the doors and step into the various rings. Wrestling is its own melting pot, perhaps the best kind ever invented. Personalitys and styles vary as greatly as anywhere else in the world. To stand out in the mess of athletes is a tough task, let alone to set a standard few can match or excel. In 1995, an obscure wrestler nicknamed "Flash" entered the unknown DWA. Not much was known about him at first, but soon, it became clear of three things about this person. First, he possessed an unsurpassed will. Many people in the league remarked that the things this one guy could do to try and win a match were beyond most men's capacity of imaginination, let alone ability. His stamina was incredible as well, as he could take an hour's worth of punishment, yet still find a way to hang tough. And the technical skill was also impressive. This triple strength woukld begin to define this man's career. It didn't take long for Dan Kauffman to emerge from the haunting shadow of wrestling. In fact, Kauffman didn't just emerge, he led the way for many others. Here is a look at a man... nothing more, nothing less... who has entered the wrestling world, and has come out a champion. I knew injuries were possible. I knew that with my size, I was always at risk. That never mattered to me. Wrestling was a way of life, and everyday, pain, sweat, tears and the whole nine yards, I loved my sport. I wanted to show people the right way. Perhaps that got blurred a little bit. perhaps I got caught up in the times, and that objective slipped. But I truly love the sport of wrestling, and I hoped to make a difference. AS the victory celebration is shown in the ring, it fades, and is replaced by Steven Barr and Dan Kauffman...] SB: In your early days, you were known more for your pure atheticism than anything else. You could do high-risk maneuvers, posessed great technical skill, and had a flare for popping moves out of the third row if need be. How would you define your early wrestling days? DK: It was a time where I was searching for recognition and respect. I knew I had the talent to match steps with anybody in wrestling, it was just a matter of getting some big name to notice, and making a reputation for myself based on my skills. I was still a little green around the collar, but because of my childhood near the sport, I adapted to in-ring life pretty well. Basically, I was in the ring trying to show I belonged at first, and I let my wrestling do that for me. SB: Would you change anything about those early days? DK: I'm sure I could think of better ways to have lived my career, but there's no point in thinking about how it could have been better. The past is the past, and there's no sense making it the present. Me early days had turbulence, but they were also a very good time in my life. I can't look back on them with anything but good feelings. [This part begins to fade out, and a new tempo, this one a little slower and more downbeat, starts to play. The first image to appear this time is of the IIWF Banner hanging over the IIWF Arena in Portland, Oregon. Clips of Coronation Clash start to roll... And Dan Kauffman, in said clips, walks down an IIWF Aisle for the first time. The screen splits, with the walk down the aisle on the right, and the interview on the left...] SB: You once said that your first steps into the IIWF, even when the IIWF was an unknown federation making its debut, was the most nervous steps you've ever taken. DK: That's true for a lot of reasons. Somehow, I just knew that the IIWF was going to be the league that made me or broke me all the same. There was a general felling abound, that carried through all of the wrestlers in the back, and all of the officials at ringside, that first IIWF Event was going to evolve into something bigger than anyone had imagined. We all knew the IIWF would become special. That night was definitely a nervous one for me. [The right side of the screen then shows clips from the opening match of Kauffman, against El Seguente. Kauffman hits a tilt-a-whirl back- breaker, as the interview continues on...] SB: Were you fortunate to have drawn somewhat of an easy opponent in the first round of the Coronation Clash? DK: There is no such thing as an easy opponent in wrestling. Still, the fact that I was in there with a relatively young guy helped me. The match worked to calm my nerves down a little bit, and that match got me into a flow. Yeah, that match helped, especially considering what was to come. Many say that your matches with Deathbringer defined your career. Is this so? DK: As far as the fear goes, fear is something that I don't have in the ring. If you fear competition, you are doomed. As far as how my career is defined, I suppose Deathbringer defined my career. I didn't always come up on the winner's side, and there were matches I didn't walk out at all... But I fought and fought hard, and I think people will remember me not for my successes or failures, but how hard I tried to do my best. Deathbringer and I, after the Deathmatch and even after the Snow Brawl battle, still have great respect for each other. He taught me a few things, and I think he learned a few as well. [The interview fades out. Then, another still image appears, this one of the stark, standing figure of "Quickstrike" Chris Quigley. Quigley seems to glare straight through the screen...] SB: Chris Quigley and you never saw eye to eye despite your similarities. It's almost eerie how your two careers paralled each other. Yet you don't like Quigley at all. Why is that? DK: Don't get me wrong. Chris Quigley is a great athlete, and he beat me in the middle of the ring with a lethal submission move, I'll give him that much. But I don't think Chris Quigley is wrestling for the right reasons. I can't honestly sit out here and say I've been the perfect model of what a wrestler is supossed to be like. Neither can Chris. I just think Quigley tries too hard to be THE superstar, when he should concentrate on trying his best instead. I'm not so sure he always tries his best. As an athlete, he's top of the line. But there are other facets of his personality that I don't see eye to eye with, so I avoid the guy. SB: Would you ever like to step in the ring with him again? DK: This may be surprising to a few, but whether I like it or not, he beat me, and I will leave it at that. He proved himself. [The interview session fades out, and is replaced by a video run of Kauffman receiving the IIWF World Championship on October 11, 1996. The Subway Psycho straps the belt around Kauffman's waist. Then, the picture gets darker, and over this, the interview fades in...] SB: Your IIWF Championship defeat of Otto Verhoeven is a match you've labeled "The biggest failure in your career", this despite winning the IIWF Championship in the match. Explain this. DK: Take a look at the tapes. I didn't beat Verhoeven. The Subway Psycho did. The Psycho labeled Otto with a steel chair. It was nice to have the Championship, sure. But the fact is, I never truly won the belt on my own. Too many times, I let outside interference determine my fate. I lost matches due to it, but I also won a few because of it. I wanted to win on my own, to prove that I was one of the elite. Sometimes, I feel like I am the biggest fraud in e-wrestling. The accomplishments I have achieved were great, but I didn't deserve most of them. I'm still trying to prove I belong today, and it's not an easy thing to do. SB: And these feelings led to your departure from the IIWF? DK: In a large part, yes. I truly thought I would never wrestle again, it had left too sour a taste in my mouth. SB: But you couldn't pull away from the sport you love. DK: Exactly. [The interview freezes, and several early MLWO clips from May 1997 start to appear. There are a lot of great wrestlers that don't get a hell of a lot of attention in those leagues, men like Marcus Renge, Matt Harris, Mr. Robinson, Alex Adams, Jason Knight, Brian Knight... the list goes on and on. The cage match wasn't our first meeting, as our tag teams -- Craig Anderson and I, along with Knight and Destructo -- had vied for the NOW World Tag Straps. But we both knew this cage match was the last opportunity, and we wanted it so bad. That match was by far my greatest success to date, greater than any other single win in my career. [Fade out of the interview. Then, clips of several SJPW stars appear. As the fast-paced clips flash by, another still of Kauffman is overlayed in the background. DK: I want to wrestle with guys who will fight one on one fairly and with pride and heart. I want to compete with some of the best athletes in the world, and we all know that Japanese Wrestling is one of the fastest growing forms of wrestling in the world today. I want to see exactly where I stand on a world basis. I want to face the best, and I am convinced that some of the best are right here in SJPW. I came for wars. I came for epic matches. And I promise, if there is one thing I have always done in my career, it has been performing to the highest standard. I did that in the IIWF. I did that in the MLWO. I did it in NOW, DWA, CFWF, and everywhere else I've been. And SJPW, I promise you that before I leave, I will give you not one... not two... but at least THREE great matches. The SJPW can pick my opponents. I won't wrestle for titles. I won't wrestle for recognition. I wrestle for respect and honor. SJPW, I am coming for a test of my wrestling skills. I hope I can bring excitement and class to SJPW. I look forward to the tour. SB: Last words? DK: I am Dan Kauffman. I wrestle for respect. That is all.
