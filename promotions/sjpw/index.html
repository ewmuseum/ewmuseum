A Usenet federation founded in March 1997 on a puroresu-styled house style — a Japanese/gaijin roster balance, an explicit anti-Hoganitis screening process, and a weapons ban enforced by its own in-fiction security chief — holding eight title lineages across 69 shows and 85 wrestlers. Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

Super Japan Pro Wrestling pitched itself to the newsgroup on 8 March 1997 as something different: “We're an e-fed that's based in JAPAN,” run in English, with one card a week and four championships from the start, its own FAQ later dating operations to that same March.

Its house style was stated as a rejection of what its own operator called “Hoganitis” — wrestlers' refusal to job — screening applicants with a wrestler-form question asking whether a handler would let his wrestler lose for an angle. Its own mission statement set the model directly: “I want to see matches like Dean Malenko vs Ultimo Dragon...Fast paced action, with little rulebreaking; just two sides trying to beat the other by their skill...Sure, SJPW has faces and heels, but the japanese fans love wrestlers, good or bad.” One title, the Battlecade championship, was carved out as the fed's deliberate exception for FMW/W*ing-styled extreme matches.

A leveraged buyout changed hands on 20–21 April 1997, staged as a Tokyo press conference: the fed's outgoing chief executive ceded control to a new ownership group joining the Global Internet Wrestling Alliance, its Junior Heavyweight title was redesignated the Cruiserweight Championship, and an English-language commentator was hired to pair with returning color man Akira Ono.

The Cruiserweight title carried the year's clearest storyline: Super Tengu won it from Shinji “Live” Hamada in a prearranged finish at the fed's first pay-per-view, Battlecade '97, then dropped it to Ace Killcannon at the Super J Crown Tournament that September, with Derek Mota lined up for a cage-match challenge as 1997 closed. The All-Asian Tag Team title had gone to Black Sun Rising and the Battle Arts title to Steel Serpent by the same point; the All-Asian Heavyweight Championship, its own top singles belt, sat vacant. A memorial tag-team tournament pay-per-view followed in January 1998.

Its own operator closed the promotion on 18 April 1999, citing the loss of two writers and rising demands outside the fed: “It's been fun, but all things must come to an end. Better now while it's in good shape, than later when it might just suck.”

A third incarnation announced itself in an F.A.Q. dated 22 October 2003: a former multi-time champion and the promotion's own last chief executive brought it back with a dojo owner's backing and Akira Ono returning to the booth, touring smaller Japanese venues with a signature “Zenmen Sensou” (“Total Combat”) pay-per-view brand and a biweekly magazine show, Metro. Its surviving record runs house shows and title changes into September 2004, including a new Grand Prix Heritage Championship, without a closing notice of its own.
