A Usenet federation that ran three shows a week through 1997 and 1998 on a Friday Night Raw / Monday Madness / Wednesday Mayhem schedule, its “kick Ass order” stable spending the run absorbing turned former stars on its way to the World Heavyweight Title. Five title lineages, four shows and 57 wrestlers survive. Written by AI, 2026

Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

The Supreme Wrestling League dated its own founding to March 1997, by its later first Anniversary retrospective and its own “SWL History” newsletter; the earliest surviving document is a 24 September 1997 recruitment ad promising any “great RPers” a spot on the roster for filling out an application.

It ran three shows a week — Friday Night Raw, Monday Madness, and Wednesday Mayhem — plus a monthly pay-per-view and a semiannual awards special, the Supremer Awards, its own commentary booth credited under the names of real-world wrestling announcers as a broadcast convention rather than a claim that those men called the action themselves.

Its dominant storyline was the kick Ass order, a heel stable built around Mean Man and Basic Killer's World Tag Team Titles reign, which held that belt across the whole surviving record while absorbing rivals it turned to its side, up to and including a newly turned World Champion by mid-1998.

The World Heavyweight Title passed, on the fed's own record, from American Heartthrob through Sniper and a self-posted Mr. Excellent before reaching Insanity, who dropped it to Mr. Justice at the In Your Face: Sharp Turn pay-per-view in February 1998; the newsletter later billed Mass Destruction as champion by mid-year. The Supremer Strap stood apart, held by Kraze across the entire record without a title change ever surfacing.

Its own June 1998 newsletter documents a public rivalry with a promotion calling itself the GWF, including a large flame-war thread the fed's own writers waded into directly.

Its own operator, posting on 16 January 1999, described the league as having “outgrown itself” into a backyard promotion spanning Northern California sometime after its last surviving Usenet newsletter, and used the post to defend the league's legacy — its In Your Face pay-per-view brand, the Sharp Turn match, its Real Audio play-by-play broadcast, and the Supremer Award — each, in his own words, later copied by name by rival federations. No further document confirms whether the league ever ran again.
