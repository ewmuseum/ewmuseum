Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

The International Extreme Wrestling Federation was already running by May 1997, though its own first year survives in this record almost entirely through what other feds and the wider newsgroup community wrote about it, rather than through its own broadcasts.

The one 1997 storyline that does survive this way played out publicly across the newsgroup for weeks. At a card called “In Your Park,” two of its wrestlers, El Perfecto and Adonis, burned a third, “Reaper” Rick Erickson, who then quit the fed. Other handlers picked the angle up in their own feds — one turned it into a storyline of Erickson “delusional... and trys to kill everyone” on the medication he needed for the burns — while Erickson's own retirement, and a rumored tribute card, were debated back and forth on the newsgroup by name.

A buyout partway through that September renamed the fed the Longhorn Wrestling Council. The rename is attested only third-hand: a different promotion's own announcement for a sixteen-fed interfederation pay-per-view lists “IEWF\LWC: Longhorn Wrestling Council” among the participants. Whether that particular crossover event ever actually happened for this fed isn't in the surviving record.

A 1998 recruitment post from a co-president explained away a temporary lapse by the fed's own founder, and a run of weekly Stampede broadcasts continued that autumn under an unbroken roster.

Its first surviving broadcast in its own voice is a Stampede aired 14 and 28 February 1999 from a small studio in South Laredo, Texas, called by Aaron Wexler and Biff Lincoln. “Hot Stuff” Chris Taylor, freshly stitched up from a two-on-one beating, opened the show vowing to make Sam Willis face him alone; the Blaze Brothers challenged Blue Chips for the Twin Pistol Championship; and the main event saw Thantasia defend the Rio Grande Championship against “Psycho” Johnny Rage in a Heaven and Hell match, a title that changed hands on the night. The Silver City Championship had by then passed to Tex Violence, and the broadcast closed on a confrontation between the fed's own founder-figure, Bishop, and Violence himself.

The promotion joined two other feds that year for a three-day interfederation pay-per-view. No closure statement survives under its own name. A February 2005 newsgroup thread recalled the fed as long since folded, one poster uncertain whether its own founder was even still active online; that uncertainty, not a stated ending, is the surviving record's own last word on it.
