Pilot.

17 October 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 17th of October, 2005. Pilot. Akoshi Kunamara grunted to himself as he wiped his forehead of the droplets of sweat that had been steadfastly forming over the last hour. The steaming hot kitchen of Zio Zio, an Italian restaurant owned by the Chinese family that lived across the hall from Akoshi in Chinatown, of which the twenty-five-year-old slaved over six days of the week, had taken its toll on him standing in front of a murky brown filled trough and scrubbing pot after pot, pan after pan and plate after plate. In truth, he was grateful to have been given a job by the family who owned the restaurant, after he and his girlfriend, Minori, left Japan for the second time to escape the yakuza clan that Akoshi was born from. He saw San Francisco as his safe haven away from the decadent lifestyle that his brothers and sisters so richly enjoyed. He was sick of the smell of roasted duck meat, though. And so here he stood at his sink, in the same crappy jeans and old t-shirt combination he had been wearing for years now, with his fingers wrinkled from scrubbing and cleaning as many pots and pans that could be mustered onto him. He sighed to himself again when he was passed a new pile of gravy and noodle covered plates. It had only been a week since Akoshi had inked a deal to return to wrestling with the promotion based out of Sin City, Global Wrestling, and, yet, he still worked in Zio Zio for the same piss poor wage he had been given when he first starting working in the kitchen of the restaurant when he first came to America as but a seventeen year old who was nervous to just talked to people. He just liked it here. “Oi!” he heard coming from the bar and counter through the gap in the wall. Akoshi turned around and brushed a strand of hair out of his eyes and looked at Ben, the guy who ran the place and worked at the counter. He left the hard work to the guys in the back. Ben wasn’t his real name, no; he was a migrant from the Chinese city of Guangzhou. His real name was Liang, but he used Ben for the customer’s sake. “You screwed another order up. He asked for meatballs, not sauce, on his Spaghetti you moron! Get it right.” “What?” Akoshi stammered out. He was sick of being mistaken for this one guy in the kitchen, he did not know his name nor cared, but it was damn annoying. “I don’t make the food, Ben; I just clean what doesn’t get eaten. You might want to tell the guys who make the stuff instead.” “Oh,” Ben flatly replied. “Sorry.” There was no sincerity to it; it was just one of those things you have to say when you make a mistake, or, whatever. Akoshi just nodded. And then, he grunted to himself again as he rubbed a cloth over the grim left on a plate, rubbing as hard as he could. This was routine. That brief conversation with Ben happened at least once a week. Akoshi pulled the plate out of the frothy water and marvelled at his handy work. Staring at himself in the shine of the plate that projected brightly off of the light creeping in through the cracks in the windows, he cast his mind back to the week before and a wee, little conversation he had in his kitchen at home. *** Minori Kumako was the woman who the man who masqueraded around as Radiant in flashy red and black spandex once a week lived with. She was his girlfriend, and his best friend. They had known each other since he was sixteen. She was a year older than he was, well, not quite. Minori was born in November of 1979, and Akoshi in February of 1980. Close enough. They were incredibly close to each other, and he hated making her upset about anything. It’s too bad she was upset right now. Minori hated wrestling. The very idea of organised violence was against everything she believed in, but it was something Akoshi enjoyed. He enjoyed being Radiant, and acting like a superhero. She just did not enjoy being his damsel in distress every week. But she was thinking about something else right now ? the lettuce she was crumpling in her hands, she was making dinner, and had not noticed Akoshi walk back inside after going to the store to buy some more cheese. It was three and a little bit more hours after he had signed the contract to bring him back to wrestling. Minori was not on speaking terms with him right now, and he hated it when she was mad at him. It did not happen too often. Akoshi stood behind her and wrapped his arms around her waist, but she mumbled something inaudible to herself, and wriggled herself free of his grasp. She was pissed. “What’s wrong?” he said to her, as he lent against the bench covered in shards of broken food discarded and unwanted for their meal. “Come on Mimi,” that was his nickname for her, “don’t be so upset about it. I did it for you.” “For me?” she said through clenched teeth. She sounded like one of those mothers who had just discovered her son had egged all the windows of their neighbour’s house for no particular reason. “You’re doing this for me?” The sarcasm in her voice was pretty freaking obvious. She turned her head and looked at him straight in the face. Akoshi cocked his eyebrow and nodded his head down toward a pile of books on the floor. They were textbooks, bought from the University of California ? San Francisco, where she had just started a degree in commerce. Suffice to say, it had hurt their finances enough to force Radiant to return to the squared circle, with Global Wrestling. Minori just looked back up at Akoshi and said softly, “Oh.” “Yes, “oh”,” Akoshi smugly said back to her. “Anyway, I’ll be leaving here Sunday nights and getting back sometime Tuesday mornings. Providing I’m not, like, incapacitated or anything. That just wouldn’t be cool.” Minori blinked, it took awhile for it to register in her head that he was just joking, until she softly smiled back at him. He just winked. Minori turned herself around from his grasp and lent against the bench, and said to him, “so, when is your first match?” “Not this week, the week after,” he said excitedly. He was very much looking forward to his return to the ring; it had been about two years since he last danced in the ring. Before she could ask who it was against he answered it for her, “it’s against Elia Smith. A girl.” “A girl?” Minori asked with suspicion soaked throughout her tone. “Why a girl? You’ve never wrestled a girl before.” “I know,” Akoshi said to softly. “I’m scared of, like, you know, hurting her or something. I don’t know if I’m supposed to go softer with my moves or what. I’ve never dealt with a girl in the ring before. It’d be like wrestling ? you.” “Yeah, and,” Minori began to say, taking his hand, “would you want to wrestle me” I don’t think so. Maybe you can get it changed.” “I’ll think of something,” he said to her, reassuring her. *** Akoshi scoffed to himself as he was handed yet another stack of plates to clean, back in Zio Zio’s stifling hot kitchen. He knew he had no choice in the matter ? he had to fight a girl. He had to hurt a girl to the point where she couldn’t raise her shoulders off a mat for three seconds or longer, or bring her to the absolute threshold of her pain barrier, and then a little more ? it was just not in his chemical makeup to want to do this to a girl. Akoshi Kunamara was a nice guy who did the right thing, and despite what he had grown up around, this was definitely not the right thing to do. But he knew one thing: he had no choice. Like everything else in his life, had no choice in the matter whatsoever. It had been booked, sealed and delivered to Global Wrestling’s fans that Radiant would be wrestling Elia Smith on the 17th of October in the year 2005. So, he had to do what he had always done in the nine years he had been making it on his own. “I’ll just do what I have to do to win,” he mumbled to himself quietly. “No more, no less. Nothing special, no, I don’t want to hurt her. But I’m still going to kick her ass.” He smiled to himself as he snapped out of his dazed, daydreaming funk and grabbed the nearest, grottiest cloth filled with as much grim as humanely possible and scrubbed as hard as he could, so he could hurry on up and get this horrible job over with; the same thing he had been doing for nine years. “Who are you talking to?” Akoshi heard Ben snap out from somewhere over his shoulder. He could only grin to himself, and quietly call out to the Chinese migrant, “nobody.”
