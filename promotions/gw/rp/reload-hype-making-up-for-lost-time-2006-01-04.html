Reload Hype: Making Up For Lost Time

4 January 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 1/4/06 The scene is simple, a high school gymnasium. Set up in the middle of the basketball court is a wrestling ring, with the letters PSCW emblazoned in the center, and on the skirts of the ring apron. A few feet away, a flyer lays on the ground, discarded by a crew member for this wrestling show, just a few hours off. PROVINCE OF SASKATECHEWAN CHAMPIONSHIP WRESTLING JANUARY 4, 2006 MOUNT ROYAL HIGH SCHOOL, SASKATOON MAIN EVENT THE RETURN OF A HOMETOWN SON TIM WALDEN VS. “THE ARACHNID” ERIC HAYES No one, though, seems to be in the high school yet. Save for one man, one man clad in a white singlet with white boots, running the ropes in the ring as if for the first time. Tim Walden. “I’ve been laughed at,” he tells himself, bouncing off the ropes, arm extended to clothesline an imaginary foe. Indeed he had. While working for one of his past employers, he had been pantsed on national television. He had been a lackey. A jobber. Always the bridesmaid, and never the bride. “I’ve been considered a failure,” he whispers, inspiring himself to stomp away at his phantom opponent, before dropping down to sink in a side headlock, standing up with his rival and cranking down with the basic submission. When he first came into the business, he’d been considered a hot prospect by most. His suplexes were crisp and powerful, and his submissions strong and nearly inescapable. And, to his credit, he’d made it to some of the biggest stages in the game. But he’d been a Television Champion. A Cruiserweight Champion. A Tag Team Champion. Not that Tim wished ill towards those honors. He treasured them. But like every man who had ever stepped into the ring, he wanted a shot at a world title. And he had never gotten one. “I’ve had my dream taken away from me,” he acknowledges, even as he falls to the canvas purposely from some manuever of his invisible training partner, wincing and smiling at the pain simultaneously. Again, it was true. His final match had been at least a year ago, probably more. The last thing he remembered was being picked up and dropped on his head, and then he woke up in a hospital, with a broken neck, being told he’d never wrestle again. Tonight was his first match, his return to the sport, in the very high school gym where Coach Adams had trained him in the basics of the amateur sport. He’d learned the leg lace, the cowcatcher, the crossface cradle, and many other such moves on mats where the ring he was in now stood. “But I’m back now,” he resolved, looking up at the gym lights from his back, before disgustedly rising to his feet. There would be no more Walden on his back looking up at the lights. Not tonight. Not tomorrow. Not ever. Sure, it was unrealistic to expect to never lose again, but the fact of the matter was that Tim’s New Year’s resolution was that he’d have his arm raised in victory a lot more than he looked up at the ceiling in defeat. After all, humiliation couldn’t stop him. A broken neck couldn’t stop him. What’s eighteen mere mortals in the way of a driven man? Come Global Reload, Timothy Walden’s going to show you.
