Mother's Day; Father's Day

26 May 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 5/26/2005 I woke up crying late at night... when I was very young. I had dreamed my father... had passed away and gone! My world revolved around him -- I couldn't lie there anymore... So I made my way down the mirrored hall, and tapped upon his door. August 24, 1989; 1604 hours Mexico City Superior Court 'Silence!' demanded the judge, slamming the gavel onto the block yet again. 'One more outburst, and despite your age, you will both be strapped hand and foot into your chairs, and your mouths sealed closed with heavy-duty tape!' Teresa and her sister were on the verge of tears as Lupe desperately reached over from behind them and held them back against the backrests of their seats with a firm hand on each of the shoulders. 'Do as he tells you, children,' she whispered frantically. It was bad enough her beloved husband was being treated this way by the court system... she couldn't bear to see her two daughters imprisoned as well. 'Continue,' said the prosecutor to the young woman crying on the witness stand; wiping her eyes with a threadbare, somewhat dirty handkerchief. 'I-I... I don't have the money to bring my son to a doctor,' she sobbed. 'We eat only two meals a day... his dog, Pepe, would scrounge around in garbage cans for scraps of meat. If that monster had hurt my poor Paquito--' Even though Lupe succeeded-- just barely-- in keeping her children from yet another loud cry of protest at the word monster... the slight scuffling noises as she strained to hold her children back were not lost on the rest of the courtroom. The young woman testifying cringed in fear, bringing a pleading look from the prosecutor. And I said, 'Daddy, I'm so afraid! How would I go on, with you gone that way? Don't wanna cry anymore, So may I stay with you?' 'This is your final warning,' said the judge in ominous tones, as the bailiff stepped forward and slapped a sap against his palm. It just wasn't fair. Mateo hadn't even been allowed into the courtroom... the family's utter poverty excluded them from even the most inexpensive defense lawyers. It was as though he had already been convicted without a word in his own defense permitted; an abomination his two young daughters would not be silenced on. Of course, once the heavy duty tape had been slapped over their mouths, and their wrists locked into handcuffs behind the chairs they sat in, and their bodies strapped against the backrests of their chairs, and their feet strapped to the legs of the chairs; their shoes removed and placed on the floor underneath them... their further cries of protest were garbled and muffled into incomprehensible sobs and moans. The judge seemed to be satisfied that they soon stopped struggling and thrashing about; 'encouraged' to do so by how painfully tight the straps dug into their skin until they remained fairly still. Behind them, Lupe wept as quietly as she could into a heavily wrinkled handkerchief. The same treatment awaited her if she dared utter a word of protest... and her vanity would not permit the indignity. And he said, 'That's my job... that's what I do; Everything I do is because of you, To keep you safe with me... That's my job, you see.' 'So your son only took a couple of light scrapes and a bruise?' asked the prosecutor. 'Thank God, and the Virgin Mary,' sniffled the young woman, looking up and crossing herself. 'Paquito's hurts healed on their own... he will be okay. But Pepe...' the tears rolled down her cheeks in grief. 'It will take years for poor Paquito's heart to mend.' The young woman had already testified that the savage palm-heel blow from Mateo's hand to the little dog's nose had dislocated its jaws... and its spinal cord had been bruised from the impact where Mateo had flung the Chihuahua against the wall; not to mention the wall still had a heavy dent in its peeling plaster surface. A photograph of that dent had been presented to the court as grim reminder of the incident... along with a brief testimony from the slumlord who claimed he had to administer a crude and not-so-pleasant euthanasia to the crippled animal to release it from its agony. Any competent veterinarian could have fixed the dog up, and with a little rest it would have been good as new... but as the court had gently been reminded, the young woman and her son, and the dog, lived in abject poverty; the mother struggling desperately just to keep them all fed adequately... and only succeeding, on average, for about five or six of each seven days of each week. 'I am so sorry for your son's loss, and your own pain,' said the prosecutor. 'Thank you.' Teresa and her sister gnashed their teeth behind the heavy-duty adhesive tape sealing their mouths shut. What about THEIR pain, and THEIR loss? What about their beloved papa's loss of his career... nearly all his friends... his self-respect? What about the way the two girls themselves were made to look nearly as much of monsters as Mateo, by the way the young mother fearfully glanced at them, and skittishly danced well away from the chairs they were strapped in, as she returned to her seat at the back of the courtroom? The judged banged the block again with his gavel. 'Bring in the accused,' he commanded. Later we barely got along; this teenage boy and he. Most of the fights it seems, were over different dreams-- We each held for me... He wanted knowledge and learning; I wanted to fly out west... 'Said I could make it out there, if I just had the fare! I've got half, will you loan me the rest?' Lupe wept at the sight of her husband. His face was freshly bruised and his hair wild and disheveled... his clothes torn in a couple of places. He looked and smelled as though he hadn't been permitted a bath in days. If his skin did not itch unmercifully from lice, he was indeed fortunate... though the heavy shackles he was kept in would have prevented him from scratching, anyway. The voice of the judge, castigating Mateo for 'unspeakable atrocities' against the 'poor, innocent family' that had prosecuted him brought fresh muffled sobs from his two children... and finally his head turned weakly so that his eyes caught sight of what the bailiff had done to his beloved daughters. A wild glaze filled his eyes as he lunged forward toward the bench; only to be halted as the bailiff stiffly jammed a cattle prod into his chest. Now even Lupe was sobbing audibly as Mateo slumped to the ground and had to be held up by his shoulders and elbows. 'You are remanded to the custody of the Lecumberi Correctional Facility,' said the judge in a near-funereal voice. 'Sentence, of three calendar years, to commence immediately. Bailiff, remove the prisoner from this court room.' And I said, 'Daddy, I'm so afraid! There's no guarantee in the plans I've made, And if I should fail, Who will pay my way back home?' Both bailiff and judge turned a deaf ear to Lupe's frantic pleas as she threw herself at the bailiff's feat and begged for even just a modicum of pity on the man she loved. Mateo's wife was grabbed by her hair and the collar of her patched-up dress, and she was dragged from the courtroom; deposited in a heap just outside. Not until Mateo was in the paddy wagon to be carted off to the prison, and the young mother finished thanking the prosecutor and left the courtroom, were Teresa and her sister released from their restraints; and even once it had been done, the bailiff slapped the sap suggestively against his palm in a dangerous rhythm until the two girls left the courtroom to help their mother away from the building to begin the long, lonely walk to the slum quarters in which they were forced to live... as they would be forced to do without supper if they dared take a bus. May 25, 2005, 1225 hours GW HQ; Media Commissary Noi Tsunami was a hard-as-nails, seen-it-all reporter... she HAD to be tough to pursue the big scoops and get all the big stories... but she was still only human. She had a soft spot, and it wasn't impossible to hit it. The latest piece of back-story she had gotten from Juan and Teresa Ramirez, and its tie-ins to the origins of La Herencia de Herederos, had hit that spot with pinpoint accuracy, making her ashamed of how she'd treated them in several of the earlier interviews she had done with the husband-and-wife Luchadores. I know Mexico isn't the same as the United States, but that seemed almost... medieval... in how they were treated, Noi thought to herself. Can I really blame them for having such big chips on their shoulders when talking about Mateo? Of course, when Noi thought about it... yes, she could blame them. The Ramirez family professed devout faith in Roman Catholicism... They're not very forgiving of these injuries, Noi reminded herself. Still, it all made great storyline, and great ratings... and when all the dust cleared, wasn't that what she was ultimately trying to bring all of GW's media publications? It was all about the ratings... and stories like these brought them in. 'And adding insult to injury, they added a week onto that three-year sentence,' Noi said thoughtfully. 'Maybe not a big deal to their bureaucracy, but definitely to Mateo's family.' And he said, 'That's my job... that's what I do; Everything I do is because of you, To keep you safe with me... That's my job, you see.' Teresa nodded slowly, feeling a new sense of respect for this reporter who had caught that 'little issue' that wasn't so little to her at all. 'They said the sentence 'officially' began on September 1st of 1989 because of delays in processing the paperwork,' she complained bitterly. 'My poor Pap? had to spend an extra week in that horrid prison because some clerk so conveniently decided on that exact day to go on a little fishing trip... like there wasn't a single OTHER person in the whole judicial system of Mexico City-- second-most-populous city in the whole WORLD-- to fill in for him?' 'That many people?' asked Noi, surprised. 'Possibly even THE largest population of any city in the world, if you were to count all the squatters and shanty dwellers on the outskirts, that are so poor they fall through the cracks in the census system,' said Juan quickly. But the last census tallied Mexico City and its surrounding metropolitan area with a population of over twenty million people.' 'And some people want to know why there's a food shortage,' muttered Noi to herself, and Juan and Teresa both heard and nodded grimly. Noi jotted down some more notes. 'And they wouldn't even let you or your sister speak a word in your father's defense? Or let your mother speak a word in her husband's defense?' Teresa grimaced horribly, remembering the taste of a filthy rag as it was stuffed in her mouth and then her lips taped shut with heavy-duty adhesive tape... and the threatening slap of a heavy leather sack filled with wet sand, against the bailiff's burly palm; suggesting her temples and occipital lobe would be next if she didn't stop struggling to spit the rag out. 'They might as well have not allowed us into the courtroom at all,' she nearly spat. 'It would have been far kinder.' Every person carves his spot, And fills the hole with life-- And I pray someday I might... Light as bright as he. 'This was before you had the falling out with your mother, I take it?' asked Noi a bit cautiously. She knew this was a sensitive subject with the Ramirez family; Teresa in particular. 'Yes, although not by much,' said Teresa bitterly. 'Mam? remarried barely three months later. There was no courtship; no dating, no nothing. He was being chauffeured through a working-class area of town where Mam? had gone to buy bread... she was still an attractive woman despite the toll of living in the slums. He looked at her... she smiled at him... he smiled back... a week later he was helping her file for divorce papers, and a week after THAT they were married by a civil justice. My sister and I were nauseous at how quickly she had cast Pap? to the wayside and left him to rot in prison.' 'But she got you out of the slums,' said Noi. 'And if he hadn't come by, you might have ended up in the squatter settlements.' 'I often wish we had,' said Teresa as she suddenly began to sniffle, and Juan reached out to hug her. Teresa returned the hug gratefully and turned to clench her teeth at Noi. 'Even squatters manage to find their way up into a respectable life sometimes... and if we hadn't... we'd still have lived, and died if necessary, a FAMILY. I loved my Mam? once... and still would, if she hadn't sold herself into full-time, live-in prostitution. It violates the tenets of our Catholic faith... she gave up her fragile soul for money and comfort, which passes from hand to hand, quickly forgotten and dirtying all it touches.' Woke up early one bright fall day... and read the tragic news; After all my travels, I settled down, within a mile or two. I make my living with words and rhymes; that all this tragedy... Should go into my head, and come out instead -- as bits of poetry. 'I won't try to argue that point, Teresa,' said Noi gently, as she jotted down more notes. 'But you'd better ready yourself for this counterpoint, because I know it's going to hurt... and I'm still going to ask it anyway because the fans will want to know how you react.' Juan and Teresa clasped hands and looked at each other for a long moment. Squeezing lightly on each other's hands, they finally turned back to Noi. 'We will hear you,' said Juan. 'Teresa,' began Noi-- singling her out for her counterpoint; 'If your mother hadn't married this man who became your stepfather... if she hadn't abandoned your father... and if you all ended up in the squatter settlements as a result...' Noi paused and drew a deep breath. Hard reporters and hard wrestlers had to engage in hard interviews; asking and answering hard questions. Noi couldn't afford to hold back here, no matter how the masked Luchadores responded. The fans would be judging them based on their response, and judging Noi on how willingly she delivered the 'right-between-the-eyes' hard shots. 'If you'd ended up in the squatter settlements,' she repeated, preparing to sum up her point, 'you'd never have met your husband... and your daughter would never have been born.' But I say, 'Daddy, I'm so afraid! How will I go on with you gone this way? How can I come up, With a song to say, 'I love you?' ' Juan and Teresa cringed visibly as if struck with fists. Noi pursed her lips and watched them carefully. Had she gone too far? She couldn't have afforded to have held back on that point, but... if they cut her off and refused to give her any further interviews or scoops on their back-story... Finally both wrestlers shrugged their shoulders deeply and bowed their heads with a small sigh. They're conceding defeat on that point, Noi knew, but they're too damned proud to admit it. It was another twenty seconds before Teresa finally spoke. 'Thank God that Melina is having lunch with Gaspar,' she said in a soft voice. Noi raised an eyebrow. 'Gaspar... you mean El Serpenti?' Juan nodded. 'Yes... he's taking her out for a couple of burritos. They'll be back soon.' Noi nodded. She'd gotten enough information for the magazine article, and she could tell that Juan and Teresa were still shell-shocked from the point she'd made. 'Anything else you'd like to add, then?' she said with a small smile of respect... trying to put them more at ease again. Teresa returned the small smile. 'No,' she said. That's it; thank you. I need to call my sister, anyway... today's her birthday.' 'That's my job... that's what I do! Everything I do is because of you, To keep you safe with me... That's my job, you see.' Noi almost dropped her notepad, but recovered and smiled at Teresa. 'Ah, well, Happy Birthday to your sister... how old is she?' '33,' said Teresa. 'It's been a long time since I've talked to her.' 'Give her my regards, then,' said Noi. 'I'll send you a copy of the article before it's printed. Thanks for the interview; I'll call you in a little while.' Juan and Teresa looked deeply into each other's eyes as Noi left. Teresa leaned forward a bit and gave a bit of a shrug to her husband. Juan merely sighed deeply and reached out for his wife's hand... but she pulled it away. On the other side of the closed door, Noi pursed her lips thoughtfully and jotted a few last notes in her notepad as she walked away from the Media room. 'First time Teresa has talked about this subject,' she made a note of, for herself. 'I need to be sure and probe for more information, now that she's finally opening up.' 'Everything I do is because of you, To keep you safe with me...' That's my Job, the late Conway Twitty, ? 1987
