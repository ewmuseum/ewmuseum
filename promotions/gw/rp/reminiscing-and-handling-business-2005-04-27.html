The major materials of Scotch Malt Whisky are malted barley, water and yeast. The first stage of production is the malting of the barley. The barley is first steeped in tanks of water for 2 to 3 days before being spread out on the floors of the malting house to germinate. To arrest germination, the malted barley is dried in a kiln, identifiable by the distinct pagoda-shaped chimneys, characteristic of every distillery. Peat is used to fire kilns in the drying process, along with more modern fuels. Smoke from the fire drifts gently upwards through a wire mesh floor to dry out the barley, and the “peat reek” imparts a distinctive aroma which contributes to the character of the final spirit. When dried, the malt is as crisp at toast. It starts with the malted barley being ground into a coarse flour or 'grist'. The grist is then mixed with precisely pre-heated water in a giant container called the 'mash tun'. This process dissolves the fermentable sugars contained in the grist. The liquid is repeatedly drawn off and the water replenished until all the sugar has been extracted. The resultant mixture, known as 'wort' is cooled to a precise temperature. It is then collected in another large vessel called a 'washback'. Nothing is wasted in the process of making malt whisky. The residual grist solids left behind in the mash tun are collected and then used as the basis for highly nutritious cattle food. The next stage of the process is when yeast is added to the wort mixture in the washback. This starts the fermentation action in which the sugars in the wort are converted into alcohols. What actually happens is that the yeast - naturally occurring living organism - is using the sugars to 'live out' its life cycle. Whilst doing this, it 'exhales' carbon dioxide gas, thus creating the appearance of 'boiling'. Fermentation stops after about 48 hours and at this point the wort has been transformed into the 'wash'. The liquid is similar to strong ale, with an alcohol content of around 10%. It is now ready to be transferred to the still house for the first stage of the actual distillation process. Here the wash is heated in the huge, gracefully-shaped copper pot-stills. Under the watchful eye of the stillman, the liquid gradually reaches a temperature at which it starts to vaporize. These vapors rise up, collecting between the graceful sloping slides of the pot still towards the top. Here they are led away by the swan-shaped neck of the still to cool and condense back into a liquid form. This first distillation lasts for about 5 hours and separates the alcohols from any remaining traces of yeast and unfermentable residue. In the process, the liquid has undergone another name change! It is now called 'low wines' and contains about 30% alcohol. The low wines pass to a second pot still where they undergo a further distillation. Once the vapors from this second distillation have been condensed back into a liquid, they are passed through a 'spirit safe'. This enables the stillman to monitor quality, purity and other characteristics in order to decide how much liquid should be drawn off as whisky ('The Spirit' or 'Middle Cut'). and how much should be returned for further distillation. At the beginning and end of the distillations, the quality and strength of the liquids being produced is not up to the standard required for 'The Spirit' and they are drawn off separately for further distillation. Collectively, these liquids are known as 'Feints' with the liquid at the beginning of the process being called the 'Foreshots' and those at the end the 'Tails'.` After the second distillation, the clear pure spirit that the stillman has accepted and drawn off to the spirits receiver has strength of around 69% alcohol. It is now ready to be filled into the oak casks to begin a maturation of not less than three years, as required by the demands of quality, tradition, and of course...... the law. During the maturation process, as much as 10% of the whisky can be lost through evaporation. This is known as 'The Angel's Dram' , or 'Angel's Share'. However, less angelic intruders are unlikely to get very far. In each bonded warehouse, the whisky is protected by a special high security lock, with the key being kept by the distillery manager. The oak casks for storing whisky during the long years of maturation have been used previously for storing sherry or bourbon. As time goes on, the whisky absorbs traces of flavor and color from the wood, imparting yet another subtlety to the character of that particular malt. If the warehouse is situated close to the sea, as on Islay for example, the whisky will absorb traces of the flavor of the damp of salt-laden air.
