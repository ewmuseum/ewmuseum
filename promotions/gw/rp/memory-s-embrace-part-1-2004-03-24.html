Memory's Embrace part 1

24 March 2004
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 3-24-04 Some people look at me and think that I am dead. In some ways, they’re right. My eyes are baggy and dark, showing all too well that the last time I had a good night’s sleep was at least a decade ago. I don’t sleep any more than a few hours a night, if at all. Part of it is involuntary, but part of it is also my choice. Sometimes I would do anything to keep from closing my eyes. Every time I lay down at night I don’t get the same experience most might. Some people lie into the bed and feel comfort, feel the soft, warm arm of sleep overtake them, the soothing whispers of the sandman seducing them into voluntary submission. But sleep is nothing like that for me. Nothing. What to most people is a soft, warm blanket slowly rolling over one’s body is more like a coffin being sealed for me. Every time I lie in bed, I feel cold. Cold as death. My bed isn’t a soothing coddle with a loved one, a salvation from the cruel harshness of reality. It’s like laying in a tomb, a grave. The cold, freezing fingers of death run down my back, bringing shivers to my spine, and the suffocating, agonizing embrace of memory overtakes me, grabs me tightly and doesn’t let go. I fall not into a safe, wondrous world away from the world I reside in, but into a chasm that has a period in my life frozen in time that I have spent an eternity trying to forget. Because the past is a horrific thing; even though it has ceased to exist, its spirit lingers long in our minds, and for me the most common place it reveals itself is when I want to escape most, when I am sleeping. When my eyes close the memories flood back in, crashing through the invisible walls I have spent a decade building and enforcing to keep them out. When the memories do overtake me, I can only watch the images in my head and nothing more. I grew up under the tyrannical arm of a militant father, and lived a military life. Literally. When I was old enough to walk, my father, who had grown up and lived the military life, sent me to a Military Academy for young boys. How can I express the thoughts that ran through my head, when I learned I was in a place made specifically to turn bad boys into respectful citizens, without the slightest knowledge that I was a bad person to begin with. I had always done what my father ordered, and helped my mother, who was Hispanic, whenever she needed it. We were poor, you see, and neither of my parents could find the time to care for me, so my father felt I would be better off learning discipline in the military. I think he just didn’t want to worry about looking after me. Every time I tried, as a child, to get close to my father, he pushed me away. For whatever reason, he didn’t feel the need to acknowledge me or try to connect with me in any way. My mother sometimes said it was because he grew up with a harsh life that made it difficult for him to interact with others. But I was his son. His goddamn son! My mother was a different story. She was beautiful, had light brown skin and long, black, flowing hair. Her eyes were a deep hazel and her smile warm and comforting. She would hold me at night when I couldn’t sleep, running her fingers through my hair and humming to me. Back then I had nice dreams, the kind that don’t deal with the real world and send you into places of fantasy. I would do anything for my mother, she was my only and best friend. I hated my father for taking us apart. My mother pleaded with my father that he not send me away, that she would work extra hours in her job to keep me here, but my father refused her. He said I needed discipline, that too many kids were growing up disobedient, and he’ll be damned if I grow up like that. He hated me so much, I could just tell, maybe he was jealous of my relationship with my mother. He must have enjoyed watching my tears run down my face as I clung to my mother’s hand before being dragged to the bus and taken away. I could never understand what I’d done to deserve this, and even to this day I still don’t know exactly why. But within a week of that trip, I had entered what I could only describe as Hell on Earth. Time ceased to exist as everyday was filled with tiring exercise, drills, chores, and yelling from instructors. I didn’t feel like a human being anymore. I felt worthless, disgusting, like a speck of dirt, like I was less than nothing. I was also the youngest of the kids there, and every day and all night they bullied me, made fun of me, and treated me like some sort of animal around for their amusement. The only thing that kept me sane all that time was the fact that my mother came by once every week to visit me. She would hold me in her arms as I told her the unjust and cruel things they did to me, and she would hum the same familiar songs from my childhood. Back then I was so happy to return to the past, the days when I was with her. She was the reason I kept living at that time. She gave me hope, gave me a goal, that someday I would be out of this place, and I could return to her. I didn’t have dreams of a future back then, no. All I wanted was to return to my mother. That was all. So I continued striving to be the best of the academy, to maybe impress some of the instructors, and hopefully gain some respect, so that I could work my way up and find a way to get out. For years it seemed to work. The adults saw I was a hard worker and praised me, gave me respect. They had never seen such a vibrant and honorable spirit as I. But this only seemed to make the bullying from my fellow soldiers jealous, and the beatings and bullying became worse and more angry. They would conspire and curse and berate me, often finding ways to get me alone and beat me until I lost feeling in my face. I tried to fight back, but after awhile I saw that it was pointless, and simply gave up any hope of overcoming their numbers. I would just stand straight and wait for the first blow, and let them proceed to kick and punch me as I lay on the ground, until they got tired. I found it much more beneficial, as I would be just as hurt but less tired from the beating, and would be able to proceed with my duties instead of lying in the infirmary. The instructors didn’t really do anything to fix this either, because I was still less than nothing, still a worm, although deep inside they respected me, and wanted to do something about it. But one day, everything changed. I was about a year or so in the academy, somewhere in my early teens, when I heard that we had a new soldier. He was about the age of the other boys, maybe older, and he was pretty big for his age. He was quiet, however, and this made him a natural target. On the day he arrived I was walking across the training field where we do our drills, and noticed a large group of guys circling somebody. The new guy stood in the middle of the bull’s ring, silent, his eyes looking left and right as the boys around him mocked and taunted him. In an instant, somebody screamed and ran towards him from behind, and you would swear he had eyes in the back of his head because instantly he ducked a right hand punch and countered with a knee to the boy’s gut, sending all the wind from his lungs out of his body and crumbling to the floor. Another boy attacked and with a loud CRACK the new guy sent a left hand hook into his face, almost sending the boy into a back flip and blood shooting upward like a fountain. Two more boys then attacked, and with a swiftness I though impossible for a guy his size, he dodged one sent a stiff elbow into the back of his head, then kicked the other in the crotch and lifted him over his head, then flung him into the crowd of boys around him. He continued dodging and striking the boys until some senior officers found about and broke up the fight. The new guy didn’t have a scratch on him, and the entire gang of boys was sent to the infirmary for a week. One of them even had to have surgery to repair his face. This incident, however, only resulted in the new guy being isolated from the rest of us. He was given more grueling drills and chores to do, and he kept entirely to himself, never seeming to speak a word. Many began to speculate that he was mute, or that his tongue had been cut off or something. One day, though, I saw him sitting alone at his bed, which was separated from my own because he was older and had to sleep in the same room as the older boys. He was faced the other way and appeared to be sobbing. I slowly approached him. “Hey,” I said. Taken by surprise, the new guy quickly looked at me and began to wipe his face. “What you want?” he said with an annoyed tone. “I saw that big fight you were in when you first got here.” I said, sitting on the bed behind him. “You really showed those jerks. I wish I could fight as well as you, maybe then they’d stop beating on me too.” “Go away.” He said, still trying to wipe the tears from his face. “Sorry, but I noticed you’ve been away from everybody else. The other guys don’t like me either. I’ve been here a year and they still treat me like I’m new. If you want, I could try and help you out when you need it. You know, just so we can watch out for each other.” “I don” need none yer help,” he said. “Ah’m doin” jus? fine by m’self.” “Yeah, I know,” I responded. “But, well, I don’t have any friends, and we have a lot in common.” “I said I don” need yer damn help, GO AWAY!” he said. Stubborn as I was, I got up and walked in front of him. “By the way, I still haven’t heard your real name. What is it?” The new guy then got up and punched me right in the jaw, sending me falling backward against the wall. He then began pummeling and kicking me in the stomach without a word, then kicked me one last time in the chest before leaving the room. Needless to say I didn’t try to talk to him again after that for a long time. But a few days later, the other boys finally got the better of him. One afternoon as the sun was setting, I saw him walking towards his bunker when he was ambushed by the same guys who tried to conspire the beating on the first day. This time they had weapons, large heavy pieces of wood, a steel baton, and a firm grip on him. He tried as much as he could to fight them but couldn’t get loose from their tight grip. They then found a way to handcuff one of his arms to a pipe and proceed to brutally beat him to the point of massive bloodless on his part. Against better judgment, I foolishly flung myself into the battle and fought as hard as I could to try and reduce the number of guys attacking him. Surprised by this, the boys began to focus more on me and lost focus of the new guy, who Lord know how was able to break the pipe off and use it against the group, smacking them hard in the face and stomach. The two of us then fought off the group until a senior officer arrived. For a single moment before we were apprehended, the new guy and I stood breathing heavily and staring at each other, our faces completely covered in our own blood and purple with bruises. His face expressed confusion and still retained a sense of distrust and suspicion, but something about his eyes told a different story. He seemed to realize that I wasn’t an enemy, cuz what sort of enemy would risk their own health to help somebody who nearly beat them to death only days before? Within the next week or so the new guy started to sit next to me at lunch in the mess hall. He never said anything at first, but soon between chewing on his food he spoke. “Z.” “What?” I said, confused. “Z.” “What’what about Z?” “Tha’s mah name. Z.” “Z. That’s a strange name, no offense.” Z didn’t respond, just continued to chew. “Sorry, I don’t mean to make fun, I’ve just never heard of a name like Z before. My name is Ace.” We continued to talk from then on, and even began to stick close to each other whenever we could. The other guys would give us dirty looks and crack their knuckles, but anytime Z turned his head their way and gave them a vacant, yet threatening stare, they would look away and become nervous. We looked out for each other from then on, and I found I didn’t have to worry about anymore beatings for a long time. Z was the toughest guy there, and he got much stronger as time went by. I even saw him laugh once when I told him a joke. In a way, besides my mother, he was the first real friend I can remember having. A few weeks later, I went over to Z’s bunker and saw that his corner of the room was empty; his bed was neatly fixed and his clothing bag was gone. I began to ask around where he was but nobody knew. I finally found somebody who had seen what happened. “They came in early this morning, before the sun was even up,” he said. “And took the new guy away. Two large guys dressed in some fancy Marine garb. I heard something about taking him somewhere for retraining or something like that. Maybe he was being transferred to somewhere else. They cleaned his stuff out and had to give him a tranq just to keep him from resisting, I think he broke one of the guys” noses trying to fight em? off.” I ran over to one of the senior officers to ask him to confirm this story, but he ignored me and told me to go back to my bunker. I asked from then on and they told me to “forget about it.” I didn’t see him again for a long time after that, but when I did see him again it wasn’t exactly pleasant.
