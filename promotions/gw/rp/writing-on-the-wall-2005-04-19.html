Writing on the Wall

19 April 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 4/19/2005 (OOC: This is only for storyline advancement. It is not for a match, as I have asked not to be booked for this Friday due to my weekend trip to Florida.) Ramirez Home; Cochello, CA April 19, 2005, 1952 hours It wasn't over for Juan and Teresa. It wasn't the first time, and it wouldn't be the last, but it made them just as angry, all the same. And it wasn't the biggest of their issues. 'I'm worried, Juan,' said Teresa insistently. 'Melina needs us, but I can't figure out for what. And it gnaws at my breast like a woodpecker on a tree.' Juan embraced Teresa and patted her back gently. 'I'm worried too,' he assured her. 'I don't know what's bothering her so much, but I know she's having some kind of problem with something.' Teresa drew back to arm's distance. 'Do you think we're being too strict with her, Juan?' Teresa was ready to try anything now, including being more permissive with her daughter. 'Is it possible she's assimilated more of American culture than we've anticipated, and she feels stifled by us?' 'It's possible,' Juan was forced to admit. 'I hate to think the way we raise her is making her feel too restricted, but it's possible. Like Gaspar says, this isn't Mexico.' Teresa nodded quietly and decided to talk to her daughter after they'd all eaten dinner. 'Let's get started on dinner,' she said. 'We'll talk to her when we're done eating.' Juan nodded and went into the kitchen with his wife, and together they started preparing dinner. 'Melina, “la cena estar” lista pronto!' Juan called. (*Melina, dinner will be ready soon!) '?Yo vengo, Pap?!' Melina answered from her room. A few seconds later and Melina skipped into the kitchen; her sandals slapping lightly on the linoleum floor. Juan and Teresa were making Paella Valenciana, and they'd just recently begun teaching Melina the basics of cooking. With her parents and under their supervision, they felt safe letting her handle pot holders, stirring spoons, showing her how to lightly toss a salad and drain the water from a pot. 'Necesitaremos alg’n aceite de oliva para esta receta,' said Teresa, planning to show Melina how she saut’ed the chicken with it. 'Abra la puerta inferiora de despensa en la izquierda, y nos trae la botella de aceite de oliva.' (*We'll need some olive oil for this recipe. Open the bottom pantry door on the left, and bring us the bottle of olive oil.) Melina's expression changed in a heartbeat. But just at that instant, the telephone rang, and Teresa smiled at Melina as she asked her daughter to excuse her a minute. 'Hello?' Teresa answered as she picked up the phone. 'Hi, Teresa,' It was Stuart's voice. 'Just calling to let you know they're doing a smaller card this coming Evolution because of Wrestlecade being the following week. So you'll have the night off. No match to wrestle this Friday.' Teresa relayed the news to Juan, who nodded in acknowledgement. 'Okay, Stuart. Thank you for the news,' Teresa said to their agent. 'Talk to you later, then.' 'Right,' said Stuart. 'Bye.' Teresa clicked off and put the phone back on the base, and turned around, smiling at Melina. The smile vanished in a heartbeat as she looked at the bottle of vinegar in Melina's small hands. 'Juan,' said Teresa. Juan turned around and saw Melina holding the vinegar bottle. The child was quickly getting a look of contrite misery on her face; she was instantly certain she was in trouble and was going to be sent to time-out. 'Ordene una pizza de los Domin’s cercanos, Juan,' Teresa said as gently as she could; kneeling down to give Melina a hug. She felt her daughter relax in the hug, although not by much. She still wasn't entirely convinced that she wasn't in trouble. (*Order a pizza from the nearby Domino's, Juan.) Teresa gently touched Melina's chin and smiled reassuringly. 'Hablaremos despu’s de la cena, “bueno”' (*We'll talk after dinner, okay?) An hour later... Melina had eaten her pizza lethargically, and gave no signs of enjoying it. Juan and Teresa couldn't say they didn't understand why. Melina having brought them the vinegar bottle, located in the same part of the pantry as the various cooking oils, finally opened their eyes, although it raised other questions. It's not because she's so shy around people she doesn't know, Juan said to himself. Something's bothering her... something she doesn't want to share. I only hope we can get it out of her. Melina was definitely not shy with her own parents. For her to behave the same way at home, as she'd done several times in the GW facilities and arena, was a sign of a bigger problem. But what? Melina was a bright young girl, and good at Double-Dutch rope skipping and soccer. She'd befriended a few of the other Mexican and Mexican-American children around the neighborhood in Cochello, California, where they lived, and they invited her to join in the soccer games they played in a yard. The children were never alone, as most of the adults and parents in the neighborhood enjoyed watching them play; and some even helped to referee and coach. She absorbed the evening Spanish-Language news reports like a sponge and could recite recent and current events without hesitation. So what was wrong? Melina had changed into pajamas and brushed her teeth by the time her parents came into her room. Teresa and Juan sat down cross-legged on the bed side by side at the headboard, and Teresa smiled lightly as she patted her and Juan's laps. Slowly, almost hesitatingly, the child climbed up and got comfortable. '?C’mo est’s? ' Teresa asked. It was a simple enough start to the conversation, and if Melina wasn't feeling well, it was the best place to begin. (*How are you feeling?) 'Estoy bien,' said Melina. It was all she said. (*I'm feeling well.) 'Sabes que si algo te molestando, puedes hablar con nosotros, preciosa,' Juan said softly, stroking Melina's cheek. '?Sabe que nosotros te adoramos, verdad?' (*You know that if anything's bothering you, you can talk to us, sweetheart. You know we love you, right?) Melina nodded, but she was still beginning to cry. Her beautiful brown eyes glistened with moisture and her lips were fixed in an unhappy frown. 'Shhhh,' Teresa whispered, resting her daughter's head on her shoulder. 'No llores, mi chica. Est? bien, no necesitas tener una cara roja. “Es cualquiera de los ni’os en el vecindario est’n peleones”' (*Don't cry, my little girl. It's all right, you don't need to be embarrassed. Are any of the kids in the neighborhood picking on you?) 'No,' Melina whimpered. 'Ellos son todo agradable a m?.' (*No. They're all nice to me.) 'O, mi hija, te ruego que hablas conmigo y tu padre,' said Teresa, feeling her own voice become choked up at the frightening feeling of helplessness that was beginning to seep in. 'Queremos ayudarle. No lo podemos hacer mejor si nosotros no sabemos lo que est? equivocado. “Ayudemos”' (*My daughter, I beg you, talk to me and your father. We only want to help you. We can't make it better if we don't know what's wrong. Help us?) Almost half a minute later, Melina finally looked up at Teresa and Juan, but she stayed silent. So humiliating was the secret she bore, so frightened was she of how her parents would react, that she couldn't believe that it would really be all right. Juan reached out a hand and lightly wiped the tears from Melina's right cheek. He and Teresa could feel the deep humiliation Melina felt at the secret eating away at her. They could feel the grief it caused the child. Juan knew she couldn't bear them alone. 'Estaremos tristes contigo,' he said gently, hoping Melina would sense it in his voice. 'Lloraremos contigo. Si tienes miedo de algo, nosotros tendremos miedo contigo.' (*We'll be sad with you. We'll cry with you. If you're afraid of something, we'll be afraid with you.) Teresa hummed Melina's favorite children's lullaby, rocking the child back and forth. Juan joined in with the humming. When Melina was four and five years old, nothing had made her feel safer. They waited patiently, humming the lullaby together. Five minutes later, Melina's body finally stopped shaking. She turned her face into Teresa's shoulder, and her right hand gripped Juan's hand tightly. The words came loose in a tortured, agonizing voice only slightly higher than a whisper. 'No puedo leer, mamacita.....' (*I can't read, mommy...) Teresa's hand shot up to her breastbone, clutching desperately at the front of her shirt. Wasn't there a knife protruding from there? Her heart thumped painfully against her sternum, and her breathing was ragged-- Where was the steel blade? Where was her blood? The fabric of her shirt couldn't be unbroken and not torn... she was convinced a knife had been plunged to its hilt straight into her breast. 'Deme un abrazo, preciosa,' Teresa cried softly. 'Lo necesito ahora...' (*Give me a hug, sweetheart. I need one now...) Melina settled her full weight against her parents; her small arms reaching up for their necks. Juan eased forward, his body lightly sandwiching Melina between him and Teresa as he reached out to hug both his daughter and his wife at the same time. Teresa's head bowed forward, her tears becoming bitter sobs as she clung tightly to the two people she loved more than life itself. Realistically, she knew the wet sensation on the back of her head was Juan's tears, as he wept with her and their daughter... but she felt pain there as surely as if it was acid; Melina's agonized words burning a hole in her skull.... 'No puedo leer, mamacita.....'
