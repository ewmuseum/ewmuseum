Finisher: (1) Karmic Law (2) Threefold (3) Divine Retribution ((1) Katahajime Suplex (2) rolling German suplex into bridging Aztec suplex (3) inverted STF (Muta Lock))
Other Moves: Karma spent a lot of time training in technical maneuvers. One of the things that interested him in particular was learning suplexes. As a result of his concentration on it, Karma is essentially a suplex machine, having a list of suplexes that seems ponderously long to look at. Most of these suplexes he will also use in bridging variations where possible. Many of them he has adapted to work in superplex format, and many of them, he has also tried spider suplex format (where both opponents are on the top turnbuckle, but the attacker hooks his legs on the ropes and throws the opponent backwards into the ring, causing the attacker to do a deep backbend). They should be considered the meat of his technical skill, and what makes him the most dangerous, especially suplexes that come from submission holds, which are another one of his specialties.

**- This move is a trademark move for Karma. He does not do it in every match, but when a rivalry has been particularly heated, he will incorporate it as punishment of sorts. It doesn't really have a name at the moment, but people have been known to call it all sorts of things, most notably the trend of calling it 'Karma's a Bitch' on the indy circuit in England. Karma himself has no name for it.

Karma additionally has some VERY strict rules that he follows when in the ring. They are elaborated below.

The Karmic Code

.Item 1. Under no circumstances is cheating or causing a purposeful disqualification acceptable behavior. This includes getting counted out, using weapons, using the ropes to make a pinfall, and using tactics illegal to a wrestling ring, such as close-fisted punches, holds on the ropes, chokeholds, and raking the hands across any part of the opponent's body.
.Item 2. It is not acceptable behavior to lose one's temper in the ring. It is not conducive to a fair fight to do so, and in the instance of a loss, it is obvious that the opponent was superior, and thus it is fruitless to become angry.
.Item 3. It is not acceptable to know of the suffering of another person and not attempt to assuage it. This may at times include getting involved in someone else's business, but it would be a breach of conscience to allow someone else to be victimized and not attempt to remedy the situation.
.Item 4. It is unacceptable to strike the first blow in a fight, especially in anger. It is acceptable to defend oneself, but it is not acceptable to provoke a fight.
.Item 5. (Added vs. Patti Anderson) It is not acceptable behavior to compete against an opponent who may not be medically fit to do so. It would be a breach of conscience to potentially further the wounds of an already wounded competitor- all possible avenues must be taken to prevent this occurring.
.Item 6. (Added teaming w/Joshua Kosidlo vs. Reginald Atkins and John Adams) It is not acceptable behavior when in a team situation to allow one's partner to be brutalized by both men competing on the other side. While some may see it as bending the rules, it is a necessary evil to get into the ring and defend one's partner from an opponent taking an unfair advantage.

This is the whole code *for now*. Please note that it is a fluid document that *WILL CHANGE* according to what happens to him, and the lessons he learns in the ring.

Entrance:

A soft female vocal line begins to filter through the speakers. The lights go down over the stage, and blue spots swirl on the entry way.

Suddenly, the music springs to life with a hard guitar riff. The screen lights with a blurred image, which soon reveals itself to be a castle, sitting on a hill. It is a picture of serenity, and on the parapet stands a lone figure. The camera zooms closer, trying to catch who it might be- and gets close enough to see a purple cape hanging regally about the shoulders of its bearer. A golden crown perches on the proudly held head, and a long, flowing honey-brown mane of hair undulates gently in the breeze beneath it.

But only for a moment. Suddenly, that picture explodes into a thousand tiny pieces, and with it comes an explosion of white pyro onstage that's so bright it looks almost like a supernova for a moment. The song kicks into a syncopated beat, and Roy Khan's smooth vocals come in on top of it.

I am a king of honor, gold and glory
But every king must also die
Have I been just and righteous?
What is glory?
I know I've torn and taken life
And here I stand...
A small and simple man

Underneath those words stands one of Global's newest, and perhaps most difficult to understand hires- Karma.

Brian West: On his way to the ring, from Tromso, Norway, standing 6'3' and weighing in at 238 pounds... KARMA!

Who will trade his karma for my kingdom?
A sacrificial rite to render truth
The fire in my soul rejects my wisdom
Cause all you do in life comes back to you

Karma bows his head for a moment, and then starts down the ramp, fueled along by the chorus of Kamelot's 'Karma,' and, perhaps a little peculiarly, by the cheers of a good few fans.

I am a king in crisis, counting minutes
There is an ending to my reign
My sins have come to face me, I can feel it
That I have lived my life in vain
And now I know I'll reap The seeds I've sown

Karma nods a respectful regard to the fans who try to get his attention as he makes his way down to the ring. He tosses his shoulder-length hair out of the way as he climbs the steps with little fanfare and slips into the ring.

He waits patiently as the referee pats him down, and then slips into his corner to await the signal for the beginning of the match. Though he's somewhat less showy than perhaps his opponent might be, there is a quiet dignity and focus about him that is undeniable. His intensity carries him along in its fire, where more boisterous personalities might simply depend on show.
