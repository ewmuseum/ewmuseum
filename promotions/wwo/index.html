A roleplay-driven website e-fed out of Biloxi, Mississippi, whose motto was “Simply the Best.” It closed once after four years under its own owner, then reopened for the run held here: 35 shows and 99 recorded reigns across eleven title lineages, reported on in its own in-fiction newsletter, The Source. Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

The World Wrestling Organization ran out of Biloxi, Mississippi, under the motto "Simply the Best," and staged itself as a roleplay-driven promotion rather than a results-first one. Its own FAQ says so outright: "unlike most organizations who state that the RP's are the final outcome, in the World Wrestling Organization, the storylines drive the federation... no roleplays means no story. No story means no World Wrestling Organization." The site's own account puts its founding around 1995, run by its founder before he handed the federation to his successor in March 1997; its FAQ describes a first run of "four long years" that its founder closed himself, "to send a message (and to save my sanity)." The material this wing holds is the second life that followed — a 2001–2002 capture, not the federation's own beginning.

Ten championships changed hands across the held record, several of them inside the same few months of 1999: the Television Championship alone passed through five champions between January and November of that year — "Money Driven" Mike Sebastian, Apex, Latex Lex, Dark Soul and "Smooth as Silk" Miguel Quesada, who was still champion when the ledger's own notes record the federation closing. Apex and Quesada wrestled the promotion as brothers, by their own bio cards' account — sons of the same touring wrestler, raised mostly by their mother once their parents divorced. Ultimate Thrasher held the World Title three times, twice over Big Dave Grief, billed as Roundhouse; Lord Keeblor won the United States Championship twice, two and a half years apart, the second time off "The Prodigy" Tracy Hudson in the same city he'd taken the belt to begin with. The newly created Title of the Americas has exactly one holder on record: Papa Legba, who eliminated Steve Dumars from the ring to win it in June 2001.

Its own dirt sheet, The Source, ran under bylined in-fiction columnists — Mike Horan, Gary Hammond, Rumor Will, a gossip desk credited to "Dr. Mal Practice, MD" after the wrestler of the same name — reviewing WWO's own cards and running its own rumor mill alongside them. Its staff page names a full front office in the same voice: a President, a Vice President for the tag division, a Talent Coordinator, a Light Heavyweight VP position it admits sat vacant, and a bench of on-air personalities. Roster turnover is part of the record too — the held bio cards include wrestlers billed as "Former," carried over from the site's own earlier roster pages rather than dropped when they left.

Andrew "Flash" Tucker's own bio card is the fullest single biography the capture holds — a former Oakland gang member found wrestling from a hospital bed after being stabbed, whose career the card narrates through half a dozen federations before he lost the WWO's own cruiserweight tournament final to "Problem Child" Chris Stringer. The same card lists two real-world memorial tournaments, the Jay Ralston Memorial Cup and the Gordon Solie Memorial Cup, among his other interfed credits — real accolades of the sport itself, named on the card exactly as the fed's own writers named them.

The record goes dark between the two eras the same way it opens again: unexplained. Every title's own ledger stops at the first closure — the World Title's own last recorded change is "Mr. Ratings" Warren Hayes knocking out Mike Sebastian to end a battle royal on 15 January 2000 — and when the held record resumes, at the revived WWO's own 24 August 2001 Superstar Showcase, Tyrone "Purple" Hayes is already World Champion, defending against a Steve Dumars the surviving record never otherwise names. Nothing held explains the change of hands; it stands as an honest gap, not a guess. A Source newsletter issue from March 2001, midway through that gap, recovers one full card in its own words — a chaotic six-man main event crowning Tyrone Hayes the #1 seed for a World Title tournament, working alongside Ultimate Thrasher and "Problem Child" Chris Stringer against Apex, Latex Lex and Nate Knowitall — the same newsletter's own retrospective column separately recovers one-paragraph recaps of three earlier Showcases, from 1998, 1999 and 2000, anchoring the belts' own classic-era motion in named dates and cities even where most of those cards' own entrants never otherwise surface in the held record.

What ended it isn't in the surviving record. The site's own last dated material runs into 2002; nothing held says how or when the WWO closed a second time.

Storylines

The fed's own 1999 Simply the Best Awards named Carnage-a-Trois — Todd Johnstone's stable of Tyrone "Purple" Hayes and Latex Lex — its own Best Stable, and Hayes both Rookie and Heel of the Year in the same ceremony. The revival era picks the thread back up directly: at the recovered March 2001 Superstar Showcase in Buffalo (card), Hayes anchors the main event, a chaotic six-man #1-contender match alongside Ultimate Thrasher and "Problem Child" Chris Stringer against Apex, Latex Lex and Nate Knowitall. The fed's own broadcast text runs it as a genuine clusterfight: Johnny Detson runs in to pull Thrasher off a pin, Behemoth chokeslams Hayes off the stage entirely, and Hayes still recovers to plant Lex with the Fingerpoke of Doom and claim the #1 seed for a World Title tournament — the held record's own closest thing to a season-long angle actually paying off on camera.

The same Buffalo card stages the reveal of the wing's own richest single angle. William Craven's own bio card narrates it directly: signed to the fed under "the pseudonym (and pun) of 'Major Damage,'" managed by Dr. Victor Schwartz billed as "General Mayhem," and run as the enforcer of a military-gimmick stable, Operation Scorched Earth, fronted on-air by Chris Stringer. The Buffalo card's own Behemoth match stages the stable at work: Behemoth mistakes Stringer's bodyguard Luke Morningstar, in military fatigues at ringside, for one of Operation Scorched Earth's own soldiers and attacks him, drawing a DQ; "Major Damage" then runs in and applies the Dead Zone to Dark Soul until Miguel Quesada and Apex race down for the save. Per Craven's own bio card, Stringer later "gave the order" and Schwartz "pulled the mask away, revealing William Craven" — the unmasking itself lands off-page, in the bio card's own account rather than a served show, but the stable's own on-air work leading into it is staged in full on the Buffalo card.

Brothers on opposite sides of their own family's wrestling legacy: Apex and Miguel Quesada, sons of the same touring wrestler by their own bio cards' independent account, wrestled the WWO as rivals as often as allies — Quesada took the Television Championship from Dark Soul in the classic era, and the two brothers' own history (a father's abuse, a childhood spent homeless together in Miami) sits behind both men's separate feuds with Dark Soul across the held record. No single served show recaps their own reunion beat by beat; it survives only in each bio card's own telling, cited on both men's own pages.
