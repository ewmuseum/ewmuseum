Date: 7/22/09 “What saves a man is to take a step. Then another step.” -Antoine de Saint-Exupery July 13th, 2009 2:17 P.M. The parking lot of the Dike wasn't an interesting place. A bare-bones asphalt expanse, the lot was rarely occupied by more than a handful of lonely sedans, presumably belonging to the juvenile detention center's guards. Occasionally, a hooptay with rusty scars would be sitting in one of the visitors' spots, but in general, it just wasn't a place that encouraged visitors. Who wanted to come see the felonious farm system, where hope for these young men’s salvations so inevitably dimmed? I’d been only a handful of times since UltraViolence, telling myself that the pre-Colossus East Coast road schedule was hard to navigate around. But I was enjoying the job more in a post-title-obsession lifestyle, and maybe the whole reason for this…maybe it was dissipating as my life’s reclamation project got underway. The whole reason you’re here, I told myself, is to be selfless. But selflessness is, ironically, a lonely road. Living for nothing, which I’d done for the first thirty-two years of my life, was much easier than living for something. Lost in self-analytical limbo, I didn’t notice the woman in front of me until I was literally on top of her. My gait took me straight into her, my chest catching her across the face, and I started to topple forward, with her underneath me, falling backwards. I caught one foot’s worth of balance, threw an arm around her, and spun a hundred and eighty degrees, pulling both of us up with a poor man’s pirouette. We both stared at one another. “Jesus, I’m…” My hand, still on her back, detected the flurried motion of a heart in flux. “…sorry.” “I…it’s alright,” she stammered. “I’m fine.” Her smile just barely lifted the corners of her mouth, as though a great weight kept her facial features chained down. “I totally wasn’t looking where I was going,” I admitted. “You made up for it.” Her voice was as soft and unassuming as an insect touching down on a leaf. There was just the slightest hint of an accent, one she worked hard to hide. “Making sure I didn’t fall. Thanks.” “That? Pffft, you should see me on ice skates.” While I suppressed the left brain’s suggestion that flirting in this particular parking lot was a very poor idea, I noticed the smell of her hair. Honey and almonds and some other chemicals. I was going through my mind’s catalogue of hair products when I noticed the smile slipping off her face. “Sir, your hand.” She looked over her shoulder, where my savior’s hand was still resting on the small of her back. I gave her a patented smile as I pulled my arm away. “Sorry, I’ve got this savior complex thing.” “Don’t worry about it.” A natural pause provided the opening she needed to leave without being rude. “I should be going. My son’s waiting.” “No sh…no lie? Who’s your son? Maybe I know him.” Her eyes lit up before she spoke. “Oh, are you a parent of one of the residents, too?” That word, Tom Barius’s word: residents. I could just imagine his viper’s smile as he told miserable parents, one and all, that their son’s “residency” in Dike Youth Correctional & Rehabilitation Facility would change things. Then he’d turn around and tell someone like me, who merely wanted to help–and despite my ulterior motives, I did want to help…I think–that, realistically, most of these kids were fated to be career criminals. “I’m just volunteering my time to talk with some of the kids, listen what they have to say. I think it’s easy for the boys to feel like no one’s listening to them in a place like this.” This seemed to please her, as the chained-down-smile flashed again. Charm was second nature when I wanted to summon it. “I tell my son that God is always listening, that he never forgets anyone.” She was beautiful, in a broken-down way. A scar peeked out from underneath her blouse and slithered up to her collar bone. Immaculate cheek bones carried a few too many wrinkles for a woman so (seemingly) young. She had a prominent jawline, but instead of masculinizing her, it framed a wonderfully petite face, drawing attention to the aforementioned cheek bones and mercurial hazel eyes. Her features were distinctly Hispanic, maybe Dominican. “Who’s your son again?” “Dixon Morales. Do you know him?” I did. My first day in the Dike had featured a complete flame-out as I’d tried to win Dixon’s favor with PRIME merchandise. Maybe it had been an insincere gesture in a place that deserved my sincerity, but Barius had also put the kibosh on my generosity just because he could. That was why Dixon had blown up, and from that moment, I’d known Barius was a rotten fucker. “I’ve met Dixon, yep. Good kid.” “That’s right,” she smiled. But without warning, her body language slumped inward, and Dixon’s mother was the picture of embarrassment. Of course. She figured that if I’d met Dixon, I knew why he was in the Dike: he’d beat the living piss out of his mom’s boyfriend, a man absolutely deserving of a jaw-breaking for laying his hands on this woman. “I heard what you two went through,” I started, trying my hand at a tone completely without judgment. “It’s admirable.” Her silence bred awkwardness so I settled on saying something I knew she would agree with. “I’m sure Dixon will be glad to see you.” “He’s got so much anger…” She trailed off, making me think that she was thinking out loud, that she hadn’t intended for me to hear that. “But we’re gonna get through. Myself, Dixon, and the Lord.” She smiled again. Perhaps this was a litmus test for whether I was a good Christian (something I knew that my mother perpetrated on strangers all the time). I wasn’t, but I smiled back weakly anyway. “He’s a teenager. Angsty and pubescent and all that fun stuff.” It was meant to be a joke, but she didn’t laugh with me. “Seriously, I think Dixon will be fine. Barius has him in the best possible situation in there.” “I don’t trust that man.” “Funny, I thought it was just me.” “I’m sorry,” she cut in, “I didn’t get your name.” “Chandler. Chandler Tsonda.” “I’m Marisa Sepulveda.” Her accent came out when she pronounced her name, but it was endearing, as opposed to those people whose accents emerge in irritatingly forced ways (I’M LOOKING AT YOU, GIADA). Mar-EE-sah say-POOL-vay-duh. It was a nice name. “Mr. Morales is Dixon’s father?” “He passed away four years ago,” she replied. “Lung cancer.” Her manner was melancholically robotic, almost numbed to the pain of Morales’ death by having uttered over and over these same words. Almost. “I’m sorry.” “Thank you, Mr. Tsonda. I should really be going.” I knew that Barius was a bastard about punctuality for parent visits. I knew that Marisa Sepulveda was beyond damaged goods. But wasn’t helping families a larger part of what I was trying to do here? At least, that was my justification as I stalled Ms. Sepulveda in the parking lot where I’d never wanted to meet anyone. “I know, and I don’t mean to keep you any longer, but I told Dixon a long time ago…your youngest, Nilton, right?” Mild shock registered on her face. “Dixon told me about it when I talked to him. I know Nilton’s a fan of Bryan Dawkins, the professional wrestler. And I…have a connection. I’ve got a signed photo and a couple t-shirts in the back of my ride.” I gestured to the Navigator. “Maybe you could give it to Nilton as a gift from his brother?” I half-expected to be admonished for meddling in the affairs of an already-way-too-busy single mother, but Marisa just nodded. “That would be very kind.” “You mind waiting for two seconds while I run and grab the stuff from my ride?” I jogged over to the Navigator and grabbed the memorabilia, which had been sitting in my backseat since that day in late April, probably some kind of subconscious reminder to myself that there was still work to do at the Dike. I remembered to pick up one of my Tsuperstar Enterprises business cards. “Mr. Tsonda, this is all too much. I don’t know where Nilton could possibly–” “Just Chandler. No formality necessary, Ms. Sepulveda. And you know how quickly kids go through clothes. It’s just a couple extra t-shirts for him to run around in. Where’s your car? I’ll just drop these off and then stop holding you up.”She pointed me towards a late 90’s Geo, then led the way as I carried the stack of shirts to the trunk and tossed them in. “Thank you, Chandler.” “No problem, ma’am. Listen, here’s the signed photo for Nilton, and I’m giving you my business card. You can throw it away when you get home if you want.” “What kind of business do you do that I might need?” Was she being coy? Were we flirting again? She was probably no older than me, but she seemed older, and had me flummoxed as a result. “It’s not a business. If there’s anything you need, or any way I can help, I’d like to. I don’t have Chan Juniors, don’t really plan to, but I know what it’s like to get into trouble as a kid. In the wrong situation, it can determine the rest of your life, and it shouldn’t. So if Dixon needs a summer job or…ya know, anything.” “I appreciate it, but–” “Like I said, you can throw it away. Just don’t do it to my face. I’m horribly insecure and you’ll tear my heart out if you do.” I was doing it again. The grin, the pithy dialogue. Why was I flirting with a formerly battered single mother of two? If Dixon hated me now, imagine how he’d feel if his mother told him that I’d been hitting on her in the parking lot. It was a lose-lose. “Okay, then. Are you coming inside?” “I’m actually on my way out.” Her eyes...they looked slightly dimmer. Maybe this was more than just two strangers in a parking lot. “Tell Dixon I said hey.” It took a uncharacteristic self-control not to look over my shoulder as I walked back to the Navigator. /~/~/~/~/ July 15th, 2009 11:58 P.M. Gil Lavenstein was a truly awful dinner date. “Then they tell me that there are ‘building inspection’ fees for the clubhouse. What kind of country club charges the members for their goddamn building inspections?! I knew it was a mistake to leave the Jewish country club.” “Gil.” “Northaven was fine, but the greens are always in terrible shape. Adds at least four or five to my handicap and it’s just embarrassing to bring clients out there. Keith Scott Zimmerman took one look at the green on 2 and started screaming about–” “Gil, seriously.” “Sorry, Chan. But I’ve been hit just like everybody else by this goddamn recession. You know I pay three hundred dollars a week for my daughter’s clarinet lessons?” In the moments following ReV 200, I was out for an ill-advised late dinner with my agent and on-off friend, Gil Lavenstein. Trying to get reservations on Wednesday night in New York was like trying to get reservations on New Year’s Eve anywhere else. We’d settled on eating in the hotel bar, where the cuisine was inoffensive and ESPN was playing on mute. “Gil, I don’t give a hairy goddamn about clarinet lessons. Can we talk about my something else. How’s my contract shaping up?” “I wish I could be a shoulder to cry on here, Chan, but I don’t have any idea what renegotiations are gonna be like. You’ll probably have to take a paycut.” “You know I’m not in this to line my pockets anymore. Tsuperstar is weathering the financial shitstorm quite nicely, and I’m all taken care of.” Gil took a loud, squishy bite of a French fry before giving me any more details. “Then what’s all this arm-wringing about?” Gil was a man fixated on the numbers, not abstractions like loyalty or leaving a legacy. It made him good, borderline great, at his job. “I’d imagine PRIME will you give you a self-perpetuating contract. You know, fixed at a two or three mil, renewing without negotiation so long as you make ninety percent of ReVs, half the house shows, and included bonuses for the big shows.” “So….I should be set.” “Should be,” he nodded, paying great attention to his grilled chicken sandwich. “What about retirement?” A fry dropped from Gil’s open mouth, and his eyes shot from the filet up into mine. “Retirement?! You’re a spring chicken, for God’s sake!” “I’m not saying now, I’m saying when.” “When what?” “When it’s time.” I’d thought, more than once, about retirement. When that time came, for the first time in my life, I’d be able to dictate my own schedule. No jetsetting across Europe to make each country’s premier runway show. No sitting on the tarmac for hours on the way back to Tsuperstar Enterprises HQ. No living out of a suitcase in middle America just for the sake of a couple house shows. It sounded pretty nice. “Well, it’s not time for a long time. You’re a damn young man. You’re in the best shape of your…well, you’re in great shape.” “I’m gonna have surgery. After Colossus.” “Chan, let’s not have this discussion again. The doctors say that you’ll be fine for another year or two, and major surgery would just put you in unnecessary pain.” “My coming into those same doctors’ offices is paying for their kids’ braces. When I get surgery, I’ll actually be fixed. No more pain.” “Chan,” Gil worried out loud, “I’m hearing too much ‘when’ and not enough ‘if.’” “I haven’t hit the pause button for more than a couple weeks in over two years, Gil. And usually, those couple weeks have been spent recovering from some eye-popping injury. I’m gettin’ up there in dog years.” “Let’s not get ahead of ourselves. Don’t you have a match at Colossus with Wade?” “Potentially,” I shot back. “But it’s not like last year.” It gave me a mild case of goosebumps to remember last year’s Colossus. Stepping out onto the biggest stage in our world, literally feeling the kinetic energy created by tens of thousands of hands clapping, tens of thousands of bodies jumping up and down. “Well, it’s still Colossus. That means something to you, and it’s that something that won’t let you retire any time soon.” Maybe it was my time at the Dike, seeing those kids. Maybe it was just my long-delayed growing up. Something though, had kept my mind on my wrestling mortality over the past couple weeks. My body hadn’t been built like Wade Elliott’s or Duke Williams’s; it was a finicky, fragile thing, never intended for gladiatorial punishment. Cliché firmly in hand, I’d acknowledged that I wasn’t built for this shit. “Win or lose, I’m having the surgery after Colossus. I’ll be back by the beginning of 2010 for one more good ride.” “Chan, I can’t advise strongly enough what a bad career move that is.” “Career? Gil, this hasn’t been my ‘career’ since I came back in 2007. I can sit on my ass and watch Oprah every day for the rest of my life. The money is set.” “I know that, Chan. But a lot of you guys…” He stopped, retracing his verbal steps. “A lot of wresters, athletes, entertainers, they aren’t ready for what actually happens when the spotlight goes away." “What if I started a charity for kids who’re in trouble? Ya know, good kids who just get caught up in dumb shit?” “Jesus Christ…” Gil muttered to himself for a moment, pinching the bridge of his nose exasperatedly. “This mid-life crisis shit has you by the damn horn, Chan! Giving up a lucrative career to help out trouble teens? Sounds like a bad episode of Touched By An Angel.” “I dunno, I just know that I’m grown up enough to realize that I don’t want to be that guy who’s still coming back to PRIME when I’m fifty and wrinkled as all fuck and trying to play to a crowd for cheap pops.” “That’s fifteen years from now! We’ll probably be on Mars by then. Our cars will fly. Cancer will be cured. Britney Spears’ kids will have just become aware of how horribly screwed up they’re going to be. Why are you planning a future just in order to avoid something?” “Colossus, surgery, and one final run. That’s all I’ve got left in me, Gil.” “Are you pissed about ReV 200? Listen, man, that whole five-person team stuff was silly anyway. You’re an alpha individual, not a team player. You’ll bounce back!” “Has nothing to do with any show. I appreciate the enthusiasm, but I just wanted you to know my plans. Wanna make sure you’re able to plan for the future so you can keep up those three-hundred dollar clarinet lessons for the chilluns.” With a sigh, Gil returned to his food, silently chowing down on a sandwich that seemed to offer him much less pleasure now. “Listen,” I said, feeling freed of a rather large burden (although other responsibilities clogged my medulla), “I’m gonna step outside, clear my head a hit. And dude, calm down. You look like someone just gave you positive test results for the clap.” He did. It was cool outside the hotel. New York City, urban jungle to end all urban jungles, was having some uncommonly cool summer weather. I decided to revel in the placid night air, instead of worry about how global warming was fucking over all our seasonal weather. “Shorty’s like a melody in my head that I can’t keep out, got me singin’ like–” Sean Kingston’s “Replay” had interrupted my tranquil summer moment. I interrupted it, clicking the green button on my BlackBerry to pick up the call. I was bombarded before I got my first word fully out. “Hello, this–” “Chandler, I’m not pissing my pants on this one. You’re retiring and you don’t tell your top adviser at Tsuperstar?! Listen, I’m not gonna play out of the loop for this, so if you think you’re coming back here to run the day-to-day, then I can’t strongly enough advi–” “Allan, take a breath.” The lawyer-turned-businessman huffed and puffed on the other end of the phone. “Now take another one, and tell me why you’re calling me at midnight raving like a Michael Moore groupie.” “Gil just texted me. He said you’re behaving erratically, talking about hanging up the boots. If you want the company back, Tsonda, you can have it. But don’t expect me to stick around if you’re coming back to make a power play.” I glared at the window to the restaurant, where Gil had strategically buried his head in his sandwich. I hoped that whenever retirement did finally come, it would mean an end to this life sans privacy. “Allan, that couldn’t be farther from the truth. I don’t have a clue when I’m gonna call it quits, but I’ve been thinking about things, and I decided it was good if I started to look at where I want to be in five years, ‘cuz it sure ain’t wrestling some kid twenty-years younger than me who fancies himself a legend killer.” “You gotta slow down, Chandler. Talk to a Charles Schwab associate about putting together a retirement fund, and they’ll tell you all about the best ways to secure your money for the future. You want the number of my guy?” “Good night, Allan.” A click of the red button. I inhaled. City air was always a bit putrid, and the stillness of the night didn’t make it any different. I regretted taking a bit whiff of the Big Apple. “Shorty’s like a melody in my head that I can’t keep out, got me singin’ like–” “Allan, seriously, you gotta fuckin’ calm down. You’re gonna burst a blood vessel.” “Chandler, this is Marisa Sepulveda.” What. The. Fuck? The first response was instinctual. “Heeeeey, Ms. Sepulveda.” The extended “hey” is the oldest trick in the book when you’ve been absolutely blindsided. “How’s it’ goin’?” Stupid question, I immediately realized. It was midnight on a Wednesday. This wasn’t a “hey, how’s the weather” call. “Listen, Chandler, I need to speak to you. It’s about Dr. Barius.” I hadn’t expected my heart to thrash so wildly at the sound of Marisa’s voice. “Did he do something? If he did something to one of the boys, then I’ll–” “No, no, it’s nothing like that.” She hesitated. “You know what, I shouldn’t have called. Sorry to have bothered you.” “It’s fine. I’m just eating crappy hotel food with a friend. What’s up?” “You said you don’t trust Dr. Barius.” She was treading carefully. Something was rotten in fucking Denmark. “Not as far as I can…yeah, I don’t trust the dude.” “He acted very strangely towards me today. Now what Dixon did…it was wrong, he deserves to be punished. But I think something strange is going on at the facility.” “Ms. Sepulveda, I don’t like the guy, but that doesn’t–” “Marisa.” “Marisa, I’m just not sure there’s any way to go about this without seeming like conspiracy theorists.” While I doubted Barius being any fucking good at his job or suppressing his own need to be throbbing cock, I wasn’t ready to play hard-boiled detective and go after the guy. “You’re right. I’m sorry.” We both said nothing, and the silence pounded me into submission. Maybe she had nothing else, was just grasping at straws. But there was strange attraction in the air between us, and despite my better judgment–how would we be able to figure out what really went on at a facility designed to keep some in and all others out–I was pulled in. “No, you might have something. Listen, I’m gonna be back in California this weekend. Why don’t you call me on Saturday and we’ll talk to some people I know about getting to the bottom of this.” “Thank you. Thank you. Thank you.” She repeated it like I’d done something, like I’d saved her from whatever in her life had crushed her. It was a brilliant feeling. “No problem. Talk to you soon.” I hit the red button with a long sigh. Savior complex, indeed.
