Matthew, they told her, had lost a lot of blood from a fissure in his forehead that had given life to a flowing crimson river. A staple gun had fixed that. The concussion he had received, however, was more the issue for concern. The repeated blows to the head Matthew had suffered from the attack had caused a moderate concussion. The effects had been clear, allowing the doctors an easy diagnosis. Ringing in the ears, slightly impaired speech, and an uneven size of the pupils were clear symptoms. She was advised to watch his frame of mind and mannerisms. Depression had been shown to be increasingly related to concussions, especially in patients suffering from repeated head traumas. With a prior mild concussion on his record from his early days in the wrestling industry, Matthew, they claimed, could be at risk of falling into a state of depression, though he had shown no immediate signs. Had the concussion been more severe, the level and variety of concerns would have been greater. He was fortunate, perhaps to the degree of flirting with miracles that the concussion had only been of moderate nature. The doctors had been slightly baffled how the violent blows to the skull had not been more damning. She told them he was hard headed. Her diagnosis lacked the negative aura of her husband’s. For her, it seemed, recovery and clarity were the themes of their assessment. Apparently, as she had come to find out, the last three months of her life had seen each second tick away without a hint of awareness on her part. It was, a term she had recognized instantly from Matthew’s education in psychology, Anterograde Amnesia that the doctor’s told her she had suffered from. The cause of the short-term memory loss had been a violent attack that she had been a visual, as well as physical, witness of, and the resulting shock had caused a trauma that devastated certain parts of her brain. Luckily, the devastation had not been that of the corporal variety, and she had been spared the potential permanence of the mentally crippling disorder. Still, she was informed that recovery did not yet seem complete, and that she might continue to suffer from the occasional slip in memory. On top of that, they warned, coming to terms with having lived three months without any recollection would not necessarily be an easy or pleasant adjustment. The truth of that sentiment had been seeping into her blood for the past week, ever since they had released Matthew from the hospital. She wasn’t exactly sure how angry she could be. It was an understandable concept… amnesia changes more than the identity of the person diagnosed. Though the medical staff had cautioned her that the adjustment of trying to piece together three months of time as if it were one of those thousand piece puzzles would be exhausting and painful, she found the realization of the stranger sleeping beside her to be far more disturbing. But what real degree of blame could she possibly throw his way? The doctors had said that a patient recovering from amnesia would face a tough adjustment and might feel changed, but the more she found herself fading away to thought in the middle of the day, or lying awake at night… the single thought that kept occurring to her was that she wasn’t the one who seemed struggling with an adjustment to change. She had wanted to be angry… irate… to think that she had suffered a tragic event that had caused a disintegration of her mind and he had reacted by running back to the world of pro wrestling, breaking his promise, leaving her alone Wednesdays each week. But she had no idea. How many times over the last three months had he looked into her eyes and seen a blankness that made him cringe? For nearly a hundred days he’d grown older, while she’d remained still. He couldn’t fully share a single moment with her… only live it, then forget it… just like she would. What was it he had told her as they left the hospital? “I told you that I loved you a thousand times. You didn’t hear any of them.” That thought scared her as much as anything. She simply realized she wasn’t the only one who had dealt with amnesia, and where she had expected to find rage had been only sympathy. That sympathy had started to wear thin over the last forty-eight hours. At first it was a simple avoidance of the subject of wrestling. She could understand how rough things were for him… just as rough as they were for her. It wasn’t the general fact that he hadn’t wanted to discuss it in any real detail while in the hospital, or during that first day home. What was numbing the sympathy that had grown inside her for the first few days was his continued refusal to discuss why he had joined PRIME or what was the issue between Killean and himself. All he had said was “it was an escape while I adjusted”. That was all. A couple days recovery had given way to week, and at a certain point, she thought she deserved to know what had happened in their lives over the last three months. Matthew just wouldn’t talk to her about much, especially PRIME. But he talked to somebody about it. Sometime a little before ten o’clock the night before, while she had been reading alone in bed, he had received a phone call. Mary hadn’t heard much of what was said, the wall between the bedroom and living room had swallowed most of his words, but she had heard him insist that he would be “ready to go Sunday night”. It was all she had needed to piece together the context of the call. One of the PRIME officials had called to check on the status of Matthew. They would have probably expressed grave concern for his well being. Probably also would have had grave concern for the status of their main event at their big PPV show. Matthew would have then insisted he was fine, updated them in partial sincerity of the status of his condition. That was likely followed by the PRIME officials informing Matthew that they couldn’t be held liable for any injuries he might suffer if he were to choose to wrestle at the big show, and then one last chance for Matthew to sit out this particular card if he would so choose. And that’s when the reply “ready to go Sunday night” would have come. End of conversation. She’d fallen asleep that night before he’d joined her. This morning, he wasn’t avoiding anymore of anything. “Who was that on the phone last night, sweetie?” She knew it was clear from the way she asked that Matthew was aware of the fact that he didn’t really need to answer the question. He poured two glasses of orange juice, placed the carton back in the refrigerator, handed her one glass, took a sip from his own, then sighed decided based on the look in her eyes that maybe he should answer. “PRIME.” “What’d they have to say?” “They wanted to check up on my condition. Make sure that I was still going to be at the show Sunday night for the Universal title match.” Matthew took a seat across from her at the kitchen table, leaning back in his chair, cracking his neck to the left, then pushing it violently to the right. “Stop” The snap echoed in the kitchen, bouncing off the cupboards and hitting her ears from all directions. “You shouldn’t be doing that.” “I crack my neck all the time.” “But you aren’t recovering from a concussion all the time. Snapping your head back and forth like that isn’t going to help.” Mary stood and walked over to the cabinets above the stove, grabbing the loaf of bread, and dropping two slices into the toaster. She kept her back to him as she talked. “Then again, I don’t suppose I should be concerned with you cracking your neck in the kitchen when you’re gonna risk breaking it in the ring Sunday night.” “I take it you heard at least my answer to their questions.” “How can you sit there and tell them that you’ll be ready to wrestle Sunday night, Matthew? You’re lucky to be alive. They told you when you left the hospital that you should stay out of the ring and avoid physical contact for several weeks. Now you’re just gonna jump right back into the ring and risk serious problems? Does trauma to the brain not concern you? How about death? Matthew, they told me at the hospital that serious repeat concussions can cause memory loss! Is that where you want to be? He sat in his chair at the table, his half empty glass of juice just sitting in front of him. She stood at the counter, the heel of her hands on its edge, fingernails grinding into the grain of the wood cabinets below. Mary sighed.
