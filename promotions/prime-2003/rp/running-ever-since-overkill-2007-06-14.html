Date: Thursday, June 14, 2007 I was born by the river in a little tent Just like the river I've been running ever since It's been a long, a long time coming But I know a change gonna come, oh yes it will (~ Ω ~) Danny crinkled his nose and determined privately that the room smelled like ass, which was less of an accurate description and more of a general colloquialism he liked to drop in groups because it always got an easy laugh. It didn't have the same effect when he was the only member of the audience, but he'd never had trouble keeping himself entertained. Throwing a bag onto the closest chair, he collected his attire for the evening. Holding it up to inspect for any abnormalities, he felt his mind wandering again. The prior weeks had posed to him the toughest decision he had faced professionally. Then again, he used to pay people to handle these decisions, and that luxury was abandoned when those people found themselves on the other side of the argument. It was a rare day when Houston had a chance to rival Los Angeles as a destination, but the day had come for Danny. Or rather, it had passed. Weeks ago his agent, Allan North, laid down the law. "Be at the premiere," he said, "or I'm gone." That's probably not exactly what he said, but something along those lines. The message was received. Allan just didn’t get it. He hadn't been cursed with the blessing of delayed gratification. Like everyone else in Hollywood, he still walked with blinders, still thought exclusively in the short term. He didn't see the merit in missing one pivotal career signpost in favor of planting another one just a few scant miles down the path. But part of that problem was because he never understood the overall value of professional wrestling to a man's career. It was Allan's inability to think outside the special edition DVD box that led Danny to hire the goober in the shitty sports coat two years ago, a man whose overall value to the Team Ferguson cause was comparable to a new pair of shoelaces. Then again, the hayseed had never attempted a bloodless coup to get what he wanted. Once again, Allan forced Danny's hand. Once again, he found himself with no solid options but to comply. To give up this premiere and his footing in Hollywood without a backup plan was a risk, and the career of Danny Ferguson features nary a single brick laid by risk-taking. Safety, organization and contingencies - coincidentally, three things that the man himself knew nothing about - were the chief elements of the Ferguson Empire-to-be. That is, of course, if he ever made it to the theater. "We're coming to you LIVE from the red carpet at the premiere of Michael Mann's 'More Than Hardcore'!" The helium and saccharine voice from the television across the room jolted him back to reality. This newfound fixation on the shallowest forms of existentialism had once again put him behind schedule and in hot water. He glanced at his BlackBerry and grimaced. "3 NEW MESSAGES." Fashionably late was no longer fashionable. With a sigh, he set the timepiece on the table and rushed to get dressed. He hoped Allan would eventually forgive him. (~ Ω ~) MANN BIOPIC USES ALL THE RIGHT MOVES from Entertainment Weekly It's hard to believe that a movie about professional wrestling could feel completely real and natural, but that's what Michael Mann did with More Than Hardcore. That feat alone should earn him an award. He breathes life into the story of Terry Funk, a prolific and impossible-not-to-love bruiser who traveled the world as a wrestler for nearly three decades. Funk is played to near-perfection by another man with ties to the ring, Hollywood lightning rod Danny Ferguson. Ferguson has been in the news lately for all the wrong reasons, including a well-covered public brawl with NFL draft choice Irakoy Kincaid that subsequently got the linebacker banned from the league before he'd even agreed to contract terms. Despite all this, it's hard to deny the man's work behind the camera. After turning 2005's Hard Money into a summer hit without a decent director or a solid script, he could have slid right into position as America's new action hero, the modern era's answer to Rambo or McLain. Instead, he signed on to work with Michael Mann and tailor himself to the character, instead of the reverse. It could have been disaster, but the finished product is anything but. Either Mann kept Ferguson's ego at bay during filming or the former child star sucked it up and kept himself in check for the good of the movie. His experience in the ring were invaluable to the movie - not only were the action sequences done in long, swooping shots instead of jump cuts to allow for stuntmen, but the wear and tear of the ring on a man's life feels twice as heartbreaking when the actor really knows how it feels. More Than Hardcore might not be as tangibly successful as Hard Money, or even Mann's last summer-era film, 2004's Collateral, but the two men should be plenty proud of their work. Whispers put both men on early Oscar lists, but politics and conservative thinking will surely knock Ferguson off the ballot quickly. No matter - if his career continues to go in the direction it is pointed after this film, he'll get his recognition soon enough. (~ Ω ~) It's been too hard living but I'm afraid to die Cause I don't know what's up there beyond the sky It's been a long, a long time coming But I know a change gonna come, oh yes it will (~ Ω ~) Shielding their eyes against the setting California sun, a few shoddily dressed wrestling fans (is there another type?) craned their necks and search for high ground. Unique for both their foolish embrace of denim and for their unusually high level of interest in the event unfolding before them, they provide a grating contrast to the sharply dressed filmgoers who are less there to see any movie and more to be seen seeing a movie. One of them, finding a temporary soapbox in the form of a standing ashtray, perched on the edge and reached up high enough to attract attention. It was his moment of truth, the one time he could stand above this crowd, and surely he had prepared his remarks for such an accomplishment. "TEEEEEEERRY!" he bellowed, gathering some snide looks and some cocked eyebrows. Realizing that his interjection needed a follow-up, he obliged: "You fucking rule!" In the middle of the bedlam, Terry Funk raised a clenched fist in his direction, single-handedly (pun!) legitimizing the boy's existence. He gives that warm Funk smile to the small crowd of wrestling fans before his attention is forced back to the 24-year-old blonde reality-TV reject shoving a mic in his face. "Terry, the rumor was that you weren't going to be here this evening." He sneers a little at the question and looks down at the E! logo on the mic. On his best behavior, he gathers himself and avoids giving this poor girl the Flair treatment. "All I can say is you shouldn't believe everything you read," he drawls, looking straight into the camera. "So you have nothing to say about the report that you were unhappy about the movie?" "How can I be unhappy?" Terry beamed, as if he didn’t hear the question or what it implied. "It's all about me!" The reporteress was flummoxed by the man's dexterity, especially since she’d never encountered true charisma before in her career. While she attempts to stammer a response, Funk takes the reigns like a man who had done this hundreds of times before. "Let me tell you a story," he says, almost throwing an arm over the girl's shoulder before thinking better. "When they told me they were making a movie out of my book, I bitched and moaned. When they sent me the script, I bitched and moaned. When they invited me to come watch them shoot the movie, I bitched and moaned like I hadn't said a word the first two times. But when I met Danny Ferguson, I stopped bitching and moaning. "We went out for a beer and he said 'Terry, I won't do this movie if you say so.' Here's a guy, a young guy, whole career ahead of him, and my old ass is getting chapped up and ruining things. So I looked him in the eye and I said 'alia iacta est.' You know what that means?" "I-" "Course you don't. It's Latin. Means 'What's done is done', 'no turning back.' I told Danny that they made it this far and they shouldn't be worried about my bullshi-" "AND speaking of Danny, I think that's his limo right now!" Camera and reporter disappear into the crowd, disrupting the Funker's momentum but giving him something to look at while she leaves. He bides his time before uttering a final expletive, waving to the askew fans and pausing to sign a few autographs. "I'm not sure, but this might be our star! On one of the biggest nights of his career, I can't wait to see what he's wearing!" The crowd ebbed, flowed, and swelled towards the limousine as everyone in range (and a few well out of it) struggled to get a view of the man of the evening. The limo door slowly swung open, and everyone leaned forward that extra half-inch to get a good view. (~ Ω ~) HARRY SEES MORE THAN HARDCORE AND SAYS ITS MORE THAN A WRESTLING MOVIE!!! from Ain't It Cool News Hey folks, Harry here. I've made my feelings about Danny Ferguson known on this site before. I thought Hard Money was one of the worst movies of 2005, and I was appalled that it made the box office that it did. Maybe I'm just a little jealous because Yoko (my girlfriend, because I have a girlfriend who I have sex with and who I see naked because I'm not a loser) swoons over the guy every time she sees him. But I just watched an advance of More Than Hardcore, the Michael Mann biopic of wrestler Terry Funk, and I'm almost ashamed to say that I loved it. You could call the movie an apology of sorts. A lot of people came down on Mann for Ali. He picked an actor in Will Smith who could stretch himself to literally BECOME his character, but the direction and story left a lot to be desired. Now he's back with another sports-affiliated bio, and this time he gives us something to really enjoy. It's a stylized look inside a culture that a) few have genuinely seen inside, and b) rarely had a lot of style to begin with. But the craziest thing is that, once again, he found the perfect actor to play his title character. Danny Ferguson blew me away with this movie. The guy can go from kicking ass in a five-minute action scene to turning in a tender moment with Funk's brother or his wife and daughters. He's a hero actor with the chops to reach award status, or - maybe more importantly - he's a dramatic actor who can do all his own stunts. It's unfair to call him a poor man's Russell Crowe, because what the fuck has Russell Crowe done lately? Expect to hear a little rumbling about More Than Hardcore next year when award season rolls around. People at the Oscars might call it the biggest travesty since the Three Six Mafia, but I swear to you, declaring Mann (or even Ferguson) a legit nominee is not so ridiculous. (~ Ω ~) I go to the movie and I go downtown somebody keep telling me don't hang around It's been a long, a long time coming But I know a change gonna come, oh yes it will (~ Ω ~) "Where the fuck is he?" It was less of a shout and more of a deep, threatening hiss. The phrase had been repeated so many times in the last 20 minutes that an outsider might mistake it for an Allan North trademark. Redundancy was not his chief concern. One-by-one, every intern, tech, studio gofer and erstwhile credentialed plebian was posed the question. The variations on the same response didn't soothe Allan's temper and only brought him closer to wit's end. Several hundred people here to see Danny Ferguson's new movie and not one of them could relay a sighting of the man himself. He checked his iPhone (an advance copy Danny had passed on to him), hoping that maybe he hadn't heard it ring. No new messages. "Cheap piece of shit," he grumbled as the search moved on to the next scapegoat. It was also a rare day when Danny Ferguson would pass up a moment in the spotlight and show up late to his own premiere, but this was just another single off the Greatest Hits album that the star had been dropping on him since he left prison. He thought he had a say in his own decisions all of a sudden, that he could be both a Hollywood megastar AND his own man. Did he somehow forget how this worked? Did the time apart seriously convince the man that he was mortal? The fact that there was even a decision to make on this evening was an affront to what Allan North knew of his client. If things had changed so drastically as to provide Danny another option besides applying exponents to his fame, then he absolutely had to do something. He knew he was right to lay it down on the table in front of Danny, but the right decision wasn’t always smart. These people practically needed diapers with all the coddling they receive in town. Would Allan have delivered the same ultimatum to the Danny he remembered, the one with tissue paper for skin who would threaten action if his name were misspelled? Probably not. As he tore through the hallways in the theater, he considered that this last misjudgment of the ex-child star - thinking that he might have finally matured enough to be spoken to in a grown-up voice - was a mistake. Now he understood that this destructive and disgustingly free-thinking Danny was still as petulant as ever, and that this stunt, arriving late to his own premiere to put pressure on Allan, was proof of that. One quick phone call and a "good cop" apology would get things on track, but that was only a theory as long as he wasn’t picking up his phone. The only way to reach Danny when he’s having a tantrum is through His People, a totem pole which Allan stood atop until recently. While he practiced his fake groveling, he searched for Danny’s manager, the next person down the line. At the same time, he came upon another sock puppet, a studio assistant in a UCLA sweatshirt who had been practicing her own answer for when Allan proposed to her his new catchphrase. "Danny, I-" Allan continued, while the intern quickly spat "I don’t know!" "Excuse me?" "Did you just ask me where Danny is?" "No...why, have you seen him?" She collects herself and stares at the cheat sheet on her clipboard. "Sir, I have not seen Mr. Fergu-" "Whatever. I need you to do me a favor. I need to speak with Vicki Siguchi. She's probably in the green room." "No, sir." Thinking momentarily that his request had been denied, Allan spun back towards the clipboard. The look in his eyes precluded any question, so the intern skipped straight to the stammering response. "Sss-h...she's not here, sir." "I know she's not HERE here, she's in the fucking green room," he barked, about three seconds from sacrificing this young assistant in front of everyone. That is, before the coed committed another sin and interrupted him. "Ms. Siguchi never arrived, sir," the young lady whimpered. Allan's nostrils doubled in size, but he calmed himself enough to avoid an outburst. "Oh," was all he managed. The gears were turning in his head, and he finally came up with something before one of the sprockets dislodged. "Then I need to see Phyllis Stein." "I'm sorry, sir-" "Phyllis - PHYL-LISS..." "No, I heard you, sir. She's not...she's not here either." This time the outburst was unavoidable as Allan's personal boiling point had been breached. He nearly slapped the clipboard out of the woman's hands in a fit of rage. The eyes of everyone on the balcony were on him, and he considered quieting down before having an "I'm Keith Hernandez" moment and throwing mental caution to the wind. "Can someone explain to me why Danny's manager and publicist have decided...to skip...his...premiere?" Recognition set in before he could complete the sentence. Before he managed a follow-up to save himself some face, the sound of a throat clearing stopped him in his tracks. He wished he had kept the volume down. With a deep swallow, Allan turned to see Michael Mann standing in the doorway that led to a private suite in the theater's balcony. The director was not the kind of guy you could pick out of a lineup, nor was he easily pegged as a "Hollywood-type." Consensus around town was that he's the kind of guy you only recognize if you're consciously trying to avoid him. "Allan," he monotoned. "A word." North closed his eyes momentarily and made a brief visit to a God he only kept for moments like this. Then he followed Michael Mann into the side room. (~ Ω ~) "HARDCORE" HAS ALL THE DRAMA OF PRO WRESTLING, NONE OF THE CHEESE from Variety Until this weekend, Danny Ferguson has been given a free pass through Hollywood. As someone who’s most universally tabbed claim to fame was inexplicable staying power, he, more than any one else in the industry, has been allowed to do what he wants when he wants it with repercussions that are rarely even worth it. He is Paris Hilton with a sitcom instead of a sextape - sure, people don’t like him, but they always want to read what happens next. Fortunately or unfortunately for Danny, those days are about to end. After his performance in Michael Mann’s brilliant "More Than Hardcore", it’s time for him to pull up a seat at the adult table. Why does America hate to love Danny? Because he’s a child actor who moved on without an addiction or an arrest (prior to 2006’s legal mishap)? Because he’s aged better than Feldman or Bonaduce? Or is it because ever since "Fours Is A Crowd" went off the air, he’s never graduated beyond kid stuff? Danny Ferguson the action hero was no more palatable than Danny Ferguson the child star. "Hard Money" racked up big bucks two summers ago on the talent of it’s star alone. Likewise, "Four Is A Crowd" rode on his back through every ratings war. Both times it became grating to see what seemed like a particularly skilled actor get wasted in relatively low-brow material. This feeling was tripled when he decided to prep for "Hardcore", a movie about professional wrestling, by actually becoming a pro wrestler. Spare your arguments about method acting - this was a kid at play with a new toy, no different than Tom Hanks and the giant piano in "Big." To an extent, you can’t blame him; wrestling provides a tangible marker of success. Fan support is direct feedback from thousands, a roar of the crowd instead of the ringing cash registers at the box office. Every famous person in Hollywood wants to cash that next paycheck and sleep on a pile of money, but what some of them want even more is to hear their name chanted, to feel a person-to-person response to a performance. In theory, you can’t blame him any more than someone who moves from screen to stage, even if he chose a more base form of theater. He’d earned his time to indulge fantasy and have a little fun, but this recent performance shows that he’s ready to step up, stop carrying weak projects and start performing like the actor he can be. From this point forward, we should no longer treat Danny Ferguson like a child. We’re sure he appreciates your cooperation. (~ Ω ~) Then I go to my brother And I say brother help me please But he winds up knocking me Back down on my knees (~ Ω ~) The crowd continued to buzz, mill and perform other collective verbs outside the theater as the doors were about to open. Whispers and speculation had rippled through the crowd like a wave, or at least one of those wave-demonstrating exhibits at your local science museum. Thirty minutes until the movie and no sight of the star. Was he already in the theater? Had he planned something over the top? Into the cacophony limped Allan North, rocking dead eyes and a broken spirit. His eyebrow twitched faintly as he pushed into the crowd. Much is made about a man’s ability to stand up, to declare intentions in the face of adversity or persecution. It takes mettle, grit, balls, whatever your high school sport coach growled at you before the big game. Surely it’s a tough thing to do, on par with hitting a baseball or telling her you love her. The most important thing you can do, though, is leave something in the tank. As tough as it is to put yourself on the line for what you believe in, it is roughly eight times tougher to have someone call your bluff. Can you stick to your guns when someone fires back? Allan North is a man who hadn’t prepared for a shootout. He was somewhere between slipping into depression and going batshit crazy right here in front of everyone. Not a person in the crowd could ID him as an agent. He might be known in this town, but he isn’t played by Jeremy Piven and that makes him a virtual nobody. How long could he carry a meltdown before people recognized him for who he was? Unfortunately, one person DID know him, thanks to a little crash course before E! went live n the air. The 20-something reporter tries to call out to him as he shuffles towards the street and an incognito exit. "I'd rather not talk right now," he manages, but she displays a persistence that came from a surprising abundance of ambition or an expected dearth of tact. "Very quick, Allan - what kind of projects is Danny considering for his next move?" "I..." he tries to pull away, but the simple question stirs something in him. The metaphorical ball was back in his court. Who said he needed to fold against adversity? They’re asking him what he thinks. Is he Allan North or is he Danny Ferguson’s agent? With internal voices talking over the external crowd, the jilted agent’s thoughts eventually settle on three simple words: FUCK THAT GUY. "Actually, you'll have to ask Danny yourself." The microphone holder, not prepared for a moment of true journalism, cocked her head like a dog whose food had been taken away. "But, as his agent, shouldn't you kno-" "Here's your scoop, Candice: not anymore." The blank stare made Allan want to channel his inner Maivia and commandeer the interview, but he was remarkably placid at the moment, a change that could only be attributed to the detail he was about to reveal to the world. "As of this evening, I am no longer representing Danny Ferguson. I wish him well in his future endeavors and I know he wants the best for me, but we've each had some tough choices to make and tonight he made it clear to me that we should part ways." "So you've spoken to Danny?" "A message was delivered and I heard it loud and clear." He punctuated the sentence by staring straight into the camera, all but ensuring that the clip would be hitting YouTube and CNN - the two bastions of modern American news - within the hour. The reporter, however, remains confused. "So..." "I don't have anything else to say at this time," Allan beams, giving the woman a warm pat on the shoulder before exiting stage left and disappearing from the scene. (~ Ω ~) FERGUSON IS "MORE" THAN ANOTHER JOCK from Roger Ebert, Chicago Sun Times It's unfair to say that I was pleasantly surprised by the performance of Danny Ferguson as professional wrestling luminary (if there is such a thing) Terry Funk. The truth is that I shouldn't be surprised at all. This is an oddly positive assessment for a man with Ferguson's public image, but he has consistently turned in this kind of effort. He has been good or better in everything I've ever watched. Unfortunately, you don't become a breakout star on one performance. This has proved to be Ferguson's problem every step of the way. Once it looks like he has his teeth in something, he disappears or moves on in a different direction. If Ferguson does manage to stick in one place this time, he might find himself at the forefront of a new movement in Hollywood. The trend of the last decade has been the slimming down of the action hero - putting smaller actors like Paul Walker, Christian Bale or even Nicolas Cage into the butt-kicking, lady-winning, villain-conquering roles. Danny Ferguson combines the solid delivery of those well-heeled thespians with the physique and screen presence of Stallone or even Schwarzenegger. He's a studio's dream: he can deliver the bank in summer and a statue in winter. His career growth in this industry is more tangible than any of his generation, more than enough to offset his extended foray into the pro wrestling world. That is, of course, if he starts taking the offers that are made to him. While the accomplishments and influence of Mann are not to be discredited, More Than Hardcore may be remembered less as another chapter in Mann's film legacy and more as the turning point in that of of Danny Ferguson. After years of ambivalence and frustration, he seems to have finally found his niche. (~ Ω ~) There been times that I thought I couldn't last for long But now I think I'm able to carry on It's been a long, a long time coming But I know a change gonna come, oh yes it will (~ Ω ~) The television across the room continued to play the same premiere special, although the words had largely lost meaning. Danny's clothes were strewn everywhere in between, each absorbing the light before it reflected off the cement floor. A faint, rhythmic hum accompanied the television, almost in tune with the host's inflection. "Ladies and gentlemen, we've been treated to some big news tonight and I urge you to stay tuned to E! News later tonight for further developments on this story. We're going to a commercial now, but when we come back..." The sugary voice finished the rundown and the program cut away to a commercial for a local Houston steakhouse. The ad only made it a few seconds before "MUTE" flashed across the top in bright green letters. The hum continued. Safety, organization and contingencies had created Danny Ferguson the icon. But they had done nothing for Danny Ferguson the man when his world was upended, when the gray walls of a prison cell took away his ability to see things in only black or white. The carefully manipulated Ferguson image was no longer suiting the man who had emerged. It was time to start taking risks. Danny held the remote control for another five seconds and mulled things over. Then he set it down and turned a dial on his treadmill. His pace increased and the rhythmic hum of his footsteps sped up accordingly. "Alia iacta est," he declared to the empty room, knowing nothing of Caesar or the Rubicon. On the table beyond the treadmill, Danny's BlackBerry buzzed again. Unacknowledged, it vibrated its way across the surface like a wind-up Happy Meal toy. "22 NEW MESSAGES" flashed across the screen. With one final buzz, the phone slides off the edge of the table and dropped to the ground.
