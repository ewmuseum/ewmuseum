Who I Am

2 April 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: April 2, 2006 Do I like people to be afraid of me? I have to say... it's useful. It's kind of nice that when people look at me, they think that they need to watch their steps, that they need to not piss me off. It makes everyday life a little simpler, but it also makes things harder. I can walk into a restaurant and be seated pretty quickly, in one of the best spots, provided I can fit in it. But I also have a really hard time talking to people. When I flew to Japan, I had to sit first class and, well, believe it or not, I like to talk to people. Not one of them that sat near me was interested in talking with me. The people next to me actually moved back to coach in order to avoid me. In some ways, it was really the best way for my old line of work. What is an MP? Military police in American armed forces. But what do they do? Law enforcement, including criminal investigation on military property and concerning military personnel, installation security, close personal protection of senior military officers, management of prisoners of war, management of military prisons, hunting down deserters and just a whole load of other things. But me? I didn't do all of that. I was a ball buster. I was the kind of guy that they brought in to deal with unruly prisoners, investigations and finding deserters. The rules in the IDF are a bit different; they have to be. If we're going to deal with people that are willing to kill themselves as long as they bring down a few of us in the process... well, let's just say I didn't have to play nice. I'm not kidding when I say I made a few prisoners piss their pants when I started questioning them. Let me put it to you this way: I'm not that mean of a guy. If my task involves taking someone down, I will. But away from work, the last thing I want is to be aggressive. I enjoy a good comedy movie, I like to have a quiet cup of coffee as a break from the day. Hell, I even prefer to have a relationship than nailing down one-night stands. But being big and intimidating, well, it just doesn't really lend itself to that kind of thing. I can even stand to tell a story on myself about this. I had a long-term girl once. She was a little Israeli girl, tanned and thin, about six foot and curved in all the right places. She was beautiful and I really cared for her, but she didn't exactly have a stomach for what I did. Now, understand, in Israel, everyone serves in the IDF in some way. She wasn't real keen on my particular field. The interrogations are what really bothered her; not so much mine, but the stories she heard. Being an interrogator was really more of a study of psychology and breaking a man down. For me, it was about exploiting what I had and what the man in the chair didn't. I usually left him to sit in a dark room with a dim lightbulb overhead for a while. Sure, there was a one-way mirror, but I'd come back and see how he was behaving after I had gotten a snack or something to that effect. I wasn't the type to demean when I did this, but more of a way to just let his mind fall apart. That sounds cruel, but when someone is holding something you want to know, and they know it, your subject gets real antsy. I'd step in the room and hit another switch that'd killed the dim bulb and light up something that could be called a low-power spotlight. From where I stood, it lit up behind my shoulders, giving a silhouette to the subject. This is where my Shadow Mountain codename came from. The subject would look up for a while and wonder how they'd pissed God or Allah off. It'd just start with basic questions. “How are you?” “Do you know how long you've been here?” “Do you know why you're here?” Follow with basic questions about their condition: Hungry? Thirsty? Tired? Because once they answer one of those, you have something to promise them after they answer some questions. Yes, they'd have some water after they told me what I wanted to know. Sure, he could rest after he told me the names of his friends. Of course, you'd always get the tough guys that'd sit there and try to keep clammed up. They weren't going to crack, in their minds, they were going to be stones. In all honesty, they were the easiest, because I could just leave for a period at a time, giving them the spotlight treatment each time I came back. You never touch the subject. You don't hit him, choke him, grab him, anything like that. But that doesn't mean you can't manipulate his surroundings. Sometimes it'd get real cold in the room, something no one from the Middle East is real comfortable with. Sometimes I'd have the heat turned up so they were sleepy and maybe even a little agreeable. Other times I'd make it humid and uncomfortable, just so the subject was sweating. And always use the subject's first name. Build that rapport of trust and understanding. I know how you feel, Mohammed. I know you're hungry, Benjamin. Always try to keep that tone low and even and keep eye contact. The subject couldn't make eye contact with me, but I sure could with him. Sometimes those two tricks together would do it. They would be faced with someone that was acting like an angry parent and couldn't handle it. Sometimes it didn't work. One the ones that really pissed me off, though, I'd just turn all the lights on and come in. It'd be every bit of me and that little man cuffed to the chair. I went through a lot of chairs. Because you didn't touch the subject, but I'd sure handle the chair the subject was in. I kicked them over, left him on the ground however he fell. On a few occasions I even grabbed the arms of the chair and lifted him up to eye level with me. It was the possibility of making me even madder that did a lot of work there. And then there were guys that you just passed off as P.O.W.s or as plain criminals and let them rot. Oh, they'd try to cut a deal for a plea bargain, or they'd try to plan a break. Something asinine like that. Not my problem. But my problem were the stories that liberal reporters from the west would gobble up from these little dirtbags. That interrogators shot them, that they dislocated jaws and that sort of garbage. I think I even heard accusations of Chinese water torture and electroshock treatment in there. Not that they ever came to the IDF for a quote or anything. But those days drained me. I hated them passionately and really didn't enjoy having to break people down. So one day, I headed home, tired after a rather long day. I'm not sugarcoating it, but I didn't kick any chairs around either. Some humidity here and a spotlight there and we had enough to make a raid on another cell that was planning to hit another barracks. But when I get home, I have a message on my answering machine from her, telling me that she can't stand to date a monster anymore. She'd been told about the horrible things I'd done and how repulsed she was. Not good, huh? So I ripped all my furniture up and tossed it out in the street, right? No. It hurt, but it didn't make me angry. I sat down, I had a a few moments' thought and a cup of decaff and went to bed. Boring story, huh? It's not to say that every job was bad. Installation security was something else I spent a lot of time on; how could people try to break or force their way in? How could it be stopped? It was those days that I felt better about my job. I would leave work knowing that I had made a barracks or headquarters safer for the people that would use it and that... that just made life easier. But that's all gone now. It's not depressing or upsetting, that's just how it is. The only thing I miss is living in Jerusalem. For some people, faith is easy to question, but not for someone that grows up in the shadow of Golgotha or has guarded the Church of the Nativity. No, those kinds of things affirm faith and make the world a little more sensible. I'm the kind of guy that knows someone's looking out for me and quite secure in that. Don't scoff. I've heard it all from brainy intellectuals that spent all their time in the ivory towers of universities and sneer at those that hold the Gospel close. They're the same little snits that think Israel should be dissolved. But the fact of the matter is that I'm here now. I live my life in the United States... usually. The travel schedule for a member of the PRIME roster isn't one that stays in one country, I've learned. Hey, I made my debut in Japan, for crying out loud. But I'm still on the roster. I'm here because I feel like it's my duty to be here. I'm the one that coordinated the witness protection of the Matthews family and associate, and I'm the one that didn't do a good enough job. So now, they're living on a United States military base in Japan to stay safe. And since I forced them out of this life and left a gaping hole in this roster, it's my duty to fill it. And let me assure everyone, I do nothing, NOTHING halfway.
