Spirit May

8 April 2008
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 4/8/08 “The pain passes, but the beauty remains.” -Pierre-Auguste Renoir March 31st, 2008 12:24 P.M. Another fearless draft swept up the uncovered back of the medical robe. The MRI room was effing freezing, which went nicely with the unwelcoming, impersonal aesthetic. I wanted desperately to lift my head or scratch any of the dozens of itches on my body, but that would mean another hour in the shrieking machine. I felt like an astronaut, strapped into the space shuttle. My minor claustrophobia had convinced me the small tube was closing in and there was no calming voice on the outside. The only thing I heard was the swift whoosh of air as doors swung open and closed with medical indifference. And when your back hurts to high holy hell, lying flat on it, inside an oversized toothbrush carrying case, ain’t exactly the prescribed treatment. But Culture Shock had me…hurt. Not to speak of my new mélange of frustrations, my back was acting up enough to send me running for a doctor. And when PRIME’s in-house quacks told me to “put it on the shelf until the summer,” that sent me running for a second opinion. I had no illusions about what I sought: I wanted a man in a white coat to come in and, with hesitation in his voice, tell me that it was up to me. That I could do irreparable damage but that, ultimately, he could find justification for clearing me, if that’s what I wanted. So far, though, the staff had done nothing differently from PRIME’s people. Fihlguud was a good doctor, I imagined, but he was still living off company loot. Company loot that would seriously be threatened if a disgruntled, crippled employee came back and said “the company doctor said I was okay, so why the fuck do I walk like Quasimodo now”. And this wasn’t the end. Troy couldn’t be the end; I was replaying the match in my head as the machine groaned into slow action, the platform sliding out like molasses. “Not bad for a first timer.” The male resident was cordial enough to suggest that he wanted something from me. “MRI’s usually freak people out.” I shook my neck from side-to-side as I sat up. And I felt stiff as a motherfucker, with no intention of making small talk. But the real doctor was nowhere to be found. “The results take a bit. MRI’s still aren’t an exact science.” Comforting words, to hear that my ability to maintain normal movement couldn’t be pinned down to science. This fellow hadn’t gotten the lesson in doctor-patient relations yet. Always be objective. Vagueness is key. “I assume the MRI’s will tell me what I already know,” I said, releasing more scorn than I meant to. “That my back is making war on the rest of my body so that I’ll stop jumping off of shit onto other people.” I cracked a half-grin even though I didn’t want to. Remembering that my ass was showing, I lessened the grin. “You ever think about taking it easy?” His eyes were younger than the rest of him as he confessed. “I mean, I’m a passing fan. And…from a medical background, I can tell you that your style is suicide.” “Oh?” I raised an eyebrow to hear the same thing I’d heard since day motherfuckin’ one. Tsonda, put on some pounds and go ground-based. Chan, you’ve got the build to be a great technician. Fact is, I like the rush everytime I go big air. “Your medical background probably advises against being a professional wrestler in the first place, methinks?” He could sense that I was jesting at real debate and pulled a meaningless chart up to his face. He probably wasn’t that much younger than me. But he was just getting a leg in the career world and I was staring down the potential end of mine. It was a stupid, but obvious, progression of thought. If this kid were the archangel of death for my career, it would be black, laughing irony. “Dr. Bihar should be in here any minute.” I pictured the old Indian rubbing his fingers together anxiously in the hall. My foot was pumping up and down and I couldn’t stop it. The resident slipped out with a nod of the head. * * * * * March 16th, 2008 2:04 P.M. After Culture Shock’s second dismal night, I did something radical when I finally roused myself from bed. I got dressed and habitually threw my shit into its standard pocketry. But something didn’t click; sticking with the old reliable MO couldn’t work. You don’t just walk away from the biggest loss of your career, shrug, and go “well, guess I didn’t try hard enough”. I was still in my street clothes from the night before. My True Religions were rough on my legs as I stood up. Both hands in motion, I pulled my BlackBerry out of the right pocket and my wallet out of the left. Pondering the butterfly effect, I thrust each into the opposite pocket. Was I buying the notion of “luck?” Steadfastly, I reminded myself that something had to change. So with awkward weight distribution on my waist, I moved into the kitchen, not peeking at the ruffled bed set. * * * * * Things had continued to descend since then, but when you don’t have much in the way of faith, small things sneak into importance. Flight from Vietnam had taught me to keep hope tucked away dearly; I leaned on small, ghostly hope. The karmic pocket switch would deliver me. I had to hope so, as Dr. Bihar pushed through the double doors, very medical-looking documents in hand. “Mr. Tsonda, I’ve got the results from your tests here.” The voice was dead with clinical indifference. My nerves coiled, ready for two completely polar responses. “Let me show what we found.” He began to step towards me, armed with MRI’s and charts. “Doc, as much I appreciate the candor, I think I’m better served without the hand-holding diagnostics.” I tried to keep my voice from shaking. “I’m sorry, but please,” I urged, “don’t do anything for my own good. Just tell me if I can go back to work.” Rubbing his fingers together, he started. “I can’t ignore the signs. You’re headed for a complete blowout of everything important in your back. The integrity of your spine will be compromised with another six months of this behavior.” The epigraph was pathetic, a supernova burnt out by its own spectacular flames. “The danger you put yourself in getting back into an athletic setting is undeniable. I’m sorry.” He lowered his head for a second. He must have realized that looking at my eyes would only implicate him more in this shameful finale of things. But I had to wait for him to lock eyes with me. I needed him to know that I was not thrashing against Death’s dangling arms. “You’re wrong,” I blurted. “Not medically, I understand. But you just…you can’t be right here.” “The hospital has many programs for dealing with withdrawal from things like-”” “Doctor, I’m not denying anything you say.” The time had come to throw myself at the mercy of the court. “I’ve sat through it twice now, I’d better be familiar with exactly what I’m throwing away. I know exactly what I risk losing.” “It’s the responsibility of any healer to protect his patients. Even from themselves, Mr. Tsonda. And, from your file, you need to be protected from yourself.” He was talking about the tweaked knee, the oft-sprained wrists. The body frequently rebelled against its tenant. “Dr. Bihar, there have always been a handful of reasons to keep me out of the ring. I just need one right now to stay in it.” This ancient Hindu didn’t want to be a thief of young ambition. But our stewards of life have a duty. “I completely understand your responsibility here. But I’m asking you to make an exception. And I know that sounds cliché, but I can’t make you understand what I need any other way.” He was rubbing a grey eyebrow intently. His face apologized for what he could not. But his silence was progress. “Mr. Tsonda, I see this a handful of times every year. You are young and well off enough that you don’t need this at all. Why should I let you put yourself in a wheelchair just for competitive spirit’s sake?” “What better cause is there? Would you spare a poet his hands, then?” I took a breath, shifting course only slightly. “Unlike most of the patients who come in here and cry and plead…I know exactly what I want. I don’t want some miracle cure. I give you my word as a man that I can live with whatever self-destruction I have in store for me.” My left hand closed tightly around my BlackBerry, the icon of karma’s reshuffling. “Say,” he imagined, “that I give a ludicrously irresponsible sign-off for you. You blow discs, shift vertebrae out of place, and have surgery every year until you’re too frail to walk. How do you suggest I live with my decision?” I wasn’t sure if the doctor really took this to heart or whether he was just fighting off my advances. “That’s on my conscience, as you well know.” His twitching left foot said that he was sincerely uncomfortable with loosing me onto myself. “Doc,” I deadpanned, “I suggest you shrug it off just like the hundreds of people who die in this hospital every day.” I’m sure someone along the way had taught a bright-eyed young Dr. Bihar that he couldn’t save the whole planet. “I’m only your responsibility until you let me walk out that door with my medical clearance.” “Legally speaking-” “I’ve got enough money,” I spat back. “I don’t need to drain your malpractice insurance. Besides, you’re just signing off on my condition right now. You know those fancy lawyers can come up with something in court about how my condition deteriorated spontaneously once I got back into the ring.” He was a man running out of excuses. We were both finally quiet. “It’s inhuman,” he whispered. “Why let this Grail quest destroy you?” I shrugged. “It’s become something else. I need to measure myself against something, against a goal. And now that I know what that is, I’m not sure anything else matters quite as much anymore. Hell,” I chuckled, “I even switched the pocket I put my wallet in to make the change part of my everyday life. Pretty stupid, I guess.” Even before I stopped talking, Dr. Bihar had been scribbling lazily. He looked up at me as I stopped talking, then continued writing without a response. “It’s a prescription,” he announced as he handed me a small leaflet entitling me to some unknown curative. “For pain meds. Tylox. It’s dangerous.” “So, are you clearing me to wrestle?” “No, I can’t do that.” “Listen-” “Here,” he said, offering another piece of paper, “is the number of a doctor with significantly less moral qualms than me. He can give you what you want.” I nodded slowly, somewhat awed. “Tell him I sent you to get clearance and he’ll have you written up quickly.” I nodded again, somberly. Dr. Bihar would probably find his own pain in watching my self-destructive capability. “Thanks, Doc. I probably won’t get a chance to thank you from the top of the heap, but I appreciate the help.” “I haven’t helped you,” he sighed. “Have a nice day, Mr. Tsonda.” He gave me a curt nod and left, pushing through the double doors with sufficiently more hunch in his shoulders than when he arrived. I wondered if he was the first of many people I would leave in my unhappy wake, trying to climb into PRIME’s history books. I looked at the prescription tablet in my hand. Dr. Bihar, human as he was, had offered me his wondrous alchemy. A cure for the body, but for the mind? Maybe for the mind. Maybe never.
