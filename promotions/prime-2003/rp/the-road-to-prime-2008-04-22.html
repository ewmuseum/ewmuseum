The Road To PRIME

22 April 2008
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 4/22/08 He’d been waiting for the phone to ring for years. The last time that it had rung had been Xtreme Ultimo Wrestling calling him, asking him to portray the comedic character Professor Wisdom. He’d done so, and captured the company’s Television Title. And in doing so, he’d sold himself out. David Walter Smith was not the typical wrestler. He’d never had a passion for the sport growing up; he’d been too busy with his head buried in school textbooks. Even when he’d been first approached while working part-time as a bouncer at a New York City nightclub, he’d thought it was silly. And yet, the first time he stepped into a ring, he felt like it was his true calling. Wrestling had been called a game of human chess so much that it was almost cliché, but that was how Logic saw it. His acumen and physical abilities against those of other capable individuals. No match was easy. Wrestling was his passion, though. Not sports entertainment. Wrestling. Unlike most competitors, he’d launched himself to the arguable pinnacle of the sport through the Survivor IV competition of the fans Wrestling organization, eventually getting a job with the fWo. He hadn’t quite realized at the time, though, what Disney running the company meant. It meant silliness. It meant ridiculosity. Suffice it to say that Logic’s career never really took off in an environment entirely lacking it. So he’d been out of the sport, save to compete on occasional independent shows. He’d usually be recognized, but as the guy Lindsay Troy made cry, or as the guy who had good matches with Sonny Silver, Brand Frontier, and Keith Scott Zimmerman, only to come up short. And yet, his feelers were always out, to see if a promotion would be interested in Logic. With the fWo dead, and the sport in decline, he’d never quite been hopeful. But as life so often did, it threw the Pensive Punisher a most welcome curveball, in the form of a telephone call from PRIME’s new boss C.P. Cantrell. The offer was simple, a one year deal at an offered price, take it or leave it. Logic took it. Even two weeks later, after debuting on the program and having a week that even Logic couldn’t reason out, he still wondered why PRIME had hired him. The company did have a dearth of big men, with Hoyt Williams seemingly vanished and Captain Justice seemingly the only man hoss enough for Vince McMahon to glance twice at. Maybe it was the fact that Logic had issues and history with so much of the roster. Sonny Silver, Lindsay Troy, High Flyer, the Codemaster, and Mega Job had all been involved with the Immense Intellectual at various points and with varying purposes. Crucifix, another former fWo star, had put together an unbelievable performance in the Dual Halo, so maybe former fWo talent meant something more in the present environment. In truth, Logic didn’t know, and for once, not knowing didn’t bother him so much. Because, after years away from the limelight, he finally had what many perceived to be the one thing Logic lacked. ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~ David’s mantle was pretty bare, especially compared to most of his contemporaries in Survivor IV. Rana Venenosa went on to win Meltdown, and held several fWo championships. Even more distressingly, Doctor Curiosity went on to dominate PTC, winning the GTT4 tournament. Logic had no such grandiose accomplishments. All he truly had was an XUW TV title reign under a comedic alias and a third place finish in Survivor IV. But he did have something else that resided on his mantle, really if only so it wouldn’t look pathetic. Next to his framed degrees, undergraduate at Brown, Masters at Princeton, and PhD at Yale, sat a fourth degree from the no less prestigious Craig Miles Professional Heel Academy. In proud, bold letters, the degree proclaimed David Walter Smith, alias Logic, a graduate with a degree in Heel Trigonometry. For the uninitiated, Heel Trigonometry involves such things as the following question that appeared on Logic’s final: Suppose x equals the distance between you and the corner turnbuckle post. Let y be the amount of time it will take you to undo the top turnbuckle pad. Let z equal the slope of your body when putting your feet on the middle rope. If you successfully ram your opponent’s head into the exposed turnbuckle and pin them, how long will it be before you are sleeping with a Vietnamese prostitute who may or may not have male genatalia backstage? Yup, fun stuff like that. Ol’ Craig’s a real card. Regardless, Logic had always remembered Craig Miles’ words to him about how to succeed. You had to have a chip on your shoulder. You had to be pissed off at something. Heel Trigonometry wasn’t an art that was performed in a vacuum. You had to have a goal, a purpose, a point. And Logic knew that he’d never really had one…at least, until Doctor Curiosity of all people had shown him that Lindsay Troy had a, gasp, lover, in the form of one Joey Melton. That had been enough to snap the lovestruck Logic out of his mopey funk. And he’d responded with the biggest win of his career in a bloody brawl with the Queen of the Ring that featured barbed wire, steel ring steps, and Chinese throwing stars. But after that, with vengeance attained…he’d lost his edge. Craig was right, Logic realized in the here and now. He HAD needed that chip on his shoulder. And Sports Entertainment gave him the chip he needed. ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~ Johnson City, NY National Guard Armory “PRIME, huh,” a nondescript looking, slightly pudgy man mused, looking up at the mammoth Logic. “Good for you. Always knew you were too good to be hangin’ around with schmucks like us.” Logic looked right back at the man known to the triple cities area of Binghamton, Endicott, and Johnson City as the South NY Strangler, and offered a smile. “You guys know what you’re doing. There’s gonna be three-hundred people in this building tonight just like always. Place’ll be hopping.” “Damn straight it will. I ain’t just a pretty face,” the Strangler smirked, through a gap-toothed smile. “I run the damn territory. Three-hundred people in this armory every Friday night for fifteen years. City’s been goin’ to shit since IBM moved out, but the people never stop loving their wrestling. It’s an escape from the shit they’ve got to eat every day, always has been, always will be. Hell, it’s the same for us. I’ve got a wife and two kids, I work a nine-to-five, but when I put on this outfit, I’m somebody.” Maybe that was the chip on his shoulder. The desire to be recognized; the implicit truth that he was bigger than most people in the business. Smarter than most people in the business. That he should have been able to rise to the top and annihilate everyone in his way…but inexperience and emotion had gotten in the way. Never again. Reaching into his pocket, the Strangler pulled out three $20 bills, handing them over to Logic with a grin. “Bonus tonight, kid,” the middle-aged promoter said. “Last night in the territory, so I gave ya somethin’ special to do.” “Who’ve I got tonight,” David Walter Smith asked, putting down the duffel bag that he carried over his shoulder, and fumbling through it for some wrist tape. “Yer up against Denny in a Johnson City Street Fight,” came the reply. “Come as ya are, so please don’t put on those godawful black and white tights of yours, they’re the fuckin’ shits.” Logic chuckled a bit. “I’ll stick with the jeans then. Thanks for the heads up.” The Strangler paused for a moment, and then stuck out his hand. “Thanks for everything, David. I appreciate it. Most times we get guys comin’ in here fresh offa television and they’re a bunch a primadonnas. Don’t want to do anything, always causin’ shit…and you’ve been working for us for a year every Friday, and no one in the back’s got shit to say about ya. You’re a good kid.” David’s mammoth hand engulfed the Strangler’s as they shook. “No problem. I don’t start problems. I solve them.” His mind flashed to PRIME, to the figures from his past that he knew were there, demons that he’d have to exorcize to ever truly succeed in the business. “You guys have been above board with me from the beginning, and I appreciate that.” In truth, it was part of why he’d agreed to work this last date, when he’d be hopping on a plane the very next night to make his first ever appearance as a part of the PRIME roster. This thought triggered something in him, and he grinned broadly. “You watch PRIME, don’t you?” “Shit, who doesn’t?” came the honest reply. “We’d give our left nut to be there, and even if we never get a call, we watch to see the guys who do, see what it’s like at the pinnacle with pyro and shit.” “You see that “THE WORLD IS FLAT” bit last week,” David asked, letting the smile on his face grow. “Hell yeah I did,” the Strangler began, before putting two and two together. “That’s for you?” David nodded. “Shit, shoulda known. If there’s ever weird thoughtful shit, it’s always your ass.” The Thinking-Man’s Grappler laughed openly, shaking his head. “It’s got a point. It’s an entirely false statement, but everyone believed it was true for so long because no one ever bothered to challenge it. It’s like perceptions of who I am, perceptions of PRIME. I’m a crybaby because I had a crush on Lindsay Troy once. I’m not good enough for PRIME, not good enough to be a top star. And you know what? Just like Columbus, I’m gonna prove that everyone else’s perceptions are full of crap…with fact. Cold, hard logic.” “Just logic,” the Strangler asked, gesturing to his watch. “And oh, by the way, you’re on next.” Kicking his duffel bag over to the side, Logic walked past the Strangler, sliding the sixty bucks into the pocket of his jeans, ready for his last night working for the Triple Cities Wrestling Alliance. “Just logic,” he concurred, a smirk on the corner of his mouth. “Oh, and maybe dropping people on their head a few times.” ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~ “OOOOOOOOOHHHHH!” Three hundred voices winced in unison as the body of Dennis “Denny” Knox was dropped head over heels on a steel folding chair. Knox’s back and neck compressed on impact, causing the man to flop over to his stomach, courtesy of the backdrop driver that Logic called Proof Positive. And on this night, as it had been every time he’d executed the move in the TCWA, Proof Positive meant the end. Three smacks of hand on mat later, and the official was raising Logic’s hand in victory for the final time in a TCWA ring. While Knox lay face down, Logic wasn’t without his own indications of the war he’d just been through. A gash ran horizontally across his forehead, drip-dripping blood down his proud face. His chest was bruised to shit courtesy of the chops executed by the man locals liked to call the Ric Flair of Endicott. There’d be bruises of all sorts on his body the next day, but it was worth it. Victory always made it worth it, no matter how ghastly the price. The fans applauded both men respectfully. Most knew that it was Logic’s final night, and yet they refrained from the sort of “YOU SOLD OUT!” bullshit that marred the northeastern independent scene. Logic had been with them for a year now, and in that sense, he was one of their own, getting a shot at the big time. Reaching down a mammoth hand to one Dennis Knox, Logic helped pull his opponent to his feet. Knox was wobbly, as one might expect after taking a backdrop driver, but still just coherent enough to nod at the big man as Logic raised his arm in the air to the approval of the crowd. “Knock ‘em dead in PRIME,” Knox whispered, just loud enough for the Pensive Punisher to hear. Logic nodded. “Thank you,” he whispered back. “When the PRIME house show circuit comes through, I’m gonna try to get you guys some work.” It was typical, even among the biggest promotions, for house shows to feature local talent. “No shit,” Dennis Knox replied, voice in awe. “That’d be somethin’.” “Same goes for the rest of you guys. You’ve helped me love what I do again. There’s no amount of money I can put on that.” Just as the Strangler had earlier, Dennis Knox extended his hand to David Walter Smith. This time, the resulting handshake was greeted by the approval of three-hundred raucous TCWA fans. “LO-GIC! LO-GIC! LO-GIC!” The chant was taken up by the faithful, and the referee retrieved a microphone from the ring announcer, handing it over to Logic. When Logic looked questioningly at the ref, the ref shrugged and said. “Tradition around here. Guy on his way out says his goodbyes to the crowd.” With a nod of his head, Dennis Knox rolled out to the floor, beginning the walk behind the curtain, as did the official, leaving Logic with the TCWA partisans. “I’ve been coming here for a year now,” the Thinking Man’s Grappler said, quietly, looking out at the armory crowd. “Over the course of a year, you’ve cheered me, booed me, wanted me to win, wanted me to lose, and reminded me of why I laced up my size sixteen boots to begin with.” His words were met with moderate applause, and Logic nodded. “I’m headed to PRIME, as pretty much all of you know. I’m going because it’s time for me to take care of a lot of things in my past. I’m headed that because I know I can succeed on the highest level. But last, and certainly not least, I’m headed there because of what the Triple Cities have reminded me. This line of work isn’t about the ability to be eloquent, or at least, not entirely. It’s not about fancy costumes. It’s about what you can do against your fellow man or woman in a contest of skill and acumen.” David spoke for about three more minutes, praising the company and its fans, but he had truly already spoken his message. It had taken years to recover from the damage done to his career, but Logic was ready for prime time once again. And in a few weeks, he’d have his chance to prove it. ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~ Thursday, April 24th Night of ReVolution 158 Dennis Knox sat with a cold Bud in his hand on a stool at a local bar, a grin plastered on his face. To his right sat one South NY Strangler; real name, Rich Reynolds. Their eyes were glued to the television, where ReVolution 158’s opening sequence was just beginning to play on the bar’s television. “David makes his debut tonight,” Rich said, grinning like a proud parent. “Against that Douglas kid. Eighth in the halo, he’s got a pretty nice pedigree for himself.” “Maybe so,” Knox replied, “but David’s our boy. I’ve got faith in him.” “Me too,” came the reply, and Rich raised his own bottle of beer. “To Logic ruling over PRIME,” he intoned, and Knox nodded, clinking bottles with his friend and promoter. Fifteen hundred miles away, with the hopes of three cities on his shoulders, Logic was ready for battle. He had entered the business innocent. He now re-entered it experienced, ready for the rigors of life on and off the road. Twenty five now, he knew what people could do, and the machinations they utilized. For one night, though, he could forget that. Tonight, it would simply be himself and Troy Douglas, opening ReVolution before the eyes of the world. It had been a long and winding road, but finally, David Walter Smith could say that he was back.
