Date: April 15, 2009 He hated Denver. Not the city, or the people, or the buildings or the elevation, but the memory of it. The memory of a Rocky Mountain summer night, a hotel room, a balcony and a choice. The choice. The choice that made the difference as to whether Troy Douglas was back in Denver four years later or he was little but a scant footnote in history, a mark on the Denver PD blotter reading "suicide". As he sat in front of his laptop, frustrated, Troy Douglas was trying desperately to think about anything else. He wasn't being paid to write about that, and if he had to force his fingers to mash the keyboard in correct sequence so he could get this chapter out on time. Autobiographies weren't supposed to be this damn hard, were they? For God's sake he was still seven weeks short of his 33rd birthday. It wasn't like he was trying to distill Gandhi's life into 27 easy chapters of pithy wisdom, he was a professional wrestler. Granted, he was a professional wrestler who seemed to have a lifelong affinity for trauma and insanity, but he wasn't exactly trying to write the second coming of The Grapes of Wrath here. But, still, Troy's fingers clacked idly across the keyboard, coherent phrases intermittently appearing on the screen between sips of coffee and bites of the room service turkey sandwich he'd been nursing so long the edges of the lettuce were starting to fray. Writer's block had been a frequent demon of Troy's during the six month endeavor that was penning his autobiography, and there never seemed to be a consistent remedy to overcome it. Sometimes, the Gods of literature were kind to him and the words just popped into his head and through his fingers in exactly the way he wanted. Other times, there was a steel curtain the likes of which the Pittsburgh Steelers would be proud of between his mind and his fingers and he'd be forced to sit and bang his head against the wall until he made a big enough hole to let the ideas through or he finally quit from frustration. Tonight, as the sun set over the Mile High City, Troy Douglas was ordering from Column B. Thankfully, a knock on the door spared him from several more hours of tooth gnashing, brain scrambling boredom. Relieved to have a break from his self-created mental hellscape, Troy slammed the computer shut and turned towards the door. "Who is it?" "Wouldn't you like to fucking know." The voice was a smooth, low baritone, unremarkable to most, unmistakable to Troy Douglas. Curious as to how a voice based in New York ended up outside his hotel door in Denver, Troy's pace quickened as he undid the dead bolt and flipped open the door to reveal Paul Fields, literary agent and publicist extraordinaire. "Speak of the devil," Troy said. "You know, this damn book you conned me into writing is keeping me up nights." "Oh, so now you're blaming me for your damned insomnia. Excuses, excuses." "Pardon me, oh King of the Witty Comebacks." After rocking on the balls of his feet and waiting in the hall for a good 15 seconds, Paul, backpack in its familiar spot slung over his right shoulder, finally slid into the room at Troy's insistence and whipped the bag onto a spare chair. Troy had, at many times compared Paul to a living, breathing incarnation of an Aaron Sorkin character, moving and talking at a thousand miles an hour, rolling from subject to subject and place to place with the sharpness of a razor, the ferocity of a machine gun and the subtlety of the Second Airborne Division. His state of perpetual flux was legendary among his circle of friends, and it was on full display tonight. "So, Paul, now's the part where you tell me why you're a couple thousand miles away from the home office. It can't be to see my boring ass." Paul grinned and turned his head away. "Aw shucks," he said. "Ya caught me. Yeah, I was on my way to Aspen for a conference, and when I realized you were in town, I gave the PRIME offices a call and found out where you were so I could check up on you and the last chapter." Now it was Troy's turn to look away, only he ended up staring right at the damn laptop, which only caused him to shake his head in abject disgust. "The last chapter. The final repayment for my complete incompetence in freshman year graphic design. One goddamn required course and 15 years later you shoehorn me into a fucking autobiography that nine people will read." Paul took this chance to lean back in his chair, rest his hands behind his head and kick his feet up against the nightstand in complete satisfaction. "Hey, it wasn't me who said I'd do anything to pass Kate Collins' class. Not my fault you couldn't tell leading from kerning if they danced around in front of you wearing giant neon sandwich boards. My boss asked me if I knew anyone who'd make a good autobiography subject, and you just happened to be at the top of my list." "Some list," Troy said flippantly. "Who was on it? Me and the guy in front of you at Quizno's with the club foot?" "Hey, don't knock that guy. I'm pretty sure he was in 'Nam ... or something." It was then that Troy noticed something he'd somehow completely ignored. Every few seconds, Paul would reach back with his right hand and casually scratch his neck. For anyone else, it was little more than a throwaway gesture, maybe a sign of discomfort or itchiness. Not for Paul Fields. "You're not here to talk about the book, are you Paul? We set everything up over the phone two days ago." Paul blanched and sat straight up before leaning forward, bringing his elbows to rest on his knees. It was then Troy picked up a few things he couldn't believe he'd missed earlier. Paul's normally disheveled state had been multiplied exponentially. His shirt was wrinkled an untucked, there was a noticeable coffee stain on the left leg of his khakis and his standard five o'clock shadow had grown out in scraggly patches all over his drawn face. "What the hell is going on, Paul?" Troy leaned forward, unconsciously bracing himself for what was to come. "I ... uhh, I went to this conference in L.A. about a month ago," he said. "When I got back, the apartment was half empty and there was a note on the kitchen table. Sara had left, and she took Emma and Danny with her. Said I wasn't the man she married anymore." Troy leaned back again, listening as another one of his friends told him about the complete unraveling of their life. Out of the corner of his eye, Troy spotted the laptop. The steel curtain was gone. ***** Several hours later... There's a story my dad used to tell me about an old farmer lying on his deathbed. His three sons, long since having left the family home to make their own lives, were summoned back and came to their father's side as their father took his final breaths. "My sons," he said. "I have sent for you to tell you something. Only one of you can inherit my home, my land, my farm. Please, my boys, tell me why it should be you. The first son, who was a man of great strength, said, "It should be mine, father. I can keep your farm running on my back alone." The second son, who was a wealthy man, said," No, father, it should be mine. With my intellect, I can make this farm more profitable than ever before." The last son, who was a printer's apprentice with a wife and young son, said, "I can offer you no such things, father. I have no great strength or skill, and I must care for my family." With his last bit of energy, the father smiled, held the third son's hand and said, "And that, my son, is why I leave it all to you." There were a lot of times as a kid when I didn't quite understand that story. But now, as I sit in a hotel in Denver, dutifully clicking away these final words, thinking about the thousands I've written before about what I've lost, about what I wish I still had, the tale of the farmer and his sons resonates more than ever. I had a family, once. I had a father, and a woman I was going to spend the rest of my life with. I don't anymore. I wish I did. As a wrestler, there are many who consider me to be, like the first son, a man of great strength. With all honesty, I can tell you that's not the case. I say a lot of things that sound good on TV, but when I lay down in my bed, unable to sleep because of the searing pain in my back, I feel as weak as a three-year-old. There are even those who consider me, like the second son, to be a man of great wealth and intellect. On the latter, I know so many men smarter and better than me that I wouldn't even begin to make that presumption. On the former, though my bank account is certainly in good health, that's always been because of the help of people far smarter than I am. I'd give anything to be like the third son. I've traveled the world, met some amazing people, wrestled in gargantuan stadiums on national TV and pay per view, and, for the most part, I've loved every minute of it. I've got no plans of stopping anytime soon, either. But, I'd trade it all in to have my family back. I guess things just work out funny like that sometimes. ... ... ... A quick click of the mouse, and he was done. He closed the laptop, set it onto the side table and reclined onto his bed. Maybe Denver wasn't so bad after all. THE END
