Not Your Average Joe (ReV 118, Alias Title Defense)

1 February 2007
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 01/02/07 (Thank you for the extension, Pete!) A hulking, long haired thirty-something man stands opposite me in his apartment, a large home-made milkshake full of bananas, yoghurt and peanut butter in one hand while he holds a slowly burning home-rolled cigarette in the other – the apparent contradiction seemingly lost on him. He could be any one of thousands of bodybuilders in this state or any other, but tune in to FX on a Wednesday night at 10pm and you’ll see his distinctive long silver hair, hear his gruff Scottish accent and (as near as possible) feel the raw power as he powerslams someone to the mat. “Heh, I can’t remember the last time I actually powerslammed someone…” He laughs and shakes his head a little at my outsider’s knowledge of the ‘sport’, gleaned from clichés and the brief glimpses I’ve caught in the past decade or so. Apparently the move is still used but it’s really not the be-all and end-all it was ‘back in the day’, as he puts it. It’s difficult to take your eyes off a man like Adam – longest serving recruit of wrestling’s top promotion, PRIME – he’s not a particularly handsome man (“I ain’t even a diamond in the rough, I’m just plain unsightly”, he heartily laughs as he finishes off his shake) years in the ring have taken their toll on him, his nose crooked from too many breaks one of the most evident features of the abuse. No, it’s just his sheer imposing physical bulk next to me – Joe Average, house in suburbia, gym once a week if I’m lucky. As he rummages through a cupboard for more to eat, slim cigarette hanging limply out his mouth, I feel incredibly small and scrawny. When he stands back up, towering above me at 6’4”, I realise that these guys don’t seem quite so huge and muscular on TV because they’re surrounded by guys just as huge and muscular all the time. I can’t decide if he’d look bigger standing here in real life dressed in his ringwear – baring his (dare I say it?) rippling chest and washboard abs, covered in sweat – or if the ostensible absurdity of seeing him dressed down in a t-shirt and old jeans, pouring himself a bowl of cereal, makes his presence more commanding. He laughs again when I ask about this – he seems capable of only hearty, gruff, belly-rumbling guffaws – merely offering to go and oil up if it’d sate my curiosity. I decline but use the opportunity, while on the lines of his massive physical form, to enquire gently about steroid use in ‘the game’ – instantly his mood sours and his grinning demeanour gives way to what’s almost a sneer. I find out quickly, thankfully, that his disgust is not at me but at those who do choose to partake in the darker side of things. “I do understand it, to an extent,” he says, “you need the body and the bulk to be in this thing – but I’ve worked my arse off from age 17 to get the body I live in, and I work every day to keep it. Why should I be nudged out my spot by someone who decided they wanted to take the quick way?” He acknowledges this is a very simple argument to cite, and that the issues related do involve much more than just taking the fast-track. “Suffice to say PRIME has a very strict policy on it and adheres to a testing program that does not allow you a second chance for missing a required sample.” We move on shortly after, the topic obviously one that he doesn’t like to dwell on. I don’t want to leave the impression, however, that the very one-dimensional argument presented by PRIME’s sole Scottish employee was the result of his lacking of brains in place of brawn – I was very pleasantly surprised at the depth of the man in the few days I spent on the road with him. He’s a voracious reader, never without at least one book for every two days’ travel as well as copies of New Scientist and Playboy (“For the articles – seriously. I was a subscriber way before the TV deal [PRIME had a deal with Playboy TV before they inked to FX] and I honestly read every issue cover to cover.” To this I will attest, having seen him systematically work through a full issue making sure he’d read everything of interest). In fact, it seems that every second thing the man says is prefaced with “I read an interesting article the other day…”. Magazines like those don’t satisfy his need for information, though – a Jack-of-all-trades potted knowledge doesn’t interest him. “My main interest is in geological articles, and I will make sure to follow up anything that piques my interest with a read through a review paper before going properly into in-depth scientific articles. I worry about this planet, sometimes, I want to be at the forefront of knowledge with what’s happening and how we can work to help solve any problems that do arise.” He’s also a music aficionado with a collection that puts most to shame, a full wall of his living room taken up by his records, mostly on vinyl. He demonstrated himself as a talented bass player too, demo tapes of his band that he played me showing potential for him beyond the ring. “I don’t want my name associated with the band – I’d rather have limited, well-received praise for anonymous work than to do national tours with the band riding on my name. I like to keep the separate areas of my life separate.” To understand how a man from Glasgow, Scotland – 3,000 miles from the East Coast – ended up wrestling for the world’s premier sports entertainment provider we need to roll back the clock a little. “It’s not really a British thing to do; you play football [soccer], maybe rugby, or possibly you take up boxing. Wrestling is an American thing.” (There’s a scornful upturn of his nose at the mention of ‘American’ but he qualifies this as the sentiment expressed to him as a teenager wanting to go into the sport, as opposed to his personal view. “I love the US, man, just not the politics – but it isn’t my place to press my views about that on anyone.” And, true to his word, although we talked a lot about the state of this nation’s government I was requested to keep it all off the record in this interview.) But despite the objections of friends and family Adam found his way into the wrestling business and started off wrestling catch-as-catch-can [a very mat-based, submission- and hold-heavy style] with a local promotion at age 17, and was spotted on his one-and-only UK tour two years later by a US talent spotter. “That was a stroke of luck; I left a sinking ship – the promotion was in serious debt, I didn’t end up getting paid for that tour, it was a last-ditch effort to claw back some funds. If I hadn’t been spotted I’d have ended up back home, penniless and in a mind-numbing job that I’d still be working today no doubt.” Being spotted was hardly his ‘big break’, though – years of hard slog followed, including working in some less than legitimate organisations. “This,” he pulls his t-shirt up to show a long, ragged white patch of scar tissue running from his left side to just under his sternum, “I got when I was hit with an uppercut that was packing more than just a fist.” Three blades more than a fist, in fact, something which forced him out of wrestling for two long years and pushed him and his determination to their very limits in order to come back. “I was back home [in Scotland] recovering and thinking ‘What do I do with my life now?’, because by this point it wasn’t a choice between a mind-numbing job or trying to get back into wrestling again – it was genuinely wrestling or death to me. I ran up debts you would not believe working every day at the gym, spending money when I wasn’t making any, but I knew I had to get back to it – I was not going to quit if I had the slightest chance of being able to get back to the ring again.” Note the choice of phrasing: “back to the ring”, Adam decided at this point that his days of fighting in warehouses were over. “People had died – I could’ve died – in fights like that, I knew I couldn’t do that again. No way.” Further protests from the few left close to him by this point again went unheeded as Adam packed up all he had on the chance that a verbal offer for an upstart Canadian promotion was going to hold. The FWWF (which became the FWF under later re-branding) held their bargain giving Adam his first TV exposure, leading to a tour of Canada and some US states. The contacts made on these tours would later prove invaluable as the FWF struggled under the burden of mounting financial worries and Adam was again forced to jump ship, a brief stint in the CWWe amounting to very little before he moved on to end up with the NTICW. “I went through so many promotions then, I’d take any spot going just to keep my above water – I had howls for my head on a plate from banks in the UK, I was sending back any money I had spare – which wasn’t much, even though I was living on pittance.” But the hard work the Scot put into whatever appearances he could get paid off in the end when he took a job offered by PRIME in September 2003. The company was just gaining its reputation for dominance when Adam came aboard and since then the ride has been nothing if not wild. Within his first month he picked up his very first piece of ring gold – taking the mantle of the Tag Team Championships with the English Luchadore in a forced pairing that ended up worked very well between them. “I think it was someone’s idea of a joke to get a British team together, but we gelled quite well.” The real success, however, came from his friendship with an unknown rookie who joined not long after him, one Ignatius Lisieux – one of the fastest rising stars PRIME (and indeed the rest of the wrestling world) has ever seen. Lisieux’s rise was meteoric; breaking records in PRIME by successfully defending the Global Title [now the Universal Title] and becoming one of the first double champions. Sadly, as most sensation stories go, things took their toll on the young star and a lot was left to Adam to help keep his buddy sane and stable. “I didn’t ask for the job and it wasn’t offered to me, I just kinda fell into it – I never felt like a carer to him; I was just being a good friend, as best I could.” At this point, however, I’m asked to stop, “Enough can be garnered on Iggy’s life by reading up elsewhere. If we go any further it’ll really become personal, and I want to keep my personal life out of this. I agreed to talk about my professional life and, much as it does overlap, I’d like to leave things at this.” For years Adam has been known as the Eternal Underdog, a tag even he is unsure of the origin of, but he’s been trying to break that mould recently. “I spent a long time in Iggy’s shadow – I want to state now that I do not think he held me back, nor that I rode on his coattails – and a lot of people have a hard time seeing me without thinking of him, but I do feel that I’m finally establishing myself as a force in my own right.” In 2006 Adam returned to PRIME after a lengthy break for personal reason he refuses to discuss and, while still tentatively re-establishing his friendship with Ignatius Lisieux, he pinned the two-time Universal Champion Killean Sirrajin in an undisputed three-count. Just a few months later he won his first ever solo gold, the Alias championship he has proudly displayed on his apartment wall and which is carried on-board our flight to Barcelona – successful defences have followed and he recently made the semi-finals of the prestigious Jewel In The Crown tournament, losing to the eventual winner, Tony Gamble, after a long submission-filled match. That match put Adam out of commission over the full Christmas break and the following three ReVolutions with a torn hamstring, but he’s been working hard again – no stranger to it now – to make sure he’s fighting fit to go back to the ring. His first match back, as he joins the ReVolution world tour halfway through, is an Alias Title defence against the formidable Vangelus Olsig, the longest ever reigning Intense Champion. “I’m not going to say outright that I can take him, the only time I’ve ever been in the ring with him was at Culture Shock [PRIME’s annual February pay-per-view extravaganza] and I don’t think I actually came across at all in there. I’ve nothing against him, and I sure as hell respect him for what he did in the Intense division – he still defines it for me – but at the same time I’m not going to say he’s got an easy run ahead of him if he wants take my belt from me.” He grins a sly, knowing smile at this and, to be honest, it really wouldn’t surprise me if very little of that was cockiness – as an established ring-veteran he exudes an air of knowing that is all too often lacking in place where confident self-assurance runs a distance second to out-and-out cockiness. We’ll have to wait in order to see how 2007 pans for the ambitious Scot, though his upbeat outlook may mean he finally sheds the title of Eternal Underdog and takes to the spotlight as a star in his own right. He’s optimistic about his chances in the Dual Halo, the one match to overshadow all others in wrestling. He’ll be the first man to compete three times (2004, 2006, 2007) and he sees himself more qualified than ever to take it home. “It’s a while off yet, but I’ve got experience in there that no-one else has. I set the very first distance record in there [he lasted an impressive 72 minutes in the first ever Dual Halo from his unlucky start as first contestant out] and I feel in better shape than I ever have – cliché as that sounds.” “Last year I was as close as I’ve ever been to the top, twice; I beat a Universal Champion and just got nudged out by the guy who’s as good as set to pick it up in a few weeks. I feel good about myself; I don’t see the JITC as a loss. I swear that I think this’ll be my year.” I believe the man when he says this: when he says something it’s not just a hunch, he’s not trying to convince himself or anyone else – it’s a measured statement of fact that he has carefully considered and it’s almost certainly going to be true. Jonathan Owen, The Times, Sunday 28th January 2007
