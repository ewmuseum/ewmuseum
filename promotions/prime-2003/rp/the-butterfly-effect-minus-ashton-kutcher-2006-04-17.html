The Butterfly Effect (minus Ashton Kutcher)

17 April 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: April 17, 2006 April 10, 1951 Dear Journal, They always think they can laugh at me. Like I’m just some clown the school hired to be push around for the bemusement of any onlookers who might chance upon the scene - I’m really getting tired of it. Mr. Doyle, the guidance counsellor, says, “It’s just a phase, son. Don’t worry it’ll pass.” Well, that’s easy for him to say isn’t it? He’s not the one who gets beat up after school three days a week. He’s not the one who everyone calls ‘freak’. He’s got it easy with his stupid suit and tie and his stupid 9 to 5 office job. Tommy’s the only one who understands me - the only person who ‘gets’ me. It’s because he’s been there, too. He’s had the masses of frenzied, approval-starved drones call him ‘fag’ and ‘freak of nature’ he’s gotten it all just like me. He understands me. I think I love him. He’s sorta cute and the way he smiles… he’s about the only good thing in my days. I wonder if he’s really gay. Maybe I should make a move on him? I don’t know. I’ll try to get some sleep - I’ll need it if I’m going to face tomorrow. April 11, 1951 Dear Journal, Greg and Brian think it’s so funny to play jokes on me when everyone else is watching. A crowd of gawkers. No one bothers to step in and break it up. I can see it on their faces, they don’t like it. But a group of cowards leaves little reprieve for a hero in a subculture build on the foundations of social pressures to be ‘normal’ - whatever the hell THAT is. I don’t know. Maybe I missed the seminar where they handed out normalcy. Those onlookers are just as responsible as the ones who did it - maybe even more so. They’ll get their comeuppance. There was a bright spot in my day, when I spent the lunch hour with Tommy today. We went over to the Burger Shack and had a banana split. The more I talk with him the more I think he understands me. I’ve made up my mind, tomorrow I’m going to ask him out. April 12, 1951 He said ‘yes’! This may be the happiest day of my inconsequential life! Nothing could bring me down today. Even… them. They saw us together, holding hands. They shouted ‘faggot’ and ‘queer’ and all the other things they could think of. It was cruel and unnecessary, but I didn’t even hear them… well, obviously I did, but it was of little consequence! Nothing can bring me down! April 14, 1951 Dear Journal, I can’t believe it. I’m still shaking. How could they do something like this!? I need to tall someone and you’re the only one who’ll listen. They couldn’t stand seeing me happy. I smiled. They couldn’t bear to see me have anything good in my life. They tied me and Tommy and threw him in the trunk of their car and drove off with him I was tied up in that alley for almost an hour - even though it felt like days - and I couldn’t manage to get free. I still have the rope burns on my wrists from struggling against the bonds that held me. They brought him back after that hour. They were laughing like hyenas. The sort of cackle you hear from a witch in a cartoon. Evil laughter. What are they trying to prove? Do they want me to tell them they’re better than me? Fine! They’re better! That won’t make them stop. They need to prove their dominance by beating up on the vulnerable in hoards. When they brought him back and opened the trunk they hauled him out like an old sack of garbage and dumped him in the alley beside me. There was blood all over his face. His beautiful blue eyes were glazed over as he looked helplessly into my eyes. Then I hear them say, “Shit, man! There’s blood all over the trunk! This fag must’ve cracked his head open on the jack or somethin’. Shit! Shit! Shit! Let’s get the hell outta here and clean up this mess.” They just left him there, bleeding and bound. And I was beside him, unable to do anything. If we’d gotten him to a hospital in time he would have made it. He’d still be alive. But that’s not how it is. We were there all night until a kid found us the next morning and told her parents. I stayed three feet away from him all night, unable to help or consol or even talk to him. I watched him die, bleeding to death right in front of my eyes. They took away the only good thing in my whole life. This will not go unavenged. It won’t. It’s can’t. I will not let it. April 17, 1951 Dear Journal, It’s been a few days. My family made me go with them to grandma’s place. Big Easter thing out there. I forgot bring you. Sorry. You’re the only one left that I can talk to. In any case I had a lot of time to think things through. I’m not so angry anymore. I’ve had a chance to calm down, but vengeance still needs to be had. The ache in my gut needs a release and there’s one way to pay them back that’s fitting. And eye for an eye as they say. I saw the answer in dad’s gun cabinet, and I know where he keeps the key. This’ll be my last entry. I don’t see much point in living after this. I’m sure they’ll put me away. I’ll go out in a blaze of glory and everyone else who’s looking on can tell the story afterward. Plus they’ll have you - my life’s story - a six-book autobiography. Everyone will know how it went down thanks to you. Thanks for being a good friend, Journal. Good bye. April 18, 1951 Dear Journal, I didn’t think I’d ever see you again. You probably didn’t think you’d see me either. But I met the most intriguing man. He was waiting at the bus stop. He started a conversation with me. I’ve never had an adult start a conversation with me. They usually try to avoid me. But something was different about this man. He seemed to genuinely care - about everyone. He talked about the weather and how great America is and bald eagles. Then he asked me to ‘tell him a story’. I couldn’t think of one that I cared to share with him, but he could tell something was going on that I wasn’t sharing. So he told me about when he was a young man in the war. He told me how he met his best friend after an ambush on the beaches of Normandy. This man I met said that he had the opportunity to save this man’s life - a man he’d never even met before and that through that horrible circumstance a wonderful thing came out and a friendship that lasted years and years. He said that God made it so. He told me about a Jesus that makes good things come out of bad situations. But I told him I’d heard of Jesus before. And that Jesus hates fags and homos and the like. I told him that I didn’t believe in his God. But he told me that Jesus didn’t hate me, he told me that his God was everyone’s God and that this God loved me more than anyone I’ve ever mat. Well, this God has a poor sampling for comparison. In any case I didn’t believe him. And my bus was coming. He asked if he could pray for me before I left and I told him ‘no’. I had something to do that God wouldn’t approve of. I’m not interested in being ‘saved’. But I could hear him praying to his Jesus as I left and maybe it worked. Maybe this God did something. I shot up about 20 people and killed those bastards that murdered Tommy. I blew their brains all over - just like Lincoln. But as I went to turn the gun on myself Mr. Doyle tackled me to the ground and wrestled the gun away from me. They’ve got me at the police station now, I’ll likely be tried as an adult, that’s what they’ve been saying. I can’t really see what the point of me living was, but it just brings back to mind something that Johnny said, “God has a plan and a purpose for your life, son.” Whatever. April 19, 1970 Dear Journal, My first day of freedom, I got out early on parole. They gave me ten dollars and a new suit. So much has changed. Cars are everywhere. Huge buildings and chain stores have taken over. The world is so advanced now. But for all it’s advancements we still haven’t found a way to live in peace. The war in Vietnam rages on. They were going to solve their problems the same way I did. As I was walking away from the jail house I ran into a young man, probably 17. He had blue eyes and blonde hair and asked me how old he looked. I told him he looked 17. He asked if he could pass for 18 and I said, “maybe.” He looked happy with that and started running off when I called him back. I asked him what he wanted to do with his new age and he said he wanted to join the army and fight for his country. A noble cause, I told him, but then I said he was too young. He didn’t seem convinced, but I told him that if he wanted to fight why not become a boxer or a wrestler? I told him to wait until he was old enough if he wanted to fight and not to just rush into something that he wasn’t meant to do. He seemed really interested in wrestling. He asked if wrestlers were in the USO… I wasn’t sure. I wonder what he decided. Everything, anything can change and shape our world and our lives. Who we are is directly linked to where we’ve been and what we’ve done. One comment can spark a change that alters the entire course of our lives. In 1970 a group of soldiers from a Tacoma recruiting office was sent to Vietnam, they were part of the first wave to take back Hill 143, it was an ambush and over 50 soldiers lost their lives. One young man, Jonathan Noble, went to a local wrestling outfit, Tacoma All-Star Championship Wrestling, the day those boys were recruited. He still doesn’t know that his fate was forever altered by the actions of a convict who was befriended on a bus bench in 1951. Johnny Noble’s life was perpetually changed by a series of events that happened two years before he was even born. When you consider that anything and everything that you do in a day could have repercussions 30, 40 or 50 years later would you make the same decisions you’re making now? Would you think twice before you make them? And today, the fate of those who would choose to step up against Johnny Noble would have to thank that man on that bus stop in 1951 for the challenge that lay before them. It would be no easy task and who knows… maybe the outcome is already decided…
