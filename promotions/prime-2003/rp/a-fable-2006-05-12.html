A Fable

12 May 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 5/12/06 I watch the bar with morbid curiosity. It’s an unusual occurrence that I mind my own business, but I don’t want any particular excitement. Yes, I’m drinking an apple martini. There are a bunch of college kids at the bar. It appears that one of them is suffering through her twenty-first birthday; a truly horrid experience. She inhales shot after shot of some alien blue liquid. They’ve been doing this for a solid half-an-hour at this point. I’m merely a pair of inquiring eyes on this ritual, like a kid outside a fish tank. The girl’s birthday seems to be going well. Like every other young, bored American female, she’s seeking attention by taking body shots off other girls. I can see that gleam in the eyes of those around her; they’re thinking “God, I might get a threesome tonight,” when the only realistic expectation is that they’ll be holding back someone’s hair while they review their drink choice via visual aids cascading from their mouth. By law, the girls who go out to bars always seem very sexy from halfway across the bar. I think some of them may be sleepers planted there by owners worldwide to ramp up business from pathetic single guys. Regardless of whether she’s a countdown (she goes from a ten at fifty feet away to a five at five feet away) or just psychotic enough that you don’t realize she’s off her fucking rocker until you’ve shared too many drinks to walk away. Hell, if I weren’t so damn tired, I’d probably make a play at one of them, regardless. The bartender is a twentysomething guy. This may not seem important, but in reviewing all the facts of this situation, it’s quite the opposite. First off, this guy doesn’t want this attractive young nymph to have her birthday ruined by some asshole barkeep who says “you’ve had enough.” Plus, the two guys who delusions of group sex will immediately jump on his back if he tries to stop the swift flow of potent drink. I wish there were a place I could place bets on things like “this results in someone sobbing.” I’m so caught up in my sociological experiment that hearing “Heat Of The Moment” by Asia emanate from PRIME’s favorite electronic device snaps me upright with surprise. One of those assholes that’s too old to be sitting alone at a bar gives me a dirty look. I consider starting shit with him, but, for the same reason I’m not walking up to the bar, I just pick the Sidekick up. “Dude,” Danny Ferguson half-shouts, “where are you?” I’m not quite sure what he’s talking about, but the question seems straightforward. “Umm…in a bar. Am I supposed to ask you where you are?” Loud, scolding sigh. I’m about to get a dad speech, I can tell. “Isn’t there something kind of important in your very near future, C?” “Danny. Bro. I don’t need to be worried about Lamen. I told you already, the guy fucking fears me and wh-” “This isn’t typical fucking ‘I can go out there and do some spots, a couple kicks and be happy with taking the loss.’” He’s exasperated, breathing heavily from this pleasant little tangent. “Okay, I’m giving you the benefit of the doubt that you’ve been hitting the mini-bar hard. So I forgive you already for that last little explosion.” See, this is a rare type of night; I’m the cooler head prevailing. I hear another loud sigh from the other end of the receiver; perhaps he’s nervous about his tag match? “My point is,” he labors, “that you haven’t faced a match this big yet in PRIME. You may not have your head totally in the game.” “C’mon, I’ve main evented with you and Face before.” Maybe it’s a feeling new to my experience in PRIME, but I understand what it means to take someone on in a big-time match. I mean, I have faced Karina Wolfenden twice and taken her to her limits“Yeah, you’ve also lost twice to K-Wolf.” I guess we each have our own view of that set of events. “Don’t worry about it, Danny. Don’t you think the desire to keep my belt…the belt is enough to drive me? Am I the reluctant child who needs to be prodded along? Just chill out about it, man. Don’t worry about me so much.” He gives in, realizes that I’m illogical and stubborn, two horrible traits in someone you consistently argue with. “Go home, man. Get some sleep. Prepare for once.” That’s the final word, as I hear a flustered click on the other end of the Sidekick. I slide it closed and shake my head, then turn back to the bar, where things have taken a turn for the worse. One of the guys has a moderate frown on his face, while the other members of the group continue their boastful progression through dozens of bottles. * * * * It was not a cadaver, a corpse, or even a human body; it was a carcass. This was not a man anymore. Sure, you can talk about how it’s wretched that two eight year-olds would stumble onto a dead body, but this is not a story about what Vietnam was like when I was growing up (that story would be three words long: fucking fucked up). This is a story about the first I saw death. I only knew that this was a carcass because I’d heard my mother use the word when describing one of the water buffalo she found, dead, near the riverbank. The whole thing was just an amalgamation of blood, bones, and guts (not organs…this was ripped body tissue, lain over itself). But this had never been a human; it looked like a painfully vivid painting. Nothing more. This ex-human’s right hand lingered on a filament of human tissue. It did not look like the hand of a good man; it was tough with cuts all over. Clenched in rigor mortis, even independent of the fact that one finger still lay draped over an M16, it looked evil. You do not hunt tigers with M16’s; you hunt people with M16’s. He had never seen his assailant come from behind. “What is wrong with him,” Tuyen asked me. But he knew the answer. His eyes were big, round seashells like the ones my grandfather had shown me. “Who is he?” The quick switch in the line of questioning told me that he was afraid. Every child was taught to leave a dead body where it was and quickly run home. These are the lessons of war. “He is dead.” The man’s leg had a split right down its side. At some point, a red line had been traced through meat, opening up first skin, then fat and muscle. A tiger kills by hamstringing its victim (more often, water buffalo, but a man alone in the jungle was just as good), leaving them unable to escape. Even better if the victim was alone. The powerful cat would’ve shredded his leg, and then wasted no time in administering death. “Have the Americans killed him?” Deep sorrow tried to flee from Tuyen’s eyes, but I noticed it sniveling in the corners. Fear stood unabashedly in his pupil. “Are they coming back? Perhaps we should go.” I agreed with him, but I didn’t know why. There was no reason to be afraid. The tiger would have taken his fill and I had seen the soldiers fire their M16’s. I could defend Tuyen and myself if the tiger came back. The man’s neck was raggedly sliced halfway through. Having severed the jugular, the tiger would’ve sensed death and stopped killing to start eating. Flesh hung off his neck and flapped around softly in the breeze, the butcher having found a more suitable use for his knives in mid-cut. “Is he from the village?” Tuyen was now quieter, more resigned. “We should tell his family.” “They will suspect us.” I didn’t know if this was true, but it sounded scary and I liked seeing Tuyen whimper a bit and walk in place, which he did whenever he was frightened. “They will say ‘you killed him!’ and string us up by our hands. I will not tell anyone, Tuyen, will you?” He stood frozen in place. “No,” he stammered, “I will not. Of course. I will not!” The eyes were the worst part. They had been spared from the shower of blood but remained wide open like two balls of gio lua, lean meat pie, before my mother cooked them. Inside each was a swirl of brown, not as hollow as the soil and not as wet as the reds of the blood. He would’ve looked like my uncle Tin, if only he carried the glasses that looked like bright yellow worms. “What do you know about tiger-hunting, Tuyen?” He gasped and then tried to mask his fear by looking down at his bare feet, hairless with dirt tattooed on. “I am only kidding. Do you want to go play tienlen?” He thought for a moment before responding. Every time he got lost in thought, he snapped himself back and then stared into the trees for a very long time. I knew he would not ever see those flashes of orange and black. “I don’t like the jungle very much,” he muttered as he walked to follow me, without having answered my question. * * * * I look up from my hands and, more than slightly drunk, I am man enough to admit, I wonder whether I have been sleeping, passed out, or merely in a black space in my head. I remember the group of college kids. I look over and it dawns on me that I put my head down mere seconds ago. Nothing has happened since, although in this new light, several of the students look a bit whiter. Or perhaps it’s not the light, but their skin, blanched from their Smucker’s drinks. And then, it happens. To thank the bartender for his compliance with her birthday wishes, the newest twenty one year-old dry heaves. She goes to get up to spew her piece outside, but she stumbles. As I watch her take a moderate spill, I realize that I’m about to stuck in not a glass case, but a massive bazaar of emotion spilling from shelves. She reaches the floor about the same time as her puke. Her face comes to lie in a fine brown puddle; mom would be proud. Her friends start apologizing, but then one notices that she’s in no rush to, ya know, get up or wipe the puke off of herself or move. I can see the fear on their faces and it’s not enjoyable; the naïveté makes me cringe. The choir rings out. “Is she breathing?!” “Oh my god, should we call an ambulance?” “Prop her up and get her some water!” All these questions and commands interrupt my nice, pleasant buzz. “She’s going to be fine.” I get several different looks; disdain, confusion, and surprise are leading the way. I get up, very ready to stop drinking not because of the puke, but this hyper-sensitivity that someone snuck past the bouncers. “Are you a doctor?” The bigger guy is apparently a pussy of epic proportions. He’s cradling the girl’s hair (read: overflowing with puke) and looks like he’s about to cry, himself. I want so desperately to pull out a Holiday Inn Express line, but I think that the suggestion of laughter right now would stop every chamber in their hearts. “Listen, she’ll be fine. Take her home and make sure she gets water.” I like this whole mysterious stranger bit as it is, so I turn to leave. They continue their frantic worrying; one person stupidly asserts that “she could die.” I have to smile at that. This is not what death looks like. Death does not come calling for blue alcoholic drinks. Death lives in the jungle and wears stripes. He does not leave you on a floor, relatively comfortable in the warm, glistening product of your intoxication. He leaves you split open where the flies can feed.
