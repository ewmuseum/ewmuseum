What Might Have Been (CIII)

undated
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: April 24, 2039 Editor's Note: The following is a transcript of the commencement address given by Dr. Daniel Ferguson, M.D., Ph.D., to the Johns Hopkins University class of 2039, during an afternoon ceremony on Saturday, April 24 of that year, following his reception of an honorary doctorate in chemistry from the University. Thank you. It's an honor and a pleasure to be here. I am proud to be your first commencement speaker to have held the two most coveted awards in our race - the Noble Prize and the World Heavyweight Championship. [laughter through the crowd] Something tells me that I will be the only man to maintain that prestigious honor. At least until Vangelus Olsig discovers a new fossil fuel. [more laughter] Now, we all know that the mere mention of my former career choice has sent your deans and intermediaries into silent, fearful convulsions. 'Oh no, he's not going to talk about wrestling, is he? We didn't bring the man who cured cancer to our commencement so he could talk about bodyslams and armlocks, did we?' Unfortunately for their own peace of mind, ladies and gentlemen, they did. Now that I am a good seven years removed from the discovery that has ultimately defined my contribution to our race, I decided that it might be time to find some new material. If you'll allow me for a moment to stir up the echoes of my former life and the ego that accompanied it - I've made my share of public engagements during the past decade. In between the presidential dinners and the Larry King interviews and the - I hate to break this to everyone, but this afternoon is not my first commencement. Although I am certainly proud of my accomplishments, all of this attention and all of these public oratories have brought to mind one unfortunate realization - somewhere between when I took off the tights and put on the lab coat...I became a very boring man. If you recognized my name when the University announced me as today’s speaker, then you’ve probably read my thesis on cellular decomposition and its applications in the fight against cancer in all forms. If you didn’t recognize my name, maybe you read a recap or Google’d my name or bumped the kid next to you and asked who the F is this guy. (mild laughter). No matter what, you know who I am and why I’m here. So I don’t need to talk to you about medicine or chemistry or my Nobel Prize or the other achievements that follow my name any time it appears in print. I’m here to talk about choices. 32 years, seven months and 19 days from this evening, I arrived at a point that I now consider the fulcrum of my life. In Tampa, Florida, at PartyPoker.com Stadium, which was then known as Raymond James Stadium, I was a part of a wrestling show against a gentleman named Kyle Lamen. Kyle and I never got along, and I’m comfortable only now in admitting that was 100% my fault. For as long as we knew each other, Kyle tried to make me realize that life was too short to not enjoy, and I tried to make his life miserable. That night in Tampa, after I’d done everything in my power to ignore that life lesson, he beat it into me. I don’t think there was a butt in a seat, or a dry eye in that six-figure crowd when Kyle beat me senseless and finally shut me up for good. It wasn’t until several years after that August night that I formally gave up acting and wrestling and embarked on my new calling, but the wheels started turning that day. It’s not about a win or a loss, just like it’s never the earthquake. It’s the aftershocks. Those of you who have been involved in competitive sports have an idea of what it feels like to lose to the nemesis, the person or entity who you are naturally set against in that world. It doesn’t sit well. It’s a natural red alert; your body shuts down. I don’t remember the two weeks after it happened. I had spent so much of my time and energy hating this man, and then shoved it back in my face, and all I could think about was...”How did I get here?” What had I done - to myself or to Kyle or to anyone - that set me up for a fall so hard that it shook me loose from what I had been up until that point. Had I made the right choices to get there? How could I have, when I become such a physical and mental wreck after a loss in a wrestling match, of all things? Truth is, I had never made any choices. As I child, I was an actor, first and foremost. I was never destined to discover my natural talents for chemistry and medicine. I wasn’t taught with the intention of development. I was taught - in aluminum trailers and Hollywood backlots - as an afterthought. They taught me English as a precaution, and they taught me History to fill quota. You take two hours from your daily shooting schedule to sit in a makeshift classroom and let the studios and producers circumvent any claims of child abuse. We’re “learning,” but we never developed. My point is not to assault the academic standards and practices of our motion picture industry - I will leave that kind of self-righteous indignation to our newspaper columnists and weekend Entertainment Television pundits. My point is that I was involved in a world and a lifestyle that was ultimately determining my path, despite not having any kind of input of my own. Being a “child star” gave way to being a mediocre grown-up actor, which gave way to moonlighting as a pro wrestler, which helped turn me into a star all over again. But when did I ever want to be a child actor? My parents wanted me to. They made the decision for me. I loved them...to an extent, and primarily in a retroactive context, but it was love nonetheless. But I spent nearly thirty years of my life locked into a career that was unnatural to me. I was out of place, and unhappy. This is a truth that no one from Los Angeles will agree to: Everyone there is unhappy because they know that they probably don’t belong there. I was lucky to get out alive. It is time, now, on the eve of your release upon our fair civilization, for you to understand that you determine your own future. That is more than the choices I didn’t make...it was also in the complacency with which I accepted not making them. If you are to eventually leave this world satisfied with yourself, you need to become an active participant in the life you shuffle through on a daily basis. Kyle Lamen is no longer with us, but if he were, I would make every effort to thank him for teaching me that lesson - just as I once made every effort to ignore it. If that fulcrum hadn’t come along in Tampa in 2006, I would’ve continued living the Hollywood life, the shallow, self-driven and purposeless life that I had. I wouldn’t have had the opportunity to change the world like I will when I finally cure this modern epidemic. I wasn’t ever going to be a scientist or a doctor or Time’s Man of The Year, because I would never have broken away from what I had been “taught” to know. I am here today to tell you that what you are taught is nothing more than a foundation. Your parents do not determine where you go with your life. The classes you took during sophomore year do not tell you what you want to do with your life. Your degree does not pigeonhole your career options. You are not computers, where information is inputted in and the best available solution is determined. Choose where you want to go, what you want to do and how you will best do it, and live the life you want for yourself. I will be remembered, when my name is out of the newspapers and my tombstone has started to gather moss, for my contributions in the fields of science. I will be remembered for what I gave back to this world that we all share. However, until the day that I move on, I will enjoy every second, and I will remember what I took away, what I learned. I will always remember the day when I first learned how to live. I hope that today, I created a similar landmark for many of you. Thank you, and good luck. [Applause] Editor’s Note: Due to the fact that chain of events leading up to this address were sparked by Dr. Ferguson’s loss to Kyle Lamen on August 13, 2006, it bears noting, that the address, just like the loss, never actually happened.
