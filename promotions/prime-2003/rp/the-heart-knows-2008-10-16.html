The Heart Knows

16 October 2008
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 10/16/08 “Never let your sense of morals get in the way of doing what’s right.” -Isaac Asimov October 15th, 2008 3:33 P.M. “Vince, you’re on in five, four, three, two…” The producer extended his index finger, then pointed at Vince Howard. It was time for the voice of PRIME to get on his grizzy and deliver the weekly podcast. Shooting the shit beforehand, he’d told me all about the podcast. How it was born out of Cantrell’s endless marketing schemes, how he’d been Faith Rodriguez-style strong-armed into it, how he hated pretending to care for an extra four hours a week. “It’s like this, Chan,” he’d explained. “It’s just a job. It’s a hell of a job, but that’s all it is. I don’t go home and lose sleep over Dusk, or Gamble, or even Lindsay.” But now it was time for Vince to pretend. “Welcome, welcome, welcome, PRIMEates! You’re listening to This Week In PRIME and I’m your host, Vince Howard. Good to be with ya, folks, we’ve got a great lineup this week as we gear up for Great American Nightmare 2008.” I wiggled each of my fingers, anxious to get this over with. This wasn’t the week I wanted to be answering questions. There were too many lingering loose ends, too many chinks in the armor. “We’ll be talking about ReV 175, possible sleepers for the Jewel in the Crown Tournament, and oh yeah, the Universal Champion is in the house!” He winced as a canned “The champ is here!” sound bite came from the studio sound system. “That’s right, Chandler Tsonda is here to answer some questions. Plus, he’ll be signing 10 copies of last year’s Great American Nightmare DVD, so make sure you listen for this week’s trivia question.” Vince hated the trivia question. “First off, lemme say that it’s great to have you in the studio, Chan.” “Thanks, Vin. Good to be here. Quite a studio, dude.” “It’s where the magic happens.” “You mean you–” “Not that kinda magic, champ.” I had to fake a chuckle, not because Vince wasn’t a funny dude, but because we’d rehearsed that exchange dozens of times over the past hour, as per orders of the podcast’s producer. With the fake dialogue over, I’d have to face the gauntlet. “Well, I know you’re a busy man, so let’s get right into some web questions.” Vince nodded to me, a reassurance that I had nothing to worry about. But, at the end of the day, it was the producer’s job to choose the questions, and Vince’s job to read them. There was no guarantee that he’d be tossing softballs. “Let’s do it.” “Alright, our first question comes from Anthony in Chicago.” I watched Vince’s eyes widen ominously as they read what his voice hadn’t yet announced. “Anthony wants to know your reaction to what went down at Cataclysm.” /~/~/~/~/~/ September 19th, 2008 10:46 P.M. I remember the quiet. There was no one running around backstage, not even Cantrell’s stagehands whipping around corners, wielding their clipboards like medieval weaponry. Every eye that had just been glued to my match was in front of a TV, watching PRIME’s last bastion of hope. Not mine, though. I was on the only chair in my locker room, ice taped onto my back and around both shoulders. The night was a disaster. All our–fuck that, my rhetoric about PRIME standing for something, while standing up for itself…it just seemed wasted. I was studying my quavering hands and taking deep breaths to counter the comedown off adrenaline, when one of the interns walked in. “Mr. Tsonda?” The young woman, Shari or Mary or something, adjusted her blouse nervously. “What is it?” The question came out a lupine growl. “You need to come see this.” Running rough, still-sweaty palms across my cheek, all I could feel was weary. Pain shrieked through my body like a banshee on a cold night, and it nudged me ever closer to the pill bottle tucked away in my bag. “What is it?” “Sir,” she stammered, “it’s about the Roulette. Xavier Kannon just…well, I’m not sure exactly what he did, but Cantrell’s geeking out.” Blinking to stave off heavy eyelids, I stepped into the hallway, where my meticulously selected interns were huddled, in a horseshoe shape, around the TV. I couldn’t see the screen, but I could hear their reactions. “What’s Cantrell doing? The guy turned his back on the company!” “Cantrell understands looking out for number one. What’s wrong with playing both sides to get the best deal for himself?” Parting the sea of bickering interns, I fixed my gaze on the television. There he was, the smiling crimson-haired traitor, watching as Cantrell played the role of personal genie, granting wishes until there was nothing left to wish for. The interns tittered no longer, intimidated by the presence of a grumpy boss. We all watched with quiet awe; first, the in-ring humbling of PRIME, and now this? “Mr. Tsonda,” the same female intern asked, her voice faltering with trepidation, “what the hell is going on?” “PRIME is negotiating its first hostage crisis, junior. And that ginger fuck is pointing all the guns.” It was probably a subconscious way of ignoring my own loss, but for the first time in months, there was a big neon sign pointing out the right thing to do. No more pesky moral ambiguity, no losing sleep over the butterfly effect. Like some kind of evangelist speaking in tongues, I was struck with the weight of moral righteousness. I’d lost hours of sleep thinking about the Universal belt and about my place in the grand scheme of all things PRIME. And now, minutes after I’d allowed some of the belt’s prestige to be stolen by an upstart fed from Nevada, I understood. “What are you gonna do?” The voice came somewhere from the herd of interns, couldn’t tell you which one. They were only tangentially linked to PRIME, by virtue of being in my employ. And yet these twentysomethings, with dreams of entrepreneurship and law school dancing in their heads, wanted Xavier Kannon to pay. The oldest definition of the word champion is “a person who fights or argues for a cause on behalf of someone else.” I was going to finally act like a champion. “I’m gonna make that motherfucker’s dreams of being a martyr come true.” /~/~/~/~/~/ October 15th, 2008 3:38 P.M. “So like I was saying, Cataclysm wasn’t our best night. But it’s the bad nights that keep you hungry. No one likes to say it, but sometimes it takes a loss to ground you.” Sometimes, it takes a loss to get mad enough to do something radical, like assault the most famous man in wrestling. “Spoken with all the political correctness of a champion, Chan. Our next question comes from Conrad in Oklahoma City. He’s wondering how your mother is doing and whether you’re planning on taking any time off to help care for her.” /~/~/~/~/~/ October 2nd, 2008 9:12 P.M. “Ma?” My knuckles knocked loudly against the bedroom door, but nothing stirred inside. There comes a certain time in life where you worry. For most people, it’s when you have kids. But as a lifelong stag, I began to worry when my mom first showed signs of age. It hadn’t been the first grey hair or even the day she got diagnosed. But it was hard to remember when I hadn’t felt responsible for her. Now, the worry manifested itself in closed doors and afternoon naps. Anytime she was alone, my veins ran a little bit colder. I was ashamed to be so constantly aware of it, but I rarely stopped thinking about whether she might take her now painful life into her own hands. “Ma?” I struck the door with the bottom of the fist, feeling the fibers loosen up. “Ma, it’s me. Can you open the door?” After another agonizing silence, I heard shuffling inside. She took her time coming to answer the door, and when she did, she opened it only a crack. “Chan,” she squeaked, sounding very tiny in the dark room, “what is it?” “I’m sorry, Ma. I didn’t mean to wake you up.” She shook her head reproachfully. My impromptu visits had become more frequent, and she undoubtedly felt like I was prying. “You’re no babysitter,” she scolded, rubbing her eyes. “Last night, Ma…” If she knew about Nikkie, she showed no indication. As she pushed the door open a crack more, I could see the same turquoise nightgown she’d worn my whole life. “It was a bad night. I just wanted to come tell you that I love you.” “About a girl? She did something to you?” The world girlfriend had been out of my lexicon for more than a year now. Where I’d once used women as Band-Aids, I had Tylox and booze, neither of which was planning on staying the night and trying to make me share my feelings. “No. The kid I was telling you about, Dawkins, lost his fiancée. She was pregnant with his son. I was supposed to be the godfather.” Better to let it all spill out at once. “Oh no,” she moaned, throwing her hands over her eyes. She felt dangerously light as she stumbled forward into my chest; she hadn’t been eating. “Ma, what’s wrong?” “Oh, Chan,” she sniffed, showing more public emotion that I’d seen in years, “good people dying all around me. So many sad people. God just…takes them and I don’t know why.” “You mean, why them instead of you?” She couldn’t look me in the eyes. This was how our conversations went now, both of us silent amidst the crashing waves of shame. “Maybe you’re stickin’ around because I still need you.” “You’re all grown up, Chan. Don’t need me. Big and strong and rich and handsome. So many people love you now. I hear how they cheer for you. Makes me…” She searched for the word, pushing off my chest and wiping at her eyes. “Proud.” “What do I tell Dawkins?” She hesitated, rubbing her thumb and index finger together as she thought up what she would say. “What was her name?” “Nikkie.” So soon after the fact, she was. Past fucking tense. It was such a blunt way to look at things. My mother flicked the switch and lit up her austerely decorated room. Bed, nightstand, dresser, with two pictures the only indication that this was more than a guest room. One of both of us in front of the house, another of me before my first day of school. “Chan,” she said, after a long pause, “do you know Penglai?” ‘No, who’s he?” “Not who. Where.” “Where’s Penglai?” “Oh, I don’t know. A mountain they talk about in old stories, where you go and live forever.” “C’mon, Ma. If you go and live on some mountain of eternal life, who’ll pull up the weeds in the garden?” Smiling for the first time in the last twenty-four hours felt predictably good. “Chan,” she replied, in a tone brimming with swagger, “Penglai has gardens. Big gardens with hoa dao flowers that live all year. I know.” “How do you know?” “I know, just like I know God.” She fingered the crucifix around her neck, the one that had traveled halfway across the world, from a Stone Age village in Vietnam to a million-dollar home in the San Diego suburbs. “Do you ever know something in your heart, Chan?” “I dunno.” “You get old, like me. You start to know with the heart,” she explained, wrapping both her tiny doll hands around one of mine. “A good feeling. Your good heart…it will know.” /~/~/~/~/~/ October 15th, 2008 3:43 P.M. “So, ya know, we’re still fighting. But we appreciate the feeling, whether it’s letters from fans or just a pat on the back from a PA, that we’ve got the support of the PRIME family.” “Absolutely, we’re behind you and your mom all the way. Time to wrap up this mini-interview with our final question.” Vince looked down at the placard in front of him and made a throat-cutting motion. He pulled off his headphones as he looked around the studio at everything except me. “Ron, what is this shit?” The same well-dressed producer opened the studio door and stepped in with all the slimy haughtiness of…well, a radio producer. “What’s the problem, Vince?” “I said I didn’t want to run turn this show into a tabloid, and then you go and put this on the air? Do you know how large a cow Cantrell’s gonna have if he sees the way you’re throwing the talent under the bus?” “Cantrell gave us carte blanche, Vince. He wants full disclosure as much as the fans do. Hey, look what happened to T.O. He turned this type of thing into a whole new level of celebrity for himself.” The wispy smile that accompanied this statement gave me the distinct sense that my time as a bystander was over. “Vin, what’s up, man? Something I can do to help?” “Chan, they want to ask you about the whole deal last week.” The whole deal was the most euphemistic reference I’d heard yet. “Now, Ron, I’ve been a team player, but I am not about to– “Don’t worry about it, Vin. I’ll answer the question.” “Sounds great. Now let’s take it from the third question cue,” Ron said, still smirking at the fact that he’d gotten his way. He stepped out through the same door, giving Vince the green light with a corny flick of the index finger. “Chan, seriously. Only if you want to do this.” “Makes no difference to me, and that motherfucker outside in the Salvation Army suit jacket will be a pain in your ass if I don’t.” “Uhh, Ron can hear you.” I turned around and gave a wide grin and a wave to Ron, whose own facial expression was significantly less shit-eating. We slid the headphones back on and Vince was back on his game, as though we’d never stopped. “The question comes to us from Matt in Brookline, Massachusetts, who describes himself as the most fervent PRIMEate on the planet. He’s also in the midst of raising one of our younger PRIMEates, a seven-year-old named Nathanial. Matt’s wondering if you have any advice on how to explain what happened last week to his son.” “Great question, Matt, and I’ve been looking forward to clearing up some of the nasty rumors that are out there. Can I start at the beginning?” “Have at it, champ.” I took a deep breath and looked down at my watch, heart fluttering. Three Tylox before the show, and I still felt a clammy tightness in my throat. This wasn’t the iron grip of withdrawal, but the rubbery give of nerves in the clutch. I told myself to keep breathing. “I was at home and all of a sudden, my back seized up. It’s no secret that my back’s not right, but this was the worst it’s ever been. I literally fell to my knees in pain. The EMT’s told me that I clenched my jaw so hard I started to grind away layers of enamel on my teeth.” “Sounds terrible, Chan. And this is a lingering injury?” “Aww yeah, it’s more of a day-after thing.” Colossal Lie #1: it was an everyday thing, an every minute, every second thing. The only reason I was able to get up in the morning was with a healthy dose. “Okay, so you’re in horrible pain on the floor. What happened next?” “At this point I’m half-writhing, half-crawling towards the couch, where I’ve got my prescription painkillers. When I have a particularly bad day, I pop one before I go to sleep, so I don’t have to wake up stiff as a corpse.” This was a lie so egregious that it didn’t need a number. “You’re incapacitated at this point?” “Yeah, and my BlackBerry’s charging halfway across the apartment. When I realized I was farther from the phone than the couch, I figured I could deal with things myself. That was probably the worst decision of the night. I’m not a big doctor guy; I just figured it was something I could handle.” “I’m sure people can understand that. Why waste time at the ER when you’ve got a prescription for something that’ll do the same job as a doctor?” “Exactly. So I reach the couch, and I was in some pretty agonizing pain, a nine on a scale of one to ten. Only then do I realize that I’m not gonna be able to choke down this pill unless I have something to drink.” “Not much of a pill guy, either?” “You got it. Couldn’t swallow one if my life depended on it. I forced myself into the kitchen, and the first thing I see is a bottle of wine. Without a second thought, I put the pill under my tongue and take a healthy slug of pinot noir, because I think I’m gonna pass out from the pain.” Most of the story, up to this point, was true. Sure, it was vodka instead of wine, and five pills instead of one, but by changing the semantics, I’d changed the story, all the while telling myself that it was for some higher cause. “So you’ve made the mistake, at this point, of mixing medication with alcohol, but it’s a dire circumstance.” “Right. No excuse, it was stupid, but it was instinctual. So right after this, I start feeling woozy, and the next thing I know, I’m waking up in my cleaning lady’s arms, confused as all hell and wondering what happened.” I didn’t tell Vince Howard or anyone else that I’d downed nearly half a liter of vodka. It honestly wasn’t a suicide attempt. But fear of pain is sometimes worse than pain itself, and the fear in me hadn’t seemed like it would ever stop. So the vodka had been insurance of a kind. I knew, at the time, that I wasn’t going to feel much of anything for a very long time. “Your cleaning lady is the one who called 911?” “Yeah, Jeri. I owe her the world. She really helped me out in a bad situation.” “Jeri, if you’re listening out there, Chandler’s got a huge bonus coming up for competing in the Roulette with your name on it.” While Vince laughed at the joke, I laughed at the charade. I’d decided shortly after the fact that no one could ever know about my dependency. “So, just to finish up here, what would you tell Matt in Brookline? How does he explain this to his son, to someone who sees Chandler Tsonda as a superhero?” “Matt, I can’t pretend to know a damn thing about parenting, but what happened was an accident. No different than if I’d fallen and needed an ambulance. If you don’t believe me, go check the medical records. They’re public record.” They were. Except that my BAC had been taken hours after I’d arrived, and it didn’t represent the full irresponsibility of the act. “I’m not some struggling addict. I’ve got a successful career here in PRIME, as well as business interests across the globe. That doesn’t happen if you’re some kind of drug-addled libertine.” I was the exception to my own rule; besides, more interested men had taken over the day-to-day interests of Tsuperstar Enterprises. “So tell your son to stay in school and keep working towards his dreams.” “He’s got looks, money, the Universal Belt, and the wisdom of Confucius. Ladies and gentlemen, I’d like to thank Chandler Tsonda for taking time out to chat with us.” “Thanks for having me, Vin.” I slid the headphones off, as Vince launched into this week’s trivia question. After a sly wink for Ron, I slipped out. The lies were airtight, the situation contained, but there was no rush of relieved satisfaction. It would take more than the tidy sequestration of a potentially career-ending fiasco for that. I had to know if what my mother had said was true. Dawkins would know a good heart if he saw it. /~/~/~/~/~/ October 16th, 2008 6:49 A.M.. “Sir, there is absolutely no way you’re getting in there. Visiting hours start at 9 A.M.” The receptionist in front of me was just trying to do her job by stopping me. Sure as shit she had dozens of crazies to prevent from entering the hospital everyday. I struggled to think of a way to explain the situation. “Listen…Marnee,” I said, reading off her lapel, “do you maybe recognize me from TV?” “Right, you’re some kind of TV star and you just happen to be in Hilo, Hawaii at seven in the morning?” “Pretty unbelievable, huh?” She nodded at me, finger at the ready to dial security. “Okay, can we make a deal?” “I don’t make deals. I tell people when visiting hours are and when they aren’t. And they aren’t for another two hours and ten minutes.” “Listen, Marnee, I just wanna see my godson for the first time. Can we be adults for a second?” Taking her silence as a yes, or at least potential ambivalence, I continued. “I’ll give you my ID and you can Google my name. If you can’t find something to convince you I’m worthy of a little special treatment. Please.” Without saying anything, she snatched my driver’s license and started clicking away. She let out a gasp. “Ohmigod!” “Lady, I swear that I was young and the photographer told me it was the only way to get into modeling. I’m not proud of –” “You’re hot!” “Uhh…thank you?” “No, I mean in this picture.” She turned the monitor towards me, offering up a picture from nearly ten years ago. I was wearing a semi-ridiculous….okay, fully ridiculous fur vest on my bare torso, and ripped jeans. “Right now,” she scoffed, “those bags under your eyes are too big to fit in an overhead compartment.” “I…okay. Can I go now or do you need to call me ugly a couple more times?” She handed back my license, with a sort of “you got me” smirk plastered across her fairly unremarkable face. “If anyone asks, you’re his lover.” “Gotcha. I’m gayer than an off-Broadway production of Rent.” She chuckled as I shot down the hall towards Room 3233. Dawkins would have an answer, but I’d have to tell him the truth. And not knowing if I was ready, I figured it’d be best to just get it over with. The room was eerily quiet, though. Immediately, I saw why I’d come. Dawkins, whom I’d called “a stupidly good dude” just a week before was standing sentry over the small hospital bed. The only sound in the room was the hum of Nicholas Chandler’s incubator, a small plastic bubble-like structure. Approaching the incubator, I laid eyes on my godson for the first time. Seeing his tiny digits squirm, moved by a cherub’s dream…well, it got a little dusty in the room. I slid a finger across the corner of my eye, not wanting Dawkins to wake up and find me all weepy. But now, what I’d come for, Dawkins’s ear, wasn’t available. “What’re we gonna do, Nick?” If you ever wonder why people are always having one-sided conversations with babies, go try it. They’re the best listeners in the world. Talking to him, I was simultaneously self-conscious and lost in conversation. “You and me and your pops. I’m a mess and you’re…well, you’re just a baby. That’s his support system, right there.” “I’m trying to have an existential epiphany and figure things the fuck out…can I say fuck around you? I dunno, we’ll have to establish some kind of ground rules, but let’s just hope this isn’t a formative memory. But like I said, I was trying to storm in here and put it all on the table, but now I’m talking to a baby in an incubator.” I had to laugh. It was ridiculous to think that airing grievances to a barely conscious being would chance anything. And yet, it was hard to stop. “It’s like this, Nick. I guess I fancy myself some kinda crusader these days, but if I want to keep fighting the good fight, I’ve gotta do a bad thing. I’ve gotta lie about the pain, about the pills, about how much easier it would be to not give a fuck again. Does everybody…resist doing what’s right, especially when it costs so much?” Nick had no answer. “I could throw it all away. I think about it more than they’ll ever know. It sound wishy-washy and selfish to be this shining star and want to leave it behind, but it’s the truth. ‘Cuz I can’t just rest on my laurels anymore. I decided, after Culture Shock last year, that if I was gonna stay, I was gonna do it for the right reasons. Integrity and honor and making sure PRIME stays PRIME, ya know? I didn’t wanna be me-first anymore. And your dad and Rayne, they’re true friends, I think. Guess I wouldn’t know a true friend if he snuck up behind me and tried to break my neck in the Halo, eh?” Here I was, nervously laughing to a two-week-old about long-gone Danny Ferguson. “But I think, when I see you and the ki…err, your dad, I think that’s my answer. I think that’s why I gotta stick around, even if means doing that bad thing. I can live with a dependency, but I don’t think I can turn my back on what I’ve got goin’. My ma, she said…uhh…she said I’ve got a good heart, and I don’t know if I should believe her.” My heart, black or golden or whatever it was, was beating fast, saying something. “But she’d know, right? She’d know. Anyway, I’m probably interrupting some great baby dream. Maybe about your mom or something. She was…well, I dunno what she was, but she sure made Dawkins happy. When he heard about you, he just walked around with this stupid grin on his face for like two weeks. And now you’re here, so we gotta make sure he makes it, alright? Can’t let him mope too much.” As I thought about Nikkie, Penglai inexplicably made its way into my head, and I could see the peach flowers, with petals as big as a fist. “I dunno, I’m rambling. What I’m sayin’ is that if you don’t quit, I won’t. Deal?” I wish I could prove to you that this was how it happened, but I shit you not that as I asked Baby Dawk that question, his lids opened just barely. I saw his eyes, tiny little flecks of emerald floating in white space. He was quiet as he looked up at me. “Morning, Nick.” “Bruh?” Dawkins was on his feet before I could turn around and explain myself, fists balled. I put my hands up as a sign of surrender, and his muscles visibly relaxed as he saw my face. “Jeez, Chan, ya can’t just go sneakin’ around like a thief in the night. You coulda been a baby snatcher.” “Trust me, I don’t think anybody’s gonna mess with you, Papa Bear.” That eternally youthful smile spread across his face as he approached his son, looking down. Father and son smiled at one another as Dawkins put a long finger on the top of the plastic. “What were you doin’ in here anyway, bruh? Aren’t you ‘sposed to be in D.C.?” “Life’s not always about showing up where you’re supposed to be. Besides, couldn’t have the runt walking around spoiling my good name as godfather. Had to make sure he’s okay to wear my name.” “And?” “Something tells me he’ll be alright. Him, you, all of us." "Bruh," Dawkins said, squinting at me, "sometimes I wonder about you." "Me too, kid."
