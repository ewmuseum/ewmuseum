Reservations About Change

12 February 2008
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2/12/08 Skepticism is the beginning of Faith. -Oscar Wilde February 10th, 2007 3:04 P.M. When I asked Dr. DiPendo for sage advice on the proverbial next step, I didn’t expect him to refer me to the red man. In the wake of ReVolution 150’s upheaval, I deeply desired his mind’s calming touch; instead, he seemed even more distant than before. “But why would I go talk to an Indian shaman?” Changing like the seasons, the same office that had seemed comfortable weeks before now seemed brutally standoffish. The orderly books stood at attention like firm Soviet Reds. Instead of imparting an air of culture, the various sculptures were muted; this room was a museum, a place that the dead make their own–the living can only linger. “Chandler,” my shrink started, sighing, “there are steps beyond my ability here. And when you told me about the experience with your mother in her church, my theory was revitalized.” I raised a brow, having momentarily forgotten about the consistent, furtive workings of DiPendo’s mind. As soon as (or often before) I said my piece, his mind whirred into action, decoding it in terms of Oedipal desires or repressed carnal pleasures. I wondered if the man could ever turn the switch on the Psych degree off. Noticing my returned blank stare, DiPendo whipped his hands from his sides, gesturing manically as he explained himself. “I initially imagined that yours was a crisis of faith. It turns out it was a crisis between real and imagined self. Much easier,” he nodded reassuringly. “But now, in the interim between your recent moment at the mountaintop and whatever’s to come next…” He shook his head when he saw the smirk curling across my face. Winning Jewel in the Crown and its immediate euphoria had faded, but I wasn’t even close to tired of other people bringing it up. “I’m sorry,” I lied. “I was totally listening. My goal for the interim.” Hearing me repeat the words verbatim didn’t do anything to convince the doc. It’s tough to trick somebody whose job is to be within the machine of your brain. “Keep going. Please.” “Between now and then,” he restarted, “you need to find something to believe in. And not just your own status as a paramount wrestler.” When he spoke this time, his hands were still. “Doc, c’mon. A man of science can’t truly believe that my admission of deities beyond scientific reason has any real value for me. And it doesn’t matter what color the people who invent those deities are.” I sighed, nearly lamenting my own words for a moment before perking back up and concluding with a strong voice: “I can barely believe what my eyes show me; how on Earth could I believe things they don’t?” “You sound like a Hallmark card, Chandler,” he chided. “Your response is typical not of someone who doesn’t want to believe, but someone who refuses to examine belief at all. Do you remember my story about Odin trading his eye for the ability to see the future?” “Yeah, before DDT.” “Do you think my dallying into superstitions is purposeless? Rhetorically asked, but truly. I would hope you trust my intuitions.” Heat shimmered off his voice, genuinely dismayed with my faithlessness in him. “Both professional,” he grumbled, “and personal.” “Doc, I’m a curious dude.” The sunshine barely snuck through under the blinds, leaving the room in the hands of sickly artificial light. “You can’t take it as a shot if I don’t just take your word for gospel.” He folded his arms and in his eyes I could see a man trying to step into my moccasins. “Listen,” I implored him, “the depth of the shit I’ve walked through to get here is unimaginable for most people. You see the things that I went through just to get to this country as folklore, a possibly true account that’s too far removed from your own way of the world to really affect you.” He was silent, pulling his right hand up to the side of his face, pulling lightly on his trapper’s beard. DiPendo’s conversations with me had long ago forsaken the use of a notebook; I thought I wasn’t his average case (some balding middle-age divorcee). I was beginning to wonder, though, if the lack of a notebook meant that my problems weren’t memorable at all, just visible, cosmetic blemishes of the mind. “There are still landmines blowing kid’s legs off all over Southeast Asia. And that speaks to some Creational force that is either bored with our failures or unspeakably cruel.” Knowing that he would respond to my next question without answering anything, I finished that train of thought, squinting slightly. “Don’t you think that even being fated to do something is better than living like that?” He finally smiled, exhaling. Stroking that jolly beard of his, DiPendo slid me a piece of paper. Of course, he waited until I picked it up before he said anything. “You don’t think you’re fated to do anything.” “Too scary,” I admitted, my voice cloudy as I chose my next words. “…to think that the universe has already played out the rest of my life somehow and decided what I do and where I go.” Qualifying my statement, I muttered, “I imagine that’s why the sheep gravitate towards religion making life some meaningful series of decisions. Ya know?” DiPendo nodded and I looked down at the piece of paper. There was a name and an address, reminding me that my time with the doctor was done. I glanced to make sure there was nothing on the other side and stood up. “Free your mind a bit,” DiPendo urged as he rose to his feet. “Acknowledging that you don’t know what my friend has to say to you is the first step towards accepting it.” I could tell the doctor didn’t really think he would get me to do away with seeping prejudices–no tabula rasa. The best he could hope for would me listening indifferently. * * * * February 11th, 2007 1:19 P.M. It was your average home, which surprised me. I’d passed onto the reservation fifteen minutes ago, only to see no change in the human or natural landscapes. Now I know that modern day American Indians are not Mohawk wearing, hatchet thumping savages. But my limited cultural involvement with these people was dropping a couple large on booze and cards at their seedier institutions back east. And I really dug The Last of the Mohicans. With slight trepidation, I knocked on the door. DiPendo would probably check in with this guy to see whether his star pupil had followed through on the soul-searching campaign. Almost instantaneously, the door swung inward. Framed by the doorway was a burly, earthen man with shoulder-length hair as black as singed earth. His girth was…well, impressive, even to mine eyes that consistently see steroidal anomalies of the human body’s limitations. He must have been close to 6’5, with approximately 300 pounds of broad body. “You must be Chandler,” he purred. His accent was barely detectable, and he certainly carried more ardor in his tone than I was used to. “My name is Arnold Greyeyes.” We shook hands and he led me slowly into his sitting room. Trinkets of his people littered his house, small totems hung on walls and wampum belts within golden frames. Without much thought, I realized that whatever curatorial trait drove DiPendo’s design scheme was in place here. And so my wondering continued: could there be another man as mechanical in thought as DiPendo? “What are all these furnishings in your home?” Deathly curious, I was unable to keep my mouth shut. “Sadly, very little of that material illustrates my own culture. But I can appreciate art from other tribes, just like I can appreciate art from another country in a museum.” His words meshed with my thoughts almost too succinctly. “What…is your culture?” I felt stupid asking, but figured this was the type of question DiPendo had prepared him for. “What’s yours?” His quick-witted response came with a smile, though, reassuring me. “I mean, what tribe are you from? What exactly does DiPendo see in your beliefs that would strike me as important?” More than anything, I wanted to get to the point. His furrowed brow told me that I was offering up a confusing first impression. “I am Kumeyaay by birth and blood. But I am have Spanish, Mestizo, and Irish blood in me.” He rubbed his hands together, leathery palms crafting an organic whisper. “I believe in great creator-gods and Manitou of all kinds.” “Manitou?” This was not the place to stay mum about my complete ignorance. If I was going to learn, only my own questions would lead me to answers of faith. “Spirits. Inside you. Outside you. Within animals, plants, trees, rocks, lakes, rivers.” Harshly, he growled, “this does not make me a believer of some cosmic harmony. Your time will be wasted if you see our way of life as only a pagan worship of nature and all its Creations.” His words cut through any preconceived notions I might have had. “Noted, no hippie shit.” A slight smile spread across his weathered, shaven face. “Balance and reciprocity.” “Huh?” “Those are the two key tenets that we live by. They’re the way that we bridge old values with our modern everyday life.” The strength behind his rough, sympathetic voice was not that of religious zealotry, but of pragmatic faith. “And we have one sacred item that represents this above all else.” He pulled some contraption from behind his chair, propping it up on the table. “Umm…I feel borderline blasphemous for asking, but is that a pipe?” It certainly looked like a long pipe, with extending tendrils and a short, shallow bowl. Arnold nodded. “It’s a calumet, a peace pipe.” He began packing something into the pipe while he continued to explain. “Right here, this is balance and reciprocity. The stem is the male symbol, the bowl is the female symbol.” This struck me as strikingly similar to your average pagan belief system, but I said nothing. “Balance between the sexes. And great leaders would often extend this pipe even to their enemies. Reciprocity means that providing, not accumulating, is the key to all honest relationships.” With his explanation over, he handed me the pipe’s long, slender form. “I’ll be honest. The Catholic Church never tried to get me high, so you’ve got major points there.” He chucked a bit, a deep guttural thing. “It’s tobacco, my friend.” I’m not a big smoker, but I felt I owed Arnold’s reciprocity a shot. Not as a belief, but a life experience. “Gotcha.” I pulled the stem to my face and let Arnold light the bowl with a match. Inhaling steeply, I brought the smoke into my lungs, feeling its tight grasp on my chest. And finally, when the pressure was too much, I pushed my breath out in ugly-looking O’s. Arnold did the same, his sitting room becoming boxed with hazy, acrid atmosphere. But the feeling was pleasant. I reclined into my chair, sinking in the warm buzz of the tobacco. Arnold and I didn’t need to say anything for a very long time.
