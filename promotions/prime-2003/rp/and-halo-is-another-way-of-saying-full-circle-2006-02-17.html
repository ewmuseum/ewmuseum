...And Halo Is Another Way Of Saying 'Full Circle'

17 February 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2-17-06 Welcome to my travel journal, detailing the time spent in Japan for the Culture Shock 2006 trip for PRIME. This is done for two reasons: one being to pass the time on a boring-ass flight...and the other being to make some sort of record, as this will probably one of the things I leave behind after I'm gone. Yuck. Let's move on. I love Japan, but I fucking hate this flight. I still haven't figured out if I really believe it or not, but I chose to take the same type of flight that I did last time. Northwest Airlines, flying high above the Arctic circle, touching down in Osaka after nearly 24 hours of mind-numbing plane ride and layover. Feeling a little rusty on my language skills, which were never good to begin with. Older Japanese woman sitting next to me on the flight, despite flying out from the U.S., she still manages to smell like daikon radish. Unnerving, but strangely comforting. Granted, I have no intention of trying to make small talk in order to practice the language. There will be time enough over the next two weeks. Talked briefly to Jiro, and his cousin has stopped apartment-sitting, and gone back to his parents' house. Hope he did not leave natto wrappers all over the apartment. I really like most of the food in Japan, but that shit is still gross. Been in the air for maybe an hour after leaving Detroit, and already want to die of boredom. The slate of movies that they're showing boils down to: Batman BeginsZathuraNorth CountryMiss Congeniality 2: Armed And Fabulous I can do Batman Begins, obviously...but the rest of this is a wasteland. Maybe if I swallow half a dozen Asahis, I will be alright. It has been four seconds, and I already doubt this seriously. Nervous about Culture Shock, and the Halo match. Have a chance to win belt named after my old wrestling federation. This is both ironic, and nerve-wracking. No one told me this would even be an issue until yesterday. Silver lining on a really, really shitty day. Hate losing, especially to A-List. Makes me feel like a chump. Now my only close-thing-to-allies in PRIME, the Replacements, are out of the company. Things get worse right before I step into the ring with 34 other people. Awesome. Fat guy in the seat in front of me is passing gas loud enough for me to hear. Trying to get face closer to radish woman without her noticing/thinking I am hot for her. Miss Congeniality 2 starting. Wonder if I can get the oxygen mask to deploy, or kill self with folding tray in front of me. Must be dead before I have to smell the meatloaf for in-flight dinner. If God exists, he is laughing at me. Note: He may actually be in league with Hoyt Williams after all. * * * * * Kyle stepped off the train, over the yellow line, and finally set foot in Japan proper. No more airport, no more train ride. The train shook briefly under him, and then he landed at the O-CAT building, hub of almost all transportation in Osaka. He took a step forward and breathed in the alive air of this urban area. Didn't smell like much of anything yet, that's Japan for you. Awfully different from the odors of downtown NYC or (god forbid) LA. He started to hike up a flight of stairs, pressed up near several dozen Japanese going in the same direction. There's the long walk down the long alley at the end of the O-CAT. The typical sterotypes flashed by...older man with heavy-lidden eyes, sarariman...sarariman...six more sararimen,,,,office lady, office lady, three middle school girls in school uniforms and wearing sock glue...nerdy-looking gaijin with pretty Japanese girl in skirt...sarariman...and finally to the end of the tunnel, opening to the big, circular and two-story “entrance” to the O-CAT. And that's where you hear the music. Kyle Lamen, for once in his life, was really really excited to hear “The Switch” by Will Smith. His legs were tired, body pressed to the point of exhaustion after the plane rides and layovers...but there was an extra spring in his step as he made his way to the railing, and looked out over the balcony. Below him were ten or twenty Japanese youths, anywhere from fourteen to twenty-five, and they were gathered in little cliques. And they were dancing. A couple of the boys near the center were dressed in sideways trucker hats and baggy jeans, and they were trying to lay down some freezes and six-steps, obviously beginner breakers. One kid was doing spins down on his hands over in the corner, that would've put the b-boy crowd in Asbury Park, urban central Jersey to shame. Five girls in too much makeup and fake tans that stood them out from their counterparts with fairer skin shook their asses, a fact that didn't go unnoticed by a set of middle-aged salarymen who stared openly. Kyle smiled. It was good to be back. * * * * * Believe it or not, Japan is so completely different from the rest of the English-speaking world...you really have no idea until you've set foor on the soil. Or, more likely, the concrete. A lot of wrestlers come to Japan. They love pro wrestling over here, and seriously...can you blame them? The lights, the spectacle, the energy...with none of the stigma that tends to exist back home in the States. Anyways, coming to Japan as a wrestler is pretty cool. The locals treat you pretty well, especially the otaku and the kids that train at the dojos. You can kind of be a celebrity. I only wrestled in Japan once or twice, and it was back when I was working with CCW. But I digress... Now, coming to Japan as a regular person. Sacrificing two years of your life at the Nihon altar...that's something else entirely. When I first came to Japan, I had stopped dying my hair. I had done a quick (for me) romp through China, India, and England. I'm sure I sounded like a weird version of the Beatles but hey, I was looking to find myself. My friend Kensuke told me to go to Japan, and he'd help me out with contacts and accomodations. Let me tell you, no matter what happens in the future, I will ALWAYS be indebted to him for introducing me to this country and culture. But I was talking about my hair, wasn't I? So I'd stopped dying my hair by the time I'd gotten to Japan. For some quick background, let me tell you WHY I dye my hair in the first place. It's not because “that's punk” or “that's edgy”. Yes, I do it to draw attention to myself, but I like to think that I'm different because I have a sense of self-awareness about it. It's because I, like much of the disenfranchised youth of America, grew up painfully normal. My mom made it to middle-class status without having a dad to back her up, and even though she supported both me and my older sister. Anyways, I go to Japan to blend into the crowd, to just be. And you know, it's probably a good thing I didn't dye my hair right away. Because if you're a gaijin in Japan, you probably have too much attention drawn to you in the first place anyways. And when you're six-four and a cult celebrity to a couple thousand otaku, you've got that strike against you to begin with. * * * * * With a start, Kyle pulled his head up from the pillow where it rested, even before he opened his eyes. When the heavy lids finally cracked open, they were pointed at the red digital lights of the alarm clock next to his bed. 5:56 “Shit.” Heart beating unnaturally fast, Kyle waited for a moment, hoping that his eyes would adjust to the darkness. Little by little, they did, even as the black skies through the outside window began to lighten to ash gray. With some effort, he pulled his body into a sitting position, the white sheets falling off of his bare chest to pile in his lap. He swung his feet off to the side, and his eyes flickered with momentary surprise as they hit the tatami floor, instead of hanging off the bed. Right. Japan. Not sleeping in a bed, but sleeping on his futon. Duh. He tossed off the rest of the sheets and shivered a little as he rose to his feet. He found the remote control for the air-conditioning unit and flicked it to slow down. The light continued to gather outside throw the sliding window to Kyle's small balcony. He felt drawn to it. He cautiously stepped over his fallen blue jeans from the night before, and a few of the natto wrappers that Jiro's cousin invariably left on the floor during his house-sitting spell. Kyle wondered if the smell of fermented beans would ever really leave the apartment. He made a quick mental note to by air freshener, if he could figure out where he might find some. The conveni down the street? He absently kicked his gym bag off to the side, and out of the way so he could slide the door to his balcony open. With a quick step, he crossed the threshold to the outside, where the chill of a February morning chilled the inside of his sinuses and the top of his throat. He assessed the skyline, and the sun rising in the east just barely struggled to break free of the horizon line. The colors swirled in the sky, mixing with clouds and stringing about like cotton candy, curling and congealing, simple strands mixing together like oil paint or hot melted sugar...soft blues and faded oranges fighting in the sky for dominance against the cold, consuming gray. The light and energy raging against the night, claiming some space for itself. And sooner or later, the underdog lights would prevail, and it'd be day, for real. The world would start again. Later on, he'd try running about the neighboring blocks, but the bustle of traffic and too many odd glances would cause him to call it quits for a while. He runs with a windbreaker on, in case of rain, and the sight of a windbreaker, or any coat with a hood on the streets of suburban Osaka tends to draw alarming gazes from too many of the locals. He'd duck into a 100-yen place and grab a few cans of pineapple, a small piece of chicken or fish, and a few eggs for breakfast, and later Kyle'd see if he couldn't find the right words for the melody he'd found a few days ago. He'd train, eat, and otherwise have a normal day in Japan, kind of like he used to when he lived here. He'd watch the bicycle garden grow outside the train station down the street, as all the commuters leave their bikes outside the station before they head to work or school or whatever. He'd try to communicate in a language he didn't have mastery of, and forge small connections with people he'd never see again. But for right now, he'd put up with the cold. He tucked his arms together, folded against his chest, and stared out past the bustle of the Nankai line, towards where the sun was making it's inexorable pass towards the top of the sky. He smiled a lopsided smile. After all, how many times would he get to see a view like this before he died? * * * * * Misplaced Lyrics It's not the first timeI've thrown away a chance April always hurtsAnd autumn's always the bestOn the train map in my headMy heart runs east to west Anywhere but hereAnyone but you Anywhere but hereAnyone but me …I should give up on songwriting. Seriously. No time left for it anyways… * * * * * The rain kept coming down on the Osaka evening, and the umbrellas about the streets of the city opened like blooming flowers in a garden in speed-motion. Kyle’s umbrella was one of the cheap, 500 yen ones that you could pick up at any corner conveni store. Black, naturally, because it was cheap and easy. He joined the throng of moving people on the streets of Osaka moving between the raindrops. Down about thirty blocks from Kyle’s apartment, he was weaving through uptown Osaka, where the big high-rise construction was taking place. Kyle’s feet sloshed about his feet being dampened inside his sneakers, but he trudged on, one thought in his mind: Lightly grilled meat and fish. Up to the big elevated rail line, where the city started to get a little more run-down, a little rustier. The pavement started to run spider-web cracks, which turned into massive gashes. Here and there, the Osaka homeless start to amass. Near an abandoned building, the area in front is festooned with blue waterproof tarps, the sign of homelessness in Japan. There are a few small fires, and a number of middle-aged Japanese men sitting below their tarps, waiting for a future that isn’t coming. Kyle gave them a few nervous glances, not because he’s worried about what they’ll do, but feeling sorry for them, and not knowing what to do. He finally got to a small sign that read in romanji “Yamamoto Yakiniku”. Kyle shook off some of the rain that snuck in under his umbrella as he caught up to the storefront. He closed up the umbrella and stepped into the restaurant. “Irrashaimase!” The hostess at the front of the restaurant bowed deeply to Kyle, and took the umbrella out of the hand of Kyle. Kyle did his best to offer up his order for the evening. “Um…watashi wa yakiniku ga tabemasu kudasai.” The hostess offered some help. “Hitori desu ka?” Kyle just nodded, and held up one finger. “Hai…arigato gozaimasu.” The hostess pointed him towards a crude wooden booth with a small cushion on it. Seconds later, as he was sitting, someone brought out a small brazier filled with coals with a grill atop it. Kyle nodded appreciatively at his server, then stood up and wandered over to a cooler, with several plates and different trays of meat and vegetables on it. He carefully used a pair of chopsticks to pick out some choice pieces from the cooler, grabbing some chicken cartilage, some squid, and more than one tender piece of beef. He grabbed some fried potatoes, pineapple, peppers, and returned to his seat. Kyle Lamen went back to his booth, and started grilling the food on it quite carefully, by himself. * * * * * The thing I can’t really shake is the fact that I’m alone here in Japan. I mean, it’s kind of the reason I came here. I made some mistakes, sure...so I came here to escape them and find out what was important to me. Pretty quickly, I figured out that there wasn’t anything to get away from. The people. They’re everywhere and you can’t get away from them. I mean, you can, but you can’t escape people. They are everywhere, and unless you lock yourself up in a room by yourself, like Jiro used to, you can’t avoid them. So I move to Japan, and again, I’m surrounded by people. I met a girl. I think every American guy who comes to Japan meets a girl, and yeah, it’s a little cliché. But I did, and she was beautiful, and a little famous, and she and I had a torrid two month romance before she disappeared completely. And I met my band here. And my tenant, and Jiro…and Mr. Sennosuke who taught me how to make swords. I mean, you meet people. That’s life. I’m not really interested in leaving people behind. But hey, you can’t write your own ticket. Now I get to leave people behind again. This time, I’m not real fond of doing it. Can’t go back someday when I feel I’m ready, you know. Anyways, I’m really fortunate for the people I’ve left back home. Thank god for friends like Kensuke back home. For people who mean something to me. For people like Jason, and Kensuke, and Mom and Bekah, and Sarah, even if she never speaks to me again. I’m really grateful for the few other people in my life that’re still around. The few people I can call friends. * * * * * Kensuke Suzahara paced nervously across the floor of his Seattle walkup office. His cellphone, a SLVR, sat more patiently than he felt on his desk. The desk was immaculate, everything clean, neat and organized like his owner. Kensuke loved to be in control…of his sports management company, when he was in the ring and wrestling…even in his interpersonal relationships. He’d been friends with Kyle since a little after he came to the country to wrestle, but it’d always been one of those friendships of convenience. Kyle Lamen was a key to other things. He’d always been that. Paul knew that too. He knew that Paul would be calling soon, he always called around five on Tuesdays, even if it was just to check up on Kyle’s schedule for the week, making sure there wasn’t anything going on. Paul’s interest in Kyle baffled him, even made him a little envious, although those feelings could typically be pushed aside for the sake of control. Kensuke hated the phone calls with Paul a little. More than a little sometimes. He hated the way Paul would talk to him like his subordinate, hated feeling like he was a cog in a grand plan. Paul had a way of making people feel very small, but also a way of making them do anything he wants. Ever since CCW, ever since Kensuke had first apprenticed himself to Paul as Neurosis…there’s the tug. The neocharisma. The phone on Kensuke’s desk buzzed, and Kensuke didn’t even have to look at the name or phone number that came on. He hit the green button. “Paul.” “Kensuke…my friend, how are you feeling on this blustery old February day…” Kensuke’s eyes rolled back. “Please Paul, cut the crap.” “Crap, Kensuke. I’d call it being sociable. You’re getting more and more hostile. Don’t-“ Kensuke stiffened a bit. “Come on. Don’t tell me what to do and don’t threaten me, Paul.” The voice on the other end of the phone was unmoved. “Really now, son. Relax. You know why I’m calling.” “Paul, it’s the same as every week. Kyle’s not doing anything out of the ordinary. He’s got a PPV this week. He’s in Japan. He’s not going to see a doctor, and he’s not going to do anything to jeopardize our lie.” The voice on the other line was harsh. “Would you stake your life on it?” Kensuke sighed to himself. “Of course. I’m doing this for him, same as you. Helping him deal with his situation.” The voice of “Paul” on the other end was little more than a self-satisfied chuckle. “Sure there, Kenny. You’re not doing this for yourself, and your curiosity any more than I am.” The thought of that chilled Kensuke a little. “Tell Kyle good luck for me. I mean, don’t tell him from me…” Kensuke suppressed the urge to spike the phone. “Of course. Say hi to Nancy and the kids for me.” “I wouldn’t dream of it. Goodbye for now, Kensuke.” Paul’s side of the phone clicked, and Kensuke was left alone in his office. His stomach roiled and rumbled inside him, at the thought of his lies to Kyle. He suppressed it the way he did every time he thought about it… …he thought about the things he’d buy later that day with his money from the Arovnik Foundation. * * * * * Danny Ferguson. I can barely bring myself to write his name. I don't like him, I never have, and I probably never will. And it's not really about how he dropped me on my head and injured me, and it's not even really about his towering ego. It's about the fact that he could be so much better than this. I don't know if you saw the same person I did when he wrestled in RUSH as El Spiffy, but that looked a hell of a lot different than this Danny Ferguson. Even when he was on that dumb-ass TV show years back, he didn't seem like this...he didn't seem broken. Shallow. Obsessed. He used to be a really great wrestler. I dunno. He can insult me all he wants. Then we'd fight, and that'd be the end of things. I mean, that's how it typically goes in wrestling. But there's something different about Danny. Something that makes him something I can't stand. I think it's because he's truly insincere. I donate 1.5 million dollars to the American Cancer Society, and the next week, he donates ten. Why? While I'd like to think it's about him wanting to help people in need, I know that's not the case. It looks like a cheap PR ploy at best, and at worst, a way to make another movie. I've got a lot of faith in people...faith that they can be better than what they are. And I truly hope that that faith isn't misplaced. When you talk about the Five Star belt, it means something very different to the two of us. To Danny, Five-Star means fame, means a review. A push to his ego that says “this movie called PRIME wrestling gets this critic's approval. Good family fun. I'd like to see him in other projects.” It's this sharp little ego boost, and another shiny trophy to put on his shelf, and make him smile and say “look how great I am.” To me. Five-Star represents my past. It shares a name with another past federation I wrestled for. It reminds me of all the times I came so close to achieving, to putting myself on the line when the pressure came down. And every other time up to this point, I've failed. To achieve that Five-Star belt would be a way of saying that this time, you can surpass everything you once were. You can no longer look at yourself as the guy who washed out, the guy who couldn't hack it at a top level. That finally, before you die, you've actually made it. * * * * * Kyle Lamen sits outside the steps of the theatre in Shinjuku, the theatre district of downtown Japan. It used to be where all the kabuki theatres and stuff was, years back...but has basically turned into a hedonist's playground. Movie theatres. Convinis. Tons of “massage parlors” and hostess bars. Little ramen shops and little electronics boutiques. Kids dressed in Visual K. Preteens dressed in French couture. Wannabe Yakuza prowling the streets like tiger cubs. Kyle Lamen sits and watches as a previously isolated culture absorbs images, emotions, actions from a host of other cultures, and throws them all into an atom smasher, and lets nature take its course. This square in the heart of Shinjuku isn't a melting pot...it's a blast furnace. It's a nuclear reactor. Fireworks shoot off overhead, painting the sky in streaks and shimmers of color. Midori - a flash of light green and a pop. Shiroi – pure white light and a sharp crack. It seems like there’s every night fireworks here. It's, ironically enough, a cultural atom bomb. Like the Dual Halo. * * * * * I left Japan in May 2005 for a couple of reasons. I'd been running from the life I'd had before, all the failures I'd amassed over the years. I come back now, February 2006, because I've got the biggest night of my life. I'm doing exactly what I wanted I'm really glad that coming back here, and finding that I still love this place means that it wasn't the escape that did it for me. There really is something about this country that touches me. It heals me. A lot of people want to win the Dual Halo. 36, to be exact. Some want it bad. Some want it REALLY bad. Some even go out of their way to say they NEED it. I get it. You wanna win the biggest match of the year in the worst way. And why? Is it for the glory? Is it for the one wish, that you can use to gold-dig through the fed, and get what you see as a well-deserved shot at the Universal Title, despite the fact that you haven't earned it the old-fashioned way? One or two people have the right idea. Karina wants to cement her legacy. I buy that as reasonable, credible motivation. This is not because I want to get in her pants (I kinda do), and this is not because she's the one wrestler in this company I admire most from a competitive standpoint. It's because she's honest about her motivations, and honest about herself. Me? I've got ten months left, give or take, to make my mark on PRIME. A lot of people are gonna cash in for a Universal Title shot, and be lock step and gone. But I'm trying to establish a legacy of my own, a rubber stamp on a life filled with moments that blow away like sakura leaves in the wind. And PRIME tells me that I have the option of earning myself a dying wish? On the first chance I get to wrestle in my second home, in Japan? In the biggest match of the year? One in which I can cement a legacy as performer and entertainer? After I FINALLY settle my issue with Danny Ferguson, and help bring back the Five-Star Title? It's like a dream come true. Now all I have to do is not fuck up when the big spotlight is shined on me. And this is something I've never done before. When it comes down to it, every time, I fold under the pressure. I do my best impression of France during [insert name of war here]. I fold like a card table. Not this time…at least I hope. There’s still hope. * * * * * In downtown Tokyo, there’s a giant Shinto temple. Not a whole lot of people travel by it, because it’s based in the downtown commercial area. Also, because it’s filled with Japan’s homeless. But it’s a great, solid wood temple surrounded by big low-hanging trees. In the center of the courtyard is a giant cherry tree. In better times of the year, it’s covered in pink cherry blossoms, sakura, they’re called. There’s a tradition, where you tie a wish to the tree. You either write it on a slip of paper, or on a wooden block that has some rope or string attached to it. And then, in the end, the wish or the prayer comes true. Sometimes. And so, Kyle Lamen stood, huddling against himself in the February cold. The tree stood bare before him, mirroring him. Having nothing but wishes and hopes. Carrying nothing. Surrounded by the cold beauty of a Tokyo shrine, of a smooth, cold day. He scribbled something on a small whitish yellow piece of paper, he then folded it, and tied it to the one branch he saw that had a cherry blossom bud starting to bloom on it. The one sign of hope left on the tree. The paper only had two circles on it. * * * * * I dunno if I’d call it karma or something. But I believe that in the end, everything comes full circle. In some form or another. First, I came to Japan to escape…now I’m here chasing my goals. I’m wrestling for a belt named after my old federation, which exemplifies my prior failures and moving past them. I’m wrestling Danny Ferguson, who’s ego’s what I could’ve been if things had gone differently in life. He’s got all the media success I wanted. I’ve got the unfortunate task of going up against every single person on the roster, with no real allies. No friends, just enemies. Same way I came into the company. And if I lose here, it’ll be just like every other event I’ve ever been to. I’ve got to win the Halo to pass through the cycle, to start something new. I can’t lose. Can’t do it. Everything comes full circle in the end. And the end’s coming soon. * * * * * Epilogue: Miho Kurosaki stood in line outside the Tokyo Dome, waiting on line for Culture Shock 2006. She wore big aviator sunglasses, and a wide coat. The glasses hid her almond-shaped eyes that had captivated the Japanese public for nearly three years of idol popularity. The coat concealed the body that could still sell thousands of calendars. Miho didn’t hate wrestling, but she had no particular use for it. Culture Shock was a bit of a different story. She hefted her son up higher in her arms, which were starting to get a little sore. Kairu Kurosaki was remarkably even-tempered for a two-year old child, and naturally loved his mother very much. He really had no desire to go to a wrestling show, primarily due to his having no idea whatsoever what wrestling was. But his mom was here, and she was warm and smelled good, and he was happy. If Kairu knew that he was going to see his father, whom he’d never seen before in his life, he’d probably be happy, because kids like to see their fathers, I think. Inside the arena, Kairu’s father stretched and got ready for his two matches, completely unaware that the woman who’d disappeared from his apartment three-plus years ago was waiting on line to see him wrestle. Miho’s stomach gurgled in nervous anticipation. She wanted to see him again, wanted to see the father of her child. Yet…she couldn’t. Kairu giggled happily, perhaps knowing what was to come. Kyle Lamen would never know that his son, a son he never knew existed only would see him once, and that was from a distance, as his father fought for his life inside a wrestling ring. Just like how his father, Johnny Lamen never knew Kyle existed until much later in life. But Kyle doesn’t have a “later in life” like his father did. He’d be destined to never meet his child….destined to repeat his father’s mistake… …because everything comes full circle in the end.
