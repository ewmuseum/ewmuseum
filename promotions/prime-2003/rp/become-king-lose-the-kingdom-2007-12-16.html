Become King. Lose The Kingdom.

16 December 2007
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 12/16/07 The "prequel". [A] mother is one to whom you hurry when you are troubled. -Emily Dickinson December 14th, 2007 11:28 A.M. As I knocked on the familiar screen door, a light wind disturbed the sleeves of my t-shirt. I missed this feeling, being able to walk around dressed down. You get in the business world and all of a sudden, you need to wear a three-piece suit just to do yardwork. Here, I was just a man again. The interior door swung open and my mother’s short frame emerged. That smile could have raised the dead. I stepped inside and wrapped my arms around her, sharing no words yet. Retreating from the embrace, I handed her a vividly colored bouquet. "For you, Ma. Something to give the place a little color." She looked down at the flowers and smiled with a little less warmth than I was used to. But it was a fleeting glimpse, and she hurried down the hallway to put the flowers away. "Chan," she started from around a corner, "they are so pretty." Even after almost three decades in the States, she maintained an impressive accent. It was all about her pride, though. She refused to let me pay for an English tutor; hell, she wouldn’t even let me help her study for her citizenship test. No one who has ever met my mother wonders why I’m as mule-headed as I am. "They are so pretty," she exclaimed again, emerging with a clear vase in her hand. "Ma, don’t worry about that." I had never seen signs of her advancing age. Her sparkling eyes were as young as the day we’d set foot in America. But for the first time, she looked older. The curvature of her spine, a sad byproduct of life in Vietnam during those years, seemed worse. She had a slight hunch to her gait now, although with the way she zipped around like a livewire, you’d never know. Ignoring me completely, she fiddled with the flowers, fluffing and primping the petals to her liking. Her nimble hands trembled as they traced the flowers. "Ma, you’re shaking. Let me turn up the heat." She was lost in her flowers, so I went and manually adjusted the thermostat. "Is sixty one alright?" "Chan, I don’t need-" "Don’t be ridiculous. I didn’t buy you this house so you could freeze to death." Our stubbornness made us both hard to love. Maybe it’s why we were so often alone. "You buy me this house...so I will be closer to your wrestling place." Words like "arena" were not part of her vocabulary. But the real reason I had bought her the house? Because that’s what Americans do when they get a lot of money; they buy their mothers nice, new houses. And after everything I’d put her through, the woman deserved at least a house for her troubles. Done with her floral preparations, my mother turned to the serious issues. "You must eat," she said with life-or-death urgency. "You look skinny." In truth, I was as pumped as I’d been in years. My unofficial weigh-in had me at 202 pounds, a bona fide goliath. For the tournament, I had gone beyond primping my beach muscles. But such was her motherly duty. Her love often expresses itself in food. "Later, Ma. I couldn’t leave without some of your cooking," I replied with a slight kiss on her cheek. "Can we just talk for a little bit? I haven’t seen you in…a while." I fumbled with the words, embarrassed at how long it had been since we’d seen each other. It seemed like wrestling always ended up tearing me from more than I liked. It was easy to say that gold and glory were paramount in my life, but when I sat down at my mother’s kitchen table, it was a far cry from the truth. "Are you winning lotsa matches?" I smiled at her feigned enthusiasm. We had come a long way. / / / / November 26th, 2003 11:37 A.M. "You mean…your job?’ "Ma, please, just listen." She threw her hands up, getting up out of her chair and puttering around the kitchen with her back turned to me. My mother just couldn’t understand. "You have money. Why do this?" She started attending to the dishes, taking out her frustration on menial tasks as she usually did. "It’s a long story, Ma," I confided. It was hard for me to put this choice into words. "Wrestling is like…it’s a better way for me to be me. Does that make any sense?" It was easy to tell that she was pissed off when she started mumbling in Vietnamese. This time it was an idiom, a phrase that roughly translates to "the fool’s bolt is soon shot". "I can hear you muttering to yourself. You’re overreacting. You said it yourself; we’ve got enough money that I don’t need to work anymore. But I can make more this way. For us." "Greedy Americans…you turn into your father?" Whenever my mother found something wrong with Americans, she immediately ascribed that trait to my father. Suffice to say, we did not talk about the old man very much. "That’s not the only reason, Ma." In truth, the money was a pretty good lure. I had squandered some of my modeling money, only to find out through some friends that the top wrestlers were being paid seven figures a year. But it was really the competition I missed. That absolute sense of being better than the man across from you. "It’s something I have to do. It’s a self-fulfillment deal." "Fulfillment?" She clearly didn’t know the word, but spat it out with vitriol. "You fulfillment all you want! Then you break your neck and I left with nothing!" It was the same reason she’d tried to stop me from playing high school football. Her nightmare was outliving her son in this alien country, dying as just another immigrant. "I promise I’ll be careful." I lowered my gaze, knowing that I could do no such thing. Sure, there were safety measures, but injury was inevitable and death not impossible. "I have to tell you something." "More bad news?!" Once she got riled up, there was no sense trying to reason with her. "I’ve already finished training. I’m going to New Jersey in two days. A man named Ron Starr-" "You…leaving?" She turned and leaned against the kitchen counter, taken aback enough to stop her dishes. Her eyes were wide with what I could only imagine was despair. "When do you come back?" My face was hot with blazing shame. "I don’t know. I’ll be out there during the week for a while, but I’ll come back every other weekend to see you. No matter what." But the answer had no impact on her. She was numb with anger or sadness or whatever it was. She was paralyzed. "Ma, it really won’t change our life that much." She nodded robotically, exactly zero percent convinced that our lives were not going to change. I was racked with guilt, even more so in the face of the fact that I was excited about this new opportunity. The Phantom Booker didn’t just run another wrestling endeavor; the dude made superstars out of nobodies. And from modeling, I was already a somebody. In that somber kitchen, dreams of championship celebrations danced in my head. / / / / December 14th, 2007 11:35 A.M. "I mean, it’s all relative," I lied. Yes, I had been winning a couple matches here and there. I didn’t have Tchu’s streak yet, but I was gunning for the very same Jewel in the Crown title that he had held just two years ago. And now dude was in the Hall of Fame. Back in the beginning, that was what I knew I wanted. A plaque with my name on it and immortality. It seemed quite the pipe dream back then. "But yeah, I’ve won more than I’ve lost. That’s what counts, they say." "Good boy, Chan." I couldn’t read the way about her today. I knew she didn’t care about wins and losses, but like any good mother, she cared about my happiness. And that seemed to be linked to success in the ring. "I saw Calvino," I blurted out, no longer able to restrain myself from the real reason I was here. "I…he used me." No one knew the hurt of being used like Sam Thi De. I hadn’t thought about her birth name in forever. These American names that we first used like toys were part of our real identities now. "He made me part of his operation for something." She didn’t spring out of her chair, but those sparkling eyes were intense with a need for answers. "Chan," she whispered, "I don’t care what you did. You are a good boy. And whatever he made you do…" She couldn’t finish the thought out loud, imagining all sorts of diabolical acts. "No, nothing like that." Saying it out loud gave me a breath of relief; I realized that, in the grand scheme of things, he hadn’t taken anything from me. Sure, I had a new respect for bathtub drownings, but I hadn’t compromised my humanity. I hadn’t sunk to his level. "I’m not…like him." I don’t know if I meant that I wasn’t a killer or that I was just not my father’s son. She was content with either. "He is a monster," she stated mournfully, as if she was just realizing it for the first time. But I knew that all those years alone in this had left her plenty of time to realize the monstrosity of his actions. In that instant, she looked distant, lost with her own thoughts. "I did not expect to hear about him anymore." "I’m sorry, Ma." We sat there in silence for a moment and I put my hand on top of hers because I didn’t know what else to do. She didn’t pull her hand away, but I didn’t feel the heat of her returning the grasp. "It’s probably too much to say that…he seems like there’s a lot of pain in his life." She looked away, overtaken with emotion. I thought about my father’s words, that he looked for a way to make things right with her everyday. But his answers were in God, never in actions. "Chan," she smiled weakly, "I feel...so sorry." I had learned long ago to wear my heart on my sleeve because of her. Because of the way her emotions always snuck out just enough. "Don’t be. I’m happy if you’re happy." She offered a long, drawn out sigh. "Chan, I’m sick." She met my eyes for a moment, but then looked past me. "Okay. Let’s go get some medicine." But in a spreading hole in my stomach, I immediately felt this was not the whole story. "Ma, we’ll get some medicine for you." And her head was down, shaking and muttering softly in Vietnamese. "Ma?" "No medicine." My heart plunged. "For me, no." She finally looked up and smiled weakly; it was a terrible, bittersweet thing, that half smile with her mouth closed. "Ma…" I knew I couldn’t pry this from her. She needed to tell it herself. But she didn’t need to say the words. What difference was a medical diagnosis going to be? It could be death one way or the other. Changing the typeface on your gravestone doesn’t bring you back to life. "They call it Oldsheimer’s," she flatly added, butchering the word. "And tell me I will forget things. First little ones and then big ones." Somehow, the elementary way she expressed herself only made the disease sound more heartbreaking. I was destroyed. "You…" "I am still young. They give me years." The weak smile had faded; she was thinking about counting down those years. About losing everything. If her nightmare wasn’t coming true, it was close enough: she was going to die without anything, no memories, no love, not even hate. She was going to die as just a vessel for organs, a purely cosmetic being. "Not too many, but years." "Ma, I’m so sorry." I had come to tell my silly little gangster fairy tale and my mother bit her tongue on telling me she was dying. "I love you," I repeated for the millionth time, trying to mean it more than ever in an unsteady, choking voice. "I…love you." She grasped my hand and I felt my face burn deeply. No shame this time, just slow, rolling tears. "Chan, no," she said, trying to coax me out of crying for her. But her resolve in this was weak and when I saw her face clenched hard to stop the tears, I lost it. I broke down in a way that grown men don’t. I put my head in my hands and I sobbed. I heard the meek cries from across the table, but my mother had already mourned her own death. She was crying because she saw that I couldn’t keep my composure. Couldn’t tell her that it was going to be alright. Couldn’t even pretend to hold out false hope. My shredded heart was on my sleeve. / / / / October 1st, 2005 11: 36 A.M. "Chan, please no." She was pleading, a pervasive desperation in her voice. "Ma, it’s just for a little while." I was back in her kitchen, explaining again. "Chan, do you remember last time? Something…you change." She looked at me with eyes that scolded and remembered things, terrible things. "I know. But Ron Starr…he said he could promise me things if I did what he wanted." She never saw a second of PWC, but she heard through various grapevines about my exploits inside of , and most importantly outside of, the ring. "You do terrible things then!" In her passionate disapproval, her English broke down. "Terrible things." "Ma, I’m still the same person." I didn’t know if even I believed that. The idea of leaving a legacy had been replaced by the cutthroat politics of being noticed at all costs. Starr had promised me the world and, one vehicular assault and a lost soul later, I was going back to the well. PRIME: the new frontier of wrestling. "I’m your son." "No, no. Not the same person!" Her small voice didn’t gain anything when she yelled, but it didn’t stop her from trying. "You lose your soul. You lose the good." It was a pretty hard argument to counter. I had become reprehensible in so many ways. Starr said to let the most extreme parts of your persona come out. People would hate you or love you, but they would remember you. They would feel something every time your name was mentioned. And those hideous portions of my character that came out…people remembered them. Every boo fed the beast. To be hated universally is not something that other professional sports can boast. I was sick with power. "They want me back in less than two weeks. I’m facing…an old friend." Hoyt Williams held no such title, but I had to bend the truth. Sam Thi De was silent with frustration. Her son was an addict, hooked on the adrenaline rush, the burning heat of the spotlight. And like any good addiction, it was slowly destroying its user. "Soon," she spat, "I don’t recognize you." I wondered if she was right. "Then you forget all about who you are. No more." We stood there, tension pinned in by the walls of the kitchen, and said nothing. But as much as her pain affected me, I was selfishly certain that coming back was the only way. "Chan," she lied through gritted teeth, "I support you totally." It went without saying, in that room, between us, that she couldn’t forgive some of the sins I was about to commit. / / / / December 14th, 2007 11: 59 A.M. I wanted to hurt for days. I wanted to say something, but all the good words were used up. Everything sounded trite in my head, like lines from a character in a Hallmark movie. So after we were done crying, we sat there in near silence. The refrigerator hummed softly in the corner. "Ma," I croaked, my voice hoarse, "I’m going to get you the best medicine on the planet. I know people. There are experimental treatments." I was going to put back on the face of resolve, of false hope, of everything that is both logically wrong and intrinsically human about facing death. Pragmatism says go live your life to the fullest until you drop dead; compassion says use modern medicine to maximize the quantity of time left on Earth. "Maybe we will, Chan." I didn’t know how to tell her not to be resigned to her fate. You get an incurable disease and that’s pretty much that. "Maybe." "Don’t start. We’re gonna fight this. But you gotta want to live. To see your grandkids. To see me win a world championship. To see your future." Those are e all good things, but so is life. And when life becomes a bad thing…how can there be those good things again? "It’s about us." "We don’t need to talk. Not right now." All of a sudden, the woman in front of me sounded world weary, tired of living in this foreign country with all these people. "You should rest," I urged. I tried to pull her up out of her chair by the hand, but she didn’t budge. "What? Can’t I help a young lady to her room?" "You stay here for the rest of my life? You throw away the job and the wrestling?" It had crossed my mind. I would never admit it to my opponents, but the Jewel in the Crown had seemed almost a pitiful goal for those long minutes when I first learned my mother was sick. "What do you think?" The woman had crossed oceans, worked thankless jobs, and gone neck-deep into debt for me. I couldn’t stand there and tell her that finally coming through and wining the big match was more important than her. I would throw it away if she asked, I assured myself. And that would be it. "Chan, you tell me what you came here to tell me." She sounded sure again. She sounded like the same woman who spanked me when I threw paint all over the kitchen walls. "Before you know I was sick. You did not come here to tell me that you saw your father." I reached down and wrapped my arms around her frame. I hugged her because she was sick and because I hadn’t always been the best son and because I could. "Ma…" And with my china nestled into the side of her face, I looked around the kitchen that I designed for her. And in my heart, I knew I couldn’t let this become her tomb. So there was only one choice. "I came to tell you that I’m gonna win the Jewel in the Crown tournament in two days."
