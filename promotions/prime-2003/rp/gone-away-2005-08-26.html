Gone Away

26 August 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: Aug 26 The Jeep Wrangler grumbled under its breath as Scott hung a left and moved from the two lane road he'd been on onto the gravel passage that ventured through the trees and up the hill ahead of him. Scott reached down, pulling the gearshift from the overdrive into third gear. It wasn't necessary for him to do it in an automatic transmission, but he still liked doing it. It just plain felt good to shift it into a lower gear and ramp up the engine. Now if he'd been driving a stick, that would be a different story. He preferred sticks himself, but his father... his father... Scott mumbled at the thud from the passenger floorboard. He thought he'd wedged the paper bag in with the seatbelt pretty well, but he'd also forgotten just how unforgiving the hills and turns of Pike County could be to inanimate objects that resided in his Jeep. So instead he simply grabbed at the brown paper bag and shoved it underneath the seat in order to keep it from slipping around. As his hands closed around the hard, square form inside, Scott had to swallow hard to resist an urge. Part of him wanted to pull over and chug the contents of the bottle down right then and there, but another part of him knew that he had something more important to do with it. He came around a curve slowly, creeping his vehicle through a wrought iron gate that read “Pine Mountain Cemetery”. With soft, almost reverent care he let the automobile edge its way forward. Scott never really did believe in ghosts, but he had monsters. He thought he'd lived with one for the first eighteen years of his life. You could say he was coming to slay that monster... only this would be another journey into the cave to fight that same dragon. With the crumble of gravel under his tires slowing, he brought himself to a stop and swung the door open. This Jeep had been his father's, as had the responsibility of supporting the family. With long, ground-eating strides, Scott's booted feet carried him across the soft grass that rustled softly in the wind. He walked straight through the rows and up the columns, never wanting to cross the graves of those that had gone before. But he stopped cold when he reached his destination. There it was, just as he remembered, carved in light gray granite. A headstone with the American flag etched into it, waving in the wind. Underneath was written the name of his father, his rank of sergeant from the Army and a handful of words. “A loving husband and father, you will be missed.” “Bullshit,” Scott spat, reading the inscription for the next countless time in his life. Ever since that headstone had been sunk, he'd hated it, spat on it. “You know,” he said, addressing the headstone as he bent to put the paper bag down on the ground next to him, “you were a sonofabitch and I always thought so.” With that, Scott jerked his white t-shirt straight and almost seemed to dig his heels into the ground. “And when you died, I knew you were a complete and utter piece of shit.” The big man tossed his head, whipping his long hair out of his face. “You never were satisfied. I'll never miss you for that.” To Scott, every memory seemed as fresh and raw as if it had happened last week even though the most recent was at least four years old and the oldest was as early as he could remember. “I can remember at you telling me to grow up when I was five and got attacked by a dog. I was a kid, scared and bleeding from an arm that was all torn up, but you? You stood there and told me to grow up, quit being such a baby and quit crying. I wanted to hit you back then. But it wouldn't have done any good. I was five years old, you miserable shit, and you couldn't let me cry because I was slashed open for the first time in my life.” Scott jammed his big hands into the pockets of his denim shorts. “It was never enough. Do you know what it was like to grow up with a dad that was never proud of you? That was never satisfied with what you accomplished? I kept my grades up, and you always yelled at me when it was a B instead of an A. I couldn't satisfy you by staying up late to try and study, because I was too stupid to really learn it, wasn't I? That's what that told me. I couldn't be good at football because then I'd be a dumb jock and they never got anywhere.” Scott's eyes were red-rimmed at this point, but he refused to let the tears fall. After that time at five years old, he'd never, ever let his father see him cry again. He'd run off, hidden himself, he'd built up emotional walls in his heart to protect himself. But it had taken him, Scott Matthews, quite a long time to gather the courage to come up here and give his father the beratement he deserved, live or dead. “So high school was sheer hell for me in trying to please you, you disgusting old fuck. It was me working as hard as I could, trying to keep my grades at a three point five and still be a star on the football team. And you told me to get better grades, to quit pussy footing around in games and yelled at me for not having a job. You think about that a moment, if you can from your own little corner of Hell.” Scott took a moment. His voice had wavered, and another trick he had taught himself was to stop talking until he was in control of himself again. If his dad had kept yelling, after he got composed, he'd just yell back. It was easy enough, especially that his father wasn't here to try and yell back. “I led the whole state in sacks, Dad,” Scott managed to spit out in a gravelly voice. “I broke the state record and got into the National Honor Society. I'm not smart, I got in there because my teachers went a little easier on me and because I wouldn't sleep some nights to try to get that garbage done. And it still wasn't enough.” Think about this a moment. A six foot, six inch former football star and wrestler was standing there, airing his dirty laundry to a grave. All around Scott, the beauty of the mountains shone, resplendent in its glorious green leaves, tall trees, wildflowers and waving grass. But Scott paid no mind to the panorama of nature that surrounded him. For him, there was only a maze of anger and hurt that had been years and years in the making. “So what was that, Dad?” Scott continued on, his heart rending at the words. “Why was I never good enough? Why was it that you couldn't just accept your son, and be proud of him? Shit, I should've stood up there and said all this at that funeral of yours, so everyone could know what a sonofabitch you really were. I could stand there and tell everyone that I hated you since I was five years old and that I still did. That I still do.” Scott's hatred was harbored deep and festered without check. Very few things could distract him from that same heartache or rage. Scott's love with Alyssa and his competition in the ring were sometimes enough to keep his mind away from the burning anger and hate he had towards his father. “So what is it now? I'm the one taking care of Mom and Lacey because of you. I never thought I could've hated you more... but you found a way to do it.” Scott shuffled his feet a moment, almost as if setting himself to charge. “You died on that damn track and told your friends to tell me you were proud of me and that they were my responsibility. I can't believe you'd lie like that just to guilt me into taking care of them. So I'm doing better than you ever did.” Scott bent down, pulling a bottle of cheap whiskey He flipped it over, looking at the bottle of amber liquid. “Yeah, I remember you watching guys like Gorilla Monsoon and the Midnight Express on Saturdays. So I got into wrestling because it paid better than anything around here. You'd probably have told me to quit wasting my time.” Scott took a moment, chewing over the next few words. “Well, let me just tell you, I sacrificed more for Mom and Lacey in the last few years than you ever did. I slept in that damn Jeep every night for two months, I sent home as much money as I could for them. I worked hurt, drove tired and went hungry so they'd be comfortable. What did you ever do?” Scott pointed the bottle in his hand towards the gravestone like a weapon. “You would drink and yell at us and berate us. So in the end, between the two of us, who is the failure. They know how much I love them because I do all I can to take care of them. You? You did nothing without acting like it was the end of the world.” With that, Scott pulled the top off the bottle and started to pour it on the ground. After a moment though, he stopped and reared back, slinging with all of his might. The glass hit and shattered against the headstone, leaving it streaked with tiny shards of glass and the glitter of alcohol. “Rot in hell, you bastard,” Scott spat. The gentle rustle of a late summer wind swept through the trees as Scott turned his back and walked away from the grave of a father he hated. The gentle wave of the branches tried to utter the cry of the heart wracked by a man that loved his son, but could never express it. “I'm sorry, Scott.”
