The Girl in the Cardboard Box (PTC Unified Title RP vs. Vivica J. Valentine)

undated
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: Between ReV 156 and ReV 158 Her name was Corrine, and she only took this job because mommy and daddy refused to keep sending her money only to find it going towards tequila shots and dismal grades. She knew she should have privatized her Facebook profile, but how was she to know that her parents knew how to do computer-related wizardry that extended beyond word processing and the occasional pointing and clicking? Now she knows. And, according to the G.I. Joe DVDs she was putting on the shelf, knowing is half the battle. Toys R Us wasn't a terrible job, per-say, but she was told that it was only a matter of time before she started referring to it as "Customer Service Hell." Her coworkers had already been bitten by the Bitter Bug but, since she was still relatively new compared to them, it hadn't found its way to her yet. Shockingly. "Just wait until the slackers come in expecting us to still have pools in stock," Roger, with the perma-sneer and lingering tobacco-breath, had told her two days ago. "You'll want to order one just so you can drown yourself." Ignoring the rather large impossibility of being able to consciously do such a thing in a shallow wading pool, Corrine had politely nodded her head and smiled before walking away with a box of Polly Pocket toys in her arms. She was normally a happy, bubbly person, the whining to her parents about her need to procure employment aside, and was determined not to turn into a Surly Suzie if she could help it. On this auspicious mid-April afternoon, she was back on restocking duty. It wasn't so bad, really. She didn't even mind stopping what she was doing to help a wayward customer find something, except she had a habit of forgetting the store layout and ended up leading people in the wrong direction. Thankfully, any potential tirades were diffused by a, "I'm awfully sorry! I'm still new" statement and a widening of her emerald green eyes. Either the warm weather was making people less irritable, or Bostonians were more understanding than she had thought, because her pleas for mercy and pity hadn't failed her yet. "There. Last one," she murmured to herself and placed the final DVD with its mates. She stepped back to admire her handiwork, then stepped forward again to straighten one of the movies out; it was crooked. With a smile, she stooped to snatch the box off the floor and marched proudly out of the aisle and toward the stock room to find another box to work on. No sooner had she passed the Harry Potter section when she noticed a woman staring intriguingly at a long shelf of learning aids. She had to have been the tallest woman Corrine had ever seen, and that was counting the center on the women's basketball team who she had chemistry lab with. The woman looked frustrated, sighing and running her fingers through her curly brown hair, the red and auburn streaks catching in the light. Corrine approached from the left, body twisting to hopefully catch the customer's eye before she was right on top of her. "Excuse me. Can I help you with something?" Lindsay Troy sighed again, placed her hands on her hips and let her eyes roam the shelf in front of her. "Yeah. Well, maybe." Corrine placed the empty box on the floor again and folded her hands in front of her. Ready to assist. "My niece's birthday is coming up soon. She'll be three," Lindsay started, still looking at the merchandise. "I don't get to see her often since my sister lives in Texas and I travel a lot. Her other two birthdays, I got her the things she needed, y'know? Clothes, shoes. Couple of stuffed animals. But the last time I was there, she recited the whole alphabet to me. Numbers up to a hundred, too. Mispronounced a lot of them, though." She shook her head in amusement. Corrine smiled at the image of a tiny child fumbling over the words. "So I was thinking, maybe I should get her some of those learning toys. Help stimulate her brain. I just don't know what would be appropriate. She seems to be ahead of the curve." Lindsay turned her head to look at Corrine. "Think you could help?" The smile left Corrine's face then. Her head recoiled back slightly and a gasp, unable to be concealed, escaped from her mouth. Underneath the woman's right eye was a large red, and slightly flaky, mark. She covered her mouth, partly in embarrassment at not curbing her reaction and partly because the wound looked really painful. Troy frowned and sighed again. She had been doing a lot of both lately. "It's really not as bad as it looks," she commented, brows furrowed at having to repeat that line for what seemed like the thousandth time. "I'm sorry," Corrine stammered, composure coming back around. "That was awfully rude of me. And I shouldn't stare..." She purposely made her eyes look away. "You wouldn't be the first one," was the blunt retort. "Look, this," Troy motioned to the burn on her face, "is nothing. It's healing. It'll be a faded memory soon enough. And it's not like it's never happened before." "You've had marks like that before? How? What happened?" Lindsay smirked then, which caught Corrine even more off-guard. "You're rather quizzical." "Oh. Um. I'm s-" "It's fine," Troy reassured, the rest of the apology waved off. "I really would like to get something for my niece, though. If it's all the same to you." "Right. Right, of course. Well," Corrine started again and confidently lifted her eyes to meet Troy's. "I don't have any younger siblings or cousins or anything, but I've heard that the Leapfrog and the Clickstart stuff are both really good from other customers." She stepped around Troy, walked a little ways down the aisle, then motioned for Lindsay to follow. "There's this table thing," a finger pointed to a Learn & Groove Musical Table, "but if she's ahead of the curve like you said, you might want to get her something a little more intermediate. How much are you looking to spend, if you don't mind my asking?" "Doesn't matter." "Okay. Well, there's this Clickstart First Computer. There's a bunch of different software you can buy to go along with it. It's ages three to six, and I think if she knows her alphabet and numbers already then it'd be a good companion tool for her." Lindsay pulled a Toy Story: To 100 and Beyond cartridge off the hook and started to read the back. "Yeah. I think this'll be good for her. My sister can help her get the hang of it." She turned to look at Corrine again. "Thanks for the suggestion." "Oh, anytime. That's why I'm here!" she smiled, a genuinely sunny sort of thing that matched her bright blonde hair. "If you need anything else, my name's Corrine. I'll be around the store." With a wave that she would later think was awkward, Corrine walked back to collect the empty cardboard box and continued on to her original destination. Lindsay smiled to herself, collected a computer and a couple more cartridges, and then walked towards the checkout line. She didn't understand the feeling she got that made her give pause on her journey to the front, but as she passed an aisle or two of action figures, she made a slight detour. What she will eventually tell her sister was that she felt a slight twinge of curiosity and decided to follow it instead of leaving like she had planned. She normally wouldn't go looking for herself amongst the wrestling figurines that toy companies put out of wrestlers, even though she always got complimentary ones before they were produced for mass-market. Something about seeing a Mini-Her made her feel almost self-conscious and slightly critical of the toy portrayal. Perfectionism at its finest. But there she was, standing in the aisle, eyes meeting the painted-on ones in the last cardboard home that could be found. A smirk dared her to return one in kind. Lindsay obliged her tiny counterpart without much hesitation and plucked the box off the shelf, turned it over and started to read the narrative, much like she had done with the gifts for Cecilia. Chuckles at the description, boastful as if she had written it herself, poured forth from her mouth and her eyes eventually wandered over to the right, where a list of accomplishments proudly burst forth in gold type against the black of the box. Her smirk softened to a smile as she read down the list, but her eyebrows eventually returned to a furrowed state. The toy, in all its brilliance at representing her accurately looks-wise, was outdated. "Universal champ," she muttered, not trying to hide the dejection her words put forth. "But still the Unified champ," came the tiny optimistic voice in her head; the one she often buried. Optimism should not be confused with cockiness. Lindsay turned the box back over and noticed for the first time the two titles that rested at her plastic feet; titles that begged to be put around her waist. She lifted her eyes roofward, felt them close as florescent lights shined down. "Perhaps not for long," her physical voice stated. Eyes eventually returned back to the toy in her hand. Thoughts wandered back throughout the years. The Queen, who had never lost a same-gendered, one-on-one fight. The Queen, who dominated the ranks and files of men who marched toward her. One loss. It only took one loss to a woman, if Cozen really was a woman, to question twelve years of experience; to question the joyous exaltations of victory, the bitterness of defeats, the lessons learned, the titles won and the opponents vanquished. "Am I still the Queen?" Mini-Troy held no answers. She just smirked, cockily. Confidently. The way she always did, no matter what. Not one to be put on a shelf for long, not in real life and most assuredly not in Toyland. But back she went to her metal platform to wait for someone with unshaken confidence to whisk her away from this temporary kingdom, while Troy's own conflicted feet took her further down the aisle, back to the store proper. "One loss does not unmake a Queen." Lindsay's face contorted into granite as her sneakers padded against the floor. "The path to vindication passes through a Heart. Diamonds do not crack so easy." "No allusions," she scolded herself and held the presents closer to her body. She quickened her pace, now in a hurry to leave. She almost made it out of the aisle without further introspection when a bin at the end of the row now called her name. Labeled 'discounted', the container held inside its gaping maw a rare treasure trove of classic, well-known figurines. In mid-step, Troy gave pause and cast her glare downward. Clinton Sage sneered back at her. Rich Rollins, red bandanna permanently glued to his head, smirked self-assuredly. A talking Jason Snow, in his 'Try Me!' packaging, smugly mugged for unseen cameras. And Tchu, stoic as he ever was in person, saw right through her. "This can't be right. Matt hasn't been gone that long." Lindsay picked up the Tchu figure. Sure enough, there was a 'sale' sticker plastered on the front. "One loss does not unmake a Queen. And if you give up before your time-" There was a long, arduous pause before the realization hit. "I'll end up right here." She peered back into the bin, at the careers with cheap price tags on them, deemed by people who never had one to be unworthy. Is a legacy left behind so inconsequential that it can be tossed aside to make room for a newer guard? Accomplishments disregarded because they are not garnered in the present and slip-ups being the precursor to professional damnation? "The path to vindication passes through a Heart. Diamonds do not crack so easy. A fall does not mean vanquishment." This Queen would not abdicate her throne. Not for a new guard. Not for an impostor. A champion she was. "A champion I'll remain." The smirk slowly crept back along her mouth. Legacies weren't meant to be forgotten, as long as they were worth remembering. For all of her faults, all of her misgivings, and all of her mistakes, Lindsay Troy would not be forgotten. Powerful rulers stood the test of time, even if they stumbled. Their setbacks gave way to skepticism; not from themselves, however. For powerful rulers, even in defeat, never lost confidence completely. But a chink in the armor always seemed to open the door to misgivings. It offered opponents and would-be usurpers the confidence they assumed a ruler had lost. When a new opponent rose, however, they would learn that the most powerful of rulers would never waver. Never fall. That it took more than single revolutionary to topple an empire. More than a single voice to end a legacy. Vivica would come with confidence. She would come with bravado and courage. She would come thinking that she would be the one to further break the chink in the armor. But she would learn, as so many others have learned, that rulers do not fall so easy. Stumble, yes. But never fall. She would learn. As they all must learn.
