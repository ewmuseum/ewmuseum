A 'How To' List

17 February 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2/17/06 Their story, our story, can wait for now… or perhaps it’s here, between the lines. It all depends on whether you subscribe to the notion that there can be more to a story than just the words it’s composed of; it all depends on just how deep you’re pushed to read. And isn’t that the way it is with all literature? A story is only as deep as it persuades its reader to dive. Maybe that’s a good place to start. How to write something. Anything. A short story, a novel, an RP… if you’re into that goofy e-fed thing. I: How to Write a Story There are a lot of different types of writing… poetry, novels, short stories, roleplays, prose-poetry… and they all have their own unique style and substance. For instance, poetry gets all crazy and entirely metaphoric and usually doesn’t make one damn bit of sense to anyone who doesn’t love and frequent the world of poets. As George Carlin once said, “more people write poetry than read it”. Short stories and novels generally lack the philosophical ambiguous nature of poetry. They’re more direct in presenting their message, though it’s still often necessary to read between the lines and understand that not everything should be taken in the literal sense. Short stories move at a rather quick pace… they frequently focus on a narrower concept. The characters and plot have to develop more quickly than in a novel where the juice of the story can be slowly consumed. A short story heats things up at a fast pace, a novel allows themes and characters to simmer. And then there are those roleplays… better known as RPs in the e-fed world. Competitive short stories… almost like chapters to a novel, presenting the prestige and perils in the life of a character who moonlights as a pro wrestler, ultimate fighter etc. Here, the writing is usually more straight forward, closer to the style of short stories than perhaps any other type of writing, though each RP usually builds on the previous, making a collection that forms a much larger story. Typical RP techniques include the examination of the main character’s thoughts as they pertain to wrestling, some trash-talking of the nemesis, declarations of the importance of winning a match (both in-character and out-of-character.) The most recent trend in RPing is to write roleplays that trash and spit on all these often (and perhaps over) used techniques. In fact, ripping on the clichéd aspects of RPs has become more overdone and clichéd than just about anything those people choose to rag on… but who cares, it’s fun to rip on other people’s habits and styles, right? I guess that’s where the whole “life is a circle” idea came from… eventually, everything turns on itself (rolls, betrays, turn… whatever). The old and clichéd becomes novel and exciting, and the new and cutting edge becomes lame and tired, and the process begins again. Kids grow up going to school, hating class and wanting to get a real job so they can make money. Then those kids evolve into adults and get jobs and immediately start counting down the days till they can retire. Then old age rolls around and brings retirement with it, and six empty and lost months later, a tired old soul decides it wants to get a real job and not waste the day away (in some professions that begat early ends to careers, the retire/unretire phenomenon is repeated numerous times and the circle rolls around on itself at a much faster pace See: Michael Jordan, Terry Funk). Anyhow… Many things go into telling a good story… many more when it comes to writing one. All these different pieces to the puzzle can have a life of their own. But none of these pieces matter if you don’t have a general idea what the puzzle is supposed to look like. Is it a winter scene? A bouquet of flowers with its brilliant collage of colors contrasted by the purity of the white cloth it rests upon? An old man, sitting in an even older rowboat with grandson on knee holding a cane pole, its string making ripples in the pond. Has this metaphor run too long and lost its meaning? Probably. The point is, before you start to put everything together, having a general theme is a good idea. a: How to Develop a Theme Any college professor or published author will say that one key to being a good writer is to read a lot of good writing. They’ll probably explain it as a way to soak up all sorts of little techniques that will spice up your own work. As if reading a thousand short stories and a hundred novels and dozens of RPs will allow you to marinate in good writing technique and take on the properties of the marinade yourself (of course this only works if you read the right things… a bad marinade will make your story taste like shit). There is, however, another obvious, though less often spoken, reason for reading the work of many other writers, and that is simply as a means to find good ideas. The work of other individuals can be a gold mine for potential themes and characters and so on. That’s not to say that thievery is condoned in the world of literature, but…inspiration… well, there’s nothing wrong with a little bit of that. Some of the most amazing stories, characters, and events of all time have come from other stories, characters, and events in literature. There’s nothing wrong with taking an idea in someone else’s writing and using it as a spark to create something new or different, or bigger (from one Matt to another, thanks for those couple short inspirational paragraphs on funny RP habits in that GAN roleplay). Even Shakespeare adapted his plays from the works of other writers. His classic play Hamlet was born from a short story written by another author. Shakespeare merely took the short story, expanded on it, deepened it, and added a few new plot devices (such as Hamlet’s hesitation). If Shakespeare can not only get away with it, but be considered a God for it… then it can’t be that big of a deal. It’s not stealing, it’s just taking an idea and making it your own, so it’s cool to do it, and its fine to fess up to it as well. Whatever you do, don’t lie. Lying just gets you in trouble. In fact, all lying will do is make things fall to pieces all around you and cause your audience to walk out on you to go live with its parents. Once you’ve got your main idea for a story, however you might have come up with it, you basically have your theme. It helps to make your theme clear from the beginning, but its also import that the theme remains the focus of the story, and ties together in crystal clear form at the conclusion. If you start off by stating how wonderfully delightful mashed potatoes are, then halfway through your tale find yourself explaining why carrots, motor oil, and clothespins are an erotic combination, then you’re probably going to fail to deliver on your theme (and you’re also probably going to give your audience the creeps). The theme is more or less the backbone of your story, and it needs to connect the two ends and hold everything together. It’s like a promise. Don’t break it. If you keep to your theme or you promise, all is well. If you break them, everything gets messy… it’s just a matter of whether your story becomes a mess, or your life. b: How to Develop Characters Creating strong characters is crucial to convincing the reader to believe in the story being heaved their way. Toss them something too dull or terribly disgusting or just generally not worth relating too… and they’ll heave it right back. Characters need to be strong personalities. Not strong in the sense that they curse a lot and do all kinds of crazy and creepy shit so that the reader sees the character whenever they let their eyes rest, and sees the figure’s face in their bowl of cereal and all those other clichés (oh yeah… always try and avoid clichés), but instead, strong in the sense that the reader can relate to the character or gain something from the character’s thoughts, opinions and actions. You want to make sure that the characters are memorable, but have a realness to them so that the audience has things about their life and beliefs that they can relate specifically to something about the substance of the characters. Unless you’re writing some psycho horror stuff, you don’t want your reader to be having nightmares as their way of recalling the character. Ten years down the road, the reader should remember a character because they connected with them or found in them an entertainment that has stuck with them through the test of time… a decade down the road they shouldn’t remember a character for being too bizarre to understand or too lame to allow time to heal the pain caused by their dullness. The trick lies in depth. One dimension gets very old very fast. A character should be like fine wine, getting better all the time, or however that saying goes. What it shouldn’t be like is a keg party. All great at first… “Woohoo! I’m wasted! This rocks!” but then the audience gets a little more of the same, and a little more and a little more and the next thing you know, your reader has their head in the toilet throwing up all the garbage they were just fed and vowing “damn, I’m never gonna read anymore of that character again!”. Having a defining quality or trait for a character is fine… it’s a good thing; however, not having any conflicting qualities to their personality is just…well…that gets back to the puking in a toilet thing, leaving the reader feeling sick from too much of one trait. You’ve got to be careful that your character doesn’t become so obsessed with one thing or one way to live, that they grow shallow and unentertaining. Obsession is blind to everything around it, including those closest to the obsessed. The reader means so much to you, like a soul mate. They are everything that holds your world together and makes it matter. It isn’t worth it to lose the one who means the most to you because of some volatile obsession with wrestling. Some people like to do something a little different with their characters… something that pertains to, or possesses a more personal nature and put themselves on paper (or computer screens thanks to advancements in technology… but that doesn’t fit as well with the alliteration as ‘paper’). Don’t be afraid to put yourself in your characters, it can be a very powerful concept. Doing so certainly doesn’t display a lack of creativity… sometimes, the hardest thing to do is find a creative way to write yourself in a fictional light. Using your own personality or appearance isn’t a shameful technique. Some idiots don’t even bother to change the name of their character from their own… ……… …Anyways, since pen and paper were invented (maybe even as far back as stone tabs and chisels and all that Ten Commandments stuff) people have been using writing as a way to get their feelings out of their mind and into the great wide open. There are those who say writing can be thoroughly therapeutic. Sometimes instead of standing toe to toe with the turmoil, its easier to face the problem with a computer or a book or a squared circle or an office or a pad of paper and a pencil; but, if you’re gonna use writing or whatever to confront your problems, you may want to use it to actually go ahead and work through the problems instead of just complaining about them for others to hear. Putting yourself in your characters can be an exhilarating write and an exciting ride… not too mention the fact that it can lead to an epiphany or two for those involved. What it shouldn’t lead to is a confining non-fiction prison. Just because you may put yourself into a character, doesn’t mean that the character then has to say and do everything that you would do in the situation. It’s also not necessary to put the creation into situations that have only been experienced already in your time on earth. Fiction offers freedom that no other event or entity in life can provide. Forgetting or fearing that freedom is tragic and criminal; and the greatest loss a man can suffer is failure to find self-control in the face of that freedom. It’s when we fall victim to our own inability to handle the liberties all around us that success has truly passed by. No man can be blamed for the oppression forced upon him, but becoming a slave to free will is total defeat. When one thing controls your life, the way you live, the way you love… or write… you’re the product of your own oppression. Don’t let that happen…ya know…with writing, I mean. c: How to Say It: The Prose Thing What you say is not usually nearly as important as how you say it. Yeah, that sounds stupid, but that’s just because I didn’t say it cool enough, which further proves the point. What you say often pails in comparison to the majestic grace and raw power with which you proclaim it to the reader. Way cooler, huh? Hopefully more effective as well. If your story reads like its being narrated by a robot, then no one will give a damn what you say. The language has to be enticing enough to persuade the reader to stay entirely involved. If the words you use don’t contain an alluring quality, the reader won’t have any vested interest in the tale being told. Prose is the key to this, the words, the way their used, and the style and sound they create. Variety is crucial to keeping the flow of a story from feeling stunted, broken, or robotic. Using short simple sentences from start to finish, with the same basic words over and over… it ends up sounding like those Dick & Jane, “See Spot. See Spot Run. Spot runs Far” books that teach children how to read… that’s not good (well, unless your writing a children’s book to teach kids to read, in which case, god bless you for that act of kindness). A pleasant mix of short and simpler sentences, combined with longer more complex sentences can go quite a way towards keeping a smooth and pleasing flow to any story. Sprinkle in an extensive vocabulary and you can create a poetic sounding story that contains the best of the poetry and fiction worlds. Don’t go crazy with the big words, though. The audience shouldn’t have to travel through a story with a dictionary tucked firmly on their lap to help make any sense of what they’re reading . Twenty big words in one paragraph takes your story from poetic to pompous and kills the flow by causing the reader to have to pause every other sentence to look up the definition of a word. A strong command of the use of vocabulary is impressive, but using every large and rare word you know doesn’t show a command of anything. It’s just Overkill, and that’s only good as a supershow on a Sunday night. (bonus points for a future PPV plug in an RP!). The language of a story also has to establish tone. Without proper tone, a reader doesn’t know how to interpret many of the thoughts and actions they come across as they read. In real life, we have the luxury of easily understood tones. Sitting down to talk with your friend or co-worker, your tone can be understood through… well… tone of voice. In the world of literature, we can’t use actual inflections of the voice, so tone has to be portrayed through careful word choice and placement. Sure, you can express a few emotions in writing without this careful choice. Anger can be expressed through lots of use of capitol letters and an overabuncance of “!”… BUT GODDAMN THAT’S ANNOYING!!! Instead, choosing the right words at the right time can do wonders in conveying the tone of the narrator or story. If you want to display a tone of depression, soft and sad words will do the trick. Don’t use “misplaced” just to sound smarter when “lost” will do the trick and holds more of a sorrowful sound. For that matter, use the right sounds in words. Long ‘O’s fit with sad tone… while short, hard vowel sounds can convey a tone of rage. Words with short vowels and hard consonants can express a strong voice of anger or frustration. Don’t be afraid to use the right sounding words just because they’re simple or common. Words often sound like the emotion behind them. “Sorrow” and “gloom” sound sad and depressing. “Hate” and “fight” sound rough, crude, and angry… just like their definitions imply. It’s the one thing about the English language that actually does make some sense. Saying the wrong thing can ruin it all. You have to be able to express how you feel and do it the right way. If you can’t talk to your reader about your feelings and beliefs, then your marriage is bound to fail. You have to find a way to express yourself, and in the right voice. Telling them you’ve changed your mind about something they had grown to love about you is one thing… telling them in an unapologetic tone, “Sorry, its who I am. Don’t you get it?” is a whole ‘nother world of hurt. d: How to Make Use of Symbolism and Stuff Symbolism can be a striking tool for use in improving any story. No one is exactly sure why, but there’s just something awesome about using one thing to symbolize another. Perhaps it shows stunning creativity and masterful grasp of language. The best use of symbolism, however, isn’t just to say one thing when you mean another for the sake of being cryptic or cool… symbolism should allow you to say one thing and get you’re point across about it, while also making a point about something else. It’s a way to tackle two topics in a single phrase. There should be a relevance to what you’re really saying and what is being said between the lines. Sometimes symbolism can come in a short idea or sole sentence, sometimes… entire pieces of literature are symbols of something else, holding two ideas… one on their surface another in the core. It’s vital to remember that things can look one way on the surface… and stand for that idea… but still look and feel very different underneath. A lot of bad things can come from the simple inability to show not only what’s on the surface, but what’s at your core. A lot of bad things… * * *Enough with the writing… or whatever that was about. I don’t know why I chose to start a ‘How to’ list with writing. Probably because I can’t cook for shit, so a ‘How to Bake a Cake’ topic wouldn’t have done a bit of good. I’m also not very mechanically inclined, so ‘How to Fix Your Automobile’ would have looked something like… If the little needle points to the big ‘E’ then locate your wallet and the nearest gas station. If anything else is wrong (i.e brakes, tires, engine) then you’re in trouble. I suppose writing was the one hobby I felt like I knew something about… not a lot… just something… words to throw out there, at least. Writing, I guess, just kinda struck me as relevant… prevalent to a lot of things. Everything. When you write, you write what you know. That’s all there is to it. When you live, you live what you know. That’s all there can be… unless you don’t know who you are or what you believe or what you need. In that case, you get your brains kicked in a couple nights a week in order to earn a nice paycheck and have the opportunity to fail in front of a million onlookers. And you listen to all those who claim to be better than you, and you let it boil your blood, and you try to defy their claims and rise above them…defeat them… but you have no idea what for. That’s what you do when you don’t know. Writing, like other things, can be an escape, a place to avoid the truth. It can consume you and quickly fester into addiction, at which point, the probability of recovery is minute. Still, writing isn’t everything, nothing is everything. Still, there are other things out there, in here… more important, grander things… and it probably wouldn’t hurt to add a few of them onto this list. II: How to Discover the Meaning of Life Keep your ears ready, but don’t push them against any doors. Keep your eyes open, but don’t look. Get out and walk around a lot… and who knows… you might just get lucky and step in it and ruin your shoes. III: How to Watch Success Collapse Around You First, you have to have some success, so… a: How to Gain Some Measure of Success Make a touching and beautiful promise. Say all the right things. Find a powerful hobby. Create a beautiful story where you defy odds, fight through tragedy, and overcome the King of Kings b: How to Lose Everything Break your theme. Use the wrong tone or word choice. Gain an unhealthy obsession. Become a slave of your own free-will. IV: How to Live a Good Life, Be a Good Man, Take Care of Your Family, Be successful and Just Genuinely Happy Stick with writing. It’s a lot easier.
