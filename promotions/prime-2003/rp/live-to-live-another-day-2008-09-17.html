Live To Live Another Day

17 September 2008
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 9/17/08 "I had seen birth and death but had thought they were different." -T.S. Eliot September 14th, 2008 2:12 P.M. The cemetery wasn’t eerie or spooky. There were no phantoms in the mist, no murky sprites, no mausoleum doors creaking open in a dire wind. It was easy to forget the legions of cadavers underfoot, bodies turned to mush and bone. It was a beautiful fucking day. “Chan, don’t walk so fast.” My mother puttered along. The black silk of her blouse twirled in the lazy wind. Emblazoned on the blouse’s right shoulder was a tall white crane, and with the motion of the fabric, it seemed to hover on the breeze. Her long black skirt put her right at home amongst the scant mourners we passed. I guessed that being all dressed up gave her the courage to confront this moment. “Sorry, Ma.” I backtracked, offering her my arm. She shook her head, champagne cheeks puffed up with indignation. As long as she could, she would walk alone. “Too good to be seen on the arm of your son?” “I can walk fine, Chan.” She never hesitated at using my name for emphasis. Chan this, Chan that, as if I constantly needed a reminder that I had her attention. In the early afternoon sun, vivacious green sparkled everywhere. In the lush grass, the headstones seemed all the more lifeless. Life persevered, even where death had laid its claim. “No Vietnamese buried here,” she said, after passing by the umpteenth Robinson grave. “You haven’t been Vietnamese in decades,” I scolded. She folded her arms unhappily, twisted frown tattooed to her face. “What did you tell me on Ellis Island Day in fourth grade?” The slightest shiver in her pupil told me that she knew, but that, in her sulking, she wouldn’t answer. “That we’re all Americans?” She was studying all that remained of one Wesley Dillard, born 1918, died 2001. “That all it takes to be American is courage and determination? That you and I were just like those people at Ellis Island?” “We never go to Ellis Island. I never use those big words, either.” Today was a bad day. Today, even for me, she couldn’t put on a smile. This place was a grim admission that the disease would come for her soon, that her memories would cease to be. Conversely, I was soaking in the aftereffects of three lunchtime Tylox pills. I told myself that they were for her, so that I might balance out her melancholy. But she was immune to cheer, and it only made me feel worse for continuing my addiction in her name. “No, we didn’t go to Ellis Island. But between you and me, Ma, I’ll take the San Diego weather any day.” Not even the ghost of a smile. “So now you wanna go back to Vietnam? We had a nice twenty-five year vacation, but now it’s time to head on home?” “No, no, no.” I stopped, thinking she might do the same. But she kept her pace, short, sturdy legs never faltering. Now that I was looking at her feet, I noticed that she was wearing the black Armani flats I’d gotten her for her birthday. “You wore the shoes!” She stopped and looked down, as though this was a surprise to her. “Have to wear black shoes with black skirt,” she recited, before she picked up her businesslike gait once more. “You look beautiful,” I said, jogging to catch up. She gave me a sour sideways look, then continued to survey the myriad graves. Two weeks ago, she’d been joyous to have me around every day. Things were so good that I’d almost stepped away from PRIME. Having felt the redemptive power of the Universal Title, it could’ve been time to step away and share with my mother her last few lucid months. But things had played out in the funny way they usually do. I was at the forefront of PRIME’s collision with Sin City, and my mother was as miserable as I’d ever seen her. “Are you still talking to Reverend Spencer?” “Not for two weeks,” she replied matter-of-factly. “Ma, you need someone to talk to. Someone who knows how to deal with what you’re feeling. And if you trust Reverend Spencer with your faith…” There was an argument to be made here, but today didn’t seem like the day that facts alone would persuade the woman from whom I’d inherited all my stubbornness. “He talks about the pain. And what I want from God.” The moments where my mother discussed religion with me were few and far between. When, at age fourteen, I’d rejected the church as an institution, she’d grounded me for a month. And every Sunday morning, from then until I left the house, she’d leave the Sunday service program on my nightstand. It wasn’t insistent, just a reminder. Now, I was weirded out by her sudden candor. “And…what do you want from God?” “Now Chan has a God?” Her tone was accusatory. But she’d finally opened her mouth about something that mattered, something about what she was about to face. And it would’ve been pure cowardice to turn away. “I’ve always been ready to believe that there is a higher order. Some…thing inherently better than us. I just don’t subscribe to the idea that when it showed up on Earth, it wore a toga and sandals.” “Christ redeems.” “You didn’t answer my question. You’ve been going to church all my life, so what is God gonna do to pay you back for all those years of piety?” The wind died down as we kept walking. It was a long time before she wanted to say anything. “Nothing,” she whispered, her words sitting in the still air. “Nothing.” “Yes, nothing,” she repeated, with conviction. “You want God to sit back and watch you die?” It came out louder than I’d intended it to be. My mother closed her eyes and stretched out one arm at the shoulder, catching the wind as it picked back up. “Not so angry, Chan.” I was angry. Angry that genetics or bad luck or fate or God had descended upon my mother with this disease. Angry that she could look me in the eye and confess how perfectly acceptable her own death was. Angry because I couldn’t feel any other way. “I can’t…” The words were hard to string together. “What do you want?” /~/~/~/~/~/ September 15th, 2008 5:36 P.M. “I want you to be the godfather, bruh.” “Wait…huh?” At my side was Hawaii’s finest export since grass skirts, Bryan Dawkins. The remnants of Ike were crawling up the Eastern seaboard, which had left us stranded in the Philly airport. And despite the hiccup in itinerary, happy hour was happening right on schedule. We were seated at two adjacent stools, The young fella’s second pineapple and vodka was nearly extinguished and a brand new Tanqueray and Canada Dry, still fizzling, was safely in my right hand. “I said I want you to be the godfather.” “Of soul?” “Of my son, bruh.” The request had me speechless. And it wasn’t naïve, wayward Dawkins who was asking. In his eyes, I saw a confident, poised young man, ready and willing to take on this newest challenge. Quite a change from the first time I’d seen him, when Dawkins had been a blur of perplexed inexperience. “You sure there’s not some buddy from the Big Island who should be doing this?” There was too much burden here, too much involvement. My rule had always been to stay detached, to let the ring be a place outside of life. It was awful to think that my first response was to measure how it might violate my privacy. “No, bruh. This is all you,” he beamed. All I could do was wonder why Dawkins didn’t have a better candidate. “Unless you’re saying no…” “No, no, that’s not it.” I scrambled to stall Dawkins, and another lie easily slipped out, nearly on its own. “It’s just that kids and Tsonda go together like booze and driving. Not a good mix,” I asserted, taking a long sip on my drink to avoid the questions for a moment. “Well, ya get a clean slate with this little guy. I’m sure you’ll do fine, bruh. Not like I’m an expert,” Dawkins laughed. Every time he talked about his forthcoming progeny, a juvenile grin came to dominate his expression. “And I haven’t told Nikkie about this, so keep it a secret.” “Nikkie doesn’t know?” “Not yet. I wanted to double check with ya before I bothered her about it.” “Bothered her?” “Bruh, it’s kinda annoying when you just repeat what I say in question form.” I took down the rest of my drink, which had a pitifully small gin-to-ginger ale ratio. I’d learned my lesson before: the Tylox had to stay in checked luggage, but that meant I’d been without it for several hours. Booze was the next good substitute. “Sorry, kid, I’m just not totally clear on what the deal is. I mean, what does a godfather do?” Dawkins had come prepared. He launched into a description that was right off the Godfathering For Idiots Guide. “Godparents are just part of the family, bruh. You’ll be part of the little guy’s life, and once in a while, if Nik and I need to have a night out, we’ll enlist Uncle Chan for babysitting duty.” He was still beaming. “Excellent. All the benefits of being a single mother, without the hassle of lactating.” “Maybe havin’ a kid around’ll stop you from being such a grouch,” Dawkins laughed. I didn’t. “And one of Nik’s friends will be the little dude’s godmother…” There was a long pause, and stone cold seriousness washed Dawkins’s face of its youthful exuberance. “And the most important thing is that, if anything were to ever happen to Nikkie and me, the godparents would be there to raise him.” I quickly turned back towards the bar. “Gin. Double shot. Canada Dry. Ice.” I didn’t turn back towards Dawkins until the drink was in hand. “To be honest, Dawkins, I don’t think you want your bundle of joy anywhere near me.” “I do, bruh. Thought about it long and hard.” “Listen, kid–” “No, you listen, bruh!” Dawkins was highly animated, waving his hands as he laid into me. “I come in here and try to invite you into my son’s life, and you just wanna throw yourself a pity party. Tell ya what, Chan, if you wanna be left alone, all ya gotta do is say the word. You and your gin look like you’re havin’ a fine time on your own.” Dawkins couldn’t have looked more disappointed, brow furrowed as he sighed and shook his head at me. To put an exclamation point on the sequence, he tossed down two twenties. “Enjoy.” I rubbed my hand wearily over my forehead. I wasn’t in my right mind, not with the pain creeping back in. With my left hand, I lifted the clear drink, taking it to the dome. But in my gusto for liquor, I’d forgotten my own limitations. Suppressing my gag reflex, I could barely choke it down. As much as I liked to wallow in self-loathing, I’d never reach the level of being able to stomach straight booze. Chaser or bust. With the drink gone, and my stomach swirling unpleasantly, I dropped my own cash on the bar, and headed for the gate. /~/~/~/~/~/ September 14th, 2008 2:19 P.M. “What do you want?” “So many things,” my mother replied immediately. “A cure. New roof for the church. You find a nice girl, settle down, grandchildren. More garden time. Many things,” she repeated. “So why don’t we go home and garden? My flight’s not until tomorrow, and we’ve got at least five hours of sunlight left.” Three of those things were more or less out of my control. But gardening? That wish I could grant. “I need a…” Her long pencil-thin finger tickled her bottom lip, which meant she was thinking. My own tendency to scratch my chin whilst pondering was some evolutionary cousin of her own thinker’s tic. “Plot,” she stated flatly, having come up with the right word. “Some other time.” I put my arm around her narrow shoulders, and though she didn’t recoil, I felt how powerfully uncomfortable she was. “I need a plot for being buried, Chan.” She’d always lived for me, and for a glorious afterlife. Religion was tailor-made for people like her: no formal education, few career prospects, stuck between a home she’d abandoned and a society she’d never call home. I’d seen no sense in all of it until the diagnosis. But now, in ways I wouldn’t admit to anyone, I was glad for the hypnosis of religion. It gave her a way to cope. “Ma…” The words stepped lightly off the edge of my tongue, reluctant to go. “I’m no good at talking about this stuff. It makes my skin crawl.” “If not now, Chan, when?” “I dunno.” “Not enough time,” she nodded. Finally, a slit of a smile. The realization, I imagined, that none of us have ample time to live in tomorrow’s dreams. A strange moment to see happiness flash across her face. “We should garden,” she said, nodding her head. On another day, when hues of gray dotted the sky, and the iron arms of winter grabbed us closely, we’d find my mother a burial plot. We would feel all the feelings that one is supposed to in a cemetery. Today, though? Today, we would go home and be among the living. /~/~/~/~/~/ September 15th, 2008 6:58 P.M. When I walked onto the plane, Dawkins was already sitting in our first class seats, perusing the in-flight magazine. My entrance was greeted with searing indifference, not even a head nod. I plopped down in the seat next to him, wondering how many times Dawkins could forgive my incurable inclination to shy away from people. “Hey,” I started, with no idea how I might make amends. “I…uhhh…” Dawkins stayed shielded behind the magazine, unwilling to give an inch after the way I’d behaved. “Sorry.” He lowered the magazine, peering over at me. His eyes stayed trained on me until my cheeks burned with embarrassment at not having more to say. “I know, bruh.” “If the offer still stands, I’d like to…be the lingering bad influence on your kid.” Dawkins’s face brightened, smile beginning to stretch across his cheeks. “I already talked to my seedy connect. He says he can have a fake ID ready by Nikkie’s due date.” “So now I gotta worry about the evils of the world and Uncle Chan?” “S’pose so.” It was good to feel free for a minute. “I mean, somebody’s gotta make sure he turns into a third-generation wrestler. Hundred bucks says I can teach him to moonsault before you can teach him to walk.” “Got a feeling Nik’s gonna keep him as far from the ring as possible.” “Smart cookie.” Dawkins chuckled and turned back to the magazine. I leaned back against the seat; my BAC would ease me to sleep. I didn’t think about back pain. I didn’t think about waking up to the hunger for Tylox. I didn’t think about the mounting pressure to be PRIME’s face at Cataclysm. I thought about whether I knew any lullabies.
