Hero for An Hour or Showing Heart.

16 January 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 16/1/06 Unless you knew what you were looking for, you'd miss it. Hidden and nestled in an alley on the western side of downtown Cleveland, lies a dirty, non-descript iron door with a disgustingly tarnished, formerly copper 'B' emblazened on the top panel. It wasn't strange to see older men walking down the alley, completely unafraid of any sort of attack like you'd see in New York City. Cleveland wasn't a city anyone actually lived in. More or less, it was office buildings, shopping centers and more offices, filled with people who merely lived from day to day. If you were to open the door, you still wouldn't be impressed with what lay behind it: a dirty floor, a few tables and a bar. And yet, this was Cleveland's oldest club. Formerly a speakeasy during the days of prohibition, it's still remained open and closed to most of the general public since then. A few people who've heard the legend of 'The Boar's Nest' have wandered inside and been welcomed by the regulars, but, for the most part, it was an untouched relic since the early 1960's. Most of the people who frequented the place, came because their father did, and their fathers before them. Steelworkers and salt miners by trade, they were gritty, blue collar men. Their views on the world didn't pace with the views of society. It wasn't strange to hear a vile display of racial ephitets spewed with little remorse from the speaker, and even less from those who heard it. Women were still broads, except for their wives and mothers, and life was simply from a different time. It was inside this very bar that Chet Worth had his first taste of alcohol. He was fourteen, and his father brought him to celebrate his fourteenth birthday. A double shot of whiskey found it's way in front of Chet, and he looked around for acceptance. 'Go ahead,' his old man said. 'Drink 'er down.' Chet lifted the dirty hiball glass and smelled in deeply. The scent of the whisky was replusive, but, he did what he was told, and made the customary face as if he'd just swallowed a bag of mosquitos. 'That's my boy.' Like Chet Worth, rules simply didn't apply to the Boar's Nest. The legal drinking age inside was variable, depending on the parent or grandparent inside. If they thought their twelve year old was ready for a drink, the bartenders and owners certainly weren't going to turn down the money for the drink. After his mother passed away, he and his father found themselves there quite a bit. The Boar's Nest patrons became absentee parents for him. The Boar's Nest cooked for him, fed him, and even provided a place to stay when his father was having one of his 'sad' days. It was here Chet learned the facts of life. No, we're not talking about how to live his life, but rather, he learned the important things: When to hold 'em, when to fold 'em. When to walk away, when to run. How to hit a guy and knock his head clean off his shoulders, and how to deliver a 'one-hitter quitter', a face punch so devastating, there was no chance Chet could ever throw it in the ring for fear of being brought up on assault charges. When other kids were learning General Mathematics, Chet Worth was learning to count cards, or calculate the spread, something the old timers called 'practical' math. While the others were learning about U.S. History, Chet was learning what would be akin to the Redneck History of the United States of America. And while he had no idea about the Angle Side Angle theorem, he certainly knew exactly how to bank a pool shot off three cushions every time. Seventeen years later, Chet was still welcome, even after his old man passed away two years ago. He got the cancer, and rather than fight it, Peter Worth knew it was just his time to go. And to his credit, he went rather quickly, moving from the strong man with the strong, kobby, calloused hands, to a thin, wiry, tired old man in just a matter of months. It's what he wanted; to join his wife wherever the two happened to end up after life was over here. And so, two weeks after his debut in PRIME, Chet Worth entered the door to the cheers and adoration of his friends. He'd grown into something of an icon with these men, each of them wishing they could get paid to kick ass for a living. They were brilliant fighters on the battlefield, depsite never having fought in a war. Before becoming a speakeasy, the Boar's Nest was a bit of a precursor to a VFW hall, a place where veterans of the Civil War, and WWI could come in, have a drink, and talk about the old times. The patrons still thought of themselves as veterans, but, not of a war, but rather of a different fight: A fight against life itself. Most were born at an awkward time, too young to fight in WWI or WWI, but too old or hurt for Korea or Vietnam. A few served, but, they were few and far between. This, for many of them, this damn near condemned alcove in downtown Cleveland was the only escape they had from life, and they took full advantage. Hugs greeted Chet profusely as he showed his first on screen smile since joining PRIME. This was his home, after all, and he was surrounded by his people. Drinks followed behind as Chet sat down and regaled the guys with a few of the tales from the road, what different people were like behind the scenes, and things of the like. Soon, drinks were poured all around, and glasses were raised to Chet Worth, the most famous man from the Boar's Nest with the customary toast. This one's for you,This one's from me.And buddies forever,We shall be.But if you and meEver come to disagree,Then fuck you!This is for me. A cheer went up in the stale air, and each man took a drink of their spirits, forgetting the world outside, for a few precious hours. And for those few hours, Chet Worth was a hero. 'Think you're mighty now, huh?' asked Peter Maxwell, again behind the wheel of a rental car. 'Because those people out there don't give a shit about you.' 'That's fine,' Chet replied. 'I don't need a fan reaction to wrestle.' 'Yeah. Wrestling. If you can call it that.' 'Well, I did get a win, didn't I?' 'You got an opportunistic win after Vetra damn near killed himself.' 'But, it's still a win, isn't it? When was the last time you...' 'Stifle it.' 'That's what I...' 'AH-AH! I said stifle it.' 'Okay.' For the next hour, while passing under the Allegheny Mountains the two were silent. The unseasonable warmth that had enveloped the Ohio Valley had passed, leaving a thick blanket of winter snow. Under the clear night, the moon sparkled seemingly every snowflake it passed, the new fallen snow looking more like a field of diamonds. Worth looked out the window, and for a fleeting moment, everything was clear. As a man with Bi-Polar Syndrome, moments like these were a treasure. The constant fighting inside his head was a torrent of swirling emotions, struggling for control. He had good days, he had bad days, but mostly, he had indifferent days. It was what he'd become since the age of fourteen, and it was what he'd be, forever. 'What do you know about Lamen?' Maxwell said finally. 'I know he's going to be tough. I don't do so well against guys with speed.' 'Yeah. I know. Did you think about what I proposed to you?' 'Huh? Oh. Right. Yeah. I thought about it.' 'And?' 'What choice do I have, really? I need the cash.' 'Yeah. We need the cash.' 'Right. We need the cash,' Worth replied, sarcastically. In reality, Maxwell had absolutely nothing to do with his career, and really was more of a chauffeur, than a manager. 'So, how it goes down is fine with you then?' 'Yeah. I'm fine with it. I just feel bad for the kid.' 'Why? He's out to beat your ass and win. You should beat him.' 'What is it with you and winning and losing? I don't give a shit if I win or lose. Who cares? The fact that I get a little W by my name in the record column should matter to me? Lamen's sick. No matter what, in a year, I'm going to look back on this, win or lose, and realize that some guy I beat is dead. I shouldn't be kicking the crap out of some sick kid.' 'He's just another wrestler.' 'Yeah. Keep telling yourself that.' No matter what, Lamen was injured. His body could still be in perfect shape, but, his mind had to deal with something no man should ever know: The fact that his death is not only certain and inevitable, but the near exact date of said death. It's a terrible burden. One Chet Worth was hoping to ease for just a few moments on Wednesday Night. He may be an asshole, but, for some reason, his heart went out to this kid. But, why?
