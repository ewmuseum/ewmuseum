Rest In Peace (ReVolution 72 - Fourway Lumberjack Match)

Date: Timeless Bruised and exhausted, grasping at life with stunted gasps, The Deville lay sprawled in the centre of his ring, floating in the euphoric freedom of exceeding his body’s limitations. The canvas beneath him was damp with sweat, teaming with the total darkness of the room to create a cool womb that tempered the forge of his workout, bathing searing skin and boiling blood with delusional respite, numbing incapacitated muscles. Torpid imprisonment of body liberated forceful flight of mind, spreading wings of introspection otherwise denied. “ . . . and the Crimson Angel, Angelo Deville!” It had been years since he’d been placed in a number one contendership match, since he’d been paid the tribute of faith to compete at the acme, to battle for the opportunity to steward his promotion. The last time had been with the Pro Wrestling Circuit, and that was nothing more than a farce; even if PWC hadn’t been a purely angled screenplay, Deville would have never genuinely contended for anything – he merely provided the federation with credibility and stature, inspiring the necessary fanbase for Ron Starr’s shameless repayment of behind-the-scenes favours to Jim Axtell, the eventual inaugural champion, to mean something. It hadn’t been faith or tribute at all; it had been exploitation. In reality, the last time he’d been elevated to contend for the ultimate championship was in WEEF, where he became the longest reigning champion in the promotion’s history, a sovereignty that was only overthrown by untimely incarceration. He’d won the right to battle for the belt by ravaging his body in a cell of barbed wire; he’d won the belt by powerbombing the five-hundred pound champion, Soulfire, off the roof of Madison Square Garden; he’d defended it almost weekly for nearly four months. Aside from tournaments, the Academy, and a brief stint in WIW, that was also the last and only time he’d been in a genuinely competitive environment. When he joined PRIME he knew it was merely a matter of time before he was bestowed with the opportunity. Now that it was drawing nigh he felt like dancing. Not out of joy, not out of bliss, but because he had to, as if every joint and extremity had a string attached. This was everything he hoped for, everything he strived for, cocooned in a briar patch of insecurity and doubt. He hated cluster matches. With every aching fibre of his being he loathed them. Wrestling was supposed to be about supremacy between two gladiators, with one overpowering, out-manoeuvring, out-thinking, or wearing down the other; it was not supposed to be a circus of bedlam, with victory achieved by virtue of luck, interference, or opportune timing. It wasn’t supposed to be a celebration of passive mediocrity. To use that method to determine the contender for the pinnacle of the business, to cheapen the nobility of conquest in such a manner, baffled Deville. Give him Youngblood one on one and he’d break out of the Gridlock, again, and make him tap out, again. Give him Olsig one on one and he’d teach him that the sport is about leading, not following, then make him cry for mercy, perhaps in tongues, or maybe Olsig would forget who and where he was and simply ramble incoherently until he passed out. Give him Aimz, Easton, and even Nova one on one, and he’d take great pride in showing them they were outmatched . . . not by pulling a fast one while they were distracted or their back was turned, but by out-wrestling them. Give him Hoyt Williams and he’d find out how far the conman’s prayers really reached. But give him a cluster . . . Give him a cluster and he’d have victory swiped from beneath him, torn from The Soprano to beat on jealous and bitter interlopers like Baltasar while an ingrate like Aimz scooted under the radar and scored the lucky pin. Give him a cluster and he was doomed to fail. Never in his life had he won the aptly branded clusterfuck. Never. Not once. And now, everything he’d fought to earn for himself, his dreams of becoming Universal Champion, rode on the outcome of one. It felt as scripted as PWC, drawn up in advance with his failure as the ultimate design. His match for ReVolution 72 was the same. It was billed as a fourway, but it was a cluster match in disguise, with the fifth man coming in the form of Ellis Easton. His involvement as referee didn’t bode well for anyone, but it was worst for Deville. With Eleanor perched on his shoulder, Easton would revel in the opportunity to screw Deville, exacting revenge for an imagined slight. It didn’t matter that Deville had fired her to free her up to manage Easton exclusively, to provide him the tutelage and advice Deville no longer needed; she saw it as a slap in the face, and he’d have been twisted to see it the same. Again, Deville was baffled. The only conceivable reason for Easton’s inclusion was to sabotage him. It made him wonder what Hin See had against him. He never missed an event, never skipped a promotional appearance, and he never let the opportunity to spike the ratings pass him by. He never threatened, never made demands . . . he didn’t even make a scene over being booked against Karina. He’d wrestled more matches than anyone else on the roster since being hired, never taking more than one week’s break between, and those seldom. He’d paid a million dollars out of his own pocket to entice Xavier Kannon out of retirement for one final match on PRIME television. It simply didn’t make sense for Hin See to demoralize him or set him up. Yet here he was. His shot at the title dashed by a cluster match at Tropical Turmoil, his match for ReVolution 72 dashed by a referee with an axe to grind. Deville would have considered it a preview for Tropical Turmoil, something to build heat between the participants and the champion, except for the inclusion of Kyle Velocity. That was yet another tripwire seemingly placed specifically for Deville. Velocity had static with Hoyt, sure, but after FSW, See knew perfectly well that Deville had more of it with Velocity. Velocity was another on a short list of undeserving people Deville had gone out of his way to put over in the choreographed charade of this great sport. However, as soon as the foundation was laid, as soon as other plans had been pushed to the sideline to make way for him, Velocity disappeared without so much as a single word, leaving Deville and the Primetime Title division to stagnate. Deville had recovered amicably, of course, but it was the principle of the matter that galled. Even months after the fact Velocity didn’t have the courtesy to explain himself, not even by postcard. Years later, in PRIME, with Deville sharing the arenas with him once more, he still lacked the civility to explain, the fortitude to apologize. It was a situation that begged nothing but revenge, and Hin See counted on it. The Asian Cowboy banked on it. It was the Seven-Way Hell In A Cell all over again, with Easton taking the saboteur’s place of Baltasar and Velocity taking the distracting role of Aimz. It was a recipe for disaster. For failure. Maybe Deville needed to have a talk with See. Obviously he’d done something to upset the man, but he’d be damned if he could figure out what it was. He fancied himself a model employee, and he knew he worked wonders for buy-rates and promotional acclaim. What did the old bastard have against him? He should try to find out. And while he did, maybe he could clear up the misconceptions about Kari. Deville knew better than anyone that she didn’t always put the best foot forward, and her lack of diligence regarding duty was far from endearing, but in her heart of hearts she was trying to do right. She loved PRIME. She’d break herself in half just to see it do well, to hear that she and PRIME had put on a great match. That had to be more important than appearing at Best Buy to sign autographs, or showing up late from time to time. It’s not like she ever missed her matches. Deville could make him see she didn’t deserve the third degree. He knew he could. Sighing into the darkness, Deville found he had the strength to move and gave his head a rueful shake. Karina wouldn’t want him talking to See on her behalf. She’d take offence to it, and though it would likely inspire her to come and talk to him, when – if – they spoke again, Deville didn’t want to be addressing the underside of her nose. There were a lot of things Deville instinctively did and thought to do that Karina disapproved of, or took offence to. Worse, most of the time he found himself agreeing with her after giving it some thought, despite the initial sense it made to him. Perhaps even worse still, he found himself trying to cull that instinct – or maybe it was a programmed response – from his mind. She made him want to be a better man, a better person. That thought made him sit bolt upright. He barely noticed the lightning strikes of pain such sudden movement stimulated, focussed instead on his mind dancing the fine line between inspiration and panic. He could make out the silhouette of the ropes encircling him, but such insignificant presence registered only on a tertiary level. If he— The chiming of his cell phone, nestled in the pocket of the jacket strewn across a chair beside the ring, pierced the thought, whipping Deville’s head toward it. The temptation to ignore it was fleeting, swallowed by vain hope. With a groan, he rolled across the mat and under the bottom rope, landing gingerly on his feet and stumbling to where it lay, snatching up the jacket in as much of a hurry as he could muster. Maybe it was her. If it was, he didn’t want to miss it. She’d never left a message before, and . . . The gruff, thickly accented voice that responded to his anxious greeting was definitely not Karina. It wasn’t anyone he was expecting or hoped to talk to. “Guess who just fuckin’ resurfaced,” Frank Restelli, a long-time acquaintance and . . . business-partner . . . of Deville opened without greeting, the lordly tone of his voice leaking an image of the broad smile he undoubtedly wore. Restelli had always been huge on rhetorical questions, and he always waited until they were answered, enjoying making people probe blindly. Deville sighed, tilting his head heavenward. “Jesus Christ, come to avenge the idiocy perpetrated on his hallowed behalf and lay down the smack.” Silence stretched for a moment. “What?” demanded Frank, again rhetorically, before barking a sharp laugh. “You drunk, Angie? Why didn’t you invite me over?” Another pause, barely the span of a breath. “Shouldn’t that be ‘lay the smack down’?” “Can’t end a sentence in a preposition,” Deville explained. “It’s bad grammar, and the cops are always listening.” Frank laughed again. “Tell the pigs to off fuck.” Deville grinned, shaking his head, throwing in an “Up shut,” and somewhere in a mythical land of elitism, the grammar nazis cackled with insane delight. “We back in English class or somethin’? What the fuck was that about?” “Nothing. Just amusing myself and an omnipresent friend of mine who likes woman.” “Woman? Just one?” “Nah, he loves all of womenkind.” Clearing his throat, Deville realized it was time to move on. “Who resurfaced?” “Oh man,” Frank gushed, “you’ll never guess.” “So then tell me,” Deville said tiredly. “This suspense thing really isn’t doing it for me.” “Alright alright.” Frank paused to take a deep breath, as if preparing to make a stately announcement. “Ciolo Valentini.” Wracking his brain, Deville could only come up with “Who?” “Are you serious? Man, you really are drunk, aren’t ya? I think this is the first time I’ve witnessed you fergettin’ somethin’.” Frank laughed some more. “C’mon, you remember. Ciolo . . . The asshole with the stupid hair. You vouched for him, then he set you up for a f--” “I remember,” Deville interrupted, clenching his jaw as the face materialized in his mind. “What do you mean he resurfaced?” “I mean he’s struttin’ around like he don’t remember. Same name, same look, same fuckin’ attitude. I haven’t seen him personally, since he’s still walkin’, but I’ve heard.” Frank paused, and Angelo nodded, considering. If Frank had heard, it was true, and if the man was still alive, Frank definitely hadn’t seen him. “Figured you’d want first crack at the piece a’ shit.” “Good man,” Deville said, still nodding, rubbing a hand against his chin and up his face, considering how to handle it. “Call me back in twenty. I’m in the middle of something here.” ~><>…<><~ “And behold!” resonated a powerful voice befitting an infomercial exercise guru, warbling reverently on the strong vowel sounds, “I looked unto God, and God looked unto me, and He said unto me, ‘Boyd, my brother’ – for while I am His son, we are also like brothers! – ‘we need more fucking money!’” “AMEN!” came the unified cry of those gathered, bobbing in their seats like buoys. Shaking a hand above his head, eyes clenched shut with divine insight, Boyd continued channelling his conversation with the Almighty. “Because we need better script writers!” “AMEN!” “And depth of character!” “AMEN!” “And character arcs!” “AMEN!” “And compelling settings!” “AMEN!” “And most importantly, Boyd, you need a shiny new Cadillac! And fancy duds! You gotta represent with style the heavenly kingdom!” Stomping to help emphasize both syllables of kingdom, drawing the word out and deepening his voice to better convey its majesty, Boyd threw up his other hand. “Can I get an Amen?!” “AMEN!” “AAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAA-MEN!!!” Boyd shouted. A throat clearing somewhere in the back of the assembly popped one of Boyd’s eyes open, rife with damning suspicion. He knew that final amen was slightly lacking. A young lady with tangled hair and an awkward smile rose unsteadily to her feet, surveying the rest of the flock for support and finding none. Clasping hands behind her back to stop their shaking, she cleared her throat again and licked her lips. “Why does God think you need a Cadillac? I mean, doesn’t he devalue material poss--” “HARLOT!” Boyd thundered, descending from his makeshift pulpit like the fiery wrath of Sat—God. Thundering down the aisle, he stabbed an accusatory finger at the trembling, gaping woman. “DEMON! Who are you to question the contradictory words of YOUR lord and saviour?!” Mumbled apologies fell on deaf ears, and before the woman could buckle her knees to sit, Boyd had a hold of her sleeve. Casting her down, he pointed at her again, then addressed his wide-eyed congregation. “This woman has been possessed by the devil! Take her to the exorcising chamber! I’ll be down to purge her of her lus—of Lucifer when the sermon has finished! So mote it be!” Two large men who should have been wearing masks standing guard at the front door swooped forward and scooped up the now screaming woman, dragging her from the room as the rest pretended nothing happened and their charming pulpiteer resumed his station. On the other side of the back door, Deville shook his head with wonder. Mote? He didn’t know what the hell a particle of dust had to do with anything, but it made about as much sense as the rest. This was his first experience with a cult, and from what he’d heard in the brief time he’d been eavesdropping, he hoped it would be his last. Deville wasn’t a bible-thumper by any skewed definition of the term, but he’d been raised Roman Catholic and attended church weekly until he was in his teens. He and the pope didn’t see eye to eye on everything – Deville didn’t consider condoms to be an instrument of murder, for example – but in general he liked the spirit of the message, no matter how retardedly the messengers butchered it in their self-serving translation. With this tool . . . Deville had seen and heard less hollow and transparent schemes at the county fair and on late-night television. He’d never believed it possible for anyone to give televised preachers a bad name, but Boyd did that and more. He wouldn’t even have been there if not for agreeing to do a favour for Ciolo. Apparently his brother was among the brainwashed, and when Ciolo showed up alone to drag him away, Boyd tried to convert him. With The Deville at his side, he’d argued, that sort of nonsense would be quelled quickly. Deville hadn’t known Ciolo long, but he seemed like a kid with promise, even if his punctuality left a lot to be desired. Checking his watch again, Deville sighed. Ciolo was supposed to meet him half an hour ago. If he had, they’d be done with this already. If he had, Deville wouldn’t have to listen to the ramblings of a deranged conman. “I see that some of you are wearing silk clothes,” came the booming from within, “and lo, the Lord hath told me there’s a pawn shop down the street where you can convert it to heavenly moolah! He also tells me there’s a bank next door, that in times of great need a little biblical pillaging can save your soul! Can I get a hallelujah?!” “HALLELUJAH!” Fuck this. I’m not waiting anymore. If this wasn’t three years ago and I knew who the hell he was, I’d be wishing for a Sirrajin promo. The sermon stopped short when Deville barged through the door with the familiar self-assurance of Easton into a beauty salon. The pulpiteer eyed him askance, but only briefly; taking in the exquisite material and craftsmanship of The Deville’s three-piece suit, Boyd licked his lips and proffered the welcoming smile of a used car salesman. “Greetings sinner!” he boomed, pointing to the ceiling. “Surely my father has sent you to me so that I may alleviate you of your guilt, gild, and gold!” Deville favoured him with a sneer, but otherwise ignored him. “Which one of you is Valentini?” he posed to the glaze-eyed crowd. No one stirred. “There’s no one here by that guinea name, sinner,” Boyd responded heatedly, climbing down from the pulpit. Pulling a jeweller’s magnifying glass from his pocket, he bent down and examined the glimmering ring on Deville’s pinky, then bit it, slobbering covetously. “Hey! Fuck off!” “Avast! That be gold!” Deville yanked his hand away from Boyd’s greedy cesspit. “Don’t bite my fu--” Face contorting from rage to confusion, Deville eyed Boyd curiously. “Did you just say avast?” Leaning toward him, Boyd lowered his voice, “I used to run a scam as the captain of a pirate ship. Don’t blow my cover. These rubes actually believe this—er . . .” Boyd looked around nervously, but he needn’t have worried; his zombies were waiting with the vacant stares of true believers. “Repent, sinner, and you can be saved! For only $999.99 to start, then weekly instalments called tithing!” Deville turned back to the crowd, shaking his head. “Which of you is Valentini?” “I told you, sin--” Deville wheeled on Boyd, stealing the response. “If you say one more idiotic word, I’m gonna shoot you right in the mouth.” Boyd backed up, trembling like Hogan. “Let me tell you something, brother, you don’t know who you’re talking to! I’m God’s son!” “Oh for . . . Will you read the fucking book? We’re all God’s children.” “Not like me, sinner! Not like—HEY!” Deville, having had enough with pseudo-pleasantries, produced a pistol from his breast pocket. “Prince!” Boyd shouted in near panic, turning to a freaky looking tool with blue hair, too much make-up, and a guitar standing along the side of the room. “Save us from this murderous heathen!” Prince nodded, then ever-so-slowly turned his guitar over, detaching a sword from the back of it, which he raised high overhead. Forged of kryptonite in a virgin volcano under the tutelage of no less than sixteen shaolin monks, three of whom were dead but no-selling it, tempered by the sin-erasing tears of newborn babies, it was truly a fine blade. It shimmered like divine lightning as he moved across the room on the balls of his feet, eyeing Deville dangerously. Deville sighed wearily, summoning the will to resist telling the fool the middle ages were long past, then shot him in the face. “Eek!” cried Boyd. “EEK!” cried the sheep. “Freeze!” cried a hippie-haired man in the middle row, leaping to his feet and pointing reprovingly at both Deville and Boyd. “Danny!” hissed a pudgy man beside him, tugging desperately at his sleeve. “Sit down!” But Danny wasn’t having any of that, or even his name. “My name’s not Danny! It’s Vesuvelangius!” he shouted proudly. Everyone stared blandly. Shifting uncomfortably, Vesuvel—Danny took a moment to recover. “Detective Vesuvelangius!” More bland staring. “I never bought into any of your festering preaching, Boyd!” Danny went on, smugly folding his arms. “I was never a follower! I’ve been a plant all along, waiting for the evidence to gather against you! And today it has! I know it was you, Boyd, in the kitchen, with the candlestick!” Deville and Boyd exchanged a glance. The rest continued to stare blandly. No clichéd thunderclap resounded, but despite it being midafternoon, crickets chirped. “And the candlestick was really your son all along!” Chirp chirp. Chirp chirp. “And you want to know what the moral of the story is?! Don’t piss me off, Boyd! This is my town, and I’ll strike down upon thee with great vengeance and furious anger!” Chirp chirp. Chirp chirp. “And now I have you, too, Deville! Valentini told me you’d slip up,” Danny went on, removing his wig to unveil a video camera. “And now I’ve got it on tape! Muahahahahahahaha!” More bland staring. Another sigh. The unmistakable feeling of being lost in a terrible whodunit without a point. Another shot to the face. Muttering about people being too stupid to have survived so long, Deville walked over to the camera, picked it up, and set it on fire. He considered hiding it in an uber-secret place, but in the end he had to decide on actually destroying the evidence. The mindless followers all quaked in their boots as Deville returned to the pulpit, producing a crisp hundred dollar bill from his pocket, which he handed to Boyd. “Convince these morons none of this ever happened so I don’t have to kill the rest of you.” Boyd snatched up the money with a vigorous nod, bowing to his one true God. Deville sighed again. “Can I get an Amen for the road?” “AMEN!” Leaving in sour spirits, Deville wondered what the hell Valentini’s problem was. Not only had the sonofabitch tried to set him up for a fall, he’d wasted his fucking time, and for no conceivable reason. ~><>…<><~ A shudder reverberated through aching bones as the stainless shovel’s head scraped a stone among the mud, screeching protest at the deed for which it had been designed and bought. Tufted, fluffy clouds made a mockery of the moon’s feeble yet romantic illumination, washing the world in darkness, but had anyone else been in the field they would have plainly seen Angelo’s silhouette, knee-deep in the grave. It was hours since Frank had called him back, and the intervening time was spent with anything but languor. Pant-cuffs creased and heavy with dirt clung and hung haphazardly, defying the pristine white of his shirt, mottled only slightly by the sweat he’d coaxed from strangled pores. Another scrape, another grunt. Another heap of muddy clay plopped atop the pile to his right. Another thoughtful glance thrown at the bodybag framing the other side. It was deep, yet all too shallow; he didn’t think it possible to bury it deep enough to satisfy, to suffocate the remnants of his revenge beneath enough dirt that he could escape the haunting screams. Leaning on the shovel to rest and catch his breath, feeling strained muscles spasm like electricity, he decided it would have to be adequate. He stepped up form the grave and stabbed the shovel into the supple surface dirt beside the bag, then moved around it so it separated him from the grave. His heartbeat pounded like it never had with a body, thudding so deep in his skull that that it pulsed in bloodshot eyes. Part of it was panic, part of it was excitement. Part of him shouted to turn back, to undo what he had done, but he was as resolved to this as anything before. Grinding his teeth in an almost-smile, he planted a foot beneath the bodybag and shoved. Carrying it down here had exhausted him, but when he tossed it down it was like the world had lifted, as if all the corpses of his past had taken flight from his shoulders. The bag was light and cooperative with his foot, turning easily, and the muffled, scraping clang of metal against metal barely registered as it toppled to its final resting place. Frank had scoffed and mocked him when he told him he didn’t care to kill the traitor, but the barbs sailed over him as poignant advice from those he loved always had in the past. He didn’t care what Frank thought, and he certainly didn’t care what Ciolo thought, or what the man was up to now. They’d both end up digging their own graves one day, and though Deville still didn’t fear the prospect, he was tired of digging his own. This one was for something else. He missed Karina deeply, but he’d come to the realization that she was gone, that there was nothing he could do to bring her back to him. That pained him, the failure of it, the emptiness of her truant smile, but it was something he could grow from, not something to whittle away his spirit. She made him want to be a better man, a better person, and admitting that to himself, acknowledging that some of the things he did were wrong, resolved him to try to change. She was gone, but he could still become a better person, still strive to improve, only for himself. He didn’t need her to change, he only needed to know that it would make him stronger. He unclipped the pistol from his shoulder holster and stared at it, bleak and dull in the lack of light. It had brought him so much pain, so much anxiety, stripping him of the integrity he’d always fancied inherent. It had— He couldn’t blame the gun. It had been him all along, sabotaging himself, chasing spite and revenge and excuses and martyrdom instead of pursuing what mattered most, looking for ways to falsely succeed through failure instead of unearthing ways to achieve his noblest desires. Not anymore. It would be hard, harder than any facetious quest he’d ever undertaken, but he knew that it was the best thing for him, the only path he could take. The obstacles were immense and daunting, but that didn’t make him balk, it made him smile eagerly; the more difficult they were, the stronger he’d have to be to overcome them. “Rest in peace,” he whispered to his gun, favouring it with one last look before pitching it into the grave atop the bag, atop all of the weaponry formerly housed in his estate. The time for revenge, for excuses, was passed. The time to fill the hollowness he’d long denied was dawning, ushering in an era of veracity. He was going to make those he loved and whom loved him proud, and just as importantly, he was going to feel pride of self. He was going to do right because it was right, and nothing in the Universe would stop him. It was time to control the chaos, to leave the doubt behind, buried beneath his reach. It was time to start succeeding for the right reasons. It was time to be the better man.
