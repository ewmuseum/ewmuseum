The Patriarch: Contact

undated
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: GTT5 RP Ettore Calvino was twenty-two in 1969, the year that the United States went crazy. During the summer, he had been glued to the television at the news of the horrifying assassinations of Bobby Kennedy and Dr. King. He was rapt with attention for Seymour Hersh’s revelations about My Lai. Everything about this American war puzzled and fascinated him; the issues of the Truman Doctrine and Communist containment washed right over him and the wave of false nationalism swept him up. He was a second-generation American; his mother and father had come from Naples after the humiliation suffered at Versailles. No longer proud of their home country and with few economic prospects, the two children of the old country broke the bank for two tickets on a boat to the United States. On the day they stepped onto American soil, they were de facto citizens, no matter what a green or blue card said. The most Italian thing about the family was the shoes Ettore’s father owned. Nonetheless, Ettore’s parents were aghast that their son would want immerse himself in American politics. After all, the important politics they had observed in America were those in marketplaces, large corporation buildings, and factories. The streets were not paved with gold as promised, but society’s cracks and flaws were caulked with dollar bills. Ettore announced in earlier 1970 that he was joining the military. His mother took the news badly, but she saw that he was committed to proving his own worth. When Ettore gave the speech to his parents, he was full of joie de vivre, talking about how his going into the war would ensure that no uninterested, disaffected American youth would be drafted to go and how he was going over to defend the freedoms of the people in Vietnam. This same misguided optimism from crooks in dark blue suits had slowly siphoned American lives on the ground in Vietnam for years now. Following basic training was deployment to the Pacific; however Ettore ended up posted at a U.S. naval base in the Philippines, doing menial tasks for higher-ups. He was not one to rock the boat, but he was itching to get over to the Vietnamese mainland, to fight. He is written up and reported for insubordination the first time in December 1970, after which things were relatively quiet for almost a year and a half. However, at this point, the U.S. government had fewer and fewer young men who were ready to go and go and fight this fruitless war. Calvino was among said soldiers, so as troops were being cut down to 70,000, Ettore was shipped out to a base camp near Saigon, in the South. From all reports, Ettore was a marginal soldier. His service time was largely uneventful, as he experienced relatively little contact with guerilla forces. However, as his immersion with the Vietnamese culture became greater, he began to show more and more fervor for the cause of the people. He spent time in villages handing out aid when few others would and on more then one occasion laid down his weapon, refusing to hurt or kill unarmed Vietcong soldiers. Ettore Calvino was written up and punished internally by his base camp commander for such actions. However, this time the punishment had no effect. Calvino continued to sympathize with the common Vietnamese person. He wanted to help the people who had already been betrayed by the United States once. He was reported AWOL in April, after the peace accord had already been signed and troops were now in full withdrawal. Ettore Calvino lived among the Vietnamese people for over a year after this, but he was forced to live like a nomad, evading the wary Communist government at every turn. After months of bouncing around, trying to help people (using money he had gained from selling off military equipment to both sides of the conflict), he ended up in a small village outside Hanoi. There he met a young woman of seventeen, with whom he quickly became romantically (and clandestinely) involved, named Sam Thi De. De and Ettore were tremendously happy with one another; Ettore told her that he would take her out of Vietnam, away from all the war and the new threat of Pol Pot’s neighboring Cambodian regime. Ettore was certain that he could bring De back to the United States, where they would start a family. It was June 1975. Ettore disappeared one day and left a pregnant De all by herself. She was shunned by some of her family and moved to the United States with her son, Sam Duc Che. * * * * My full name: Sam Duc Che. Don’t ask me how my mother and the INS Americanized “Sam Duc” into Tsonda. I’m thinking about names as I’m staring at the Apu Guy in front of me. His name tag says that he is Ranjit. The entire situation is so racist that it’s making real life clichéd. The clerk at this 7/11 is Indian and his skin is dark, like expensive lacquered wood tables. He’s one of those Indians who can pass for black if he cuts off those slick “my last name is Patel” curls and starts wearing those ridiculous flat-brimmed Negro League hats. But he doesn’t; he’s intrinsically Indian. Something at his core compels him to wear his white dress shirt and burgundy sweater vest to his shitty job. There’s some wretched nobility to the ordeal of being Indian. He’s checking my ID as I’m checking out of the store. I only bought a case of Heineken but Apu Guy is scrutinizing my ID like it’s the fucking word of Vishnu. I remember right after 9/11, some crazy rednecks got together and beat the living shit out of some Indian guy who ran a convenience store. The worst thing that’s happened to us Asians was Ice Cube coming out with “Black Korea.” Well, ya know, other then the whole internment thing. That was a pretty dick move, ol’ U.S. of A. And of course, like any good pining at tournament time, this starts me thinking about Armaan. But I don’t think about him for very long. Not yet. I’m an epic procrastinator and I’ve got days until I meet him. In the meantime, there’s a PRIME Pay-Per-View and the whole daily life thing to be done. “Here you go, sir.” His tongue clicks the back of the throat as he calmly offers these words. It’s a dreadful accent, if you think about it. The British basically enslaved these people and gave them what amounts to just a shittier version of their own accent. I mean, really, have you ever heard an Indian guy say “I’m gonna fuck you up” and believed it? Scratch that; have you ever heard an Indian guy say anything threatening? I mean, c’mon. When the most notorious countryman is famous for basically leading himself and other people to massive beatdowns (to accomplish a better life, sure, but you can’t tell me Gandhi didn’t get served by those North African and British guards; I’ve seen the movie), it’s hard to be seen as threatening. I put my ID back into my wallet and he starts to put my things into a bag. People still don’t recognize me as anything but good-looking (and let’s not discount that character either; it’s important to note that I’m the sexiest motherfucker on earth…that still does his own grocery shopping and beer runs). That brings me back to GTT5. That’s why I’m here, in the loosest time-space sense of the word; at this junction in life, I entered the tournament to finally become a distinct figure in the wrestling hierarchy. My things are bagged up, so I grab them and ignore the sad puppy coin collection jar sitting on the counter. If a ferret or some other pet asks me for a quarter, I’ll gladly hand it over, but if animals are stupid enough to be enslaved by humans only to sit in houses all day then fuck if I’m going to lose a dime over it. “Thank you, have a good night sir.” The rolling ‘r’ invades and pierces my brain like the sound of glass rubbing on glass. I’ll have absolutely no problem with motivation for this match if Armaan comes to the ring and says “Tank you, sahl” like Rajit Apu Guy just did. “Yeah, you too buddy.” There’s a difference between being a prick and being a horrible person. I can be both, but only a truly horrible person would kick this guy when he’s obviously down and say all the things I’ve been thinking. I go around in life looking for reasons to fly off the handle; at least I pass the litmus test of not being a horrible person. So three cheers and this whole case of Heineken for me. Plus it was my birthday last week. I don’t want to talk about being thirty. At all. I step through the double glass doors and out into the fresh San Diego night. I’m back home to soak up a couple nights’ rest at my apartment before it’s off to Great American Nightmare, and then the Great Indian Nightmare to knock the Shiva out of Armaan. There’s no place on earth like San Diego, though. A cool breeze crawls into my jacket but never does it feel uncomfortable. Plus, I’ve already got one of the Heinekens in hand and the Germans have done their best to create a product that’s cold going down until it reaches the very top of your chest, at which point it unleashes a very small grenade that heats up your lungs. For being so violent in imagery, it’s quite a nice feeling; it’s the first exit you pass on the road to a nice care-free night of solo drunkenness. Then again, the mornings of solo hungoverness are also unrivaled in the “boy, this is depressing that this is my life” department. But that’s a risk I’m willing to take. My phone rings, loudly. Cursing myself, I set down the bag and the open Heineken. I pull open the jacket and get my phone. It’s my agent and he’s doing his little Jewey thing where he talks way way too fast for me to possibly understand him. “Slow down, Gil. I can’t understand you.” He takes a deep, nasal breath with his all-encompassing hawk nose. I can actually see his reaction to whatever is happening right now. He’s breathing hard, sweat’s forming on his brow, and he’s shaking his head over and over, as if doing it enough times will slow the rest of the world down. He says that he was contacted by someone who knows my father. I hang up. He calls back and I look at the phone and the Heineken. Reluctantly, I go back to the phone and pick up, my words slurring together from the combined shock and rage. I want to knock Jew’s fucking teeth in for pulling this shit. “What the fuck is it this time, Gil? Another freeloading relative? Tell those Guido fucks that they’re not getting a cent from me!” I spit at the end to emphasize my point; I know he can’t see me, but the sound of spitting is a rather disgusting one and I hope it conveys my meaning perfectly. He says this time is different. He slows down his cadence, that off-key Brooklyn yip crawling along to tell me that my father needs to see me for some reason. “My father can rot in hell.” Gil pleads with me to consider. He says that the intention from whoever contacted him was that this is not an attempt to apologize or reconcile, but it’s a family matter that must be attended to. He points out my father’s failing health. Clearly, Gil Lavenstein has not just been sitting in that New York office doing nothing since I’ve signed to PRIME. He’s been monitoring my father from afar, like I asked. Just in case. “So what does his side propose?” There’s no answer for a moment and in his office, Gil is bound to be biting his lip, anticipating the rancor with which I’m going to respond to whatever his answer is. He says my father wants to see me this week. This, the week of my second round GTT5 match and my big-time Pay-Per-View match against Paddy O’Shea. I consider whether Armaan or Paddy O’Shea is clever enough to have hired someone to send me on a wild goose chase to miss either match. Armaan seems (no 9.0 Richter shock here) to be pitiable in his courageous, noble chase for some idealistic world; he’s too good a guy to pull this. Paddy’s got the brains of a sea sponge. “Where?” The answer comes that he’s living in San Antonio. I can say that I have prior business engagements and not be a liar. I can avoid navigating through his lies. I can leave the door open forever; closure is overrated, right? “Get me tickets to San Antonio. I want them for tomorrow; I can’t waste this entire week. I’ve got huge stuff going on.” And I do. But I also have doors that won’t stay open forever. And like Zach Braff in the wretched mess that was Garden State, I’ve got serious daddy issues. So it’s time to visit with Ettore Calvino, the epic traitor who is my father.
