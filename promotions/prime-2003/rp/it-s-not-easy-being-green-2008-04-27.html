It's Not Easy Being Green

27 April 2008
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 4/27/08 I try to be a nice guy. I try to be whatever passes for decent in today's society, despite all the elements forcing you towards one extreme or another. I try, every single day, to build up some karma, good faith, anything you might want to call it. Something to counterbalance the ten tons of bullshit life's thrown at me in 32 years. I try. That's not to say I always succeed. As much as some people might want to call my a Boy Scout or a goody-two-shoes, there's always going to be things in my life that I'm not proud of doing. It's not easy to live up to the reputation of always keeping the moral high ground. Because sometimes, you've got to climb so damn high to reach it that you're worn out by the time you're there. And sometimes, especially in professional wrestling, the high ground doesn't exist. Sometimes, all you can do is try and keep yourself from sinking so far into that proverbial pit of quicksand so you can still see enough to find your way out at the end. Sometimes, you've just got to go deeper into that tunnel than you could possibly imagine, just for the off chance that you'll find the glimmering light at the end. The thing is, the deeper you go, the more the pressure builds up. The harder you push, the more you end up feeling like Sisyphus, rolling a boulder up the hill only to see it roll right back down, time and time again. After a while, the pressure and the tension get so high, you get to hoping that the next time the rock rolls back down the hill, it does you the courtesy of rolling over you before you can emulate the opening sequence of "Raiders of the Lost Ark". But, for some unknown reason, I keep on trying. Like Charlie Brown and the goddamn football, I keep on trying, figuring that one of these days, the universe is bound to come around to my way of thinking. At some point, I still believe that my stars are going to align, and after 32 years of having increasingly heavier weights dropped on my back, the load's finally going to get lifted. Why I'm still an optimist I've got no idea. By all rights, I should be so depressed it'd make Devin Shakur blush. Maybe I'm just too stupid to realize a bad situation when I'm in one. Oh well. Until I smarten up and get the hell out, or until my train finally comes in, they might as well keep dropping those weights on me. What goes around, comes around, right? Right? ****** Six months ago... Six hours was enough. Six hours in a cramped, sweaty matchbox of a conference room in a tiny Greensboro office building was enough to make anyone crack. As someone with a slightly greater stress quotient than the average human being, Troy Douglas was probably a little closer to the edge than most would be. It was about to get worse, if possible. "There's no easy way to say this, Troy," said Terry Rice, senior legal counsel to the charitable trust Troy had founded five years ago in the names of his father and fiance. Terry was, for the most part, everything you wanted in a lawyer. He was smart, he had a brilliant flair for strategy, he could argue circles around most people, and he was slicker than Teflon. "No easy way to say what, Terry?" "You've got to go." "Go? I started this foundation. I'm the guy who's engineered those hundreds of thousands in scholarship money. I'm the public face of this organization." "And that's exactly why you have to go. With what's hanging over you right now, the Foundation can't survive with your name attached to it. At least, not right now." This was the problem with Terry that you didn't have with most other high-powered lawyers. He had an annoying tendency to be right. Troy knew what it was about, he knew why the emergency session of the executive board had been called, and he knew that six hours of debate were inexorably leading to this moment. He just didn't want it to have to be true. "It's the lawsuit, isn't it?" "Yeah. Our public image doesn't look all that good if our executive director is the focus of a lawsuit regarding the paralysis of an eight-year-old kid." "Now, wait a minute--" "We know, Troy. Nobody in their right mind blames you for what happened to Kevin Chapman." "I blame myself, Terry. That's why I've thrown myself into the Foundation. Because I've got to show myself that I can do something to make up for what happened." "Right now, the best thing you can do is step aside. Right now, that's the only way that everything you've worked to establish can still get done." "I don't like anything about this, but I'll go along with you. Where do we start?" To be honest, we start with the story of Kevin Michael Chapman, age 8, of Marblehead, Massachusetts. If you haven't read the headlines or seen the CNN reports by now, it's a story we've seen any number of times since kids started watching professional wrestling. One Saturday, Kevin and his 12-year-old cousin Christopher were out in the backyard, bouncing on the trampoline that Kevin's dad, who owned a car wash in nearby Salem, had just set up for him a few days before. Now, Chris, he'd been a wrestling fan since the first time his dad let him watch, and Kevin, who idolized his older cousin, had followed him into the fandom. So, like so many kids on so many trampolines before them, they decided to try out some of their favorite moves on each other. Then Chris decided to copy his favorite wrestler's signature move. Troy was amazed every time he pulled off the End of the Road that his opponent managed to walk away mostly unscathed. There's a reason you always see those Public Service Announcements about trained professionals on wrestling shows. The Chapman family was about to find out why. Chris hooked his cousins arms, bounced once, twice, and on the third bounce, he pulled Kevin backwards. And as he did, he lost his balance. He began falling backwards, taking Kevin with him. Chris rolled off the trampoline, fell the four feet to the ground and ended up with nothing more than a twisted ankle and some nasty scrapes. Kevin was still upside-down when Chris lost control, and the younger boy was far less lucky. As his cousin lost his grip, Kevin bounced once, then rolled. Then his neck hit an exposed piece of metal, and Kevin Chapman fell to the ground in an unconscious heap. In hindsight, it was lucky that Chris got off as easy as he did. Anything worse, and there was almost no way he would have gotten to his aunt in time for her to place the 9-1-1 call. Anything worse, and Kevin Chapman's parents would have buried their only child. In that sense, they were lucky. That was the only way they were lucky. Kevin Chapman lived, but he'd suffered the same neck and spinal injuries that cost Troy his football career. Only, the body of an eight-year-old boy was a hell of a lot more frail than that of a highly-conditioned pro athlete. Kevin Chapman was alive. But there was a 90 percent chance he'd never walk again. The lawsuits came a few days later. The company that made the trampoline, the landscapers who'd left patches of unstable ground, and all the wrestling promotions that the boys watched. And if it ever got to trial, Troy was going to be the centerpiece of it all. He was going to be the poster boy of everything that was wrong about professional wrestling. The guy who'd made it his life's work to be one of the few standup guys in an industry full of people that could make people's skin crawl was going to be one of the biggest scapegoats in history. The man who came back from nearly being crippled would be known forever as the man who crippled a little boy. People had told Troy right off that he needed to quit. He didn't. He said that the only way he'd get through it was to keep doing his job. He didn't make public comments, he just went about his life, dreading the day it was all going to come back and bite him in the ass. So, he signed the papers temporarily handing over the Foundation to the rest of the board. It was the right decision to make. He just wished it was one he would never have to make. ****** In my life, I've found it so hard to be the nice guy. When you think you've finally got it, there's one more giant problem, one more way for the world to turn your name into mud. But, when I got that voicemail, I finally felt like one or two of those weights had finally been lifted off my back. If I hadn't been an idiot and forgot to charge my phone, I would've felt better a little bit sooner. I knew that news on the case was coming down today. Six months of pre-trial bullshit, and here I was in Minneapolis, waiting for the call from Terry, and my phone died. So, I did what any rational, stressed-out man in my position would do. I ordered a bottle of Scotch from room service and waited out the time until my phone decided to rise from the dead. After what seemed like an eternity, the message icon finally flipped on, and I readied myself for the fact that the next six months were going to be a hell of a lot worse than the last six. "Troy, it's Terry. I just got the call, and they've made the decision to settle. There's not going to be a trial, and your not going to get dragged through a field of shit. We'll hold a board meeting next Saturday to get things started again with the foundation." My mind was clouded by whiskey and nerves, but I felt those weights starting to lift. "Oh, and I got a call from Boston." "Kevin Chapman took his first steps today. Doctors say he's got a good chance at making a full recovery. Remarkable kid, huh?" Yeah, remarkable. Sometimes, trying to be the good guy really does pay off in the end. I don't see any reason to stop now.
