Way Out West

26 October 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 26/10/06 The saloon doors swing open; sending a breeze swirling across the unswept floor of the bar, two or three heads turn to clock the newcomer but most are too absorbed in their drinks, their worries or their women to pay him much heed. The stranger gives the room as casual scan as he walks in, there’s a gait to his walk almost approaching a swagger as he crosses the room to take a seat at the bar. Pulling down the scarf from over his nose and mouth then nods to the barman and simply states; “Beer.” As it’s poured out for him the stranger pulls his guns from his holsters and sets them down on the bar, the barman finishes pouring and is about to put the beer down before him when he spots the six-shooters laying out. “We don’t want any trouble in here, mister, you’d best put them away.” The stranger responds by taking a money clip from his inside jacket pocket and peeling three crumpled notes from it. He places them down on the bar and slides them toward the balding, bushy-moustached barman with two fingers. The barman gingerly lifts the green notes, slipping them down the side of his boot, beside his emergency knife. Only when the stranger looks up and directly into the barman’s eyes, his own narrowed and steely, is the beer finally placed before him. His gruff voice, the accent unfamiliar around these parts, grunts begrudging thanks. Left alone now, the stranger begins his work – he releases the catch on his first gun; Betty, and tilts the barrel upward, letting the six bullets drop to the counter noisily. Over the general hubbub of conversation it’s widely missed, but three sets of keen ears do pick up on. As the barman hands a beer across at the other end of the bar and starts his waddling trip back to the stranger, two men playing cards by the door look up and spot the stranger. They exchange a sceptical glance, bordering on disbelief, and forget about their game to lean across and whisper in hoarse tones to each other. “I told you, sir, we aren’t wanting any trouble around here – we have enough already. I’d like to respectfully ask you to finish your beer and leave, mister.” He doesn’t get a response initially, the stranger too busy eyeing each individual chamber in Betty, one by one. Finally he sets the gun down and turns to the barman again, his face set hard, eyes locked dead-on to the barman’s own. “That didn’t sound too respectful, in my opinion.” The barman, already standing a few steps back from the counter, shuffles half a pace further away, clumsily knocking over a bottle one the back wall shelves. He catches it before it falls right to the floor, the sound of it breaking as it hits the ground replaced by a derisory, mirthless snort of laughter from the stranger having witnessed this. In his strange, lilting voice the stranger speaks again – the barman now certain the stranger must be from farther afield than his initially hazarded guess had placed him. “I’m here to stop trouble, not start it. Trust me.” With that, another three notes are slid across the counter. The barman – being plain stupid, gutsy or both – begins to slide them back. “I said we do-” The stranger’s hand quickly, quietly and powerfully slaps down, landing on the barman’s. He takes hold of it – lifts it up, turns it over and presses another four wrinkled bills into his palm. The barman, again reluctantly, accepts the money and tucks it away, down next to the first lot. He waddles away again, casting a few indignant looks over his shoulder as he goes, but the stranger’s attention is back on his guns. With Betty laid out, splayed open, her six shots carefully lined up before her, the stranger starts on his other; Mary-Lou, her named inlaid in pearl on the grip. With occasional breaks for drinks of beer, the stranger spends the next five full minutes checking each chamber, blowing through them and tilting the gun in the dim, sputtering, naphtha light for a better look at the smooth walls. Finally happy with the state of his two companions he lays Mary-Lou out as Betty lies, the twelve silver shots lined up across the table before him. As he takes another drink of his beer one of the men from beside the door finally approaches. “Do pardon me, sir, but I was wonderin’ if my friend and I could join you.” “Rather not. With all due respects.” “Figured you’d say somethin’ like that.” The card-player smiles a little as he says this, warranting a sideways glance from the stranger that wipes the smirk from his face. “And why, pray tell… did you ‘figure’ I’d say ‘somethin’ like that’?” “I do beg your pardon, sir, I didn’t mean to be, uhh… purs-… perj-” The stranger sighs and raises his head from looking down at his two beauties, knowing he won’t get any peace until his dispatches with present company. “Presumptuous.” The card-player smiles again, the stranger stays stony-faced. “I didn’t want to seem like that, sir, but see, my friend and I…” He sweeps his arm out, pointing to his partner, still sitting by the door. “Well, we’ve heard stories about a stranger who shoots with two beautiful guns, speaks with – and I mean no disrespect with this, I swear on my mother’s grave, sir – a funny accent for these parts.” “So you want to know if I’m that stranger, huh?” An eager, keen nod answers his question, to which he responds with a nod of his own – slow and thoughtful. “What stories is it you’ve heard of this stranger, then? I’ll see if I know of him.” “Well, I’ve heard that he’s been shot four times, four times that would kill a normal man, yet he’s still walkin’. See, we also heard that he likes to solve problems, and seeing as our town here, well… we have problems, we was hopin’ that you was that stranger, come to fix those problems we got.” “Really.” “Yes, sir. I- I- I do hope y’ain’t offended by my pres-… perdu-…” “Presumptuousness.” “I hope you ain’t been offended by that, sir.” “Get your friend over, then.” The card-player scurries away as the stranger starts to roll a cigarette. The two men arrive at the stranger’s side almost immediately, having all but abandoned their cards at their table. “Either of you boys have any matches?” The first card-player fumbles in a pocket and retrieves a match with a shaking hand. The stranger nods his thanks as he takes it and strikes it off the counter, shielding the flickering flame with a cupped hand as he lights up. “So… what exactly did you hear about this stranger being shot, then?” The first looks to the second, prompting him to lean in and whisper in a hushed tone to the stranger. “Well, we hear-” The stranger raises a hand, wanting him to stop. The second card-player looks confused by this, but he has to wait for the stranger to take, and savour, another long draw before he gets any explanation. “None of this needs to be secrets, boys. Just tell me what you heard, and I’ll tell you if I know the man.” The card-player starts again, still keeping his voice and profile low, hidden from the rest of the bar. “We heard – me and John there – that the stranger’s been shot four times, and any normal man would be dead from the bullets he’s taken. We heard he even got hit by one of them bullets headed right for his heart, and if it weren’t for his bible he’d be dead right now.” The stranger reaches into his shirt’s left breast pocket and takes out a bible, a near-circular hole just under a half-inch across going right through it; the pages around it singed the whole way through. The card-players’ jaws dropped, the first to approach the stranger cautiously, earnestly outstretching a hand to feel the fabled book. The stranger flippantly tosses it to him and reaches his hand back into his pocket. “Did you hear that the stranger was also shot in the heart another time, but it was his whiskey the bullet hit?” His audience, albeit a rather exclusive group, are all ears as the stranger produces a small hipflask from the same breast pocket, a jagged hole about the same diameter as the one in the bible having been torn through it. “And you say you heard that the bible saved his life by stopping the bullet?” “That’s what we heard.” The stranger snorts with laugher again, shaking his head with the first sign of a smile creeping across his face. He stands from the stool again, the two men flanking him respectfully stepping back in his midst. He takes another long, slow drag from his cigarette before dropping it to the floor and grinding it out with his battered leather wingtip boot. As the two men look on, one or two others in the bar joining them now, having had their interest piqued from overhearing the conversation, the stranger unbuttons the top four buttons of his shirt. “That bible you have in your hand there.” He gives the first card-player a quick nod. “It did shit to stop a bullet.” He pulls his shirt across, exposing his chest where a dirty, ragged tear of scar-tissue sits just below his left nipple. He holds two fingers to it, as if it needed pointed out, and after a few seconds moves them up over his chest. He leaves them resting closer to his heart, sitting on a smaller scar – unnoticeable at first glance, when compared to the first. “I trust Mr. Daniels more’n the good Lord, these days.” He buttons his shirt again and returns to his stool, finishing the last of his beer and giving the barman a wave for attention. “One of Mr. Daniels’ best.” The second card player immediately presents a bill from his pocket and slaps it down on the bar before the stranger can attempt to pay himself. “Thank you.” “What about the other two shots?” “The other bullets I took?” The five or six men now gathered around him nod reverently. The stranger allows the corners of his lips to creep up a little as he embarks on another explanation. “A lot of folks say I swagger like I own a town when I walk in, and I let them believe that – it tends to serve me well to have that impression already instilled on my arrival. But it’s less a strut and more of a limp. I just cover it up pretty well.” A voice from further back in the ever-expanding group cries forth: “I’d heard you were shot in the knee, but refused to fall ’til you’d killed the man who tried to cripple you.” The stranger bows his head to hide his smile again, his awed addressees merely taking this as a sign of his modest humility. “That ain’t quite right; Mary-Lou fired as I hit the ground – I was just lucky that shot blew half his face off.” “But you got shot in the knee and you’re still walkin’.” The stranger concedes this with a tip of his head to the man who’d spoken. “I suppose you’re right.” “And what about the last bullet then, where’d that hit you?” The smile that had almost settled on the stranger’s face is wiped clean off now as he swivels back round to the bar, turning his back on the small group assembled. “That one hurt the most, but y’can’t see the scar.” He downs the shot of Jack sitting waiting for him, his head starting to droop as he holds his eyes tightly shut. One man toward the back of the group listening to him begins to laugh as he thinks over the stranger’s last statement; putting two and two together and, unfortunately for him, getting five. “He’s a fuckin’ eunuch! He had his balls blown off!” Before anyone else can start laughing he’s knocked clean out by the stranger, who leaps from his barstool and swings a hard right – sending three bloody teeth scattering over the floor. The stranger ignores the incredulous looks all around him and simply sends a glob of spit down onto the fallen man’s motionless face. “As I was saying, before I was so rudely interrupted…” He strolls back across to his stool – simply leaving the unconscious body lying where it fell. “I got hit deeper in my heart than any bullet could’ve gone – no bible or whiskey ever could’ve stopped it.” He takes a file out from the inside of his boot and picks up the first bullet laid out in from of his ‘girls’. “I fell when I was shot in the knee, Mary-Lou took care of the one who fired at me, but that was her last round and I was slow swinging Betty round. The one that was left fired at me before I could at him. It’s true that I should be dead, that bullet would’ve hit me right between the eyes.” The captive audience wait patiently as the stranger, still with his back to them all, begins to file an X into the tip of the round he holds in his fingers. He files one more then begins again, breaking the silence that’s fallen over the whole bar. “It was a dirty round. Cross filed, designed to mushroom on impact for maximum damage – if it’d hit me I wouldn’t have had teeth left in one piece, never mind my head.” He picks up another round and begins to file, waiting until the slight wavering that had edged into his voice died away. “He was my buddy from up north, best man I ever knew. Dived in, took the shot in his stomach for me. I got Betty round and blew four shots into the bastard’s chest before he could even think about reloading. But my buddy… long gone, as soon as that round hit him it blew him inside-out. Dead before I could drag myself across to him.” The stranger sets down the last of the six filed-tip rounds in front of Betty, and starts on Mary-Lou’s. “I tried to be him for a while after that, then I tried to mould another man into his role for a while… but neither worked. I know what he’d have wanted of me though… I was always his second – I’m amazed there ain’t more stories about him rather’n me, actually, but I s’pose I’m the one still walking.” He sighs deeply and shakes his head ever so slightly; the room still dead-silent but for the sound of the filing, the whole bar awaiting his voice filling it again. “He always told me I could handle myself, and I guess I’m only just learning that he was right.” The rasping sound stops as the stranger finishes the last of the twelve silver shots and begins to clean the filings and dust off each one with a spotless handkerchief that he produced from his pocket. “So I set out to do what he always believed in – to clean things up in this world. It ain’t easy, I doubt it would’ve even been possible for him to do it if he’d lived on…” He pauses as he wipes the last round clean and begins to load them, taking great care of each individual bullet as he slides them into their chambers. “But he was a good man, with his heart in the right place.” With two loud cracks that reverberate through the room he snaps both guns back into their original positions. “I, however, am an unforgiving, vicious bastard with a vendetta, more than willing to take on the dirty-playing scumbags that roam these lands. I heard you folks had trouble with a foreigner here, and I’m here to play him at his own game – I know he flaunts the rules of common decency in a fight, and I’m gonna match him at that.” With that the strangers slings his beauties into their holsters, turns round and steps off his stool – the assembled crowd parting to let him strut, the limp visible now they knew to look for it, between them toward his coat, the door and the fulfilment of the vendetta he holds.
