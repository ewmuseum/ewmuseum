A Familiar Yarn

6 March 2009
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 3-6-09 **I think its only fair for anyone who begins to read this to know beforehand that it's incomplete. Read on if you wish, but it ends like a poor-man's Sopranos Finale.** "There is something about yourself that you don’t know; something that you will deny even exists until it’s too late to do anything about it. It’s the only reason you get up in the morning. The only reason you suffer the shitty parts; the blood, the sweat and the tears. This is because you want people to know how good, attractive, generous, funny, wild and clever you really are. Please think I’m special. You share an addiction. We’re approval junkies. We’re all in it for the slap on the back and the gold watch; the hip-hip hoo-fuckin’ rah. Look at the clever boy with the badge, polishing his trophy; shine on you crazy diamond. We’re just monkeys, wrapped in suits, begging for the approval of others. If we knew this, we wouldn’t do this. Someone is hiding it from us, and if you had a second chance, you would ask, ‘why?’." -Jacob Green Fusenshoff stepped through the front doors and immediately felt at home. He’d never been here before, but once you’ve been in one you’ve been in them all. This library looked like any other, but with an exorbitant number of windows, which he liked. A few months ago Fuse found comfort in the darkest places he could find. Sitting in a room with the lights off, drinking while a television ran in the background, too low to hear. This was what he favored, but now was a different story. He finds hope and potential in daylight now. A somber peace and serenity that reminds him he may find purpose in this lifetime again after all. He looked around at the stacks of books—a reminder of a past life. He spent a lot of time browsing these towers of information in his youth. He felt powerful looking up at them, all that potential for growth of the mind. Knowledge rarely possessed, embracing him—an opportunity for anything, and so much choice. "Excuse me miss, where can I find the classic fiction section?" He asked. The old woman looked up at him from her computer, a bin of books half-full behind her. A century ago bins like that were full—before television and video games. "It’s the eighth row on your left. Can I help you find something specific?" she inquired. "No, but thanks anyway." Fuse smiled at the woman, wearing clothes she probably bought thirty years ago and spectacles from yesteryear. "I’m sure I’ll find something by browsing." A minute later Fusenshoff has a smirk on his face, sorting through so much excellent writing. Images pass through his brain at the speed of a machine gun. Thoughts flash through his brain, one after another, as he sifts through books he’s opened before. "The Sun Also Rises" reminds him of a beautiful, reckless woman and a young matador. It also reminds him of alcohol, which takes a conscious effort to remove from his cerebellum. He passes a collection of James Joyce, remembering the difficulty he had as a youth interpreting the stream of conscious style of writing the Irishmen was famous for. Hawthorne, Eliot, Austen, Tolstoy and Whitman welcomed him back with big grins; so many names, so many memories. Fusenshoff pauses, staring at a collection of paper that piques his interest. "Crime and Punishment"; he read it in the seventh grade; "The Rascal" as he used to call him. Here was a man tormented by death. A death brought forth by his own hand in a moment he’d give anything to take back. Fuse is all too familiar with Raskolnikov’s addiction to rumination. Here’s a yarn he can sink his teeth into. Maybe it’ll even bring clarity; his path may become less foggy. It’ll be a therapist for him, consisting of just ink and paper. Hours later, Fuse is halfway through his novel. He’s completely engrossed in the moment. A horse is being brutally abused as Fusenshoff stirs in his seat. He cringes as the pages turn, a look of contempt and despair painted on his face. As the rascal escapes from his nightmare, Fusenshoff can’t help but pause. He stares up at the ceiling, exhaling deeply, wondering why he’s put himself through so much torture for so long. Moments later, be comes back to reality and looks around the brightly lit room. Surprise grips him and won’t let go. He’s shocked at what he sees just a dozen or so yards in front of him. It terrorizes his insides for a moment as Fusenshoff looks at the man who saved his life just a few months ago—Jacob Theresi. Fusenshoff is stunned by the man’s presence in this library. Sure, the man lives in this city; Troy MI, where Fuse’s life almost passed. He fell through ice while walking along a frozen pond in the middle of a park, just miles from here.
