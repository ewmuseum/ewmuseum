Black Sheep

31 October 2009
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 10/31/09 You know what really fucking sucks? I mean, what really, truly, fuck-all completely and totally sucks? It's when nothing you do is ever good enough. When no matter how hard you try, no matter how sure you are that this is finally the time when the whole world turns around to your point of view, you just get chewed up and spat right back out again like a damn piece of expired beef jerky. Trust me, I know the feeling. It's one you become quite familiar with when you grow up with a twin sister that's the Queen of Fucking Everything in the eyes of your parents. I'm only like five minutes older than her, but yet for some reason, for 31 years, our parents have treated her like she's their perfect little baby angel, capable of doing no wrong. It'd be so easy to hate her, too, except for one little thing. They're right. Ever since we were staring at each other from opposite cribs, she's been pretty much perfect. She talked first, she walked first, she learned to read first, got her drivers' license first. Beat me to just about everything, and mom and dad were never shy about pointing out all of their Little Girl's accomplishments. Made me look like a fucking slouch in comparison. And I wasn't, I really wasn't. I just did my thing, my sister did hers. Just so happened that the folks approved of what she did a hell of a lot more than what I did. That'll happen when you live with the most straight-laced family in all of Cambridge, and if you know the kind of folks who hang around Harvard, that's saying something. Mom's a lawyer, dad's the president of Greater Boston Financial, and combined the two of them are about as dynamic and exciting as the United States Postal Service. They were all about Being Practical, about doing The Right Thing, and apparently, my life choices didn't always jibe with their perfect goddamn sensibilities. Who would've known culinary school would give them such a giant stick up the ass? Yeah, while my precious sister was off as the Belle of the Ivy Fucking League, wowing professors with every word of every essay she wrote, I was paying my own way to live in a ratty studio apartment in Hyde Park, New York and spend 12 hours a day learning how to brunoise a carrot or bake a perfect loaf of bread. Sounds like a boatload of fun, huh? Well, I was damn good at it, even though the folks didn't quite approve. Our family goes long back into those antiquated Boston Brahmin roots, you know. We were scholars, thinkers, politicians, captains of industry. Not cooks. Although my parents never outright said it, they thought that cooking was "below" our family. Shit, the closest my mother ever got to cooking when we were growing up was when she burned the hell out of a couple Swanson TV Dinners one February night when our Norwegian nanny Greta was trapped in a snowstorm out in Swampscott. They didn't understand that cooking isn't just what a good, obedient 50s wife does to pass the time while her husband's bringing home the bacon, it's a goddamn art form. No, I wasn't Picasso, or even that guy who did the velvet painting of the dogs playing poker, but I was pretty damn good. Graduated top of my class from the CIA, took an apprenticeship with a commandeering Frenchman at a three-star joint near Battery Park and moved on from there. Went to Chicago, L.A., even finally got to open a little place of my own just outside of Boston. Of course, it's not like I stayed at any of those places for very long. That'd be because of the other reason my parents were never too happy with me. It wasn't like my father never gambled. He'd drag us down to Saratoga for two weeks every summer so he could bet on the Sport of Kings while Mom went to the hot springs, and he kept a regular Wednesday night poker game with four other pompous windbags that kept our sitting room smelling like a humidor for a good three days afterwards. It's just, they never quite approved of their 17-year-old son sneaking his way into a seedy South Boston bar to plunk down the money I made from my summer lifeguard job on the Bengals covering the spread. It was great when I was winning. Kept the cash bundled up in an old dress sock that I stashed between the mattress and the headboard, and nobody was the wiser. All it meant was that my parents never had to worry about shelling out cash to maintain my Saturn and my CD collection was always that much bigger than the rest of the kids at school. Then, one day, when you're on the hot streak to end all hot streaks, you go for broke. Put it all on Florida State -7 at home against stinking Clemson, and a fumble at the goal line means the Noles only win by five. Boom. Outta there. You try to turn your luck back, but you've gone cold. You can't pick a winner, and all of a sudden, you're sneaking $100 bills from Dad's secret cigar box just to keep the sharks off of you as long as possible. Then comes that moment, when you're scheme's finally caught on to. Boom. Outta there. Again. Cut off, just like that. Goodbye cushy brownstone, goodbye summer home in Providence, hello a broom closet of an apartment in the North End before I finally got to school and the hell out of the Hub. Of course, it's not like it stopped then, either. Everywhere I went, there was a guy who knew a guy who had a bookie, and I just couldn't stop. And, you know, it turns out that bosses don't approve when you blow the restaurant's petty cash on the first weekend of the NCAA Tournament. Or on the sixth race at Belmont. Or on a college football parlay. That's why I'm here now. Why, after three of my restaurants got stars in the Michelin Fucking Guidebook before the age of 35, I can't even hold onto a job slinging hash at your friendly neighborhood greasy spoon. I'm like the Black Plague of the culinary world, and meanwhile, my perfect sister is off educating the future minds of America at Columbia and just got engaged to a guy who might as well be Jesus H. Christ himself for the way she talks about him. Hey, good for Sam. Just wonder what the folks think of us now. Her on the Ivy League pedestal. Me in this crummy little Catholic school auditorium, sipping my fourth cup of this piece of shit coffee and listening to a million other sob stories about how people just like me frittered their fucking lives away. Well, guess it's my turn. Better stand up and play nice. "My name is Thomas Wade, and I have a gambling problem..." ***** Lost in time... Lost in space... And meaning. "Exactly what the fuck did I just see?" Troy Douglas pulled Samantha Wade close to him as they exited the black box theater on the fringe of Columbia's campus. Around them, assorted ghouls and goblins congregated on the quad this chilly Halloween night. Costumed co-eds, however, weren't as much of a concern to Troy on this night as the bizarre spectacle he and his fiance had just departed. "Rocky Horror's a cult classic, Troy." "Emphasis on 'cult.'" "It's fun. Besides, Jeffrey from my seminar class asked me to come, and this was the only night I could make it." "Which one was Jeffrey, the transvestite in spike heels or the guy in gold hot pants -- help me God, I just used the words 'gold hot pants' in that order. You have my permission to kill me where I stand." Sam smiled, leaning her head into Troy's side as the pair walked in unison towards the street corner and an eventual cab ride back to her apartment. Fumbling into her pocket, Sam turned her Blackberry back on, and immediately, the screen lit up with a half-dozen missed calls. All of them from a very familiar number. As they walked, she listened to the messages, and as she listened, her bright mood darkened by the second. They stopped, and Troy peered down, seeing the concern on her face. "What is it, Sam?" "I've got to call my brother." Alertly, she found his name in her speed dial, praying for a fast connection. Straight to voicemail. "Tommy? It's Sam. You've got to tell me what's going on..."
