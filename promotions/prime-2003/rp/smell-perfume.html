Smell: Perfume

undated
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: Rev 98 This woman smelled like amber liqueur, all woody and wealthy. She bathed in Chanel. She was just like in my dreams; wearing long white gauntlets and sipping cheap, gaudy champagne. Her beauty clawed at my young eyes and forbade me from looking at anyone else. I glanced at my mother, but she did not look at the woman in the same way. My mother was shopping for new clothes for both of us; on her secretary’s paycheck, she wasn’t going to buy anything more than one fashion centerpiece for each of us, but she wanted the other people in our little Vietnamese-American community to know that we could shop at this place, J.C. Penney. She would enjoy more bringing home the J.C. Penney bags and parading them through the square at the center of our neighborhood than any actual wearing of the clothes. I held my mother’s hand as we drifted through forests of suede and cashmere. For us, this was like going to the zoo, except without the admission price. Any of the clothes in that room would’ve been worth more than the house I had grown up in. Now, we had some money; not very much, but enough. Nonetheless, my first experience in the formalwear section was rather surreal. My mother went to try on several dresses. No green card or identification would make her a bonafide citizen, in her mind; it was this new dress. An attendant walked over to me, sitting obediently outside the dressing rooms. “Have you been to our store before, buddy?” Now, I’m not going to say that my beauty evaluation skills had matured yet, in my pre-adolescent stage, but, as is the sign of a true model, I could size her up. She was pretty; maybe even very pretty. However, she wasn’t too pretty not to be working at J.C. Penney. “Yes, I’m waiting for my mother. She’s just trying on some dresses.” “Would you like someone to wait with you?” “If you want to.” I replied politely, but I also kept my eyes trained on the woman from before, who had meandered over here, following the scent of credit card receipts (we weren’t that American; so we didn’t have credit card debt and we weren’t both morbidly obese). Had she changed her dress? My mind was playing tricks on me, I thought as I tried (in a purely curious, juvenile way; like the way you want to see how the insides of a car create a moving vehicle) to strip her of the white business suit she was wearing. “What’s your name?” “Chandler.” “That’s a nice name.” “It’s not my real name,” I flatly stated, knowing that this would invite several questions I didn’t want to answer. “Oh?” When she leaned down to enter the same plane as me, I could see that her nametag read “Beverly.” Certainly that was her real name. She didn’t have to explain these things to eleven year-old boys. “What is your real name then?” “I’d rather not talk about it,” I mumbled, but then thought better of being rude to this perfect stranger, especially since my mother was notorious for second-guessing each outfit until she had to try on every damn one again. “Well, my old name…my real name is very hard to say, but I’m from Vietnam.” “You speak very good English,” swam the voice into my ears, the only part of my body that was attending to Beverly, for the woman had crept into view yet again. She was sneaky, crafty, and devious. She was standing behind a poor woman who was dreaming, just like my mother and me, in the formalwear section. The woman giggled quietly; to my surprise, Beverly did not try to intervene, but merely ignored both women. “Well, I live in America. You have to speak English. It’s like the rules.” “That’s true. My grandmother taught me how to speak English; she was from Russia. Do you know where that is?” Now I was no chump and just for a second, I let it slip that I was slightly impressed with my own knowledge. It would be a sign of the Tsonda to come. “Yeah, it’s the entirety of the North Asian continent,” I spat. A silence followed, after which I tried to rekindle the conversation. “I like to look at the map and try to remember things about home.” My mother, princess of good timing, emerged from the dressing rooms at this moment, carrying several dresses in her hand. Beverly frowned, hoping that this lower middle-class woman wasn’t going to waste her money on these clothes. She also clearly didn’t know my mother; she was the type of woman who was not content to leave the clothes she didn’t want in the dressing room. My mother returned all the dresses she wasn’t buying to their racks and then carried the one purple gown towards the service counter. She tried very hard not to beam, but through her crooked teeth and worn lips shown a smile, lit very dimly but clear for all to see, like a streetlight. She asked Beverly to hold the gown for her and we moved off towards the children’s clothes. I never fit right into clothes. I was too tall for 16’s, but I barely could keep 12’s up around my waist; a beanpole, in the truest sense of the word. My mother insisted we find a suitable (lolz) suit for me to wear to any upcoming soirees. My mother must’ve picked up every pair of khaki pants in J.C. Penney that day, sizing each one up instantly with the tried-and-true method of holding them up to me and then frowning. “Chan,” she would say, with a twinge of restrained glee at the thought of looking at more pants for me (she never used my Vietnamese name in public; it was reserved for situations where a normal person would have their full name used, e.g. John Kenzaburo Parks, don’t you dare raise your voice at me!), “I think…this may not be it.” Repeat. While my mother did this, I kept my eyes trained on the mysterious woman from before. She now made no attempt to hide the fact that she was watching us. This was the 80’s, but apparently she wasn’t afraid to show off her blatant racism, this fascination that the two yellow people were allowed in this signature store of middle-class Americana. For some reason, I was not put off by her racism. After all, there were some people who simply had never encountered anyone who was different, even here in the melting pot of the world. I denied the fact that such a beautiful idea could have these flaws that made her impenetrable. The true gall, though, was that she wouldn’t look me in the eye. Either she preferred to ignore my existence or she was ashamed of her own reaction. My mother finally found me the perfect combination of blue blazer (gold buttons completely necessary, in her eyes) and khakis. I didn’t have the heart to tell her that I hated dressing up; yes, the future model would have rather tromped around in his favorite plaid shorts and a dinosaur t-shirt every day of the year. So I tried them on and I honestly didn’t look at how I looked in them: I looked in the mirror and tried to imagine if they would make me look any different to the woman outside. I decided that if appearance was going to make a difference then she wasn’t quite the woman I thought she was. I walked out to show my mother and give her the full rotation (c’mon, you know I could work it as a little guy) and there she was, in her inauthentic glory: I caught her eye and there was the simpering acceptance; an admission of infatuation with the physical. As we bought the clothes and made our way out of the store, I tried to catch a glance of her but lost her among the peaks of clothing racks. Freud would tell you that every woman I’ve ever been has been an extension of my mystery woman. I don’t deny that the reason behind these conquests may come from her, but an extension of her? That’s ridiculous. Because of course, if you listened to the story, you know the name of the girl, the one whose eye I dream of catching. Her name is America.
