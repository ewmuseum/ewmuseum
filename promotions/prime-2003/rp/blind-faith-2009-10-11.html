Blind Faith

11 October 2009
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 11 October 2009 I once heard a story about a madman in a village who, every time that the annual rains fell, would run out into the night and scream in impotent fury at the dark clouds overhead. With his head thrown back, and his toothless mouth wide-open in a gaping maw, he would ignore the lashing of the frigid water as it whipped at him; drenched from head to foot, his thinning strands of hair stuck to his liver-marked head, he would demand that the weather refrain from dousing his land. Canute like in his own insanity this man would shake his fist at the lightning that flashed over-head, he would shout and try to drown out the roaring thunder, and rage through the night against Mother Nature herself. Every year, without fail, the same tableau would play out like a scene in a theatre. When the first storm hit he would scrabble out from the warmth and safety of his hut and run, naked, into the night. Insults and abuse would be shouted out at the top his lungs, aimed at the unseen power that dared to affront the man’s dignity by raining on him. The other villages used to come out and watch the man, but, over time, they got bored of his antics and eventually the only audience that he had was the moon itself, staring down with its large, unblinking eye, never actually acknowledging his antics in any way at all; and still he railed at the unfairness of the situation. As the years passed he found it harder and harder to face the full fury of the storm, his body was ageing, his bones and muscles weakening, but still he tried. Still he ensured that the storm would not have its own way; for nights on end during the rainy season he would go without sleep and challenge the rains. One night, as the first drops fell onto the village, something different was noticed and for the first time in many years the villagers came out to watch. This time, though, they realised the single difference from before; the old man wasn’t there. When his hut was checked it was discovered that he was lying there, cold and dead. The rain fell; the lightning flashed; the thunder roared and the storm itself raged on just as if the man’s existence had never mattered at all. For, to the storm, it hadn’t. - - - - - "So, do you think that he will actually show up then?" Smoke drifted up from the speaker's mouth as he exhaled around the remnants of the cigarette that hung from his lips. A haze of nicotine floated around his head, like some parody of a saintly halo, as he adjusted the viewfinder on the video camera that rested on his shoulder. A small red light came to life, just under the lens, as the camera was pointed in the direction of the questioned person "He will if he knows what is good for him, Steve," came the growled response. "Tyler told him to be here." "Which one?" "C’mon, surely that joke is running dry by now?" Johnny groaned. "Lisa Tyler gave the order." "Oh yeah, Johnny," chuckled the camera-man. "We all know how well Jay Phoenix responds to orders now, don’t we?" Even though the question was obviously meant to be rhetorical Johnny turned to face Steve, his finger pointing out directly into the camera. "Well just like everyone else who works for PRIME," Johnny growled once more, "he has only two choices about it, like it or …" A stillness came over Steve and even though he didn’t move, he didn’t make a sound, Johnny picked up on the change in his body language. He hadn’t heard anything, no footsteps, no sound at all that would herald anyone’s arrival but somehow he knew, without knowing, that they were no longer alone. "… or what?" With a sigh Johnny fixed a false smile on his face as he turned around to come eye to unseeing eye with the man known as the Eternal Flame. A wry grin was just visible, in the gloom, on Phoenix’s face as Johnny reached out one hand towards him. "Ah there you are," he said with false cheer. "We were just wondering if you had remembered this … erm … appointment?" For a few seconds Johnny stood with his arm outstretched, waiting for Phoenix to shake his hand, but he quickly realised that he couldn’t see it so brought his arm, self-consciously, back to his side. "Appointment," he repeated, mockingly. "I thought that it was an ‘order’?" The question hung in the air, the silence thick between the two men as Steve stayed still, his camera rolling. "Erm … what I meant …" Johnny stuttered as he tried to backtrack on his own words. Phoenix brushed past him, causing Johnny to move with him, speaking over his shoulder as he did so. "It doesn’t really matter what you meant," Phoenix said with a small chuckle. "All that matters is that for once what Lisa Tyler wanted and what I wanted happened to be the same thing." Walking over to a single panelled door Phoenix reached out one hand towards the handle and then paused, looking back over his shoulder as he realised that neither of the men had moved to follow him. "Are you two coming?" he asked in exasperation. "I thought that we were going to follow you in training, Jay," Johnny queried. "You know, to see how you prepare for a big match - but the exit is over there isn’t it?" He finished, indicating a set of double doors opposite to where Phoenix was standing. "Training will have to wait," Phoenix stated impatiently. "There is something much more important that I have to take care of so you guys can either do what Lisa wanted and follow me or not. Your choice!" With that he opened the door and quickly disappeared into the darkness beyond it, out of sight. "So," Steve asked quietly from behind his camera, the first words that he had spoken in the exchange. "What do you want to do boss?" "What the Hell could be more important than training, "Johnny mused more to himself than anyone else. "When he is only days away from a 5-Star title shot?" "I don’t know, "Steve replied as he pointed at the partially open door. "But I know someone that you could ask." "I have half a mind to just tell Phoenix to go screw himself and get the Hell out of here!" Johnny said with a snarl, but didn’t make any move to leave. "… and then you might as well tell Tyler that you don’t want this job anymore while you are at it," Steve remarked simply. "Because you know that she will fire your ass if you go back empty handed after Jay actually turned up as scheduled." "Dammit!" The anger was evident in Johnny’s voice, but it was laced with a sense of impotence too, as he realised the truth in Steve’s words. Without looking directly at Steve he barrelled his way over to the door and flung it wide open, staring down at a set of darkened stairs. Reaching inside the doorway he blindly groped along the wall. "Dammit," he repeated. "I can’t find a light switch!" "Here you go, I think that I can help with that." A small light, no bigger than a hand-held torch, came from just above the camera lens, illuminating a small aisle in front of the men. The stairs came into sharp view before disappearing into the murky darkness about twenty feet down. "Do you ever watch those bad teen movies?" Steve asked Johnny softly, after a moments pause. "What?" The reply was terse. "You know, those old slasher movies." "Not since I was a kid Steve, why?" Johnny asked in frustration at the whole situation. "Well you know in all of them there was always one thing in common," Steve continued in a hushed tone. "And that was when the kids did something very … very … stupid." "Like what?" Johnny asked with a hint of curiosity. "… normally something like going into a dark cellar." Without saying anything Johnny turned to look directly at Steve, his eyebrow raised. He swallowed, his Adam’s apple bobbing in his throat, before replying. "Oh shut up, Steve," he said dryly, licking his lips. "This is Phoenix we are talking about not some horror movie freak with a machete!" Both men stood at the lip of the door, staring down at the area of light that suddenly looked so much smaller. Neither moved for a few seconds until Steve broke the silence. "Sure, you are right," he muttered, "so anyway … after you." Johnny flashed him a foul look but realised that he had no come back at all and so, without another word, he started down, Steve and the floodlit camera close behind him. Step after step they walked down into the pool of light, the oasis in the desert of darkness, until they came to the bottom. Turning a small circle Johnny peered into the gloom, trying to pierce it; the camera and the light followed his motion. "Johnny?" Steve asked quietly, his voice as hushed as if he was in a Church on the Sabbath. "What?" Johnny replied, unconsciously dropping his tone to match his colleagues. "Have you seen the Blair Witch Project?" Steve asked as Johnny turned around to face the camera, his pupils as large as saucers. "Will you just drop it with the God damn horror movie stuff!" Johnny growled, his voice shaking a little. "I preferred the Manitou myself." With a high-pitched squeal that belied his size, Johnny almost seemed to float as he jumped and turned full circle at the sound of the voice from out of nowhere. As he landed he gave another small scream as he came face to face with Phoenix once more. Seeming almost part of the darkness itself the black clothed figure blended in almost completely. "Jesus Christ, Jay," Johnny bit off through clenched teeth. "You nearly gave me a heart attack!" Johnny’s breathing was laboured and shallow, his nostrils flaring as he tried to take in more oxygen. Phoenix simply stood in front of him, the small, enigmatic smile that had become so familiar evident on his face. "I didn’t take you for someone who would be afraid of the dark Johnny," he quipped softly, leaning in closer and his smile growing as Johnny, involuntarily, pulled backwards. "I’m not," Johnny stated emphatically, trying hard to make his voice obey his orders and stay firm; not quite making it. "Why is that," Phoenix asked softly, the camera only just picking up his words, "could it be that you actually have a brain in that head of yours and you have realised the truth?" Johnny stood staring at the smaller man in front of him, trying in vain to ignore the cold sweat that had gathered at the base of his spine, but unwilling to allow his palpable fear to show. "Yeah I know the truth Jay," Johnny said, "I learnt long ago that there was nothing to be afraid of!" Phoenix stayed where he was for a few seconds, mere inches away from Johnny’s face and then with a long, melodramatic sigh, he backed off until only his face was visible in the gloom, his body swallowed by the darkness. "Such a pity, you were so close as well," he sighed. "But you are so nearly right – there is no need to be afraid of the dark at all, only what is in it – that is what people never seem to see, you know … or rather they see only what is on the surface and never look any deeper." With a small movement Phoenix disappeared completely from view, the darkness enfolding him entirely. "Almost as if they were blind." "Dammit Jay I have a job to do, I didn’t come down here to play stupid mind games with you," Johnny nearly shouted. "I am not one of your opponents, you know, you cannot twist me inside out so easily!" "You are right." Phoenix chuckled as once again Johnny gave a small squeal of fear at his appearance from his other side. Reaching out one hand, almost tenderly, he squeezed Johnny’s shoulder. "So why don’t we get on with this?" "Just what is ‘this’ anyway?" Johnny asked as he struggled to regain control of himself. "Oh, didn’t I tell you?" Phoenix asked in feigned shock. "I have been told that we have a small vermin problem down here, so decided that I should do something about it." "Vermin?" Steve asked from behind the camera and for the first time Phoenix stared directly into the lens, as if seeking the man behind it. "Yes, vermin," he repeated with another small smile. "Rats, to be exact." "… and you couldn’t get the exterminators in?" Johnny asked sarcastically. "Of course I could Johnny, my friend, but it’s not like I am afraid of getting my hands dirty, is it?" Phoenix replied with a laugh. "You can ask Jason Natas, Hunter Sabuani or the High Flyer what I mean – if you can find them, of course; people have a habit of leaving PRIME after facing me, after all." With a sharp movement Phoenix brought his arm up out of the darkness and seemed to point directly past Johnny and Steve and before they could react there was a whoosh of air, followed squealing sound. Steve quickly spun around in place, allowing the camera to follow where Phoenix seemed to be indicating and then gave a small gasp of shock as the camera picked up the form in front of him. Rolled up into a ball lay a rat, just smaller than a house cat, with a small bolt protruding out of its side. Its eyes were open, its jaws wide in rictus, with a small trail of blood trickling through its fur to pool on the floor near its snout. "How the …" Steve muttered as he turned to face Phoenix again, ignoring the shocked and immobile Johnny beside him. The camera focussed in on Phoenix as he fitted another bolt into the small hand-held crossbow – no larger than a pistol – in his right hand. "How did I do that?" Phoenix queried, voicing Steve’s unfinished question for him. "When all reports have it that I am almost blind in one eye – helpless, you mean?" Phoenix brought his hand up to his right eye, still bloodshot, and smiled. "I am pretty sure why Gamble isn’t too worried about facing me, you know – he thinks that he has already got me beaten by taking out my vision." "And has he?" "Stevie, you saw the shot, didn’t you?" "How DID you do that?" "Blind faith - reflexes – speed - the joining of mind and body in one fluid action – years of practice – a lucky shot - magic – take your choice Stevie, it could be one or all of the answers, now couldn’t it?" Steve shook his head, not knowing what the answer would … could … possibly be and stared, his eyes widening, as Phoenix slowly brought the small crossbow up, seemingly aiming it directly at Johnny who was still staring at the impaled rodent. "Johnny …" Phoenix barely whispered, but it was loud enough as Johnny finally moved, turning slowly to face the man who had called his name, his eyes widening once more as he stared directly into the wicked point of the barbed bolt. "… duck!" In the years to come Johnny would never know if he had actually heard Phoenix’s word – his order – and moved in time or if he had simply fainted at the right time. Either way he slumped to the ground and the bolt flew harmlessly over his head, even if it was only by a mere inch. Steve remained fixated on the still form of Phoenix, the crossbow held motionlessly in his hand, and of Johnny as he lay foetal-like on the cold concrete floor. "Another rodent?" he croaked when he could collect his thoughts and voice once more. "You could say that," Phoenix said. "Depends on how you look at it." Something in his tone made Steve look away from Johnny and try to stare at him. He glanced around the small area of illumination, and then paused as he listened for movement, but he could neither see nor hear Phoenix - without knowing how he knew it Steve realised that Johnny and he were alone now. Panning the camera around in the direction he knew that the bolt had flown Steve narrowed the beam of light, forcing it to reach as far as possible, and then paused as he found the target at its furthest reach – just barely illuminating the bolt where it still quivered in what was complete darkness just moments before. Directly in the heart of a cardboard cutout of Tony Gamble. - - - - - In this time, the so-called ‘modern age’, there is a dichotomy of thought between scientists and theologians. They share no common ground at all, each believing that their own thoughts, their own viewpoints, are the only possible correct and valid ones. This, though, is nothing really new and in fact can be traced back over the centuries – over the millennia – in its many different guises. From the dawn of mankind there were those that looked up at the stars and feel to their faces in awe, calling out to the spirits of the night to protect them; others, of course, did something different. They looked – and thought – deeper. They looked for the reason in the chaos. While the theologians see the stars and realise that as they cannot comprehend their existence they have to fill the vacuum of their understanding with something – anything – and so attribute the celestial light show to the hands of an unseen, and yet omnipresent, creator. It doesn’t matter to them that the existence of said creator cannot be proven by any matter whatsoever, for after all that is the true meaning of faith. Sight unseen. The scientists, of course, didn’t – couldn’t – go that route. How could they when in their World if it couldn’t be seen, couldn’t be felt couldn’t – in some way – be quantified then it didn’t exist? Condescendingly nodding and smiling at the theologians and their little imaginary friend called God, the scientists sat back and examined the chaos from every conceivable angle. They scrutinised it in every fathomable way, in all its myriad minutia – dissecting it, and putting it back together until they came to the realisation that would shape their entire being, form the foundation of their existence. They couldn’t find the reason behind the chaos. Now, of course, this didn’t mean that there wasn’t one but, unlike the theologians, the scientists wouldn’t go with blind faith and instead decided that more study, more contemplation would be needed before they could decide on the truth – the immutable facts of the matter, so to speak. So the theologians knew that there didn’t need to be a reason, simply that the chaos was only such from the human perspective – the limited perspective. For them they knew that seen from the correct angle – from God’s perspective – all was plain and clear. Pre-ordained. Every man, woman and child having a destiny laid out by a higher power and so that was reason enough for them. Blind faith versus cold, hard logic. Belief over knowledge. Hope over a certainty. Since the dawn of time it has been the basis for the simplest of diversity among men; and still it goes on with two modern archetypes. The theologian; the hopeful one, the man who looks at the stars and sees the path of Heaven laid out and has the blind faith that things will work out simply because he wants them too. The ‘scientist’; the fact finder, the logic seeker, the man who looks at the stars and ignores the beauty – not even realising that it is there – in favour of wondering what they are for, how they got there and knowing that things will work out simply because he knows that they will.
