Things I Don't Much Care For

undated
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 4112007 HAY THAT RIMES! I had a friend once who I couldn’t much stand, but he tolerated me alright as not many do, so I let him stay around because I couldn’t trust myself if nobody was around. Sometimes I’d lose my appetite, and sometimes I’d sleep for days. Other times, I’d start getting all antsy, and I’d wander around and get up to no good. I met him one night at a bar. The meeting was brief; I was just in to try to get as many free bar peanuts as I could before the bartender kicked me back to the streets. I couldn’t afford to eat much at the time, and I knew I’d get kicked out and faster with each subsequent visit. However, my reasons for returning were twofold. I’ve just always wanted to be ejected from a bar by being thrown through the window. I had seen it in movies as a boy and had become entranced by the glitz and glamour of Hollywood. Most of the time, minimal force was used, and I was just ushered firmly to the door. The bartender hated me. He was one of those slimy Mediterranean fellows. “You come in here and you eat up all-a my peanuts. You drivin’ away the business!” is what he would say, and he’d pinch his fingers together and wave them in the air for emphasis on the second syllable of business. It was superfluous. He pronounced it with an extra syllable. Anyway, this friend of mine was sitting at the bar at the time, and he was sitting by himself getting drunker. I guess he found out that his wife was leaving him or something because that’s all he was talking about all night, but I was never known for my attention span, so it could’ve been something else. Maybe he just found out she was cheating on him or something, and he didn’t have the balls to confront her about it. I’m not sure. By the time he found me, I had already been kicked out of the bar, and I had fallen asleep in a sitting position against the wall. He stumbled out. He was yelling about something or other. In his piteous grievance, he had started making passes at some women who were genuinely uninterested. He was too blunt to catch on and his pursuits were tireless until staff responded to complaints and led him to the door as well. “Hey you!” he said. He caught himself on the wall, too drunk to stand on his own. I barely batted an eye. I was content there, just sleeping on the cement. “Hey you! What are you doing there on the ground?” I tried to ignore him, but he stumbled over the stoop at the door of the bar and tried to pull me up by the cloth on the shoulders of my moth-eaten rags. The cloth ripped, exposing my bony clavicles to the cold of night, and he noticed that I was displeased and held his hands out apologetically. “I’m sorry. I didn’t mean to do that.” I mumbled something, but I wasn’t sure what it was. The connection between the mind and the mouth was never sharp, so I’d often just make noise that did not correlate with my thoughts. Truth be told, I didn’t mind much about the shoulders at first. I just wanted this guy to leave me alone. But then I got to thinking about his wrinkled dress shirt with the buttons off kilter as if he had purposely buttoned them that way to look more disheveled before he started drinking that night. Then I saw his shoulders, and how they were completely clothed, and I wondered what I had done to deserve this and what he had done to earn covered shoulders. It seemed cosmic at the time, though I admit to being on opium at the time, so everything seemed like it was drenched in the glowing blood of wisdom. I could have sat there, weeping before imaginary Athenian fountains to curse the fates or ask favors from the gods, but I never believed in any of that bullshit anyway. “I’ll make it up to you. Let me put you up tonight. I’ll rent us a motel room.” Those god damned shoulders; I almost refused! We walked down the road a bit, and he kept complaining about his wife, that bitch, that whore, until we saw a dying neon sign off the side of the turnpike. A lot of the bulbs on the sign were sickly down to their tungsten filaments which cannot be seen (due to the brightness) in fresh bulbs. The man walked into the office to rent a room and get the room keys, and I figured he’d be a while because he wasn’t used to functioning this drunkenly. The sign said that there was air conditioning and basic cable in every room, and the no vacancies sign was unlit. Back before the cops started cracking down on prostitution, it was much harder to find a room. Now, for what it’s worth, it’s much harder to find a prostitute. The sheriff wasn’t crooked anymore, or maybe he was crooked, but just in a different way. Sheriffs are always crooked in some way or another. I thought I saw a prostitute over by the road off in the distance, but it very well could have just been a lady standing there, or it could have been a sign. I was too far away to tell, and my vision was blurry. I thought of walking over there, but decided not to when I realized I had no money and wouldn’t know what to say if I did. Although in those zygotic steps before I aborted my movements, I initially hoped it was a sign to fend away the uncertainty that would have arisen from approaching another living being. It wasn’t the highest of hopes, and the walk would have been fruitless. It didn’t matter anyway, because my companion was on his way back out with the key, and he slapped me on my exposed shoulder to lead me to the motel room. He led me up the stairs to a red door with chipped paint and finagled around with the key until he was able to force the door open. The lights were off, and neither one of us searched for the switch; the light from the neon sign slipped through the blinds well enough for me to see that there was only one bed with mattress springs exposed in certain places from overuse. I wasn’t much for beds. They were too fancy for the likes of me. I slept on a mattress I stole from someone’s garage for three years, and that was good enough. I had no use for the elevation of such mattress. I just thought that added the element of danger to sleep, and I preferred to keep the two separate. My friend angled the blinds shut, and I could barely make him out in the dark anymore, but I could see his silhouette arched towards the window as he peeked through two of the blinds that he separated with his middle and forefinger. “There’s only bed for one of us here,” I said. “You can have it. I never much cared for beds. I’ll be fine on the floor.” “Nonsense,” he replied. “Now listen here, buddy. I’ve got a proposition that may be worth your while.” I wasn’t quite sure where he was going with this. “I’m listening.” “I’ll give you ten thousand dollars- half now and half later- if you kill my wife.” He let the blinds snap back together and started pacing back and forth. “She’s supposed to be at her mother’s house for the weekend, but I know for a fact that she’s upstate fucking this coworker of hers, Enrique. He’s got a cabin somewhere in the woods.” “How do you know this?” “I read her emails.” He continued without hesitation, “anyway, I’ll give you the key to my house and I’ll write my address down for you.” He reached into his pocket and removed his house key from a keychain with several keys and their duplicates attached to it. I examined the key while he took out a pen and notepad and scribbled his address on it. It was a key, alright. “How do you want me to off her? Should I knife her? Cyanide up her oatmeal?” “I don’t care how you do it,” he replied, “as long as you make it look like an accident. Now, do you know where this is?” He handed me the slip of paper. “I can’t even read this.” “Never mind, then. Just head east out of here, get on the red line going north and get off at the Berwyn stop. It’s just two blocks east and a block and a half north from there. You got it?” “Sure.” “Come back here in a few days when it’s done. I’ll be waiting with the other five grand. You can eat whatever’s in the fridge in the meantime, and feel free to sleep in the master bedroom. I’ve got fresh pajamas in the linen closet. They should be about your size.” I nodded, but a nod in the dark isn’t much of a gesture. He put his hand once more on my unclothed shoulder. “Thanks a lot, buddy. I really appreciate it,” he said. He said it like I was lending him eggs for a cake. “And don’t forget to bring some proof, like a finger or something. Yeah. Bring the one with the ring on it.” *** And I suppose that brings me to the present. I’m on the red line going north. I think I’m going north. I don’t know for sure. It’s the shared bedroom of the homeless and the drunk alike. In the back of the car, there’s a man in a thick black coat passed out on the floor with his upper body poking into the aisle, and there’s vomit by him. Across the aisle from me, there’s an old man drinking a case of Busch light very cautiously in slow sips. He looks around suspiciously to see if anyone is watching him, and then he takes tiny sips from the can, and then he looks down and starts weeping to himself, but he’s old and I think of killing him instead because he’s asking for it. I can hear a woman yelling on the other side of the train. She’s calling some bearded guy a blowjob, and the bearded guy is mumbling something to himself. He’s not trying to ignore her, but he’s having a hard time concentrating on whatever he’s mumbling to himself and can only focus on one thing at a time. I feel alone on the train, and my thoughts start to distract me as the train rolls on. I feel uneasy about the wad of bills in my pocket, and I have to refrain from going to sleep. Any one of these haggard bandits would pick my pockets if I fell asleep. They’d knife me if I woke up while they were doing it. I feel even more uneasy than the old man drinking Busch light. A man gets on the train and sits next to me. He speaks very quietly. “Can you help me out, sir? I’m trying to get something to eat.” “I don’t carry change.” “You don’t have any change? I’ll take anything. I’ll take nickels if you have ‘em.” “You heard me right. I said I don’t carry change.” “Okay, sir. God bless.” The man stands up. He’s a lot taller than he looked when he was sitting down, or maybe he’s not tall at all, but he’s so skinny that he looks tall proportionately. I thought about maybe giving him the key to my friend’s house. He could get food from the fridge. I had already forgotten what stop was mine. I look around the train as the man works the door to the next car, stepping over the sleeping man in the black coat and his pool of regurgitation. It smells like the alleyways in here. I get bored with the notion of contract killing and start staring at the old man drinking the Busch light. “Toss me a beer, grandpa.” He looks at me and defensively clutches his beer against his chest. His cheeks are stained from tears, and I don’t like him at all, but he tolerated me alright as not many do. Maybe I’ll just see where this train goes, I think to myself. Maybe I’ll ride it out until the switch, head back south to the impoverished parts of the city and waste this cash on crack cocaine.
