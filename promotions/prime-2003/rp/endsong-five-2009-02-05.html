[Endsong] – Five

5 February 2009
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 05 February 2009 Twenty-six years ago “Can we go now?” The six year old boy, that would one day grow up to become Jay Phoenix, stood beside the ever growing mound of earth and stared, arms crossed petulantly across his chest, into the rectangular hole below him. His bright green eyes sparkled with frustration as he toyed at the loose earth with one foot. A small trail, about three or four centimetres in length and nearly as deep, showed that he had done the same thing more than once. “No.” The voice, a deep, rich baritone, came out of the hole a second before the speaker’s head followed. Beads of sweat ran down the deeply tanned face that smiled up at the young boy and, in that moment, there would be no doubt who this was as the resemblance was uncanny – his father. “But …” “I said no, Jay,” his father interrupted, locking glances with his son, “and I meant it. Now you have the same two choices that you had when you last asked me that …” he glanced at his wrist-watch, smiling ruefully as he swung his gaze back to the boy’s face, “… which was all of seven minutes ago – a new record, I think – you can wait there and moan at me every few minutes or you can help me dig.” “I don’t see why we have to do this,” Jay exclaimed, throwing his hands into the air. “It is the weekend, dad, even your students aren’t here so why are we?!” “My students aren’t here because it is – as you so rightly pointed out – the weekend,” his father said with a mischievous grin. “I don’t expect them to work at the weekend.” “That isn’t fair!” Jay wailed. “You are making me work!” “Actually,” his father countered. “I am making you turn up – it is up to you if you work or not.” “I don’t want to,” the child said, stubbornly. “It is just stupid pottery and bones anyway!” With a sigh Jay’s father – Jesse Flame-Eagle – placed his hands flat on the ground and pushed himself out of the trench. Wiping his hands on his mud covered jeans, doing little to actually clean them; he knelt down and rested both palms on his son’s shoulders. Jay tried to look away but, gently, his father took him by the chin and forced him to meet his gaze. Twin pairs of green orbs stared at each other – one full of childish ire while the other was filled with calm patience. “You know that isn’t so, Jay,” his father stated softly. “You know that this is more than just ‘pottery and bones’ as you call it. This is a former site of our People – your People. This is history come to life. This is how we know wwho we are and where we came from.” “I know,” Jay agreed, “but …” “But you would rather be spending your weekend with your friends?” That morning, as they had been loading the car for the weekend trip deep into the forest covered foothills three hours drive from Flagstaff, Jay had looked longingly at some of the other children as the prepared for their own weekend. Thoughts of swimming in the lake, playing Lacrosse or just hanging out and doing nothing were running through his head as he watched more and more of his father’s equipment being laid out. Not many of his father’s graduate students knew exactly what all the equipment was actually for but jay – who had started being taught about those things from before he could walk – knew exactly what each and every archaeological tool was for. He also knew that he had two days of using them or avoiding using them. Neither option appealed to a six year old who lived for the weekends. None of his excuses made any difference, however. His grandfather was away on business and his mother was on a retreat of her own, through the very same college that his father taught at. There was no escape. It was going to be him, his father and the bones. It was going to be a long, long weekend. “It isn’t that …” “Then what is it, Jay?” “I just don’t see what the bones can tell us,” Jay blurted out. “They are just bones!” Standing up, resting one hand comfortably on his son’s head, Jesse looked around him. The clearing that they stood on was peaceful, the early afternoon Sun sending dapples of shadows dancing between the trunks of the trees that had stood there for hundreds of years. Bird song was the only thing that stirred the silence and that only added to the tranquillity. Pushing Jay in front of him, gently guiding him to the edge of the trench, Jess knelt down and pointed inside. “Those lines,” he said as he pointed to different colours of earth running horizontally through the trench, “are called strata. From them we can actually see the passage of time. We can tell when things happened; when people lived; what they did; when they died; even how they died.” “How?” Jay asked, suddenly intrigued. “You mean like in grandmother?” “Possibly,” his father nodded. “Though in your grandmother’s case she had been ill for a long, long time. When she died it was simply her time. It was gentle and peaceful.” “… and these bones are different?” “Yes.” Reaching down to the edge of the trench jess carefully lifted up a large, slightly concave, piece of bone. “This is a sternum, Jay, a chest bone.” He patted his son’s chest, eliciting a small giggle, before poking his finger through a hole in the bone. As he wiggled it at his son the laughter got louder. “What’s that, daddy?” “That is a story of this man’s life, Jay,” his father said, suddenly serious. “That tells us that this man didn’t die. He was killed.” “What?” “It is too regular to have been an accident, you see,” he explained, gently. “This is a bullet hole.” “No way? Someone murdered him?!” “No, I don’t think so,” his father said, putting the bone on the earth beside him and pointing into the trench again. Jay leant forwards, holding on to this father to keep his balance. “See that piece of flint? The one shaped like a triangle? That is part of an axe-head.” “Like grandfather’s one for the logs?” “No – like grandfather’s one that hangs above the fire.” “His tomahawk?” “Yes.” Jesse stared at his son, a small smile on his face. “A little like your grandfather’s tomahawk but this one has been used. This one has seen use. Do you see those marks on the edge? They were probably caused in a battle.” “He was a warrior?” “Probably.” His father admitted with another smile. “That is the thing about the stories that the bones tell us, I am afraid, it is never loud. They just whisper and we have to catch their words on the wind and put the missing pieces together.” “You sound like mummy!” “I know,” Jesse sighed, theatrically. “I used to be a real scientist but being married to an anthropologist has corrupted me and turned me into a romantic.” “I’ll tell her that you said that!” “… and I’ll tell her that you didn’t eat your greens last night.” “You ate them for me!” “I know,” his father laughed. “So we will both be in trouble!” “… maybe I won’t say anything then?” “Maybe that is a very good idea.” “What is that, daddy?” Jesse looked into the trench, where Jay was pointing, and squinted as a small flash of colour peeked out from the earth. Stepping into the trench he gently moved the debris from around it and breathed a small sigh as he pulled out a circular piece of stone with faded colours decorating it. “Ah, Jay,” he said, turning to hold it up to his son, “your eyes are better then mine, my child. You have found another piece of this warrior’s story, you know!” “I did?” Jay exclaimed, breathless. “Wow! … erm, what is it?” “I am so glad that your grandfather isn’t here to hear you say that,” Jesse stated with a chuckle as he climbed back out of the trench to sit beside his son. “He would whip me for you not recognising a medicine shield when you saw it.” Brushing some more dirt off the stone he held it out so that Jay could see it clearly. About the size of his palm, the stone was roughly smoothed into a circle and, on its surface, was four faded colours – red, black, white and blue. “Medicine?” Jay asked, innocently. “Did the man take it to feel better?” “No, Jay,” Jesse laughed, “and maybe I would deserve that whipping! He wore it, to protect him and help him connect with the spirits.” “Why?” “Gods, Jay,” his father chuckled, ruffling his son’s hair. “Don’t you know that why is the most difficult question in the Universe?” “It is also the most important one!” “You are right,” his father blinked, looking at him. “How did you know that?” “You told me that,” Jay said, “last time we went camping.” “So I did.” His father admitted. “Well we can’t have such an important question going unanswered … but first let me ask you a question.” Reaching down into the loose earth Jesse sketched out a square with his finger before looking back at his son. “What is one plus one plus one plus one?” “Four!” Jay shouted in glee after a moment’s thought. “And how many sides does this square have?” “Too easy, daddy!” he giggled. “Four again!” “Actually, Jay,” Jesse smiled, with a wink. “It has five.” “What?” Jay asked, confused. He stared at the square and then back at his father. “How?” “Each side of the medicine shield represents a different direction. East, West, North and South.” Jesse stated, his tone taking on the familiar timbre of a teacher. “And each of those directions is shown by its colour. Red for East; Black for West; White for South; and blue for North. Each of them is also represented by a Spirit – East for success; West for death; South for peace and North for defeat. All of our People believed – believe – that you can find your destiny through following and using the medicine. By allowing it to guide us.” “It is still four though!” Jay said, resolutely, as he knelt down beside his father and traced each side of the square. “See! Four!” “You forgot another ‘side’ Jay,” he stated, with a smile, as he poked his finger into the earth inside the square. “The four directions, the four colours, all form the circle of life around the centre … we call that ‘aiyana hakan’ – the eternal flame.” “That is like my name!” “Well, ‘Little Flame’” his father smiled. “It is certainly very close. It is that ‘inner side’ – the one that is surrounded by the other four – that is important. All the directions, all four sides, offer their different challenges, their different hopes. Each on in turn can be used to aid you or hinder you. It is what is on the inside – the eternal flame, the balance of self – that matters most.” “So,” Jay said, almost to himself. “A square has five sides, not four … Miss Davidson will be pissed when I tell her that she was wrong.” “Your teacher wasn’t wrong, Jay,” Jesse laughed fondly, “and don’t say pissed either, or your mother will kill me!” “I don’t understand though!” Jay admitted, staring at his father intently. “How can you get five sides when there are only four!” “It is the lesson itself, Jay,” his father said as he got to his feet, pulling his son up into a warm embrace.” Don’t worry, you will understand one day … for now though …” “I know, I know,” Jay said, holding his father tight. “Time to dig!” Dropping down to the ground, Jay suddenly looked up into his father’s eyes with a gaze that belied his young age. Reaching out he took his father’s hand and held it, tightly. “Daddy,” he said, earnestly. “Will your bones still tell me stories?” “They may, Jay” his father said with a small shiver. “Don’t worry, though, I am not going anywhere for a very long time!” Less than a year later Jesse Flame-Eagle, and his wife Huyana, were buried.
