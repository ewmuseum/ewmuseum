Just A Man

7 March 2008
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 3/7/08 “‘Faith’ means not wanting to know what is true.” -Friedrich Nietzsche In the empty pew, I was squirming out of my skin. Unable to sit still after two cups of coffee and impatient with waiting, I stood up again. Surveying the church was a massive undertaking, I realized, enduring a head rush that could knock out a yak. A couple weeks ago, mind and body had suffered a harsh breakup; they weren’t yet on speaking terms, resulting in strange muscle twitches and headaches. The man who emerged from the church’s bowels surprised me. His deep brown skin shined like refracted headlights. Bereft of light, the church’s interior still traced his features adeptly. His ears strayed from his head, perky, too big in size. And his face, that mold of a gargoyle, was all jowls and loose skin; it defaulted to a soft wince. A nose snuck in there somewhere, just barely visible amidst the clamor of the minister’s face. “Bertrand Russell?” Those bizarre ears perked up and he rearranged his gaze, looking me right in the eyes. He stepped out of the pulpit’s shadows, looking warmer in demeanor than I had imagined. Extending his hand, he smiled through immaculate white teeth. “Hello, Chandler.” It occurred to me that DiPendo must have let every clergyman in the San Diego area know there was a Chinaman on the loose with a crisis of faith. “Please,” he coaxed, “call me Father Russell or Bert”. I unconsciously leaned back on the pew, which Father Russell took as an invitation to sit down next to me. We did not shake hands, coalescing instead as we both looked forward to the front of the church. “This church is celebrating its 50th anniversary this year.” I nodded, unsure of how one congratulates the maintenance of fifty years of faith. The visceral urge to leave this place would not go quietly; self-interest held me in place. In the face of Culture Shock’s daunting tasks, I needed cosmological scope. My insides laughed at me for having become so cerebral. “But evidently, your wayward soul needs instruction?” “I dunno,” I lied. “I’m not quite ready to confess a lifetime of sins to someone I barely know. Honestly, I was hoping you had more…objective answers.” His hazel eyes surged with quiet life. “DiPendo told me you had been a spiritual advisor to him after he lost his wife. Anyone headstrong and wise enough to do that…” With a nod of my head, I tried to communicate a respect for my shrink that had long lived unspoken. “The doctor is a man for whom I have the utmost respect. We are very alike,” he mused. I wondered if that could really be true, but didn’t air my doubts. Despite his somnolent tone and diction, the deacon was intense, almost quietly omniscient. His words were fact. “I’m glad you’ve come.” That made one of us. I cracked my knuckles and shifted my weight. My eyes gravitated towards the hand next to mine. It dipped and shivered with some kind of palsy, a tic that Father Russell seemed to be suppressing as well as he could. A brittle burnt claw. I was humiliated when he saw the look on my face. “Don’t worry, you’re certainly not the first person to stare.” His stunted laughter only drove home my shame; this was not an issue to giggle away. In his house of God, I was leering like a wino at a peep show. “I have crippling arthritis in this hand. For years now, the pain’s been so bad that…well, you see what’s happened.” “How do you live with that pain? Isn’t it unbearable?” “Chandler, as trite as you might find this, I don’t think anything is unbearable with the grace of the Lord.” I did, indeed, find that trite, but reverently stayed quiet. Russell had more to say on the subject. “If Jesus suffered unimaginable pain on the cross for days, the slings and arrows of this world can all be conquered. This is why we pray. We pray for absolution.” Absolution. I’d been looking for it in m own life, just not through any Supreme Being. I wanted…forgiveness? No, merely a clean slate. An acknowledgement from somewhere that sins past were not precursors to future punishment. Not Hell, but my mind’s own prison. If you haven’t noticed, there’s always cosmic limbo in the space between my ears. “I guess this is as good a segue as any….but what compels you to believe? I mean, not in the psychological sense. But what makes you believe that some North African guy from two thousand years ago could walk on water by some miraculous gift of God?” What’s worse? Belief or antagonistic nonbelief? The jowls descended, opening up on a bright smile. “Facts tell us that a man named Jesus from Nazareth lived during a distinct historical period, Chandler. Sources also come as close to proof as possible in claiming that he was killed by government officials, almost definitely by Pontius Pilate.” Not facts I would have disputed, but the slight hunch in his shoulders betrayed that Russell wasn’t done. “The rest is all up to whatever you believe in. I believe that Jesus taught because he spoke to God. He had the personal relationship with the Creator towards which we all strive. I believe this not because I was taught…I believe because an inkling in my own heart once told me to go with God.” I frowned, dismayed that he and I were speaking on such different planes. Russell was still the acolyte, ineffably touched by God in a way that inherently defies explication. How was I to understand his deeply personal connection? And what good was it to try? “Father…” The words sounded beleaguered off my tongue, completely crisp and clear but driving at some blizzard of doubt. “You seem like a well-rounded dude. Not the type taken in by rhetorical bull. But how do you expect me to reconcile my own disillusionment with the idea of God? When you talk about him, you seem far away, on some island I can never get to.” His head nodded back and forth, lolling like a ripe fruit. And for a long moment, neither of us said anything. I looked forward, only catching him out of the corner of my eye. It was strange that we were still sitting side-by-side. My passionate avoidance of confessional had worked, slipped into this deep grey conversation. “This is the crux of religion, Chandler. It sounds far-fetched and hokey until you buy into it one hundred percent. Most people, good people a lot of them, who go to church once a month, pay alms, and then leave God out of their daily lives…they’re not much better off than you. We are defined by a black-and-white spectrum.” He couldn’t resist mumbling, “it’s hard work, saving souls.” “So how do you convince someone who can only believe what they see?” “Do you believe in the cellular makeup of the planet?” He retorted quickly, having heard this same argument many times before. “You can’t see that, yet we take it for granted everyday.” I was testing him, trying to find a chink in God’s holy armor where I might wriggle in. Either that or, in being repelled, could I bring myself still to not believe? Clucking his tongue, Bertrand awaited the next payload. “A century of science says it’s so.” Weak to rely on the hearsay of the scientific community, but I wanted to draw something from Father Russell. I wanted him to want me to believe. How could the most imperialist religion of all time not be interested in the soul of yours truly? “There’s an interesting set of scientific research behind the existence of God as well.” Bombshell time. “What?” I finally turned to look him in the face, to catch a fleeing grin. It escaped his face before he fully let on, but I knew. “All sorts of different things. The suggestion that the power of prayer can truly help cancer patients. Many flaws with the “Big Bang Theory” that suggest the need for a facilitator. The fact that we live in the literally the most perfect planet in a perfect galaxy in a perfect universe.” “I guess…” It was clever, but the argument wasn’t what swayed me. It was the man in front of me. Father Russell had no need to dabble in the fancies of science for any reason. His flock would receive him based on his faith and others would make their decisions similarly. Did he keep this information for the occasionally spiritual? The unpious and unwilling? “Why would you need to know all that? You’re a man of faith. Where is the place of logic in that?” “If someone would rather accept logic in following a path, I don’t want to be the thing that stops them. To ply my trade, I use everything at my disposal.” Seeing the sour contortion of my face, he reached into his pocket and retrieved something. “Here,” he said, thrusting it into my face. It was a tiny set of rosary beads, as glowing as if they’d been plucked from a nest. The white orbs gleamed in shadows. “Rosary beads. I’d like you to have these ones. If you don’t want them, fine by me. But if you want to start somewhere, please keep them. I’d like to guide you down the path of spirituality if you’re wiling to let me do so. But I think that’s enough for today.” Unimpressed with the results, but not in a hurry to satisfy my moral quandary, I grasped them. “Thanks, Father. I…I might be back. Who knows?” I shook his hand and he put both hands on mine. Without saying a word, his lips finally curled back into full-frontal smile. It was an almost morbid thing, that pained face in its deepest expression of content. Without another word, I filed out of the pew and strolled back down towards the entrance. The San Diego breeze was beautiful on my face. It stroked my hair with a feather’s touch as I made my way onto the sidewalk, carefully avoiding God’s grass. I looked back at the unimpressive, squat church. For a moment, my step was heavy but I turned towards the car and made my way. I grasped the rosary beads strongly before throwing them unceremoniously onto the car seat next to me. My rabbit’s foot.
