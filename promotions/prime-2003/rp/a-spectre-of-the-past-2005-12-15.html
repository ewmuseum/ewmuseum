A Spectre of the Past

15 December 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 15/12/05 A Spectre of the Past In the city of Chicago…As the evening shadows fall…There are people dreaming…Of the hills of Donegal… 1940’s Ireland. Open your eyes. What do you see? I’ll tell you what I see: I see people struggling to live. I see the damage the hand that harried and held has caused. I see pain, I see hurt and I see death. It’s difficult to understand how it was ever at this stage of desperation…to take in the fact that all the extravagant buildings and monuments of today, the commute and infrastructure, all spawned from a country clad in veils, covered in sheets to hide the embarrassing scene beneath. Look at the Ireland of today; look at the cities and towns. See factories humming their relentless drones, men in suits moving to and fro offices, women working outside of the home. The Celtic Tiger had roared they say, and the economy had powered into life behind it. They believe Ireland is a prosperous country. For the first time, working class parents can send their children to universities. They believe life is good. But how can it…when the crumbling foundations it was built upon are still intact? The politicians were hailed as heroes, as the men who had delivered a prosperous Ireland, a mighty nation. The people blind…they never seen the twenties slipped into back pockets when nobody was looking. Now I ask thee again, how could it have been built on such weak foundations? The answer is, the Ireland of today, is all a lie. For today, nothing is new. If you paint a cracked old wall, is the wall new? No. Ireland wasn’t renovated, fancy pictures were put up to cover the holes. So, where’s the evidence you say? If people have jobs and welfare being offered is good, where are the facts that can back up this truth? Just look beyond what you see…look beyond the bustling city centres to the Terrace houses, disguised by the shadow of an old warehouse. Look at the old, men and women of honour who’d broken bones shovelling coal so the stately of this nation could have a warm home. Look at them now as they struggle to live on ten lids a week. Look beyond the cities. Look at rural Ireland. The heart of Ireland. Open your eyes properly and tell me what you see. Poverty. Primary occupations such as farming have long since been left behind, and with it, the farmers who had produced food for a country when they needed it the most. A nation of farmers (among others), the salt of the earth, were spat on. It was wrong back then, it led to the fucking bullshit of today. It was all so simple for the men at the top for as long as the people of the cities were blinded of their comrades’ plight. They were divided by an intriguing device the government had long since found invaluable. Division. Plant a seed of doubt between the city and country folk and watch that tree of woe blossom. They were brothers, separated by a conniving Father giving a little for you and a lot for him. It’s still very much in place today. Now look at sixty years ago again, the basis of all today’s problems. Hunger raged through the country. There were no jobs, no prospects, no future. People, who had left it, would look at it years later and see a different Ireland. They would see the Ireland of today, one which they would now perceive as peaceful and prosperous. But they thought this only because of what had preceded it. Such deprivation would starve a country and choke it’s future. That’s the point. Early of the 1940’s had no future. And there was only one way out… … 1947…Was the year it all began…Deadly pains of hunger, Drove thousands from the land… Maybe those good people, who left, would have stayed, took up the bricks and built this country from scratch if they had seen they needed to destroy all the wear and tear and burn it like chaff in a fire first. Maybe. I suppose we’ll never truly know. It doesn’t matter either because Irishmen left their home with heavy hearts, a sod of earth packaged away so that perhaps when then they thought of home, they might make the vision real with it as if it was some form of talisman. The O’Shea family lost one to this travesty…a daughter and a sister, the lone sibling of the deceased Eoin O’Shea, grandfather of Paddy O’Shea. Although it pained him to reminisce, his memory was crystal clear of the event when his sister walked away. … He had scrawled ‘5/7/47’ raggedly down onto the back of an old black and white photograph before he had left the house. The photograph was ancient, a yellow jaundiced tinge showing its age. He didn’t want her to forget when she left you see…just in case time runs away on her. It was a simple photograph, three people – two girls and one boy, posing in front if their beaming mother and father. With a strong male hand clasped on his shoulder, the boy was smiling and so were his two sisters. It was simple back then, a tough life but it was without heart break or woe. The boy isn’t smiling now, as a tear trickles down his face. He isn’t a boy either but a man. The man is Eoin O’Shea, twenty three years of age. “Aye’ll write…aye swear,” a soft female voice said. It was wavering, showing the girl to be on the brink of breaking down. But her tone also suggested she wouldn’t fall to pieces now. Not when she needed to be strong the most. “Don’ make promises ye can’ keep. Ye’ll go off there and make a life fer yerself an’ forget all about us…” the man tried to smile but it collapsed mid effort and now his bottom lip began to tremble. He noticed his eyelids were tightly closed as if it would make the ordeal any easier. He opened them and looked up at his sisters’ face. She was young and pretty, her hair curly and black. She was the odd one out in the family with her hair, what with everyone else housing a red mane of hair. But that was her all over, standing out from the crowd, so flamboyant and jubilant. She was more than that to him. She was his big sister and in the dog eat dog world, she was his rock. Strong as hell, he can still remember how he came home in tears not eight years old because John Cavanaugh had hit him with a rock and left a lump the size of an egg. Well she picked up a frying pan and marched down the street and lumped him herself. Nobody messed about with Jessica O’Shea, hard as nails and twice as sharp. Eoin choked back a wail he felt forming on the back of his throat. Tears had been streaming down his face now for the past five minutes but he’d managed to keep himself silent, at least showing to be trying to support her now. He dared not look at her electric blue eyes for fear she’d abandon all her plans for him. And she had some marvellous plans. She had talked for weeks of theatre. She had the face for it he reckoned (but of course would never say) and the will to compete. But she was nervous, and the fact she was frightened the young O’Shea. He knew little of the dangers of leaving this country but somehow, seeing his sister uneasy, made him realise something… Some of them knew fortune,And some of them new fame…More of them knew hardship,And died upon the plain… …Things never go to plan. Jessica was three years his senior but oft times, with his mother dead from the chill and his father diving further and further into alcoholism, it felt like she was his true parent. When their sister died when he was thirteen from what the doctor had called ‘tube er cul o sis’, she wouldn’t shed a tear. Not in front of him anyway. Not that she was cold or heartless, but in her sixteen years on the earth she had acquired an unrelenting, uncompromising thirst for survival and strength. She took on the grief of the affair so Eoin didn’t have to, And somehow, he knew she did. Hell, anything the good Lord wanted to test her with would just bounce off her he had come to believe. She was a rock by which Eoin made his foundations on. On which he built his own life. Thank God for her because if she hadn’t have been here, he might be drinking from the same glass as his father. Eoin finally looked up. It was the same old filthy dock he’d grown up with. The rusty ships had reminded him of tin cans and he would think of how grand it would be to be on one. All around were men and women crying and weeping. It was a good day for a new life he supposed. It was noisy yes but everything around him seemed so silent. And that only made things more difficult. “Eoin…if-“ “Don’ if…” Eoin whispered now, finally bringing himself around to look at her in the eye. “Aye want ye t’ do this but ye kno’ aye can’ leave here…no’ with Kathleen expectin’. My life is here. Yours begins on tha’ boat. Don’ if don’ but, jus’ go.” They journeyed not for glory…Their motive wasn’t greed…A voyage of survival,Across the stormy seas. Now Jessica was crying and she moved desperately to stop from ruining what little make up she had on. Clad in her best attire, a light red and white striped full length dressed, she had got up that morning strong and proud, determined to do what she swore she would do and be as well presented as she could. All change, forward ahoy. Now she was stumbling, realising how much her life was going to be desolate without him. And then it happened. So quick and so fast and yet so damaging. A flicker of doubt and for the first time in a long time she lost confidence in herself. Almost bursting from her spot, she threw her arms around Eoin and began sobbing in his ear. “A-aye…aye don’ kno’ if aye can do this…wha’ if…wha’…” Eoin ran a finger through her hair and held her close, a hand clamped tightly on her shoulder. He kissed the top of her head then held her in front of him so she was looking into his face. “Jessica…aye don’ think Chicago is ready fer you but they better start makin’ plans. Jessica…don’ ye see, ye have t’ go an’ ye have t’ be strong. Yer so much better than this place. The history, the people, the land. It’s all worthless if ye can’ live yer life appreciating it. We go hungry at least once a week, Da won’ work another day in his life, he’s too damn drunk t’ even see ye off. As fer me at the coalyard, aye’m strugglin’ t’ hold onto tha’. Ye have t’ go because a country like this can’ look after wha’ ye have. Yer special Jessica an’ aye kno’ yer goin’ t’ live a long an’ happy life in America because people like you are made t’ live like tha’…” Jessica hugs Paddy again, this time fiercely. He parted the hair around her ears and whispered. “But don’ forget me…please.” “Aye couldn’t if aye tried.” Without another word, Eoin let go of his sister for the last time and took the photograph from his pocket. Silently, he put it in her pocket, both of them now not wanting to say another word to cheapen what was said. He gazed at her for another few moments and then quickly turned and walked away, his feet feeling awkward on the cobblestones below. He would be inconsolable later but for now, all he wanted to do was get out of the dock before he let it all go. He knew…it wouldn’t be easy… They spread throughout the nation,They rode the railroad cars.Brought their songs and music,To ease their lonely hearts When he was near the gate, he turned and looked. From afar, he could see his sisters red and white dress standing out. She seemed to be finally boarding the ship. It was difficult to see, even when he squinted, but it seemed she had paused on the walk way and turned around. He couldn’t be sure but he hoped to God till the day he died that his suspicions were right. That she had hesitated for him and looked back to see where her rock had got off to on this fine May morning. That she would be as lost without him as he would be without her as she sailed out of his life forever To the city of Chicago… Away away young Jessica go, and may you never forget the face of your Eoin who loved you more than any brother could ever love his sister. Later, standing in the lashing rain which had decided to pour down on him at the coalyard, he asked himself if he would ever she her face again… He asked the same question everyday until the good Lord took him away from his questioning mind. ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~ Paddy O’Shea sat by candlelight in his grandfather’s study. He is perched on a brown leather seat, his legs tucked under a desk. And he is weeping, and in the impending his silence, his sobs pierced the air. Paddy O’Shea didn’t cry. That’s not to say he never did, but instead, he had got to the stage of his life where he was now able to safely say, he could control his emotions and to the point where he could transfer this emotion into more practical uses. This had infuriated his Father when he was being punished. His father would lash at him with the buckle of his belt, welting him relentlessly, harder and harder until his face was searing red. And Paddy knew what he wanted. He wanted to break him, mould him into the shape he wanted him to be. But he never could and eventually, Danny O’Shea, leaving his son an inch from death, had to back down. Paddy simply transferred his pain into determination you see, it was all control. The thing was, Danny was never even close to making Paddy break. Danny thought he was inciting a desire for mercy, so he would eventually fall on his knees and ask for pity. And he would have gladly gave it to him. If he just backed down. Please. But he would never yield, he would never bow. Yet…despite these gruesome tests…now…he finds himself to be failing. And now, Paddy is hunched over his grandfathers oak desk, cleared save a single piece of paper, a torn envelope sitting beside it. He sobs hard; for his ducts have indeed been nigh on bursting for so so long. He sobs until his throat feels like it is lined with glass, and his vision is completely blurred from waterlog. Again and again, he heaves, misery so great it has enraptured his very heart. And all that’s on his mind is his that fateful day fifty eight years ago… He hadn’t been there obviously, but as a boy, he remembers the day his Grandfather had told him it, noting the sound of such sadness in his voice. He had noticed how every morning, he would look down the lane and expect that red and white and dress to come bounding towards him, how he would occasionally walk to the docks, almost as if he could enter back into 1947, and ask his rock to please stay home… It had destroyed his father, such grief and loss was not made for one man. He had waited fifty eight years, praying for a response, his hope never dwindling…it was goddamn hope that fucked him in the end. Paddy tried bitterly to shake the thought from his mind. He tried looking at the pictures on the wall, mostly enlarged prints of old photographs, framed. There was his grandfather now, a twenty something year old man, his high collar and broad shoulders bearing such a virile frame. He noticed that in all his years with him, he had never reached the same happiness as he had in that photograph. Paddy managed to fall silent, feeling now he was able to choke back the tears. Tears drying on his face, he pushed himself up from the chair and walked over to the mantelpiece. There was a clutter of ornaments, Toby jugs and china dogs among other things. Then a picture in the middle, his grandfather and his sister in a phoney studio setting. He had his large arm around her slender shoulders. And they were smiling. He felt his eyelids grow hot again and before he could help himself, he was crying again. W-w-hat’s w-wrong with m-me? He felt helpless because now he just couldn’t stop. Before he knew it, words formed on his lips and with a whimper, he said it to the empty room. “Aye’m…s-sorry.” Five days…that’s how long he had lasted before he fell. Before he came to the blinding revelation that his grandfather was in fact, a good man, that he didn’t hurt people but they hurt him…it had only taken a piece of paper to convince him of this. “A-aye’m sorry!” he shouted now to the empty room. “Wha’ have aye proved? Tha’ aye can cut off the people who love em jus’ like tha’? My family are all aye have…an’ aye cut off lifelines.” He fell onto his knees with a thud and began pounding the floor with his fists, his eyes scrunched up so tight, white knuckle threatened to pierce his skin. “Aye killed ye! We killed ye! Every person tha’ ye ever loved tha’ walked out of yer life killed ye. Ye thought yer sister had died on the death boats. Ye thought she had passed on days after ye seen her last. But there was something else wasn’t there? Ye hoped…an’ when hope threw it all back in yer face, ye fell under the pressure.” His breathing, rasping gasps, he clawed back to his feet and groped for the paper at the desk then threw it to the ground. From the envelope, a photograph slipped out. Scrawled at the top of it was the date 1947. Paddy suddenly fell deathly calm. “It took her 58 years t’ get a response from her. She made herself a life an’ left you an’ yer da behind. She left everything good about this country behind. Every promise she made about her links t’ here were lies. Everything she said and did had t’ be taken with a pinch of salt. Aye never knew her but this letter tells me it all. She was a cold heartless bitch…58 years…an’ in reality, fer you, she was only a week late old man, wasn’ ye…jus like her t’ wait until ye die…” Paddy got to his feet, picking the loose paper up and setting it back onto the desk. He took a handkerchief from his pocket and furiously rubbed his eyes and blew his nose, He looked at the photograph that had been returned to sender and seen the young glowing face of his grandfather. “Aye’m sorry…all aye can do t’ try and make this up, is make the people ye lived with as happy as ye aspired t’ be. It’s not worth much, but aye need t’ show ye, aye never meant t’…aye always said t’ meself aye was going t’ come back. A cold chill suddenly swept over him, despite the blazing fires only metres from him. He didn’t feel the cold on his skin, more in his mind. With the cold came a faint message, so soft you would almost have mistaken it for a figment of your imagination. But not Paddy O’Shea, he believed in living memories. The voice seemed to say two words, the voice was oddly recognisable. Don’t cry He wasn’t shocked or surprised. Instead he stroked the photograph with the tip of his index finger and muttered to it softly. “No…never again. Just once in a life maybe, a man cries but never again. Maybe aye’m cryin’ fer meself, for the lost years. Wha’ did aye pick up in those lost years? A career? Fame? Fortune? Experience? Aye hurt men until they screamed fer mercy. Tha’ be experience. Aye can choke a man out, aye can hit him so hurt his cheek would cave in. Aye saw lands ye never seen, aye seen the other sides of clouds from the skies…an’ no matter where aye was, no one ever said t’ me, welcome stranger…” Paddy paused for a second. “This is me home, you were me father an’ aye have forsaken ye both…if only ye could see me old man, the things aye’ve accomplished, aye kno’ ye would finally be happy. Because every match aye won was fer you… Paddy paused a second time. “An’ aye’m goin’ t’ keep winnin’ them fer ye.” Paddy now lifted the various papers from the table, walked to the fire and didn’t hesitate in throwing them in. He paused with the photograph, and then threw that in too. “Let’s give her 58 years.” END
