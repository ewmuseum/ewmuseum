Prologue

11 August 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 8/11/06 Mr. Clark- This is the submission we talked about to be used as a test piece. I’m hoping we can dangle it as a stand-alone for Harper’s, Antioch, and Kenyon. At the very least, I think I know people who can get good placement in the Santa Monica Review. Tell me if we’re shooting too high with this, but I feel like I might be able to turn this into something serious. You’ll have to read the piece, but I feel like this guy could mean a huge shot for me. He’s not newsworthy, but he makes a fantastic story. It’s always impossible for me to find something to write about so maybe extended, extensive subject matter would be good for me. I don’t know how far we could take this, but I wanna talk seriously with you about a collection of essays. If it goes well, I could see the makings for a book. That would depend partly on the subject, though. I’m not jumping the gun. Just take a look and see if you like it. I know that, so far, the emotional investment in the writing is choppy. Work your magic on it. -J. * * * * I immediately recognized Chandler Tsonda pushing through the automatic doors. His face is pieced together perfectly; even as a heterosexual man, I could recognize that he is good-looking. He is an amalgamation of youthful, boyish good looks and soft, adult features. I did not doubt for a moment that the man stepping into the café had been one of the most highly lauded (physically) models in the world. There was also the arrogance. The haughty aloof nature of this professional wrestler was evident in his walk, even in the way that he scanned the room. His arrogance was surprisingly not that of an athlete, a competitor; it was that of a socially-privileged individual. He walked up to the counter and made a charmer’s attempt at small talk with the cash register girl. She handed him his coffee with a look that said, this once, he had been vanquished. He let loose a slight sneer as he looked over his shoulder and walked over towards me. I stood up, not knowing the protocol for greeting a professional wrestler, and put on my most professional voice, summoning up the deepest measures my vocal chords could manage. “Jason Collier,” I stated, extending my hand, “very pleased to meet you, Mr. Tsonda.” He looked down at my hand and shook it, immediately wary of the small-framed man standing in front of him trying to sound like Barry White. “My voice; it doesn’t really do that,” I nervously laughed. He wasn’t intimidating from a physical standpoint, but I was relatively star struck. “You can call me Chandler, I guess.” I had the feeling he offered this more to suit me than because he thought I was worthy of first-name basis. “And honestly, squid, I don’t know what this meeting’s about. My lawyer tells me something about a reporter and then I can’t hear the rest because he’s busy doing the Hora.” This was a crude reference towards Gil Lavenstein, Tsonda’s lawyer. Having only spoken with Gil for minutes, I couldn’t imagine why the pious, pudgy-faced lawyer would take on a client like Chandler Tsonda “Yeah, well I’m a freelance journalist. I don’t sleep on the street just trawling for stories; I have an office and a boss, but I’m more or less my own right hand man.” I didn’t know if my statement had made sense, but I got the sense that, either way, Tsonda wasn’t quite the dumb jock I had been expecting. All my dealings with sports figures up to this point had been disappointing on an astronomical scale. His eyes flicked back and forth over me like swaying cattails. The PRIME wrestler looked disinterested. With me in mid-sentence, he regally stretched back and yawned with his whole body. The only thing missing from his spoiled prince act was a young Indian as page, fanning him and fetching him cold water. “You don’t seem to care what I have to say.” It was a bad way to start “negotiations” with this notoriously stubborn man, but I was so taken aback by his indifference that I was hit with a wave of courage. I would immediately regret this action, in terms of my employment, at least. “Yeah, well, you’re not sayin’ anything I haven’t heard a hundred times. I’ve said that I’ll deal with Newsweek, Time, or Rolling Stone. I’m not interested in giving an exclusive to make your career.” Here was the legendarily difficult Tsonda I had heard about. For weeks, I had pitched the idea around the circles of writers that I was involved in. Mostly, I got strange looks and questions about why exactly I would bother with professional wrestling. Every once in a while I heard a horror story from a guy who knew a guy who tried to get a sit-down with Chandler Tsonda and got chewed out in front of hundreds of people or death threats straight from the mouth of the “Viet Viper.” Although I doubt that he knew it, Tsonda had become his own urban legend in the world of small-time journalism. Now, I was stuck in front of the maddened (and vulnerable?) Chandler Tsonda. I had come into this knowing I would have to tread on eggshells, but now, my task was nigh impossible. What was there to say to get back on this megalomaniac’s good side? “This offer will make your career, Mr. Tsonda.” He was hooked again; leering at me, but still silent. “You’re an unknown to mass culture. In fact, PRIME has never enjoyed the global success of the WWE.” And dreams of dollar signs danced in Chandler Tsonda’s heads, his eyes glazing over with golden delusions. “You’ve got the makeup to be the first breakout star. You already run with some Hollywood types and you have the look.” “Are you asking me out for mojitos or trying to sell me your fucking newspaper story?” He remained cold, but I could tell that he wanted to know more; to see just where I saw not the wrestler, but the entertainer Chandler Tsonda going. “I already know that shit, Jay. What can a journalistic serf do that my publicist can’t already arrange? Or are you just gonna try very hard and give it the ol’ college try?” Ignoring his completely unprofessional manner, Chandler Tsonda was now goading me. Perhaps I had read wrong, in not seeing the ultimate competitor. Even if he didn’t hold the air of an athlete, he was fiercely competitive. He wanted to me to feel as insignificant as he saw me. “No one can write this story as well as I can.” I hadn’t planned on coming out with it. I knew, though, that for some reason this seemingly horrible pairing was star-crossed. I was supposed to write this story and catapult Chandler Tsonda into stardom. I didn’t plan on telling him that this would also bring me into my own rarified air. And I knew all of this; some visceral knowledge coursed through me. I had prepared for the possibility of getting this story by reading several niche books about roughly the same thing. I had covered The Last Shot, Next Man Up, and Unfinished Business. Each is a reporter following everything that happens within a sports team; respectively, the Lincoln High School basketball team, the Baltimore Ravens, and the Boston Celtics. It’s been done before. Rarely, though, has this sort of thing been attempted in the grueling individual sports. Lance Armstrong is a prickly character, no matter his personal courage and character. Roger Federer is one the most brilliantly boring people on the planet. Michael Phelps doesn’t realize he’s out of college yet. There are no great boxers anymore. So where does a budding journalist without that elusive Northwestern degree go from there? Writing is enhanced tenfold from a personal connection with the subject and whether he’s a royal prick or a gentle giant, sports figures are emotional unknowns. Tap into their personal lives and that’s the best way onto the bestseller list, outside of writing about the “actual” Holy Grail. “You want to see my portfolio?” He had shown me that he was intellectually above the average person. Surely, he could judge me by my writing instead of by my personality. After all, we didn’t have to spend any length of time together interacting. It would be a detached relationship; he would do, I would observe and record. “Not really.” “So you’re going to say no to me without ever looking at a shred of my actual body of work?” “No, you’re going to follow me around for a day. If I like what you write, we can do this project of yours for a duration of my choosing. That’s your one and only option.” There are several rules that I live my professional life by. One of them is that my writing goes completely unaltered and unseen into my editor. I refuse to compromise, artistically, for anyone outside of the big boss. Even then, I rarely will edit content matter just for posterity’s sake. I am staunchly against writing by any method other than my own. What Tsonda was proposing was blasphemous. “I’ll write it, send it to my editor, and then you’ll get a polished copy before it goes to print.” I could play hardball if it came to that. He did have one of the best cards in his hand though: I needed to write the story more than he needed it to be written. “No dice. We do this my way or you can go back to writing color pieces on the woman with nine cats.” He didn’t quite understand the situation I was in here, but he was close enough. I had been dealing with lifestyle pieces for the last six months and hating every minute of it. I didn’t want to write something that could appear at the bottom of CNN.com’s page as “human interest.” I wanted to go big; in a perfect world, the New Yorker would publish it, acknowledging its place as a piece of brilliant nonfiction. Outside of that, there were journals and bigger newspaper to try and sell it to. “What happens,” I wondered out loud, “if you don’t like what I’ve written? Are you that egotistical that you’d sabotage my work if it makes you look bad?” The smile of an empire shined back in my face. “Probably. You can get your licks in, if anything gets taken out of context, I’ll put the kibosh on this quicker than you can say Edward R Morrow.” He had thrown down the gauntlet. The rules were now infinitives, no longer negotiable. “And you don’t ever get to ask about seeing something you haven’t seen. That might not make sense, but junior, believe you me that you’ll see what you need to see. If there’s some ‘hole’ in your presentation of me, then that’s your problem, not mine.” I didn’t have any idea what he was talking about. Still, he had given me license to do exactly what I wanted and I wasn’t going to renege on my personal promise that I would take the story at all costs. Still, I didn’t know what this loophole was that I’d just agreed to. The verbal contract showed that Tsonda and I weren’t going to have a cordial affair, but we could at least have a civil one. “When will I follow you around for a day?” From someone else, I would’ve taken this promise of some alleged day in the future as an attempt to get rid of me, but for some reason, Tsonda seemed very intent on keeping this promise. “I dunno. I’ve got this thing on Sunday; it’s kind of a big deal. After that, it’s all gravy for me.” I remember now that Colossus III, PRIME’s answer to WrestleMania, was literally two days away. Why had he even accepted a meeting with me on such short notice? “Shouldn’t you be holed up thinking about the match? Watching tape?” I had to know why one of the company’s top stars would be out fraternizing and trying to advance himself in the media when his own house wasn’t in order. “Yeah, well, I’ve never been one to do that stuff, but I started for Colossus Three. I’ve sorta gotta win my match. Listen, it’s a long story and I don’t like talking about it, but you should watch it. It might give you a nice poignant, intro to the piece.” He surprised me yet again, with a better grasp of putting a piece together than I would’ve known. “I’m thinking I might use this sit-down as the intro.” “Suit yourself. It’s gonna be one hell of a story, though. Gonna be one hell of a match.” “Are you going to win?” I thought I knew the exact response I would get from my new brash comrade, who seemed younger than me but in fact was two or three years older. “For the sake of your story, I better.” He had already started towards the door and didn’t bother with the closing formalities of shaking hands or exchanging phone numbers. Chandler Tsonda knew I would be able to contact him again and that, barring a catastrophe, I would. Even after one meeting, he knew the immense value that he held for me and the once-in-a-career opportunity I presented to him. My biggest question now focused on what kind of piece I would write. Would he give me newsworthiness or would he weigh me down with the portrait that he wanted to be painted in? As he walked out the door, I had to wonder about this upcoming show. I’d heard the rumors and, not wanting to add fuel to today’s fire, hadn’t broached the subject of his match preparation. It was an industry myth, some said. Others stood by firmly the fact that he went into every match cold. And now he was confessing, as well as proclaiming a new way of doing things? I knew that I would have to watch him in action on Sunday, but I wondered how attached, already, I was getting to my story. It wasn’t that I liked him, particularly- I didn’t- but he was fascinating. The story would be as good as I had promised; I was the best possible choice to write this story. Would this be the break I’d been looking for or had I just made a deal with the devil?
