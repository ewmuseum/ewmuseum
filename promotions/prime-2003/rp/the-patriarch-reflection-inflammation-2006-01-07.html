The Patriarch: Reflection & Inflammation

7 January 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 1/7/06 The church seems taller as I step in through the large oak doors. They need another coat of lacquer, but apparently God hasn’t been able to skip out to Home Depot recently. I’m walking behind my father, who has inexplicably recruited me for this Christmas Eve service. Since PRIME shut down for the holidays after Great American Nightmare, I’m just wasting away here in San Antonio. I came under the impression that there was family business to discuss. As usual, my father is giving me the run-around, bullshitting about how he merely wants to spend time with me. “Chandler, I’ve got seats reserved for us.” He was dressed in his absolute best: those Italians take Christmas attire very seriously. He was in a black suit; in that suit, he was Angelo Deville, Joe Pesci, and Joe Mantegna all rolled into one. The fact is, he’s an old Italian guy who hasn’t held a gun since the war, but goddamnit if he’s not a goodfella at heart. It’s hard not to like him sometimes. His voice is kind to me, his cadence solid and comforting. “Here we go; I sit in these seats every Christmas.” He takes every opportunity to drill into my skull the fact that I haven’t ever spent a Christmas with him. “This is better then courtside at the Clippers,” I mutter, halfway between a deadpan and a serious attempt to make his Christmas efforts seem meaningless. There’s nothing I’d like more then to see him lose his cool at me in the middle of church. Then everyone will see what a terrible father he’s been and then they’ll go back to their normal Christmas Eve dinner with all their family members who’ve never walked out on them. Everyone quiets down as a pudgy man in sweeping green cloth stands up at the front of the congregation. “Welcome, friends. We come here to celebrate the most powerful thing on earth.” My guess is nuclear weapons. “That, my friends, is love.” Close enough. My father is actually rapt at the beginning of this oratory. “What is celebrated across the world in this holiday season is the overwhelming love that we all share.” No punchline needed. That’s just bullshit, but things are already awkward enough between my father and me. I don’t need to add by shitting on his religion, too. Zoning out the minister, I look around the spacious sanctuary. The space is almost unending, as antiquated bricks lead up to a high dome above the center of the church itself. Immediately below is a three dimensional cross, which seems prime for a tragic Christmas day newspaper headline, but somehow sits there perfectly still in suspension. There are massive amounts of people here. Criticize the churches all you want (I do), but people are still afraid of the big man. I don’t personally believe in much of anything except my own ability to kick copious amounts of punk ass, but I see the human brilliance in the structure of the churches. Man has created God as an undeniable source of love, Big Brother, and a system of controlling the masses all in one. “And God knew Joseph was uncertain with his role. Joseph’s faith was going to be tested. Could he simply take Mary’s word that this was the son of the Lord?” My father turns to me at the word “son” and I avoid his glance. With this dose of God in him, there’s no telling which sappy speech he’s going to pull out. It’s possible we’re headed for “I’ve always loved you,” but my money’s on the guilt-ridden forgiveness-seeking “I know I haven’t always been there for you.” In church, no less. At least he has no shame about blindly seeking acceptance from me. Maybe he’s afraid he’ll die without ever having me understand his side of the story. Fading in and out of the story, I’m only catching bits and pieces of the anecdotal wisdom, but nobody informed this minister that all of this is available on DVD thanks to Mel Gibson. Or one can just visit the courts where the Bible has more sway then the Constitution, so we can all learn intelligent design and save all the fetuses (someone would make HUGE bucks if they took the pictures from those old Save the Whales posters and put aborted babies on them, I’m telling you). Sorry for my green state propaganda; I’m just a minority so you needn’t worry about my opinion ever showing up at the polls. I snap out of it when we all get up to sing “Hark The Herald Angels Sing.” I feel like an enormous douchebag for not knowing the words, but more often then not I’ve spent Christmas Eve blackout drunk, celebrating the birth of baby Jesus by trading body fluids with women of questionable integrity. Before I know it, we’re sitting back down. The organ belts out the last painful notes along with the one baritone male who doesn’t know he’s entirely off-key. We sit back down, causing the church’s collective pews to groan. At the risk of sounding like an emo kid, being around this many people at Christmas feels strange. After being alone year after year, I’m not used to the group cheer or the singing or any of it. I get pretty Scroogely around the holidays because there’s not a lot to be happy about when you’re the ultimate cynic. Last year, just to show us that he doesn’t care about our materialistic holidays, God sent a tsunami to the other side of the world (or the Jews did, depending on who you ask). If I even believe in a God, my belief would only rest in one with such a black sense of humor as to bring a Christmas present of 180, 000 dead Muslims. See, there’s a reason why I’m not an avid churchgoer: the merciless God who plagued the Egyptians and flooded the earth is gone now. “The three Magi, the kings Balthasar, Melchior, and Caspar descended upon the Lord. Mary and Joseph knew their son was the son of God.” Abruptly, the minister stops and produces a large candle. “And now we light candles to show the unending love of the Lord.” Lighting it, he quickly lights the candles of those sitting in the front rows and within minutes, everyone’s candles are lit. As the artificial lights shut off, I fiddle with my candle, making sure I don’t drip wax all over myself. We stand up and begin to sing “Silent Night.” It’s even more off-key then the first song, but this time I can fake my way through half the words. “Fuck,” I mutter, as softly as possible, as hot wax hits my right hand. It takes all my coordination not to drop the candle right then and there. My father looks at me and I know that if he were a real dad and if he weren’t desperate to keep me around, he’d shake his head at his heathen son and then we’d share a laugh once we got home. But we’re not a real father and son so he can’t chastise me for shit like swearing in church. Because the years when he would’ve been able to make an impression? He was off with his real family. I’m not gonna play victim this whole trip, but fuck if I’ll let him make a judgment of any kind about me. I’m a disgrace to most people I know, but with him, I’m all set. Because he’s got the ultimate trump card in the game of who’s a bigger fuck-up. As the service ends, we all file out of the church. Others stay and crowingly discuss the Christmas parties that they’re off to, the drool almost seeping from their mouths at the mere mention of their elitist get-togethers. If I weren’t one of those people, I’d probably hate the, but we’re off to our own celebration of upper-class America’s continuing affluence and success. One of my father’s young cronies opens the door of the limo that I’ve rented for all of us. These little things are the way that I’ll show my father what a fool he was. I can rent a limo like it’s nothing; he works at some menial job and won’t be able to retire for another decade. Silence and lush blue velvet drape the interior of the limo. Ettore and I are sitting on one side and his two cronies are sitting on the other. They are constantly looking at me, snickering their stupid greasy snickers. Clearly, these are not men who have ever been to Milan during fashion week. Each of them is a walking atrocity, with their “velour suits,” which is really just a politically correct way of saying “wop gear.” There’s no “suit” about it; their garish jewelry hangs over it. Be still my beating heart at the irony of their golden crosses hanging over their clothes. Before long, we’re at the party. We walk up some stairs to a penthouse (there are penthouses in Texas? go fucking figure) that’s already glowing with Christmas spirit. I’m surprised at my father running with class of people. Everyone shakes my hand and tells me that they’re delighted to meet me. I’m not delighted to meet them; I just want my body weight in spiked eggnog and I want it right now. At five glasses worth of the miracle drink, I feel a warm hand extending its fingers inside my chest. This is real Christmas cheer, I think while I stare at the chintzy crèche scene in front of me. Among these people in their suit jackets and ties, the plastic light-up Jesus and Co. look utterly ridiculous. At ten glasses, I spill a woman’s drink on the front of her dress and use it as an excuse to cop a feel. She gives me a dirty look and walks away. “Don’t you know who I am?” I wonder aloud. She just walks away, oblivious both who I am and my existence. I look for my father, who is chatting quietly with a tall man in a red sweater vest. I want to tell him that the sweater vest is the modern-day equivalent of a coming-out speech, but I feel drunk, nauseous, and fat from all the eggnog, so I’m going to keep my words to a minimum. I’m creeping up on the conversation from behind. “Thanks for bringing your son. I mean, a real professional wrestler at our Christmas party? I’m very impressed, Mr. Calvino.” My father nods quietly, unsure of exactly what to say. Why doesn’t he just say he’s proud of me, too, goddammit? He pauses, choosing his words carefully. “Mr. Claiborne, I appreciate the sentiment, but I realize that I wouldn’t be invited here if my son, however estranged he is from me, weren’t a professional wrestler.” Hey, the old guy can still make me proud, I think, as he smiles softly, his face still portraying the kindness his words just betrayed. I can’t decide whether to be mad at my father for using me to get invited or whether I want to punch this sweat vest wearing-shitbox in the grill and give the geezer a hug. I stop creeping up on them and walk the other way towards some more people who ask me about my match against Jimmy Bong in GTT5 and am I excited and am I nervous and do I like being the underdog. I give a half-smile and hurry past them to go throw up in the bathroom sink. Having wiped viscous khaki vomit off my black jacket, I return to the party, only to find my father in a shouting match with Claiborne. “You should learn your place,” shouts the party’s host. He’s being restrained by several people, while my father is holding his ground, albeit anxiously. “You should stop being such a goddamn prick.” Ehhh, it’s not really Tsonda-grade comeback material, but the guy is a huge prick. “Get the fuck out of here. Take your piece of shit son with you. He’s a faggot, for God’s sake. I mean, he-” Claiborne face meet Tsonda fist. After several threats of having the police called, my father helps me walk out. I’m not even angry; I feel kind of bad because I definitely busted the guy’s nose. Everyone was drunk and really we should’ve just left without incident. “That was brave of you, son, but maybe you should go apologize.” “Are you kidding me? Not a chance. Get in the limo.” My father doesn’t budge. Is this traitor also a coward? Probably. His hair is almost lost in the fog around us. My eyes don’t leave his face. His wander around in the arcane night. Is he searching for something or just running from me again? “Son, you just hit him.” “Broke his nose, too.” “So?” I see where this is going: right where I want it to. “Didn’t anyone teach you that-” I don’t let him finish. “Don’t you dare, you bastard.” My voice is flat, but quivering with anger. In the middle of the street, we’re making a scene and neither of us cares. I’m sure that my penchant for spotlight comes from him anyway. “Don’t you say a fucking word about what anyone taught me.” “I only,” he stutters, no longer sure of his words. His confidence has left him floundering in my sea of hatred. “You gave up your right to accuse me of anything the day that you left Vietnam.” I knew I wouldn’t come to San Antonio without having this terrible conversation. I won’t let him hide behind his cognitive dissonance anymore. No more. It comes time that he realizes what everyone else has: he’s the fucking bad guy. I’ve never heard of someone being so happy about progressively working themselves out of someone else’s will. “Chandler...I don’t want to do this anymore.” Fuck him; is this genuine sadness or pathetic grasping at the straws of a former life? I hear him open the door of the limo. “So don’t. Don’t try anymore. No more wave of bullshit from you. I’m going to hell. So fucking be it. Let me go the way I want to. The door slams shut. The street is grey. Christmas Eve is silent again. Lights in every window shine brightly.
