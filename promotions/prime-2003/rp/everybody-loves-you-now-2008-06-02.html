Everybody Loves You Now

2 June 2008
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 6/2/2008 "I can't believe I let you rope me into another seminar." Considering his reputation for being an especially private person, Troy Douglas wasn't exactly a fan of talking about his career when he was away from the cameras. Which is why it annoyed him so much that he was in this small office in a suburban Philadelphia rec center, 15 feet away from a gathering of 20 or so aspiring professional wrestlers. But, a favor is a favor, and he owed the man standing across from him more than a couple. "Don't worry," said Jason Reed. "You go out there, you say a few words, you answer a couple questions. The kids get a kick out of it. "Besides, gives me a chance to exploit my most famous student." "Ahhh. Now the true reasons come out. Going for the big cash grab now, are you?" "Troy, when has that EVER not been the case." "Point taken." Nine years ago, when Troy had barely completed his physical therapy, a chance meeting with Jason had set him on the path that Troy never could have envisioned for his life. Then in his late 30s, injuries having put an end to his full-time wrestling career, the former star in the States and in Japan had decided to start training younger wrestlers, and the injured former NFLer ended up as the very first client. At that point, Troy was what Jason had called at many times, "the worst natural wrestler in the history of the universe." Maybe it was the fact that six months basically confined to a bed and a wheelchair had completely sapped his conditioning, maybe it was that a lifetime on the gridiron and the hardwood had given him a skill set that in no way translated to the squared circle, but it was months before Troy could grasp even the most basic concepts of professional wrestling. It took hour after hour, day after day, week after week of seemingly nonstop training inside a broken down old boxing gym in South Philly before Troy could have something that in any way resembled a passable wrestling match. The fact that Troy was where he was at now, wrestling in front of sold-out stadiums and arenas all across the planet, going toe-to-toe with the best in the world on a nightly basis was -- in all honesty -- completely a testament to the patience and skill of the man once known as "The Ice Dragon". Didn't mean appearing as a special guest speaker in front of a group of two dozen neophyte pro wrestlers -- 22 of whom probably wouldn't last the month -- was any more appealing to him. "I still hate you for this." "High price of success, Troy. You get famous, all of a sudden everybody you've ever known comes back asking for favors." "That's what, the 355th time you've fed me that line?" "353rd, by my count." "Still never gets any more salient, you know." "Hey, Master Yoda I'm not. Now wait here, young Padawan. I'll go warm up the crowd." "Don't sprain anything." Jason chuckled, then turned and left the office, leaving the door cracked slightly open. Troy sat back and smiled, not really knowing what to make of the moment. Eight years ago, when he was so bad he made sure to start his career under a mask so nobody would recognize him, he couldn't have possibly imagined that young wrestlers would want any part of his advice. Three years ago, when he thought he was walking away from wrestling for good, Troy didn't think he'd ever have to talk about his time on the road again. And now, less than a week short of his 32nd birthday, Troy still wasn't sure he should be giving advice to anyone. For the most part, he was still feeling his way through the universe, and he still didn't quite know exactly how he got by day-to-day. As Troy collected his thoughts, he could here Jason go through his introduction in the adjoining room. A few inside jokes, some cursory biographical information, and then.. Well, Troy couldn't keep his silence when he heard that story start to come out of Jason's mouth. "Not the Atlantic City story, Jay!" Troy burst through the side room, arms waving. At once, the entire room burst into laughter, including Troy. "But, it's so damn funny." "And I've got plenty of those I could tell them about YOU, but I think your business might go down a little bit." "Hey, you want to leave the grisly details out, that's fine with me," Jason said before turning to the gathered students. "Folks, it's my pleasure to introduce my good friend, Troy Douglas." Troy was greeted by some polite applause, and as he pulled himself into an empty folding chair, he settled into that familiar mode of shooting from the hip. "To be fair, I'm not exactly sure why Jason asked me here. I've screwed up in my career many more times than what's been documented on national television, and for those of you crazy enough to follow my career, you know that the entire world has seen my mistakes on more than a few occasions." That got a laugh, and that was enough to ease Troy into a spot where he didn't feel so much like the tenured professor giving his intro lecture in Philosophy 191. "Anyway..." ***************************************** Chickie's and Pete's on Roosevelt Boulevard was always crowded. With the mix of cold beer, fresh seafood and the special aroma of the world-famous Crabfries filling the air, it's no surprise why. In a corner of the bar, a plate of those fries between them and a beer in front of each of them, Troy Douglas and Jason Reed were oblivious to the bustling crowd, the constantly pumping music and the latest exploits of the Philadelphia Phillies on the panorama of flat screen TVs. "... and then, the kid says, 'You mean I was supposed to come in here in good shape?'" "You really do get the cream of the crop, don't you?" "Well, I started with you, so I really couldn't go anywhere but up." "You're slipping, Jay. Eight years ago, you actually would have come up with a creative insult." "Benefits of old age, Troy. People expect much, much less of you." Troy continued munching on the spicy mound of pure flavor sitting in front of him, not really conscious of the fact that his hands were very rapidly turning bright red with a heavy coating of grease and Old Bay seasoning. He wasn't really thinking about the food, or the conversation. Something else was gnawing at him. "Why'd you ask me to come, Jason?" "The guys were asking, I thought I'd finally deliver and let them see my one student that made it." "That's bullshit and you know it." "Well, you get right to the point, don't you?" "I know the way you operate, Jason. You're about as wild and unpredictable as a clock. You fly me in, you buy me food ... something's going on." "There's nothing going on, Troy. I swear, there's --" Troy was fed up. He sat down his beer, wiped off his hands and looked directly at the man he considered a mentor, a friend and a surrogate father. "Stop dodging the damn question, Jay. What the hell is going on?" Jason paused, took a deep breath, and finally let it out. "I have ALS." Well, Troy certainly wasn't expecting THAT. The weight of what he'd just been told pressing down on him, Troy sat, mouth agape, not speaking for several minutes. For his part, Jason didn't say a word either, just nodding his head a few times. "When? How?" "About a month ago, I started having trouble walking. There was like, a tingling sensation in my right leg all the time. I went to get it checked out, and the doctors over at UPenn told me the disease was in it's early stages." "Who else knows?" "Other than Bonnie, the kids and the rest of our family, you're the first. You understand why I couldn't just send you a text message about this?" "Pretty much, yeah." They kept talking, not really getting into the finer details, but there was one question Troy held back from asking. To be honest, he didn't really want to know the answer. Finally, as the beer and food dwindled to almost nothing, the 6'5", 260 pound man who'd been mentally whittled away to almost nothing in the last few minutes let out that question. "How -- how much longer do you have?" "Nobody knows. Doctors can't even tell me. There's this knew drug that's just been approved that's supposed to weaken some of the symptoms, but it's a progressive disease. Could be a year, could be twenty. That's the worst part." "What is?" "Not knowing." ********************************************************** Sitting on the plane somewhere over Indiana, Troy still wasn't sure how to process the last few hours. The man who he'd come to trust more than anyone else on the planet, the man who essentially gave him a chance at a life after he thought his had been taken away, was going to slowly crumble into a shadow of his former self, and eventually he was going to wither away altogether. Considering everything Troy had on his plate at the moment, this revelation wasn't exactly something he was prepared for. Mainly, he'd tried to spend the last few hours washing the conversation out of his brain with a steady flow of Pink Floyd pounding through a pair of noise-canceling headphones. It wasn't working. He'd dealt with worse circumstances in his career, but somehow, the timing of this latest piece of bad news seemed to be the most unfair of all. After eight years, he was finally on track. He was successful, he was as happy as he'd been in years, and he was performing at a high level on a night-to-night basis for the longest stretch in his entire career. So what happened? The universe had to throw one more giant pile of crap in his face. It's a good thing Troy wasn't terribly religious, because if he was, he'd be feeling a hell of a lot like Job right now. He looked down at the bag in the empty seat next to him. The bag that held his recently won PRIME Intense Title. He had priorities, ones he couldn't afford to forsake right now. He'd have to deal with life when he could make the time.
