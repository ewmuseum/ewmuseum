Meaning

28 January 2009
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 1-28-2009 Fusenshoff wakes up with the sun shining in his eyes. He looks at the clock on the bed stand. The red digits on the clock notify him that it’s past noon. Fuse never sleeps in as late as he did today, but he’s also never had a night quite like last night. He remembers Jacob Theresi much more vividly than the events that led to him meeting the man. All he can really remember is that he had been given his chance to leave his tragedy in the dust. His wish had finally been answered. He remembers drowning, abandoning his struggle. He finally felt the sensational euphoria of having that entire debilitating weight slide off his shoulders. The next thought he remembers was wondering whether he was in heaven. Fortunately or not, he wasn’t fooled. His body ached and screamed, as is customary in most near-death experiences. He was also sitting in his skivvies with nothing but a wool blanket to keep his integrity in tact. He wasn’t in heaven, that was glaringly obvious. The thing he remembers most is Jacob Theresi. He remembers the strange green clothes the man wore. He remembers his posture, his body language, his slow movements and assertive serenity. It was a quiet confidence, an unintimidating dominance that Fusenshoff found captivating. Then the reality of the situation kicked in. Fuse hated the man for saving his life and had to leave. Now, as the digits on the clock change for the third time since he woke, Fusenshoff goes searching for a drink. The mini bar in his hotel room is as dry as a potheads uvula. He drank every drop of booze in the room last night. In any other circumstance he would’ve paid a visit to the hotel bar, a local pub or the nearest convenience store. Fusenshoff recalls why he chose to pay exorbitant prices for mediocre booze last night- he left his wallet with Jacob Theresi. Fusenshoff has half a mind to go through the excruciating process of cancelling all his credit cards, renewing his driver’s license and forgetting about all the cash in his wallet. Forty-five minutes later he’s at the front door of Jacob Theresi’s cabin. He’s still fuming about last night’s affair, but his brain keeps reminding him that you can’t really blame a guy for trying to save another person’s life. Rationale can be such a vexing influence. Fusenshoff knocks on the heaviest door he’s ever come across. Twenty seconds later it creaks open. Fuse pushes the oak door open and peeks inside. Theresi’s sitting in the same chair as he was the previous night. Fusenshoff’s ready to take off already. He sees his wallet on the end table next to his chair from last night, sitting in the same place the coffee sat. “Theresi, I came for my stuff.” Theresi raises his head, though it’s still pointing at the fireplace. He closes a book he was reading and sits it on his own end table. Fusenshoff notices it’s the Kabala. Jacob replies “Please have a seat. I’d appreciate it if you’d allow me to ask you a few questions.” For a reason Fusenshoff could never explain, he chooses to oblige him. He takes a seat in the opposite chair, also facing the fire. He notices Theresi is wearing the same color green once again, this time it’s a turtleneck. They sit for a moment in silence as Theresi watches Fusenshoff. Fuse is extremely uncomfortable and shows it by shifting his weight and fidgeting with his hands. Just before he is about to react aggressively due to the uncomfortable situation, Jacob speaks up. “Fusenshoff, I want to tell you a story about a man I once met who had survived three separate concentration camps during the Holocaust. Before the days of The Third Reich, he spent his time helping others find purpose that would guide them to solving all their situations.” “You mean problems” Fusenshoff interrupted. “I mean situations. In this world there is no such thing as problems, only situations.” Theresi continues on. “During his time in the camps, he made a very interesting observation that he would carry with him for the rest of his life. He realized that there are only three ways to find meaning in one’s life. “There is the active meaning- creation. This is fulfillment derived from actively participating in the creation of something that becomes your life’s work. It can be anything from another living organism, like a child, to building one’s own home or painting a masterpiece. “There is the passive meaning- enjoyment. Perhaps one chooses to sail around the world and try to experience every culture in the modern world. One may wish to stand on the shoulders of giants by reading all of the greatest literature at one’s disposal. These are just examples. Each person may find their own, individual and unique purpose. “The third possible meaning to one’s life is suffering. This is not a purpose one may choose consciously. Those whose purpose is to suffer are forced into this lifestyle by circumstance, but still have just as much meaning to their lives as any other. “One cannot choose to suffer and have that purpose be the meaning to one’s life. “That is what you are trying to do with your life Fusenshoff. It is the reason you’re not only willing, but trying, to die. You have no purpose or meaning. It is not your purpose to suffer, or your propensity for trying to end it.” Fusenshoff says nothing as Theresi finishes his monologue. Theresi knows he’s gotten his point across. A few more idle moments pass. “Your clothes are in the armoire in the guest bedroom. It’s the door at the end of the hallway. Keep the clothes from last night and the boots you stole from me. You may find them more comfortable than the ones you wear now, but I’ll leave that to your discretion.” Jacob stands and leaves for the kitchen. Fusenshoff takes a minute to think about what the man said. Eventually he gets up and collects his clothes. He takes the wallet from the end table and stops, looking at the doorway leading to the kitchen. Fuse decides not to say anything and turns to leave. He opens the door when he hears Theresi, who’s standing in the kitchen doorway holding a cup of coffee. “The American philosopher, Eric Hoffer, said that we lie the loudest when we lie to ourselves.”
