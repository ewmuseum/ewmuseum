Granny Weatherall Had It Coming

undated

Date: 79 days, 21 hours, 54 minutes and 12 seconds since October 5, 2006 You have come, in your recent days, to sympathize with Deepstep, Ga. Two weeks ago - roughly six days before the rest of the world finally saw what you had known and declared at every possible opening - a guard had done you the regular Saturday favor of passing the front section of the paper into your cell. It disgusts you to this day that you were here long enough for something to become a "regular Saturday favor," but you've managed to not dwell on the bitterness. You never wanted to regularly read the paper, just like you never wanted to watch television - it shamed you to know the world continued spinning without you at its core. However, since reading had become one of few pleasures you'd allowed yourself, you began turning to the middle pages of the front of the Saturday paper, the pages reserved for meaningless AP stories that usually turn into "hey, did you see that thing about..." talk around the water cooler. On this particular day, the 9th, the story du jour was about a reclassification of the Georgia state map. In an effort to presumably save work for the official Georgia cartographer (who no doubt is painfully busy and needed the break), 488 towns had been removed from the map, including Cloudland, Roosterville, Ty Ty and Deepstep. The towns hadn't been removed from Georgia, but they had been stricken from public record, never to be found by curious travelers again. Their pseudo-banishment struck a chord with you, marking the first time you'd identified with any Georgia resident. The sudden, uncharacteristic empathy could be easily linked to the similarity of Deepstep's current predicament and your own. Not gone, but forgotten. 80 days had passed since you were pulled from the life people expected out of you and square-pegged into the life people thought you deserved. At least, you think it had been eighty days. You only made it to five notches on the wall before they took your makeshift knife away. Apparently they frown on that. So all you have is five lines to remind you how long you’ve been here. As if you were ever going to forget the 5th of October. There isn't even ambiance here - no bars on the windows or barbed-wire fences. Not 'real' jail as celluloid had taught, but a phony one, a holding camp for celebrities and CFOs and other "somebodys" who were deemed too minor a threat to be put in real jail. Your "room" had a new mattress on the cot, a fluorescent bulb that never flickered, and a fresh coat of paint on the cinder block walls that must have been discolored the instant they put it on. You fought at first, but gave in shortly thereafter, with your voice hoarse and flecks of paint sticking under your fingernails - which were already a full millimeter longer than what you manicurist would tolerate, even on her laziest day. Now every day is an episode of The Price Is Right, a paltry selection of reading material and at least an hour of four letter words wondering to anyone who will listen why no one has called the warden to admit they made a mistake. Granted, you can’t recall the last time YOU ever admitted a mistake, but you also can’t accidentally getting someone incarcerated. At least no one whose name you remember. For two months, you would explain this logic to the duty guard, questioning why someone had not come to their senses and realized that you could not have done what they said you did – not because of alibi, mind you, but because of the laws of science. It’s like being accused of alchemy. That was how you explained it for two months. Then, you saw him. Grinning, smiling, doling out ballshots like he hadn’t just walked out of your fucking nightmare like a toked-up pussy version of Freddy Krueger. Near as you could tell on the 14-inch black and white monitor the guards used for a TV, the man you were accused of murdering was live and in the flesh. This was no longer a case of misfiled paperwork or a shortcut-filled investigation based entirely on hearsay. No one could choose to ignore the proof on the table, that he was breathing, smoking and masturbating just as furiously as he was months before the disappearance that landed you here. It was no longer a lapse in the judicial process. This was injustice. You were suddenly twice the magician that David Blaine pretended to be, pulling off the biggest vanishing act in the history of the genre. Years from now, you envisioned a horse-faced reporteress asking you, “How did the most popular action star in the country become Ellison’s invisible man?” You would smile just enough to remind her that she couldn’t fuck you if she paid, and reply, “I had a lot of help. I couldn’t have done it without them.” And when it came time for personal thank yous, you wouldn’t need a notecard tucked into the breast pocket of your coat. You would remember them all by heart. Between that instant and this one, all you can do is reflect. You aren’t the only tagged ‘murderer’ in the American penal system who didn’t do what they say you did. Pleading in that regard only gets you so far, so those feelings usually aren’t shared with the duty guard. He still playfully calls you “Killer” when he drops off your lunch. On your worst days, you wonder how unknowingly accurate he may be. You were there at the Contract Signing No One Saw, when Kyle Lamen signed a medical waiver in front of Chet Worth and as many witnesses as the CEO could pull together. The tumor, according to PRIME’s medical staff, had grown so large and so ‘angry’ that another match would likely make it inoperable. He didn’t hesitate or even blink before signing every form necessary to ensure that he could throw one more punch at you. You made him do that. You drove him to that point and you know it. He wanted to walk away, and you wouldn’t let him. He literally signed his life away to make you feel pain. Doesn’t that make you at least an accomplice? Maybe this is your penance for the crime you got away with. One phone call is all it takes to reconcile this black mark on your permanent record, but every day until that call is another donated towards the black mark that never appeared, the silent killing that can only be linked to you in the spiritual sense. If you believed in karma or at least didn’t have that ongoing grudge with Jason Lee, you might think that forgiving yourself is the first step to finally getting out. Focusing on the moment, you’re slightly proud of the Ellison reference above, since it certainly wasn’t something you could’ve dropped in casually before. For a brief moment of frank reflection, you can admit (to yourself and no one else) that this stay has given you time to improve yourself, to become smarter, stronger, more independent. You might be a picture-perfect representation of what's right with jail, instantly remorseful and never, ever willing to subject yourself to this kind of pain and solitude a second time, forever committed to making sure "ex-con" never became, simply, "con" again. But before you finish that breath, you know you are equally everything wrong with the concept of imprisonment, because remorse takes seven days and then the rest of your time is spent getting angry and asking 'why' to no one who can give you a straight answer. You know that never feeling this way again is not nearly as important as making sure that anyone who did this to you feels the same way as soon as you can make it possible. That preventing your own misery is not nearly as important to you as making the guilty feel this miserable. Jail time doesn’t make you less angry. It just affords you the time and opportunity to collect that anger and re-direct it towards an attainable goal – the suffering of others. You know, but won't admit, that you're more of a criminal now than you ever were before prison. With a thin and misattributed dedication to Deepstep, GA on your mind, you spend each day adding to the best laid plans of the mice in the walls and the man trapped between, a man who eagerly awaits his call from the dugout and his chance to make everyone regret the day they put him in as much as the day they let him out.
