Father & Son

23 September 2009
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 9/23 "Hey, Dad! Why don't you have a cool finisher like that?" Six feet five inches of Benjamin Johnson cringed at the video playing on the family computer via YouTube. It was a fan-made highlight video of Hessian entitled something to the effect of "WELCOME TO THE MURDER SHOW," and consisted largely of the seven foot goliath executing an impressive array of power moves. What had drawn the attention of Johnson's son, however, was the neck-compacting manuever known as the Hellevator. Essentially, it consisted of Hessian lifting the opponent over his mammoth shoulder, before spiking them to the canvas on the crown of their head. It was an unspeakably dangerous move. To the best of Johnson's knowledge, the head-spike end of the move had been done in Japan, and it was an *accident* the first time. Said a lot about the state of the business (in Johnson's mind, anyway) that people were now doing it on purpose. "What's wrong with the Lone Star Bomb," Benjamin asked, a bit defensively. "It was good enough to get me my first win in PRIME." "Yeah..." his son agreed. "It's not like it's a bad move or anything. It looks sort of cool, but it's nothing like THAT! Maybe if you dropped the guy on his head out of it..." "I don't drop people on their heads," Benjamin stated, firmly, looking down at his young son, who promptly cocked his head to one side and looked at his dad in confusion. "But why not? It seems like the most effective way to win a match." "Effective, yes. But..." This was starting to echo arguments that he'd had in locker rooms all over Texas. His generation was sort of a bridge between the old school and new school approaches to wrestling. Often times, Johnson was in the ring with younger opponents who had religiously studied various wrestling tapes and innovated new, dangerous, impressive looking moves. "Let me put it this way. What's the goal of a wrestling match?" "To win," came the reply, as if it was the most obvious thing in the world. "How?" Benjamin nodded at his son. His male child was a budding athlete, who loved soccer and baseball, but his mother was forever worried that the young man would follow in Benjamin's footsteps. The fact that the young man could recite facts about wrestling by heart seemed to give the poor woman reason to worry. "Pinfall or submission, preferably, or otherwise count-out or disqualification." "Exactly. Nowhere in there does it say the point of wrestling is to hurt the other guy." "But isn't it--" Benjamin shook his head. "Yes, you have to cause pain to your opponent to be able to pin them...and submissions do by their nature cause pain. But when I'm in the ring, I don't try to injure my opponent. I try to win, and I only use enough force to do so. The Lone Star Bomb drives an opponent face and stomach first to the mat. It's designed to knock the wind out of them long enough for me to pin them. That move..." He gestured at the computer screen, where the Hellevator had been paused at the point of impact. "That can break necks. End careers. Ruin lives. There's nothing in this world short of protecting my own life, your mother's life, or the lives of my two kids that I'd do something like that to someone else for." The seriousness on the Lone Star's face seemed to be having an impact on his son. "Not even for the Universal Championship," he asked, astonished. "Not even for that," Benjamin admitted. "As wrestlers, we always get on TV and say the championship means more to us than anything. To some of us, it does, but for most of us there are lines we won't cross. A lot of us have wives. Children. Families. It's sort of a code in the locker room, I guess you could say." Kid's name knew that wrestling wasn't quite real. When you watched your father on TV getting beaten up, however, and coming home to show you his very real injuries, it was easy to wonder just how fake wrestling actually was. "Even the bad guys?" Johnson couldn't help but laugh. "Some of them, yes. More than you'd imagine. Very few people actually enjoy causing other people pain, no matter what they say on TV. There are, of course, some who do, but there's a few bad apples in every profession." The young man looked at the image of Hessian on the video. With his thick, bushy beard, terrifying expression, and immense size, it seemed unlikely that Hessian was anything but an *extremely* bad apple. He gulped. "Are...you going to be okay," Jacob whispered, unsure of himself. "He's bigger than anyone else I've seen you fight." "Indeed he is, son," Benjamin replied, voice unwavering. "Guys that big usually make it to the big time pretty quick. Usually they're nowhere near as good as Hessian is, though. He's not the typical slow, lumbering giant. Not by a longshot." "And he's the PTC Elite Champion," chimed in Benjamin's personal fountain of wrestling knowledge. "He beat Desade AND Wyatt Connors for it." "You're making your point pretty well." Johnson reached out to tousle his son's hair, and was met with a chuckle by way of response. "But as dangerous as he is, as scary as he is, this is the biggest opportunity of my life. My second match in PRIME, and I face someone as good as Hessian? If I can win..." The sentence was being finished in his head in a multiplicity of ways. A win over Hessian would guarantee Benjamin's spot on the roster. It would send him skyrocketing up the PRIME ladder, maybe even put him into contention for the 5-Star or Intense Championships. "But what can you do against him, Dad? You can't Lone Star Bomb him, can you?" Benjamin thought about that question for a moment. If the adrenaline was flowing enough...maybe, just maybe he could. Of course, his back would hurt like Hell for a week afterwards, probably, and there were no guarantees. "Probably not," he finally admitted, "but think about what I told you. All I need to do is pin Hessian's shoulders to the mat for three minutes -- I'm willing to concede that I probably can't make him submit, since he's nuts. I've been wrestling for fifteen years, Jacob. I know lots of rollups and cradles and pinning predicaments. Hopefully one of them's good enough." The Hellevator was impressive and devastating. If it hit, a three count was all but assured. At the end of the day, however, in Benjamin Johnson's mind, the Hellevator was no different from a schoolboy rollup. Both could produce three counts. Both could produce victories. That was what he was *hoping*, anyway.
