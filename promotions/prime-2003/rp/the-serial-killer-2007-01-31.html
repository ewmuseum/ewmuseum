The Serial Killer

31 January 2007
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 1-31-2007 “Look at that degenerate,” the Captain exclaimed, gesturing swiftly with his hand towards a smallish, fiery Irish fellow sitting at a table over on the other side of the café. I hadn’t said anything to spur this on. I felt that if I sat next to him for long enough, he’d initiate conversation. I wasn’t normally the type of character that Captain Horatio would keep at arms- what with him in his immaculate navy blue outfit and me in my rags. Not a thread on his uniform was out of place, and his gray handlebar mustache was trimmed and combed evenly. He had it cut to his exact specifications on a semiweekly basis. “He’s punch drunk already, and it’s not even noon.” I still wasn’t even sure that the Captain was talking to me or just complaining aloud. To be fair, he was right. The jolly Irishman was already working up a stupor. He had some of his mates around him, and he was entertaining them with stories. I couldn’t hear him from the counter unless he was laughing at something he said, which he did often. His laugh was shrill and bothersome and his nostrils whistled as he cooed. I watched the rabbits outside scurry for crumbs underneath the patio tables and rued them for scavenging their breakfasts. Still, I had no appetite for mine, so I just toyed at it with my silverware. The captain sipped at his scotch and turned away from the Irishman in disgust. “Scotch, my friend, is a sailor’s drink. My day hasn’t started until I’ve had my scotch.” He pointed again at the Irishman while turning further inward towards me. “The difference between that sudsy bastard and me is that I know how to exercise moderation.” Captain Horatio eyed me judgmentally as I took a sip of my coffee. As soon as he affirmed his superiority over me in the ways of class and diplomacy, he looked back over at the Irishman. “That dirty leprechaun,” he snorted. He then stopped the waitress as she walked behind the counter. “Miss, bring me another scotch, and bring one for my friend…I’m sorry, I didn’t get your name.” He paused and waited a response out of me. I cocked my head slightly to the side. “Johnny,” I mumbled. “Johnny!” he exclaimed to me. “That’s a fine Biblical name.” Then, he turned back to the waitress. “That’s one more scotch for me and another for my good friend Johnny.” He slapped me on the back to emphasize our camaraderie and feign a deeper rooting of it. I was never one to turn down a free drink, so I played along and tried to stretch my congeniality. I gave up on my breakfast and lit a cigarette with a match from a matchbook I had grabbed out of a dish on the counter. Captain Horatio put one hand on my shoulder and pointed over at the Irishman as the barmaid placed three cubes of ice each into two short glasses with a small pair of tongs. She then went to the rack to fetch a new bottle of scotch. “Johnny, let me tell you something about that Irishman over there. He’s the luckiest son of a bitch that’s ever lived. Otherwise, he’d be dead right now.” “Is that so?” I arched my eyebrow as I stirred more cream into my coffee. The barmaid had returned and uncorked the bottle of scotch. She began pouring the liquor evenly in the two glasses, moving back and forth between them. “He’s one of the local rats. I had seen him lurking around alleys and passed out drunk along the sidewalk while my ship was docked here before. Last month, I saw him again at this same cafe. He had gotten in a row with one of his cronies: the gentleman over there with the crooked teeth and the shaggy mutton chops. They were both stumbling drunk and had been arguing quite some time over who was the stronger boxer. The Irishman grabbed a beer bottle off of the counter and smashed it in half over a chair, temperamentally intending to slice up his companion. I took offense because the bottle was mine, and I was enjoying it. Had the bottle not still been half full, I would have thought it best to pay him no mind.” I nodded and took a respectful sip of the scotch to show that I was still following his story. I didn’t want to; it was long winded and I was finding it hard to appear attentive. My lip started twitching and my face wrinkled. The Captain paused to chuckle. He thought it must have been the strength of the scotch, but it was merely the uncomfortable patience that my duty required. However, the more the good Captain went on, the more I hated him, and the easier it would be to wipe the deed from my conscience. “I stood up from my seat and approached him ready to give him a stringent verbal lashing, but he had a point to prove to his friend and was insistent on trading balled fists with me. To keep things nice and dignified, I challenged him to a duel, and he foolishly accepted in some gibberish diatribe about how bullets couldn’t touch him because he was wearing some sort of lucky charm around his neck. He pulled out a horseshoe from beneath his shirt that was tied around his neck with some thin twine. I swear that little bastard shits four leaf clovers.” I could tell that that mustachioed motherfucker thought he was clever, and it made me grit my teeth audibly. I wanted to get this over with, but it was too crowded. The Count had paid me generously. He had given me half in advance, and it was a handsome sum. It was enough to live by, but had I chosen to abandon the job, it would be just as much to die by. The Count was a vengeful, old misanthropist that had gone mad with the impunity given to a man of his power. The truth was inconceivable enough to be dismissed as hearsay not even worth the investigation of the current regime. He was the eldest of elder statesmen, and the government was in no state to be humiliated at the potentially fruitless pursuit of the unfounded accusation that one of the wealthiest and agedly decrepit royals was in fact a vampire. “We stepped out to the back alley where a crowd had started to gather. We measured out twenty paces each before we turned to face each other at forty paces distance. I could still see the whites of his eyes, but barely. I could still see that they were bloodshot, and he was so blithering drunk that I knew he couldn’t shoot worth a lick. A bystander who had been selected to call the draw barked out the signal, and my gun was already smoking before he could even wrestle his out of its holster. I stared at him in disbelief. I stared at him like a still target. He finally freed his gun and shot wildly. He jerked too rigidly and the bullet soared safely over my head a good couple of stories. But somehow…somehow I had missed him. It had to be that goddamned lucky charm of his.” He took a slow sip of his scotch, and I noticed tears were welling up in the corner of his eyes. I had seen people cry out of sadness and that had always made me feel ill. I had heard people cried out of joy but never understood why. He was beginning to cry out of rage, so I decided that it would be best to distract him from the salty bitterness yellowing in his eyes. “I don’t believe in luck,” I said before pausing to take a drag from my cigarette. “If he was all that lucky, he would have hit you, right?” The Captain disregarded my question and called the barmaid over. He downed the rest of his scotch in one gulp. “Another drink, sir?” “Not right now.” He put the empty glass down on the counter and reached for his wallet. He pulled out a stack of notes and put them on the counter. “This is your tip,” he said, “but I want you to balance your glass on the head, stand against the wall, and stay perfectly still.” The waitress didn’t know what to make of the whole situation, and neither did I, but she saw the money on the table and her eyes grew desperately large. She stuffed the money into the low cut collar of her top and then did as she was told, and Captain Horatio began walking across the café with his back to the ignorant barmaid. I counted twenty paces before I saw him turn in one fantastically fluid flourish, drawing out a pistol and aiming it perpendicularly across his free arm. The shot rang out followed by the sound of exploding glass. The waitress fell limp, but only because she had fainted. It was an effortlessly clean shot. The café fell silent for a moment, but the silence was a mere echo of the gunshot and the glass. The Captain put his pistol away and returned to his seat as the murmuring noise of multilingual conversation filtered back through the air. “That was an impressive shot,” I said, mostly to break the uncomfortable silence. “But who’s going to refill our drinks now?” He didn’t even chuckle out of good nature. After he had shot that glass off of the woman’s head, his warmth towards me had drifted off along some swift seaward current. “Look at him over there, prattling on as if nothing had ever happened. If he only knew how the winged angel of dumb luck flew him to safe haven.” I didn’t like the way that the Captain talked. His words blew around like a hot, pompous wind, pollinating his arrogance around in such an allergenic way that it was almost literal. I never believed in luck. If he wanted to kill the Irishman, he wouldn’t be sitting there at that far table now. I believed that Captain Horatio was just a coward who lacked the stomach to murder. In fact, I was sure of it. I decided to press his buttons a bit. “If you want him dead, why don’t you just challenge him to another duel?” “What’s the use? He’s still got that damned charm around his neck.” There it was; a horseshoe around his neck that had rusted slightly so that the metal was more of a bluish lavender than a shining silver. “Come on,” I egged. “He’s too drunk for his own good…and on cheap whiskey by the looks of him.” The Captain looked me over again, wryly this time with a squinted eye; his brow pressed down over an imaginary monocle. He was a starving predator, adept at the hunt but without a survivalist understanding of the primordial hierarchy. Instead, he followed a much more sinister cycle implemented by arbitrarily conceived structure where he recognized the weak, but only toyed with his prey from fear of being guiltily sated. He must not have liked the taste of blood between his teeth, but he could tell I knew what it was like to starve. He could tell by my clothes; how they shredded to tattered rags at the cuffs of my pants. “If you’re so confident that his charm won’t protect him today, why don’t you challenge him to a duel?” “You’re a much better shot than I am, sir. I’d have to be wearing a charm to hit him.” I stood up to pull him in, and it worked. He urgently reached into his coat pocket for another fold of bills. I acted like I was uncomfortably surprised. “You’re not offering me money to try and kill that Irishman?” “No,” he retorted. “It’s just a gentleman’s bet. I bet you five thousand dollars that you can’t best that Irishman in a duel.” “I’m sorry,” I excused. “I don’t have five thousand dollars to bet, and even if I did…” I pulled my jacket off of the back of my chair and began to put it on despite having no intention of actually leaving. “Ten thousand,” he said, doubling the stack on the table, knowing that money is harder to turn down when it’s more than just a number. He watched me stare at it, but I kept my mouth consciously closed, pressing my top lip against my bottom so that both began to whiten. “And I’ll give you odds.” He smiled. “Take it. I trust you.” He chuckled a little bit as I slapped my hand on top of the stack of bills and slid them off the counter so that I could better guide them into my jacket pocket. I looked at him and smiled. I hoped he hadn’t heard the breathy laugh that rose up my gullet. I cut it off at the uvula. He smiled back and sniffed, hiding his own misinformed smirk with his mustache. I walked over to the table where the Irishman was still bandying about with his cronies, and they were all humoring him by laughing at his jokes. None of them were any good. They were all too loud and too slurred. His fellow diners were even kind enough not to laugh as he swayed back and forth and banged his head against the wall or the back of the booth. When I walked up, the Irishman’s friends turned to me, though he kept telling his story, oblivious to or perhaps merely unaffected by my presence. He was going on about a woman he was going to propose to; some stripper who did this trick with a ping-pong ball. “And then when it popped out of her, it curved a little bit and hit me smack dab in me forehead!” He thought it was some sign that the two were destined to be together, but chances are, and this is speaking scientifically, that the women’s labia was marginally thicker on one side than the other which would cause the ball to spin on a vertical axis and curve to the side as she shot it out of her vagina. I felt there wouldn’t be a more convenient time to interrupt anytime soon, so I cut into the conversation as politely as I could. “Excuse me,” I said, clearing my throat. The Irishman finally noticed me, irked with my presence. “I was telling a story, here.” I had never challenged anyone to a duel before, and I wasn’t sure what the standard procedure was. I continued on uncouthly. “Then by all means, you should finish it pretty quickly, because that man at the bar bet me that I couldn’t beat you in a duel.” The Irishman craned his neck around me to take a look at the Captain, the strangely refined man dressed in the blue naval uniform complete with a hat shaped like the cross section of a loaf of bread. The Irishman snorted. “Let’s make it quick then. I’ll finish my story after they court your carcass off under a little, white sheet.” “We’ll see,” I said with more confidence than a man who couldn’t shoot worth a lick should have. “I’ll be in the alley out back in five minutes.” The Irishman nodded and his friends stayed gruffly silent as I walked back to the bar to convey the news to Captain Horatio. The Captain was standing rigidly flatfooted, but he stood with a certain height to him, giving off the illusion that he was on tiptoes. “It’s all going down in the back alley in five minutes,” I informed the Captain. “Let me borrow your pistol.” He cautiously handed over his pistol, but it appeared to vex him that I hadn’t a pistol of my own. I had always wanted one for my amusement, but perhaps it was for the better that I never had one. As I accommodated for the weight of the pistol in my hand, Captain Horatio led me to the back alley. I could see him wincing as the familiar scenery further revived memories of his own shameful failure. Still, he found enough dignity left somewhere within to oversee the proceedings of the duel. The Irishman was already standing out there, leaning against a brick wall and coolly smoking a corn cob pipe. I wouldn’t be surprised if he dueled out here daily, and I would be equally as unsurprised if he hadn’t hit a single one of his contestants. Perhaps he really was the luckiest son of a bitch that ever lived. “Alright, gentlemen,” Horatio called. “You two will start back to back and take twenty paces forward. When I call for the draw, you may turn around and fire one shot. The winner is the man left standing, and the prize is his life.” He turned to me first. “Are you ready?” I nodded, still getting a feel for the gun by rubbing my hand over the barrel. He then turned to the Irishman. “Are you ready?” “Let’s get on with it,” spat the Irishman. Then Captain Horatio lined us up back to back, a hand blocking each of our chests. When he released his hands, I felt the Irishman stumble forward; the pressure from his back against mine alleviating from my shoulders. I have to tell you right off that it wasn’t much of a duel. The really interesting part was the aftermath. You see, I was never that good at paying attention, and I hadn’t taken my medication yet, so I wasn’t in the right state of mind to focus on fine details such as the procedures of a duel. In short, I basically skipped the instructions about taking twenty paces and pretty much everything after that as well. Instead, I immediately turned, pointed the pistol at the Irishman’s back and pulled the trigger. I didn’t feel too well about being splattered with chunks of brain matter and cracked skull, but there was some saying I kept in mind about making an omelet. “What the fuck was that?” the Captain screamed irately. “Did I win?” I asked aloofly, picking some of the larger spackles of heady innards off of my jacket. It was a nice jacket, too. They don’t make jackets like that anymore. “You forfeited victory when you so dishonorably shot him in the back of the head!” Truthfully, though, it was a lucky shot. I was aiming for his torso. “Give me my money back!” “Wouldn’t it make more sense for you to ask for the gun back first?” Insulted by my cleverness, the Captain reached carelessly for the pistol, but I wasn’t quite ready to return it yet. “Just give me the gun. The police will arrive soon, and it would be best if we weren’t here when that…” I interrupted him, though, by shooting him in the foot. He yelled and cursed and shifted his weight to the foot I hadn’t shot. I then held the pistol to his face and forced him around the building. He limped in great pain, swearing at me as I tried my best to act like it was natural for a man in a sea captain’s uniform to limp around at gunpoint bleeding from the foot. “You shot me in the foot, you fucking cunt!” That I did, and the Captain was none too happy about it. “It was a lucky shot. I must have been wearing my good luck charm.” With my free hand, I pulled out a tribal talisman I had acquired from the chief of some Pacific islanders; King Ayummayumma or something equally long and self repetitious. The driver was parked outside in a long black car. He had done his job of waiting around to drive me back to the Count’s manor, and he even opened the back door for the Captain and me. I didn’t envy him at all. He was a much more patient man than I was. In the back seat, I kept the gun locked on Captain Horatio as the car rolled to a steady pace. It was awkward; riding in the back with the man I had just shot and was currently delivering to the Count. I wanted to keep conversation frank, but the Captain was insistent on digging for answers that were of no use to a man who was as good as dead. “Where are you taking me?” Still, I felt that we were well past the point where I had to be selectively honest. “We’re headed to the Count’s manor.” His eyes widened with fear, and I could see the blood rush out from them. They were the whitest eyes that I had ever seen. They were white as ghosts. “The Count?” he gasped. “That vampire! He’s after my blood! Isn’t he?” I sighed and nodded. “That vampire! He’s going to drink my blood. That old soulless devil!” His ill informed outbursts made me laugh a bit. “Don’t be ridiculous. There’s no such thing as vampires,” I assured him. “As you know, though, the Count is an old man. He’s almost two hundred years old, and to stay alive, he just needs his blood changed out from time to time. It’s unfortunate for you that he’s chosen your blood, but there’s this old cliché I like to use sometimes about making an omelet.” “That bloodsucking son of a bitch! He won’t get away with this.” Oh, but he has before and he will again. “For the last time, Captain Horatio, the Count is no vampire, and he doesn’t drink blood. He much fancies chocolate, though.” The driver sped onward, and slowly Captain Horatio accepted his fate. He was better off that way. I lived on another day, puzzled by these cartooned characters who fought so ardently over the one common bond between them all.
