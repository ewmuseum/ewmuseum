Date: 5/18/09 “I was never one to patiently pick up broken fragments and glue them together again and tell myself that the mended whole was as good as new. What is broken is broken - and I'd rather remember it as it was at its best than mend it and see the broken places as long as I lived. -Margaret Mitchell May 24th, 2009 10:34 P.M. Sweat danced everywhere on my skin like pinpricks. I like have a sweat on when I walk out there for a match; it loosens up the muscles and the mind. Sweat that comes from shaky nerves collects on the palms, soles, and forehead. If you’re already sweating all over, you don’t particularly notice that nervous sweat. You mask the fear. Just because you mask the fear, though, doesn’t mean it disappears. It was there all the gnashing its teeth and nipping at my heels. Anyone who tells you they don’t feel it is lying. But that’s what I use the gorilla position for: compartmentalizing the fear. Tucking it away underneath tens of thousands of soaring cheers and strategy that’s been planned out for weeks. Because once you’re at the curtain, it’s too late to be afraid. In the past two months, I’d spent sleepless nights on do-it-yourself psychology, groping blindly at Snow and Johnny’s thoughts. I’d broken fingers on the punching bag, training to and beyond my body’s breaking point. I’d stared at tapes until they melted into fuzzy nothingness, blurry shapes moving across pixilated horizons. I’d exhausted every option while exhausting myself. And now, it was time to strap on the dancin’ shoes and tango. “Chan.” The unmistakable baritone broke my concentration and made me clench my fist, but I didn’t turn around. “Listen, I don’t have time for this.” Of all the inopportune moments to be sought out, this was tops in my book. On war’s doorstep, ready to plunge into a battle I’d promised not to lose, there was this. “S’a damn necessity that I do somethin’ before you go out there, boss. Ya know, by virtue ‘a how you left things with our mutual ‘quaintance.” I turned around to see, in full widescreen panoramic vision, Dametreyus Fuqueiawytas. Head of Security at PRIME by day, Renaissance man by night. My fists were still clenched; I hadn’t forgotten our earlier encounter. “You preventing me from stepping out there means a couple thing. Means you physically preventing an employee of this company from fulfilling his contractual obligations. Means you’re still letting that ginger runt run your life. Means you make me an enemy.” Dam hung his head slightly. He looked ashamed at what he had to do. The ghost of Danny Ferguson come back to haunt me right as I tried to snatch back previous glories. I’d thought it was done at King of Kings. I’d been wrong. “Fine, then,” I announced, voice never showing the doubt that I felt going up against an immovable object like Dam. “Make it quick.” /~/~/~/~/ May 18th, 2009 10:11 A.M. After my initial failure at the Dike, I hadn’t expected to be back a second time, no less a third. But my instincts had been right: there was some primitive therapy to be had in a place like this. Not for the kids, who toiled away under Tom Barius, that Nero of academia. But for me, there was something good about listening, for once, instead of talking. If I’d been able to see Doc DiPendo, he probably would’ve said that I was using these kids as I’d been using people in my life: as shrinks. Intertwining their problems with my own gave me perspective, he’d say, and allowed me to momentarily lose my habitual tunnel vision. But Doc DiPendo had deserted me a long time ago when a desperate, broken Chandler Tsonda had begged him to refill a scrip for Tylox. Or maybe I’d deserted him by locking him out of my mind and throwing away the key. Either way, the same desperate, broken Chandler Tsonda now held on to this strange ritual like a life preserver. There was something deeply humanizing here, something that I needed. I was still confined to the visitors’ area, despite repeated requests that Barius let me into the body of the prison itself. He deflected my efforts with phrases like “security risk” and “bounds of liability.” The real reason was Barius’s imagined power struggle with me; he thought that if I started lending an ear to some of his “residents,” he’d no longer be able to make puppets of them. He played the game in subtle ways, screening my calls for days at a time or changing my appointment times two or three times a week. As a result, I had no idea what junior criminal was supposed to meet with me today. Watching a meaty security guard lead the young man towards me was startling. He dwarfed the not-small-at-all guards, and plodded towards me with a soft, surprisingly limber gait. A tall, disorderly afro bounced in waves as he approached the table, which seemed entirely too small for the two of us to share. “Yo.” It was the best I could come up with. The cat wouldn’t let go of my tongue for any more than that. “Ey man.” There was a silent hiccup in the conversation before I fumbled to extend a handshake to the massive teen in front of me. “Chandler.” Still looming over me, he looked down at my hand before offering me his own meaty paw. “O.” “That short for anything?” “Omari. Got like ten African names in a row, if you wanna hear ‘em.” “Not unless you want me to.” I nodded to him, trying to gesture him into the seat so things might be a little less forced between us. “Nice to meet you, O.” “You too, Chan’ler.” He looked down at the seat. “Now, you lookin’ at me like you want me to sit, but no way me and you’re sharin’ that itty bitty bench.” He spoke with a matter-of-fact intelligence and an easily obtained awareness of his surroundings. “You won’t go takin’ an insult outta me sittin’ over here?” “Yeah, no problem.” O sat with soft thud against the cheap plastic furnishing. “You’re…umm…big.” “You’re small,” he shot back from an expressionless face. “That was supposed to sound like more of a compliment. I just meant that you’re a pretty impressively big dude for being only…what, seventeen?” “Eighteen. Just turned a week ago.” “Happy belated birthday.” “Yeah.” He looked away. It didn’t take an expert in body language to see that O didn’t want to talk about turning legal inside of this panoptic bubble. Thinking about my own 18th birthday brought to mind a few blurry clips of memory: Keri Matta, the back seat of her Camry, a water bottle full of Southern Comfort. There was no one like Keri Matta to help celebrate new manhood in a place like the Dike. A birthday was just another dreary reminder of the life that had been checked at the door like so many other personal effects. “You like sports?” “Well ‘nuff. Played football pretty good. Hoops, too.” “You ever get a chance to play at the Dike?” He snorted a laugh, bringing me back to full awareness of how alien this place was to me. “Man, don’t nobody call it ‘the Dike.’” “My bad. What do you call it?” “Me?” He sounded and looked surprised that I’d ask or his opinion. He pushed aside hair to scratch his head. “Guess I think of it like that spot, Judecca. The one where Dante gets to the end of Hell and you think he home free, but then he’s still gotta roll through Purgatory and Paradise to get what he’s lookin’ for.” “You’re…pretty well read for an eighteen-year-old behind bars.” “Maybe dumb niggas don’t read books, but I’m good with words. My English teacher in tenth grade made us read that shit. Boring as all hell.” “But you remembered it.” “Yeah, well, Hell ain’t easy to forget.” I thought about interrupting, but the weight of the look in O’s eyes silenced whatever I thought I might say. “That was me last year. Thought I reached the end of Hell’s road and goddamn if the Devil wasn’t just playin’ tricks on me.” “It ended up with you in here?” “It started with me in here, man.” He sighed, his deep voice all of a sudden sounding weathered with troubles. “Got brought on some punk charges and all of a sudden, my pops start comin’ down sick. Doctors tell ‘im he’s got prostate cancer. Just about kills Lulu–tha’s my granny–and now her diabetes actin’ up. And after I got here, moms lost her job subbin’ over at the school. Fam ain’t been right since I caught a charge.” “Charge for what?” “Twenty-nine grams.” He looked me straight in the eye, his words powerful and declarative again. “Ain’t but a slice over an ounce, and that’s how much of Ronald’s stemmed-out hood ass dirt trees was in my backseat. My ride, my time.” “Jesus, and they gave you intent to distribute?” He nodded angrily, frustration filing his eyes with fire. “Maybe weed is decriminalized for rich white folks, but they still puttin’ niggas in prison for havin’ it. Wasn’t about to ask my pops to skip his cancer drugs just so I can get off over an ounce of green, so I had one ‘a those public defenders on my case. Pled my shit down to three months in here.” The realness of the problems I heard about in here…it made me embarrassed to worry about all the things I worried about in an average day. “Nuff about that. So, apparently, you pretty famous.” “On my good days, yeah. You follow PRIME at all?” “Nah.” Well, that was final. “I mean, I respect that paycheck you gettin’, but when I got a minute outside of work and school, I work on my spoken word game.” “Young James Baldwin?” “Baldwin, Langston, Mos Def. And when I’m in here, locked up, heated and depressed at the same time…a little Mumia.” His bibliography was impressive. There was certainly more to O than his one-letter nom de guerre. “O, when do you get outta here?” He pulled up one sleeve of his shirt, revealing penned tally marks across his thick bicep. “Twenty-eight days.” “I’d like to come back and bring one of the people who works for me. I can’t promise anything, but if I give this guy the word, I could get you a decent summer job at our offices in San Diego.” “Thanks, man, but I’on’t even know you.” “What’re you planning on doing when you get out, then?” “Dunno, but you don’t seem like a dude dumb enough to know anybody dumb enough to go hirin’ a supposed drug dealin’ black man to some cushy desk job that’s meant for a white lady.” “Thing is, at my company, I’m the big boss. If I say you’re hired, everybody else abides by that. Listen, I don’t wanna push the issue, ‘cuz I feel like that was a good talk.” O shrugged his broad shoulders. “C’mon, O. Isn’t this better than sitting in there listening to Barius yammer on?” “Ey man, you aite. You wanna come back some time and talk about…fuck, whatever you wan’ talk about, that’s cool. But I’ve gotta make right with mine. I get back out there…I’ma have to go in with Ronald. More money workin’ at the Walgreens and flippin’ on the side than workin’ behind your white lady desk.” “Kid, I can make it worth your while if that’s the issue. I’d rather not see another young man of color wasting away behind bars over a pocketful of weed.” “You know what they say, man. Ain’t nobody a criminal until you lock him up.” “Listen, I’m gonna come back. I’ll bring some James Baldwin, see if I can grab that new Mos Def CD. But will you at least do me the favor of lying to me and promising that you’ll think about the desk job?” “Sure.” “I’m gonna help you if it kills you, kid.” “Seven levels’a nonsense. How come you so interested in helpin’ me if Barius ain’t?” “Because Barius is a security guard with a college degree. And I’m…somebody else. Besides, you remind me of somebody I used to know.” “Yeah? Lotta six foot eight black dudes walkin’ around yo necka the woods?” “You remind me of someone who reminds me of someone else, kid. Someone from my past. Someone I was never able to get right with.” O shot me a raised eyebrow. “You gotta get right, man. The second I get outta here, I’m bout to get right. With my pops. With the Lord. With my Lulu. You gotta step back before you step forward.” “Yeah…” I murmured and drifted off, trying to recall the poem that I had in mind. It was something simple, something from a class nearly two decades ago. But it had always seemed applicable for my line of work, for my outlook on unquenchable revenge. “It’s like that poem. Be near me now. My tormentor, my love, be near me.”* “Like,” O thought out loud, “you gotta find what hurts, what don’t sit right, ‘cuz that’s what you gotta give a fuck about.” “Right.” /~/~/~/~/ May 20th, 2009 6:39 P.M. “This is fucking stupid.” It was one of those statements you mumble to no one but yourself when you’re standing, in the rain, at a door you’ve been knocking on for five minutes without a whimper of an answer. Whoever said it never rains in Southern California must’ve been selling optional sunroofs. The rain was light until you’d absorbed its pitter-pattering for three hundred some seconds. Not I cared for this particular audience, but my hair was ruined. It’s hard to be taken seriously looking like a wet dog. I kept knocking. “I’ll stay out here until my knuckles break or you open this door, and my knuckles are stronger than your crappy faux oak door!” Appealing to his sense of compassion for a weary traveler hadn’t worked, so it was time to try his competitive streak. Get right with the Lord. That was something that required my belief in him or Him or whatever, and it wasn’t happening. But I could take a page out of O’s book and do some earthly gettin’ right. That was undeniable soul saving stuff. “C’mon! I’m soaked, isn’t that enough?” Raindrops tap dancing on the hood of my SUV was the only response. Maybe there were some wrongs that couldn’t get right. Maybe this was one of those things that would never be okay. Which brought me back to my original point. “Fucking stupid motherfucking–” “Ain’t polite to put ya feet on a man’s doorstep talkin’ that noise, boss.” “Uhhh…” “Best wipe those muddy ass joints on yo feet off ‘fore you step inside. Best thing you Asians done was invent that ‘shoes off when you step in the crib’ rule.” As I stood there, shocked to see my colleague answering the door, Dam took off from whence he’d come, still talking over his shoulder. “I be upholdin’ that Bushido shit in my spot. Saves my ass hundreds on steamcleanin’…” “Uhhh...so I can come in?” “Door didn’t open isself, boss.” Dam’s voice was in another room, but I couldn’t immediately tell where. I slipped my shoes off and studied the house’s interior. It had always been a nice house, but the emptiness of all that space spoke to me now. It told a familiar tale of an owner too caught up in his own problems to feel the awkwardly cold aesthetic of his home. Golden accolades of PRIME past, statues that deserved a trophy case, spilled out of a box in the wide open coat closet. What had once been beautifully lacquered wood was insulted by a layer of dirt and dust. It was clean in that nothing-on-the-floor-but-still-dirty way. I slowly tiptoed from the corner to the living room, from where I could hear the TV and Dam’s voice. “So yeah, thassaboutit. Money’s right, and I ain’t clownin’ around in some Gladiator costume like back in the day.” “I get offers for that filth all the time. The Surreal Life 9, Tila Tequila’s Bisexual Bonanza, Survivor: Baghdad. It’s all a joke, Dam. With no punchline and no prospects...ahh, welcome home, traitor.” Danny Ferguson caught me out of the corner of his eye before I was two steps into the room. He didn’t even turn his head to me, but addressed me as an offscreen character. “Hi, Danny.” An unprofessional layer of strawberry blond scruff marked his face; I’d never seen him with facial hair before, and I knew he grew facial hair like a sixth grader. Translation: he hasn’t shaved in weeks. He was wearing a maroon robe that I thought I recognized as a gift from Hef when we’d gone to the Mansion a couple years back. Slumped all the way into his couch, he was a metaphor for a downfall. On TV was the clincher, an-edited-for-time version of Hard Money. The fallen movie star, fallen wrestler, watching his former body of work with two ex-partners whose lives still stunk of success. “You want a beer or something, Benedict Arnold? Or have you not done enough yet? Maybe you could steal some stuff from my house. That’d be a good final knife in my back.” “Beer would be great, but it looks like you probably drank them all.” At the Superstar’s feet was a day’s worth of Miller Lite cans. Retired life didn’t seem to suit the ex-A-Lister. “Ha. Glad to see you brought some jokes with you, turncoat. Not enough that you strongarmed Superbitch into firing me after I almost took your title.” “Danny, you’re…you wereunstable. Ty, Lisa, it didn’t matter who was in charge. No one was about to hire back a guy who ran around in a mask for a month to try and steal back the Universal Title.” “Steal the title? That one’s rich and full of flavor, Chantard. Whatever. PRIME’s a dead end joint anyway. Money’s coming back to Hollywood. People are enjoying their small pleasures in the recession and that’s bringing wads of cash into the box office.” “This place doesn’t look like there’s been a wad of cash here since…well, since you stopped cashing your PRIME checks.” I didn’t know if Dam’s look to me was a glare or not, but it wasn’t friendly. The Head of Security had always been the quiet type when juxtaposed against Danny’s brash nature, but in working directly with the big man, I felt pretty familiar with his mannerisms. “Ha!” Ferguson spat out a forced laugh. “This old thing? Yeah, it looks like shit because I’ve spent all my time on building a new patio for my house in Arizona. Three doors down from McCain. We’ve had a couple keggers. Cindy’s made a couple passes, I’ve just been waiting to see if Secret Service is still keeping tabs on the old bag before I go into full throttle pursuit.” “Sounds…” Dam shot me that same look. What did he want? For me to be nice to the guy? One look at him, though…how could I rub salt on what looked like mortal wounds? “…pretty renegade, man. I keep hearing those ‘Zona suburbs are the new playground for the rich.” Ferguson sat up abruptly, sneering in my direction. I avoided eye contact with Dam. “Was that…were you just being chummy? Dam, tell me that Mr. Ex-Champion is not playing chummy with me.” “Looks to me like he got a hammer and nails in his pocket, Dan. Jus’ tryna mend some fenches is all.” Danny’s head whirled around to his former bodyguard. “Dam, you and me are boys. Don’t make me kick you out of Chateau Superstar for siding with the Charlies on this.” He glared back at me, eyes alive with old disdain. “Do you need a public notary or something? You didn’t drive here from Diego just to watch reruns.” “No. No, I didn’t.” Thinking about an apology was going to be much easier than saying the words. I took a deep breath. “I just wanted…to tell you that if things are looking up for with the movie shit, that’s cool. And if they’re not–” “Oh, this is precious. Are you faking an apology so I’ll give you carte blanche to get on with the rest of your miserable life? Dam, pull out your phone and let’s get this on tape.” Dam didn’t budge. He just stared ahead, as the 2003 version of Danny Ferguson dodged an on-screen explosion. “I’m not fucking around, man. I didn’t come for some sappy reconciliation. I just came to say that I’m sorry this is the way things ended up. I gotta get right with my side of this if I wanna be free of it.” “If you’re not free of this, it’s on you. I’ve moved the hell on while you, as usual, have gotten all post-menstrual and touchy feely.” “You’ve moved the hell on to wasting your day getting hammered and watching your glory days on TV?” “I’m not the one who drove two hours to talk my problems out, pussy.” I clenched my fist and started to swing, but I caught myself. If all I did for the rest of my life was buy into his baiting tactics, then his influence would parasitically feed off me, one more problem amongst a litany of others. “You don’t need to accept my apology. And know what, Dan? I’m not sorry for becoming the bigger star while you were gone. I’m not sorry for what Rayne did to you. I’m not sorry for knocking you out of the game at King of Kings. I’m just sorry that you blame me for all your problems. I can’t do any more than that.” I turned to leave, when I heard his slightly drunken laugh start up again. My blood was boiling. “You’re wrong, Tink. And by the way, may I say what an adorable pet name?” “I already stopped myself from whipping your ass once. I don’t have oodles of self-control to spare, jackass.” “You’re wrong,” he maintained, ignoring me. “Because you think that your apology changes something about the way you see yourself. You think that psychology, that getting your head on straight, will make you win on Sunday. What a kneeslapper.” “Fuck you.” He was undeterred, though. He’d found a loose stitch and began ripping at it. “It’s hilarious because you don’t realize that I’m just a year, maybe two if you’re lucky, ahead of you on this slippery slope. You don’t find it coincidental that damn near exactly one year after I dedicated myself to finally climbing the mountain and winning the Dual Halo…here you storm in, ready to sell your soul to climb the mountain and win back your Universal Title.” “I’ve won the big ones, shitheel. I was Jewel in the Crown. I was the longest-reigning Universal Champ. I’m still playing with house money.” “Culture Shock 2008. I get railroaded by your boyfriend. Culture Shock 2009. You get the biggest wakeup call of your career, losing to Snow twice in twenty-four hours. In both cases, the guy who ends up on right side of those incidents goes on to become the man for the next era in PRIME.” “Tell me what Snow’s proved that I haven’t!” I was trying to engage him in conversation, but Danny just wanted to lay out this theory, to show me how I was going to become him. “When you lose on Sunday–and you will lose, apostate, don’t fool yourself into thinking that two healthier, younger guys are going to lose to the diseased family dog that needs to be put down–that’ll be that. There’ll be no reason to delay the back surgery any longer, and I don’t see Lisa Tyler letting you limp around as you wait for another bite at the Universal scene. A thirty-four year old getting major back surgery? That sound like a fun bit of rehab?” Maybe it was a rhetorical question for Dam, but the big man’s eyes stayed glued on the screen. He wasn’t going to play peacekeeper for two grown men. “You’ll stick with it for a while, telling yourself it’s worth it. But, eventually, six months out from being under the knife, you’ll be only halfway to what you used to be, with metal holding your spine together like a goddamn science fair exhibit. And you’ll quit. And you’ll sit on the couch, replaying what you could’ve done to have a longer, more illustrious career. And it’ll be worse for you, Brutus. You were at the top, you dropped off, and promised everyone that you’d do anything to get back there. Another promise you couldn’t keep.” “You’re so fuckin’ delusional it’s sad.” “My smile’s been turned upside down. It’s gonna be a laugh riot watching you as you see your legacy go from ‘two-time champion, legendary PRIMEate’ to ‘just another champion.’ You wanna hear some names? Boda. Zyfloi. Guys who held the title but never mattered in the scheme of things.” “I broke Nova’s record for–” “You rule a pile of shit for a year or a day, it’s still a pile of shit. You had two legitimate defenses. Rayne and me. If you’re taking those only those two wins to the bank for your legacy in PRIME, you just don’t have the chops, kid.” “Sour grapes, thy name is Ferguson.” “Your title run was the equivalent of a sports team winning in a lockout year. Yeah, you get the trophy and the banner, and hey, you ever get a sweet parade. But everyone remembers that you were a circumstantial champion, the biggest fish in a small, asterix-laden pond.” “Glad to see you’ve developed a real humble streak despite it all. If that’s all from Danny’s Wacky World of Theories, I think my lungs have sucked down enough Hollywood smog for one…ever.” This time, when I turned to leave, I didn’t turn back. That didn’t stop him from calling over my shoulder. “Come back and visit after the surgery, Quisling. I always like company.” When I got back to San Diego, I had to make a stop at the emergency room. I’d broken another finger punching the hell out of the steering wheel. /~/~/~/~/ May 24th, 2009 10:35 P.M. “C’mon. You stand with your boy Ferguson. Do what he could never do, Dam: cost me my Universal Title.” He didn’t look me in the eyes as he took another step towards me. I crouched. With a low center of gravity, I’d get one or two shots in before he could put me on the ground. “That’s…not what this is, boss,” he offered somberly, taking a final step towards me and placing his hand on my shoulder.
