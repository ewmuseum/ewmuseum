Telegram (ReVolution 186, singles match vs. Bryan Dawkins)

18 February 2009
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: February 18th, 2009 He drove. And he drove. And he drove. With no particular destination. South, and likely west, but there was no "X" marked on the map inside his head. Just a word: "Home." But the definition of "home" gets skewed when you spend the majority of your life living and sleeping inside a four-wheel drive hunk of metal with an engine and a windshield. But still, it's a hard notion to ignore when it's time to go back. Well, more "away" than "back." The war with Sin City Championship Wrestling could test the willpower of any man, and for Wade Elliott, PRIME's Bad Dog, it did. The mark in the "loss" column after the juggernaut of an event that was Cataclysm didn't weigh on his mind. Defeat, yes, but PRIME and the wrestling world wasn't a place for Wade to succeed in. PRIME was a paycheck. Of course, that's not to say nothing else came with it. It's still life. A place to make a couple friends, more than enough enemies, and take a few blows to the head. It's consistent; Wake-up eat breathe drive drink fight drink yell drink sleep. Rinse and repeat. It works. But for a drifter, more often than not, you need to get away. Sometimes you need to go home. Wherever the hell that is. * * * * * * * Late September, 2008 Somewhere... "Pushin' my luck..." Wade released a deep yawn, blinking his sharp blue eyes and breathing in deep through his nostrils. He didn't know how long he had driven, likely the better part of the day. He rubbed the bridge of his nose with his index finger and thumb, trying massage away the exhaustion. He and Angus had made it to Kentucky, that much he knew. Where in Kentucky was a different story, not that it mattered. The route from Boston, Massachusetts was a haphazard one, a trip that would normally take half a day or so had taken three or four. He hadn't been paying attention to road signs, nor which way was north, south, east or west. He just drove. "Best hop out fer a second, stand on my feet," he muttered, slowing his black Chevrolet Silverado down to a crawl and pulling off the main road. It was a nice day, simply put, encroaching on the evening hours. Warm with a comfortable breeze, a few clouds to dull the blaze of the waning sun. A lush field sat on the right hand side, bordered by a green forest. He came to a stop, killing the engine. "Be right back, pup," he said, gently scratching the curled-up blue tick hound in the passenger's seat behind the ears. Angus slept. Wade popped the door open and hopped outside, taking in a deep breath of fresh air. He circled his truck to take a few steps toward the field, noticing a pair of dirt tire tracks winding through the grass. He pulled off his drifter's hat, running his other hand through his short brown hair, taking another deep breath through his nose. With no coffee to speak of, fresh air could often do the trick, but even the simple act of standing did wonders. Snap. The world went yellow. An odd sensation of drowning overwhelmed his senses. He stood at the edge of the same field, though the grass was now yellow and dead. His truck was no longer behind him, and the sun blazed what seemed like hundreds of degrees hotter. He started to sweat. This was not the first time he had experienced these visions, but the abrupt changing of the world was impossible to become accustomed to, though he tried his best to keep his composure. The eyes bulging from their sockets, however, would say otherwise. The thuds of hundreds of boots sounded in the distance. From the far end of the field marched many men, muskets draped over shoulders, clad in gray. Wade stood transfixed and afraid, and at the same time trying to stand up straight and not vomit, or pass out from the heavy, yellow world that had taken over his vision. The army marched closer, following the dirt path across the field. He pulled his hands across his eyes and down his bushy brown goatee, trying to gain his composure. The army had reached his position, passing him by, paying him no mind. The sharp raps of drumsticks on drums pierced his ears. Each soldier stared straight ahead, each step methodical and calculated. They were hardly alive. Faces frozen and emotionless, eyes half open and white, with no pupils to speak of. One soldier left formation, and wandered toward the bewildered 'Bama Bruiser. "Telegram, private," said the soldier, his voice hollow, monotone, and distorted. "Private?" said Wade, squinting his eyes. He looked down, and for the first time noticed that his jeans and green t-shirt had been replaced by the gray fatigues of the Rebel Army. The soldier handed a wax-sealed letter to Wade, then spun on his heel and marched back into line. The army marched by and eventually out of sight. After a moment of staring into void, Wade looked down to the letter, the front reading "Private Wade Allen Elliott" in classic script. He curled his lips inward, and broke the wax seal, unfolding the piece of parchment. TELEGRAM This letter sent on behalf of the Confederate States of America regrets to inform Private Wade Allen Elliott that his mother's illness has taken a turn for the worse. She requests his presence at his earliest release from his duties. Signed, -Robert E. Lee The confusion had reached new heights. Wade read the letter over and over again, only to eventually crumple it into a tiny ball and drop it to the ground. Sweat poured from his brow. He looked in every direction, frantically searching for a way out. He whirled around... ...and again, froze stiff. In front of him stood Karen Elliott herself. Wispy, graying brunette hair falling over her shoulders. Thin skin hung from weak bones, a nightgown hanging from her aged body. "...Ma?" said Wade, once his voice would allow words. "Glad ya got my letter, Wade," she said, voice hoarse and raspy as she took steady steps toward her son. "Ma...what the hell's goin' on?" "Ya got the message, but it's just too late," she replied, ever closer. "What're ya talkin' about?" he asked quietly, on the verge of stammering. "Ya won't make it in time," she coughed, now within inches of him. His mouth hung agape, but he could not assemble words. "I'm out've time..." She broke into a violent coughing fit. Wade's muscles locked as his mother struggled to breath. He gripped her shoulders to hold her still, but the fit only intensified. Black, rancid smoke billowed from her mouth with each hack of her lungs, filling his nostrils and stinging his eyes, reeling him back in horror. Snap. Gone. Wade gasped for breath. His mother was no longer in front of him. The field had regained its green hue. The air was no longer heavy and yellow. His truck returned to the side of the road. But he felt no different. His fingers spread out in front of him, gripping the air where his mother's shoulders had been in the vision. Eyes bulged wide, jaw hung low. He stood stiff, heart racing. The scratching of claws against window snapped him out of it. Wade turned his gaze to his truck, where Angus clawed desperately at the driver's side window. He spat, rubbed his eyes, and stumbled to his rig. He climbed into the driver's seat and slammed the door shut, where Angus was quick to nuzzle his shoulder, whining with concern. "I'm alright, pup," said Wade, his breath finally catching up with him. He scratched the dog's neck, regaining some form of composure. "I'm alright..." Once settled he started the engine, the v8 bellowing with life. "C'mon, boy," he said, putting the vehicle into gear. "We got a ways to go..." He hit the gas, tearing up dirt before pulling onto the main road, headed west. This time with a mark on the map, a direction to point the grill of his truck at. The mark wasn't PRIME or the next ReVolution. It wasn't even his hometown of Pine Ridge, Alabama. It was Arizona, in worn down, beat up house, where his mother lay in bed. "...Ma's callin'."
