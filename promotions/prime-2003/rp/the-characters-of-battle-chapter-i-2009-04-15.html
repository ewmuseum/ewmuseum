Date: 04.15.2009 The Characters Of Battle (Chapter I) There are two sides to every story though they may not always be the same sides. There are also sections that lay part on one side and part on the other, but these sections, too, can be rounded to their respective sides or dissolved across them. Take a circle, for instance, and put it in a square where the length of one side of the square equals the circle's diameter. Then draw a circle around the square where the diameter of the circle equals the length of the diagonal of the square. Is not a third, concentric circle, equidistant from the original two circles the same size, then, as the square? Conversely, then, one must assume that a square drawn around the largest circle where its diagonal meausred the same as the circle's diameter and a concentric square drawn equidistant between the first two squares would share an equal area to the second circle. Now consider a fabric of concentric circles and squares where each square shares a side with one of four squares of equal size, and each circle touches at a point with another of four circles. If there are two shapes -- only circles and squares, mind you -- which shape would remain if all the lines were removed so that only the edges remained? Why, of course, the victor! However, this theoretical expongement of geometric figures is more easily conceptualized than practiced. If, when all things are measured equally, the interior stitchings vanished, the remaining shape would undoubtedly be a vague resemblance of either a square or a circle, perimetered by alternating arcs and corners, and for very good reasons, this cannot be. There are many theories about determining victory before declaration, but perfecting such a mechanism would dull the blade of the process. It would rethink itself if figured. It would tangle like a vine shooting skyward. These theories, you see, merely attempt to hack at the vine with dulling blades. For the vine cannot be cut, but it can be untethered from itself as it grows. Each thin strand, once unwrapped from a tautness, would sheer upon the touch, and the pieces, removed from the whole, could be examined down to the tiniest molecule. Then, and even then, would the squares get smaller and the circles within them and outwith until they were all just faint blotches with an arc here and a corner there, sometimes moving like swimming. With all the circles and squares running around all interwoven, it's understandable that spotting an inconsistent shape or pattern would prove rather taxing. However, when the numbers and dispersions of these irregularities seem to form a pattern themselves, it becomes a whiff shy of impossible. In these nanoscopic frays, the outcome of battle is written, but to see the frays, one must first shave the strings of vine to sheets. *** There was old Baby Shoes, standing there holding a flimsy box with both ends open and a handful of playing cards out there on the corner. Except, it wasn't old Baby Shoes. Old Baby Shoes was a different guy who'd offer to shine your shoes for a dollar, and if you wasn't wearing shoes, he'd shine your feet or just about anything that needed shining. Old Baby Shoes would never take your money if you didn't let him shine nothing. I didn't know this guy's name. He was a tall, bald guy who had a funny way of looking at you. The other time I'd seen him, he came up to me on the sidewalk and racketeered about how he was in a coma for fifteen years, and when he came out of it, he'd gained some psychic powers. He said I had to give him five dollars if he could guess how many siblings I had, and much to my surprise, he guessed right. Of course, I lied to him about it. I figured it was risky business, lying to a psychic, but then again, he should've known I had no money to give him anyway. I was already halfway across the street when I'd seen him, and I'd seriously considered turning around. "AY YO!" he yelled at full volume. "JOHNNY!" I froze, there, in the crosswalk, and cars started honking or swerving around me over the divider. "YOU KNOW I KNOW YOU SEEN ME, JOHNNY!" and it was almost as if he knew I knew he knew. It snapped me from the pause, and grumbling curses, I made no rush to the other side of the corner in spite of the traffic. "Will you keep it down?" I, beaten, muttered. "What do you want?" "Gimme a cigarette," he looked around nervously, tapping his fingers against his palms. "If I had a cigarette, don't you think I'd be smoking it right now?" "Don't bullshit a bullshitter, Johnny," he jittered. "Besides, man, haven't you heard? I've got psychic powers an' shit." "I seem to remember . . ." I trailed off, thinking about how I had imprisoned myself. As long as I didn't give him a cigarette, I was depriving myself of one, and he was pulling me into the doorway of a closed bank. "Back in 1988, I got shot in the face and that shit put me in a coma for 26 years -- been a psychic since I came out." "Need a light?" I produced the box of cigarettes and selected one for him. "Nah, I got one," he snatched. I wondered why a man without cigarettes would have a lighter as I lit my cigarette and he lit my cigarette as well. "Your math is off," and he looked at me funny with his funny way of looking. "Hasn't been anywhere near 26 years." "I got out early," he stated, and I really couldn't argue it. Sometimes a tactical diversion was the only way out of a corner. "So what'd you pull me off into this doorway for?" I shifted tone. "I got to catch a train." "Finish this smoke with me," he strongly suggested, slapping the box down on the ground so the open flaps of one side folded out across the ground. He closed the top lid to create a surface and sat on the step. I sat down on the step opposite the box from him. *** There are two fundamental rules of battle that cannot be broken by will or by might. The first rule is that there are circles and there are squares. The second rule is that the circles and the squares do not like each other. There was no standardized way of determination between squares and circles, and what could be a square to one could be a circle to another and the other way around. At the turn of a whim, a square could become a circle and a circle a square. The lines crooked or bent, and it didn't matter which was which so long that one was not the other. You determine the circles and the squares, but it is not solely your determination that decides the ratio of circles to squares. Perhaps ratio is a charged term. For everyone has their own interpretations of what makes a circle a circle or a square a square. A circle may think it's a circle or a square, and other circles may think it is a circle or a square, and other squares might think it a square, and it might be a square. For this reason, there is no reliable way to number these shapes as even they are uncertain of themselves. Given the assumption that there are no differences between circles and squares save that one is a circle and one is a square, it would follow that their numbers are about even. However, when perception is taken into account, the numbers skew and become harder to define. Without the illusion of illusion itself, the mathematics of battle would lock dormant in stalemate. Instead, the sum of every perceived circle and square, where the total number of perceived circles or squares would equal the sum of actual circles and squares squared, divided by the sum of every actual circle or square would warrant an unevenness, but not necessarily an unevenness, between perceived shapes. It is by this logic that the sum of every actual circle or square must not be acheived by simply adding circles and squares, rather, a total of shapes that could possibly be squares and circles with no calculable certainty. Thus, the sum of every perceived circle or square is not the sum of every perceived circle and square, even if it is possible, albeit only relatively slightly, for them to be the same. *** "Two of um's red. One of um's black. Can't pick the one an' you owe me a pack," he sung, and he had shown me all three cards to prove it before shuffling them around on top of that lousy box there. I watched conscienciously, the rest of that pack of cigarettes on the line against some money he had put together hustling other people the same way he was trying to hustle me. None too lucky for him, I had eyes like a hawk, when it came to things like this. I could at least tell if he was moving three cards around on top of a box, and I could tell if he wasn't, and that's really all the confidence I needed. He finished up his shuffling and looked up at me with a big toothy grin. "Which one you gonna pick?" came the challenge, wide eyed. "This one." I pointed to the one on the left, and he flipped it over quick like he was cooking pancakes. It was red. "Guess you owe me the rest of your smokes, then." I begrudgingly tossed the pack of smokes on top of his collection of dirty coins and wrinkled bills. He interlocked his fingers to shovel his winnings towards his lap. "Hold on a second," I quickly rebutted before pulling out a second, unopened pack of cigarettes for my opposite pocket. "Double or nothing. I'll throw in a full pack for a second chance between the two remaining cards." It didn't take him too long to respond. His grin widened, and, my god, all the teeth he had. He unlaced his fingers, and I tossed the fresh pack on the pile. "I got you. I got you." his bony finger danced from card to card. "So what's it gonna be?" Without hesitating, I pointed to the card on the right. He flipped it with even more zest this time. It was red -- the color of his coughing laughter. "Guess today ain't your day." His fingers locked again. "It's okay, though. Can't win 'em all." "Hold on," I barked again, and it startled him enough for him to keep his winnings surfaced on the box. "One more bet." "I can't do that man," he reasoned. "There's only one card left. We both know it's going to be the black one." "Then I bet you it's red." Now I was grinning. I reached into a slit I had cut in the inner lining of my coat and pulled out a third pack. The third, like the first, had been opened before, and I opened it again to pull out a plastic sandwich bag rolled up and stuffed inside. "It's eight grams of hash. You split it up right, you can sell this shit for twice what I paid for it and still have some left to yourself." *** Many who identify with either squares or circles make the mistake of misinterpreting the second fundamental rule of battle which states that circles and squares do not like each other. This rule does not imply that circles must like other circles or squares must like other squares. Instead, perceived squares can be segmented into perceived squares that are treated as circles and perceived squares that are treated as squares. Any further distinctions within those segmentations, and believe me -- there are many, are irrelevant, at this stage, in regards to the imbalances between perceived and actual counts of squares and circles. If each shape, on its own, can be either a circle or a square, there is then an equal chance that a shape will be a circle or a square. In this case, even a perceived total of circles and squares may not provide an accurate probability. A perceived total of circles or squares most definitely wouldn't, and the actual totals of either circles and squares or circles or squares may not be reliable due merely to the unfeasibility of obtaining an accurate actual total in lieu of the variable nature of perception. It is thereby most logical to assume that, for any circle, anything determined to be another circle should be regarded as a square, and that, for any square, anything determined to be a square should be regarded as a circle. Every shape is a line, and a line in itself, can be trusted. Any way you cut a line will maintain its symmetry unless you don't cut the line, instead opting for the plane around the line. Once you introduce angles into the equation, there is no shape that can be cut from all approaches and come out equally all ways. *** "If we both know that the middle card's red, you'd have no reason not to take that bet," I sewed at his reluctance with reason. "Unless, of course, you're hustling me." "Hey, man. I'm just trying to be a nice guy and not take you for all you got," and I didn't need psychic powers to tell he was bluffing. "Then I'd like my smokes back," I declared flatly as he searched for options. "Heh, I can't just . . ." he nearly went into hysterics, trying to act insulted with his guilty red hands as red as the center card. "A deal's a deal, man. I thought we had a deal." "Sometimes it is. Sometimes it isn't." I offered consolation. "How can a deal be a deal when you're the only one that's dealing anything?" "That's not the way it works, man. You made a deal. You said double or nothing." "Then I want my card back." "You didn't have any cards." "I wouldn't deal without any cards," I twisted. "Seems kind of hard to do." "Man, you know that ain't what I mean by a deal." "Now, how many cards were you moving around on top of that box again?" "Three cards! You saw it! Plain as day!" "Well, there's your three cards." I swept my hand over the two reds turned up and the one red turned down between them on the box. "I guess that means any other cards would be mine, right?" He didn't answer. He couldn't. If only he had some kind of diversion. "I must've misplaced that card somewhere, though." I patted my empty pockets. "I think I saw it drop underneath that box." "Fuck, man! Just take it then! Take it all!" he stood up and flailed his arms, growing violent with his gesture. "I don't want to take you for all you got," I reached under the box and pulled out a card. "I just want my card, you know?" He froze there as I showed him the black card. "This looks an awful lot like your card, though." I tucked the card into one of my pockets. He stood there, arms folded, unwilling or unable to look me in the eye. "And those look an awful lot like my cigarettes."
