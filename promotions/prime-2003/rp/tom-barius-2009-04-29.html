Tom Barius

29 April 2009

Date: 4/29/09 \"There is no society known where a more or less developed criminality is not found under different forms. No people exists whose morality is not daily infringed upon. We must therefore call crime necessary and declare that it cannot be non-existent, that the fundamental conditions of social organization, as they are understood, logically imply it.\" -Emile Durkheim Monday, April 27th 1:17 P.M. The caged feeling isn’t a joke. You start to sweat more. Whatever level of claustrophobia you’ve got (and everyone’s got some running through their blood, whether it takes seconds or years to manifest itself), it comes screaming out at you. Your sense of smell reaches its apex, and you think you can almost smell the crimes that sent them to this godforsaken place. And that was just the visitor’s area. Dike Youth Correctional & Rehabilitation Facility was a place that happiness had forsworn a long time ago. While it lacked the utter hopelessness that I always associated with federal prisons, there was nothing that suggested rehabilitation. Only correction through severity and repression. \"Mister Tsonda, it’s very nice to meet you.\" A thinly-built man with a goatee, who would’ve looked more at home in front of a college Literature lecture, walked towards me. He wore a ridiculous corduroy blazer, and above the neckline of his unbuttoned collar shirt, an ankh swung on a flimsy gold chain. It swooped from side to side with every step he took. The whole picture assured me that, whatever his contrary argument, this guy was no more at home amongst the criminally wayward than I was. He just hid it well. He was probably a guy who thought he was doing good in the world by forcing his own belief system on others. This book’s cover was too detailed not to judge the whole damn book by it. \"Yeah, nice to be here,\" I responded, keenly aware, after the fact, that this particular greeting was usually reserved for dinner parties. It wasn’t nice to be here; it was a royal pain in the ass. I was supposed to be thinking about…well, anything but this mission of soul-saving. But this was, in some strange mechanism of a perpetually fucked up universe, where I was supposed to be. \"It rarely is,\" he quipped, as he got close enough to shake my hand. \"My name’s Tom Barius. I’m the Head Psychological Officer here, and I run Cell Block 2. The inmates have a variety of pet names for it, and it’s where we think you could do the most good for these kids.\" \"Cool. Are these the minor offenders or something?\" A nasty look flashed across his face at the word \"cool.\" He was going to be a hindrance rather than a help. \"Not exactly. Cell Block 2 is the place where we isolate the juvenile offenders most likely to be rehabilitated.\" Everything was black-and-white when he said it. He was used to playing Zeus in this shithole. \"But aren’t they all just kids? You telling me that only some of them could ever function in society? The other ones should be getting psychological evaluations and placed in–\" \"In where, Mister Tsonda? The mental health system that may be the one thing that’s more unequipped to handle its overflow of patients than the American prison system?\" He breathed heavily, trying to keep his voice level. But he was stern; the English professor thing hadn’t been far off. \"So there’s a systemic failure across the prison system. I get it, and that’s fucked for you. But how do you justify playing judge and jury with a 14-year-old’s life based on having known him for a couple months? You arbitrarily decide to rehab some and not others?\" As I glanced at the barred window, I realized this place was far more fucked than even its doom-and-gloom exterior could explain. \"We make these decisions with the utmost care and concern for these boys, but the fact is that a vast majority of these youngsters are in here as a preface to a long, inevitable career of crime. The ones who’ve made one mistake, those are the kids we’re trying to send your way, but believe me when I say that the others…they’re not in here because it was their first or second or fifth time. They got caught because they were going to break the law until they got caught.\" \"Then I wanna work with the others too, not just the kids from Block 2.\" \"Why don’t we start slow, Mister Tsonda? Today, I’ve schedule for you to meet with Dixon. He’s someone we think can really turn it around as long as he can get through his time here.\" I silently fumed, but it wasn’t worth getting into a shouting match with my one contact on the first day. \"Let’s do it.\" He nodded to me, walking over to the security station nearby, and leaving me once again by myself. It felt awkward, the confinement of the visitor’s area, as if the visitors were being punished for wanting to come see these children. The dreariness of the room was in its drab function-over-form nature. Metal seats that reminded me of my high school cafeteria were welded together to create a deceiving picture of an \"intimate\" setting. Maybe it was all part of the program. Sending the boys to a place with an embarrassing name like \"Dike.\" Giving their visitors area the same welcoming aura as a funeral home’s viewing room. Making them deal with dickhead psychologists. But if this was the program, then the program was fucked. These were just kids who played at being stick-up boys instead of playing at being Michael Jordan. There are few more messed up mental images than child-sized prison uniforms, but that’s all I could think about. It was a myth that I’d created, something that I realized when Dixon shuffled towards me in a white tee, grey sweats, and white slip-on shoes. Barius maintained a distance of not less than two steps between he and Dixon. I gave the kid a smile. Probably hadn’t seen a real one in quite some time. \"Hey, man.\" \"Hey,\" he responded quietly. He wasn’t a bad-looking kid, and certainly fit the profile of someone who looked like they might turn things around. He was light-skinned, maybe Dominican, and his well-groomed facial hair was clearly a trick to make him look older than he was. The twigs that poked out of the sleeves of his shirt proved him younger (or maybe just weaker) than he wanted to look. When he reached the table, he looked at me, trying to figure me out. As I extended my hand, Dixon looked back at Barius. The warden-cum-shrink nodded to him, and Dixon returned the gesture, sticking his hand out just short of mine. I took the initiative, grabbing his hand and giving it a firm shake. The kid wasn’t used to physical contact of any kind. \"So, what’s up?\" I wanted to keep this simple and conversational. Maybe we could talk about me or PRIME, or maybe we could just talk about stuff without him having to worry about me psychoanalyzing. \"Not much,\" he said, eyes to the ground. \"Sup witchu?\" He wasn’t making eye contact, but at least he wasn’t stonewalling me into a monologue. \"Not much. Back hurts from the show last week. Hell, my back hurts every week. You into wrestling at all?\" \"Used to watch it. Ya know, out there. Can’t watch it no mo’.\" Those eyes were trying to get away from something. I hoped it was just the unfamiliar newness that must occur in meeting a civilian stranger. \"They don’t let you guys watch TV in here?\" Barius was squirming in his seat, which probably meant that there was some kind of answer Dixon was supposed to give. \"Nah, I mean, dey do. But just not dat. Not…whatch’all do.\" I shot a glance over his shoulder at Barius, who had apparently put on his alpha male pants today, and was glaring right back at me. Was he setting me up to fail? He certainly didn’t like the idea of anyone meddling with his kingdom of slaves, and was probably eager to have me and my do-gooder complex out of his hair. \"So, there’s a rule against watching PRIME?\" \"Ain’t exactly such,\" he slowly offered. \"S’like, if ev’ybody can watch the shit without problems, then i’s’all good. But, ya know, people in Two love dey fights. People would get heated over some bullshit on TV, and ‘en we ain’t allowed to watch it.\" \"People would get in fights over PRIME?\" \"Yeah.\" It made no sense, but then again, I’d never spent time locked up. Your mind probably does horrible things to pass the time. Even getting worked up over wrestling, to the point of self-destruction. \"Sorry, man.\" \"Heard you lost tha strap, though.\" \"Huh?\" I’d heard what he said, but it was jarring. That this kid, who seemed almost broken underneath Barius’s rules, knew anything about PRIME was a shock. A pleasant shock, but still… \"Snow, right?\" When I didn’t say anything, he answered the only question in my head. \"My little brother, he only six. So when he come in here, ain’t a lot to talk about so he tells me about what he saw on the wrestling show. Nilton, tha’s his name. Said he likes you, but that Bryan Dawkins is his fav’rite.\" I laughed, and this finally brought Dixon’s eyes up to me. They were a soft hazel, faintly recording his environment from what seemed like very far away. \"Maybe next time, I could bring you some Dawkins stuff. Something to give to your brother when he comes to visit?\" The beginnings of a smile were wiped out as he looked back down at the floor. \"...or not, I was just–\" \"Dixon and I had a conversation beforehand. I told him he’s not allowed to accept any gifts from you,\" Barius explained, voice swelling with a snobbish self-importance. \"Mister–\" \"Doctor.\" \"Mister Doctor,\" I sniped back, now willing to intentionally push his buttons, \"I can literally get him this shit for free, and you can hold it until his brother comes. Dixon, that sound like a plan?\" Hazel eyes searching the floor. Maybe for the dignity that Barius had stripped from him. \"Rumors are wildfire in this place, Mister Tsonda. Someone will hear that you got t-shirts for Dixon here, and then the rest of the residents will be lining up to see what they can extort from you.\" \"They’re kids, for Christ’s sake! They’re not fucking criminal masterminds!\" \"What they are…\" Barius sneered \"…is criminals. And if you want to start enforcing the law by giving gifts to those who break it, be my guest, Mister Tsonda. But not in my facility.\" If Barius had been a guy in a bar, he’d have been slumped over the bar, two front teeth knocked the fuck out, courtesy of my fist. But my presence here had implications back in my other worlds: for Allan North and Tsuperstar Enterprises (for whom I was supposed to be getting good PR) and for PRIME. And if I wanted to help anyone who, like Dixon, had to live under this lunatic psychologist, then I couldn’t just punch Barius out. But that doesn’t mean I didn’t think about it. \"Hey Dixon.\" I didn’t continue until he pulled his head up, eyes lifeless and unresponsive. \"We’ll figure something out, alright? You seem like a good kid.\" He shot up out of his chair, glaring at me. \"Ey man, I don’t want no fuckin’ gifts no ways!\" \"Dixon, I just wanted–\" \"Do the same fuckin’ thing as ev’ybody else? Come up in here promisin’ all sortsa nice shit that I can’t have?! Fuck you.\" \"Kid, I’m trying to help you. I’m not the reason you’re in here.\" \"I’m in here because I beat tha fuckin’ shit out of my moms’ boyfriend,\" he snarled, rage boiling over, taking control because he couldn’t look weak. The only way he knew how to face rejection was with anger. \"And if you try to talk to me again, I’ma do the same t’you.\" Security officers had now encircled the boy, who wore a sneer that most wrestling heels would envy. He spit on the ground near my feet before they led him away. I didn’t let Barius see my fists shaking with rage. \"I’m so sorry. Dixon is usually reserved, but polite. I don’t have a clue what got into him.\" He paused, as if this wasn’t a rehearsed act. \"Maybe our facility just isn’t right for what you’re trying to do, Mister Tsonda. About fifteen minutes South of here is the Lawrence facility for lesser offenders. I can get you the contact details if you’re interested.\" \"I’m fine,\" I spat. Maintaining a civil tongue with this snake was hard. I told myself that trying to help some of these kids was more important than the instant gratification of popping Barius in the snotbox. It didn’t make restraint any easier. \"One hiccup doesn’t mean that this won’t work.\" \"Certainly doesn’t mean that it will work.\" \"Yeah, well, try, try again, right?\" I looked him dead in the eyes, daring him to tell me that I couldn’t come back. Then I might punch his eye socket in. \"Of course,\" he smiled, a venomously sweet grin across his face. \"I’ll try to find someone who isn’t so volatile.\" \"Fine, but I want to talk to Dixon again.\" \"He’s very scarred in regards to male figures, Mister Tsonda. The experience with his mother’s boyfriend was not a good one, and I think perhaps your alpha male scent sends him into defensive mode.\" \"If it’s my scent, then I’ll use a different shampoo. I want to talk to him again.\" \"I’ll let you know when he’s willing to talk again. He’ll, of course, need a day or two of solitary confinement to punish that outburst.\" \"Not on my account, he doesn’t.\" That same nauseating smile. \"Then I’ll let him know it wasn’t on your account when we place him in solitary.\" He looked down at his watch, another rehearsed act. \"It’s time to meet with some of the residents. Mister Tsonda, I’ll have one of the security officers show you out.\" \"See you next week.\" \"Yes,\" he replied. \"Yes, of course.\" A burly white security officer led me to the front gate, where my Navigator was waiting. \"Hey.\" After several minutes of silence, the officer’s baritone startled me. \"That was nice thing you offered to do for the kid. There’s some rotten apples in there, but Dixon’s not one of ‘em.\" \"Yeah…thanks.\" \"Whole reason he’s in here is because some mope was beatin’ on his mom. One day, Dixon comes home from school and, out of the blue, just starts beating the hell outta the guy. Almost died.\" \"Jesus.\" \"Yeah. I’m Victor, by the way. And you don’t gotta pretend, I know who you are. A pleasure to meet ya, Mister Tsonda.\" \"You too, Victor. And when I come back from now on, you can just call me Chandler. Or Chan. Or whatever works.\" \"You’re comin’ back to this place?\" \"Sure.\" \"Good luck with that.\" \"Thanks.\" I almost turned to leave, but something nagged at me. \"Hey Victor, would you tell Dixon I’m sorry about what happened today? Tell him I’ll make it up to him somehow?\" \"Mister Tsonda, I’ll do it, but you don’t wanna go getting these boys’ hopes up. They got enough reason to lose hope.\" \"I’ll keep it in mind.\" The first round of this experiment had failed. But not because of me and not because of the inmates, so pretentiously referred to as \"residents.\" It was because of one corduroy blazer-wearing douche, and in trying to drive me away, Tom Barius had given me all the incentive I needed to make this work.
