Date: ReV/CD The river was cold like dead fingers. They touched every slice of exposed flesh and even pried beneath my clothing, digging in frosty fingernails. I slipped deeper into the water, previously laughably shallow, and lost sight of all but the very edge of the halo of light at the surface. Much like prisms, this was just a foul trick by the sun god, a parting shot at a very desperate little boy. I did not go through the process; I merely fell and did not come up. How had I come to die? The story is a Vietnamese story, not an American story. If this were an American story, I would be saved and live life to the fullest every day. In the land of my ancestors, there are no people stories; only ghost stories. This is why my story is intrinsically Vietnamese. It is a story about ghosts and spirits that linger. * * * * * My cousin, my anh (em họ), was here to visit our family, with his mother, my aunt. The pretense of the visit was that our families had been apart from one another too long, but the truth was that my aunt needed to consult my mother; she believed she was pregnant with another child and she had several worries, including (I would learn years later) just who exactly the father was. You see, at this time, there were many American soldiers in the area and each one had a very important wingman in their attempts at seduction: America. Even the most headstrong and loyal women I knew would curl their toes at stories of New York City (how Big is the Apple, they would ask) or supermarkets. More than I understood at the time, the exchange of sexual acts was directly in return for these shiny, golden inklings of a utopia. On a grand scale, what these soldiers were doing was inexcusable; they created more pipe dreams everyday. Individually…perhaps they gave some people the hope necessary to survive the horrors of war. I try now, to imagine that these were merely foot soldiers, independent of what it means to be American. This is impossible. I hated my cousin Cadao. He was fourteen years old and very arrogant; his father was a higher-up in the South Vietnamese military and even in the time of war, he was receiving schooling from a private tutor, hired through his father’s connections. Whenever he came, he tried to play games with me based on speed or strength, knowing that I was the most fiery of all the cousins (the only one who would compete with him) and the least likely to ever find a contest at which I could best him. I had exhausted my options in less than a year and, even at five, I understood completely the meaning of being fed up with another human. Worst of all with Cadao was that he knew I would always continue to challenge him, no matter how many times I lost. A ferociously competitive child with no siblings, I would often tell myself that if I lost, this was the last time, but Cadao knew exactly the right buttons to push. “Có vẻ trẻ con,” he would shriek. To smell of the baby; I would always fly at him and this would start a contest in itself; a game of cat-and-mouse, where I would close to within inches of him and then he would kick on the reserve speed and flee. My stout legs pushed on after him, but always to no avail. Often, he would flee into the jungle and then plague me from the underbrush, hissing “có vẻ trẻ con” as I kept my eyes trained forward, making for our sturdy, unremarkable home. On the day that I was to die, Cadao was being his normal self. Our mothers had gone into a secluded corner of the yard, where my grandfather had helped us erect a sort of hammock. The ropes were rigid and did not give fully to one’s weight, but the two women looked unflappable, like angels sitting nonchalantly on clouds. Cadao looked out at the window and then spat on the dirt outside the threshold. “I’m bored, rác rưởi.” Punk. “Let’s go have a race.” An infuriatingly arrogant rival, Cadao was always smiling, as if he went around thinking of ways to deflate me. “No, Cadao. Let’s just sit here.” “Well, my leg is bothering me. I was thinking that my little cousin could perhaps beat me today.” More smiling. I did not know what one was supposed to do when extremely angry, but now I would desire very greatly to punch his teeth in. I had only recently become acquainted with this new type of anger that Cadao caused for me: rage. “Cadao, I do no want to race you.” As a relative newcomer to language, I was still unsure of myself with words. In my head, I was succinct, concise as elderly Mr. Thien, who chose his words as carefully as fruit from the market. “I will only lose again.” Without saying another word, Cadao was stripping out of his clothes. He began jogging down to the small pond and I called after him. “Cadao, what are you doing?!” I dared not tell my elder cousin to not participate in this potentially risky activity, for fear he would call me names and tell me I was a coward. So I had only one other choice: strip off my clothes and follow him. Of course, I cautiously placed my clothes just within the door of the house, so they might stay relatively clean. Cadao had grabbed one of our water-collecting barrels and was pushing it into the pond, all the while smiling. The pond was approximately two hundred feet across in all directions and I couldn’t imagine what he was doing. “Those are for water, Cadao.” I got no response so I moved towards my cousin and repeated myself in my bravest voice. “Those are for water, Cadao!” He turned at me and snarled. “Hush, little one. I hear there is a pot of gold in the middle of the pond.” He was going out of his way, but as sure as his smile was the fact that he was egging me on, into yet another competition. “Not true.” “Fine, but I’m not sharing.” He found a large stick and jumped on top of the barrel which, with its top on, would serve as a small raft. I don’t remember whether he knew how to swim or not, but I had no concern for my older cousin’s safety. He looked back at me. “What, you’re not going to give older cousin a push?” I clenched my teeth, unaware of why he had this effect on me every time. Still, I found myself finding a slightly smaller barrel and a slightly smaller stick. Within a minute I was pushing my own barrel into the water, mounting it and “paddling” out towards my cousin, who had already made significant headway. Cadao was about a hundred feet from shore, but I was barely a quarter of that. To make up, I pushed my small arms to their limits and began to catch up to my cousin. By the time he had traveled a hundred and fifty feet, I was only fifty feet behind. But my efforts began to catch up to me. My arms wanted to creep and linger in the water, just hanging at my sides. I didn’t know physical exertion so great and Cadao was so close. My arms hung in the water and I did my best to paddle, but my arms would no longer cooperate. I was fixated on Cadao’s barrel and I had done my best, but now my brain was beginning to alert me to my situation. I was in the pond with leeches and frogs and…what if there were fish?! I began to squirm, trying to put my legs up on top of the barrel and leave only their very edges in the water. With my new heightened sense of my aquatic neighbors, every tingle felt like the groping teeth of a fish looking for a meal. As I felt one of these tingles on my submerged hip, I spun around quickly and, pivoting on the slick barrel, fell right into the metallic blue water. I’ve never seen water so blue. All blue. All around me. Cadao would probably win the race, I thought, as my face dipped under the surface for the last time. I barely struggled, still clutching the branch in my right hand. * * * * * But I did not die like I should have. I saw Cadao’s sleek form betray the tranquility of the surface and follow me. Just like always, he was beating me. He was dropping faster than me; I would let him win, though. I felt his arms, not yet powerful enough to help in planting crops, but strong enough to save his little cousin from drowning. I am sure he was smiling as he brought a gasping, dying Chandler Tsonda to the surface. I coughed up water for almost ten minutes, my mother tells me. Cadao watched with baited breath as his mother was the one who finally brought me back to consciousness, her tender caring (even to a fault, as they were adulterous) hands compressing down on my chest or violently prying my mouth open.
