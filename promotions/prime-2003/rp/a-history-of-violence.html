A History Of Violence

undated

Date: Who knows? He would need violence. He would need blood to pass the test this week. Last week, he had needed wits and better timing. And the week before that, Chandler Tsonda had needed fortitude greater then the Illustrious Face-Eater: close, but no cigar. There had been enough failure. This was not a man who had come back to wrestling to play mid-card stepping stone. It was time to win a fucking match. But how to bottle up that violence? How could he find in his soul all the malevolence needed to grind a broken bottle into the forehead of Angelo Deville? To put Paddy O’Shea through a glass window? To make an enemy out of newly befriended Danny Ferguson by introducing his face to a barstool? Could Chandler Tsonda summon up the courage…nay…the balls to do this? In the words of Darkwing Duck, was he ready to “get dangerous?” He didn’t know. But it had been weeks since his debut win over Hoyt Williams and while that sufficed for bragging rights, he still hadn’t pinned anyone in the middle of that sacred ring. He had missed out on a Pulse Title chance and now he was being “rewarded” for his work as Assistant General Manager- *cough* His work as Assistant TO The General Manager had rewarded him with the toughest match of his budding PRIME career. And it would require gore. It would call upon him to summon sadism that rested deep within his heart. You could call him an asshole, but rarely was Chandler Tsonda physically vicious. He was of the opinion that violence was for brutes. Trickery was for the mildly cunning. But the mind games he played were for men who killed with mental aptitude. So it was time to bring everything out of the bag. And that called for bringing old memories out of the bag. Some of them were going to be painful. And it would be worth it because he was fucking sick of losing. It was time to win. It was time to hurt somebody. * * * * My mother was hurrying. Her breath betrayed a terrible fear, cutting in and out of my ear sharply. Her small frame was bouncing up and down and I struggled to keep up. She would look behind her every ten steps to make sure I wasn’t already dead, but she mostly kept her eyes on the ground. My child’s mind wondered what she could be looking for. Had she left something? My adult mind tells me now that she was looking for freshly adjusted dirt. She was looking for any sign that the ground had been disturbed. Disturbed ground usually meant that directly underneath laid those dirty indescript cylinders that had been destroying her country since the first American “advisor” set foot on Vietnamese soil. She was looking for land mines. She was making sure her son didn’t turn into that kid on the front of the UNICEF pamphlet; the one who has one leg and one arm and is making a perpetually sad face. It was dark and I kept stumbling and as we continued on, I could hear my mother crying. Her breathing became more uneven, with short abrupt cuts where she sniffled loudly, then moaned for a couple seconds, then repeat. In the relative silence of the night, it was unnerving to hear her strong, proud voice faltering every time she tried to usher me along. We hurried past dead bodies. There’s no poetic beauty about the stillness of death for a guy whose head does not exist anymore. We were fleeing in the rawest sense of the word. We were going to run until we were somewhere safe. I like to think that my mother kept running for me, but I think she kept running for everyone in her family who had been killed by the Americans or the Vietcong or the government or the random violence that ruled the countryside where we lived. When I got too tired to run, she carried me in her arms past all the blood and the bombs that jumped out of the ground at you if you weren’t careful. While the sun was coming up, we crept into the bush and my mother immediately went to sleep. I pulled out the map of Vietnam that my grandfather had given me for my birthday. I put my finger on Ky Son. That’s where my mother said we had to go if we wanted to go to America. The map was in brilliant blues and greens and it had every country in Asia on it. I wondered how long it would take us to move those two inches. It would take a long time and we would pass many more nights like that one. * * * * I was walking home from school when I saw Thanh Pho waving a gun in the air. In our neighborhood, which was mainly Vietnamese and Korean families, Thanh was known to be pretty crazy. He was mot ong gia, the crazy old man, with white hair that crept around the edges of his ears. His son worked at a factory downtown and he made his presence in the community felt by stepping out onto his porch and yelling at his son for being such a drunk. Today was no different. It was that time right before dusk when you smell the cold creeping in. Thanh was waving the gun all around, cursing in Vietnamese. The hair on his ears was shaking as he trembled in anger. He swung the gun in long, swooping arcs with his right hand. It was clear he didn’t know how to use the gun, but had it only to protect himself from the black gangs that sold cocaine in the neighboring districts and occasionally came here to beat up an Asian kid or two. Thanh Pho’s son Phuoc stepped out onto the porch. I was just cutting across the dusty cul-de-sac in front of the cluster of homes that belonged to all of us, the alien piazza. I heard the shouting grow louder between the two men and I set my school books down on a stump. As others did who came out of their houses, I peered over at the shitty old porch where they stood. My perverse infatuation with the forthcoming carnage didn’t shake me until well after the fact. Thanh’s shouting was unintelligible to me. I only knew bits and pieces of the language that my mother yelled at me in. They were a typical family to our neighborhood: Thanh had moved here with a wife to secure a better future for his wife, a future that didn’t involve serving any government that couldn’t feed its people. Well, whoops. No one in our square was eating filet mignon. I realized it was very quiet in the plaza. Everyone was either inside eating their dinner and pretending to be American or out here in their full Asian glory, watching the lỳ lẽ chồng lạ. At my house, I could see that even my shrew of a mother was poking her head out now, investigating the racket being made. She whisper-yelled at me, loud enough to be heard by everyone. “Chandler, you come home now!” She hadn’t ever grasped English fully but unless she was furious with me, she wouldn’t speak Vietnamese. I looked at her and then, unresponsive, turned back towards the two men arguing. I still could only catch parts of their conversation. I wished desperately that I weren’t American and that I could step in and say “well fellas, let’s just let bygones be bygones and move on, eh?” I stepped towards the porch and my mother waddled out to get me. Her back was bent with sclerosis and she already had frog legs, so she never walked out of the house if she had to. She didn’t want to look weak in America. As she frog-waddled at me, I heard the first gunshot of my life. It rang so loud that I thought I’d been shot. I dropped to my knees and heard laughter from one of the porches near me. It had been a misfire by Thanh into the air and a kid my age was laughing at my tense nerves. I was a fucking wreck, with my mother scolding me, while I just sat there in the listless dirt. Another gunshot rang out and things were very very quiet. Thanh looked at what he’d done, completely oblivious. The man expected his son to jumpstart back to life any second now. Phouc’s wife walked out, saw the butchery, and began to wail. She wailed in Vietnamese, holding Phouc in her arms. I’ve never been so sure of the meaning of any words in my life and I can still see her sitting there singing: Manh trang da gac non doaiMot minh luong nhung dung ngoi chua xongTrieu dau noi tieng dung dungHoi ra moi biet rang song Tien Duong The song, my mother explained to me back inside, was about a wife whose husband is killed and she contemplates suicide to join him. Soon after, they locked Phouc’s wife up in an asylum; they said she wouldn’t sing anything except the song of Kieu and Tu Hai. * * * * It was warm outside. It was June 2003. I can’t tell you the night because, as sick as it sounds, I wasn’t even thinking about the task at hand. I was so pissed off that I couldn’t have cared less about messing with a man’s life. Not messing with a man’s life. There was a possibility I was ending my tag team partner Jimmy Nguyen’s life tonight. Pro Wrestling Circuit was my first big break. Okay, it was my only break in wrestling. I had taught myself everything I thought I needed to know to survive. I had gone through the cumbersome application process at the fed’s opening. I had interviewed with the Phantom Booker himself, Ron Starr. I was in this guy’s pocket. His word was law. So I was in the parking lot, sitting in a company Honda Accord. My hands were going cold, despite the balmy summer weather. I admitted in my mind that I was slightly nervous and moved on. I sat, watching and waiting for an hour. Not one person passed through the door that Starr had told me to be watching. His words had been “don’t worry about the legal stuff, just take Nguyen out.” Now I had worried about the legal stuff, but Starr had showed me papers. If the cops sniffed around, we were going to explain the entire thing as a legitimate angle that Nguyen had been in on. But it all relied on me. I was going to be the Rikishi to his Stone Cold. Well, a really good-looking slimmed-down Rikishi to his talentless get-carried-by-Tsonda-to-tag-titles Stone Cold. That’s right. I was going to end his career because Starr had told me I could do better. Starr had told me that I was the real superstar in 2GVC, a name that makes me cringe now. We were 2nd Generation Vietcong. Nguyen was actually Korean-American, but Starr said that the average New Jersey-area fan wasn’t going to know the difference, so they’d pass him off as Vietnamese and go from there. And the booking team had decided to go with the hot hand, giving us the tag titles to run with. But Nguyen had dropped the ball. He had showed up late or drunk to shows and there was a simple Starr solution to such a problem: kill the shit out of him. I saw his silhouette first and I put my hands on the steering wheel. There was no deep breath, no hesitation. I waited until his back was all the way turned and I slammed on the gas. It was pure freedom for a couple seconds. I turned hard, feeling a duty to at least let Nguyen know I was coming with screeching tires. He turned a second before I slammed into the backs of his knees. He went flying straight up, onto the roof of the car and then down onto the pavement. I made the mistake of looking in the rear view mirror. I saw that Nguyen wasn’t moving and had a momentary lapse. I thought about stopping the car, checking to see if he had a pulse. I shook my head and hit the gas again, easing the car into a different parking spot. I walked inside to find Starr and tell him that everything was done and that a stretcher would be needed in the East Wing of the parking lot. He didn’t step into a wrestling ring again. I think he’s in a wheelchair. * * * * I don’t want that to be you, Deville. I don’t want you to be the guy who drives me over the edge. Because it may not seem like it, but I know violence. I know a violence more raw then your “Growing Up Gotti” ass could ever understand. Ferguson and I are cool, but I need this win more then he does. I’m not saying I’ll cheap shot the guy, but I certainly can’t be saving his ass if I’m looking out for my own. I think Ferguson knows that. Paddy knows exactly what’s coming. The thing is, he’s got at least one privately scheduled ass-whipping already scheduled. At the Great American Nightmare, him and me and a couple thousand fans are going to engage in some serious violence. If he wants to make it GAN, he’ll stay out of my way. I don’t want to have to rely on what I know about violence because none of the three of you would like it. You don’t want to see that primal blood lust of another human come out. It’s sickening and it makes you want to throw your stomach up. The land mines, that is. And the shooting and the car “accident.” They say always bet on the ugly guy in a bar fight because he’s got nothing to lose, in terms of a pretty face. They’re wrong. Bet on the really sexy motherfucker because he’s not going to stand for some “movie superstar”(no offense, Danny, love your work!), Irish gypsy (offense intended Paddy, go stab your eyes with a rusty nail), or Wop-o-matic 3000 (Deville, don’t think I’m proud to be half Italian; I’d rather suck down a bile shake then be associated with you and your race of lower primates) decides they want to win. Because I have decided I want to win. I need to win for the second time in three weeks. God, this wrestling is screwing with my head.
