Date: 15 - 05 - 07 Kansas City, 1978 May 14th Allan Cooper strolled with a hurried pace through the Sunshine motel, gripping his rucksack tightly over his shoulder, he raced. His heart pounded heavily in his chest as he panted. Rain drops trickled off the brim of his Dallas Cowboy’s cap, hitting the linoleum floor with a silent splash. The weather outside was ominous, a dark cloud rumbled above the quietened motel. He slipped the silver key from his pocket and wriggled the key into the slot. He heard the locking mechanism click; it opened gently, a creaking noise followed as the rusted hinges moved with his forceful hand. Cooper entered his darkened room and chucked the wet rucksack onto the ground, it was heavy under his arm, but it brought a smile to his stubble covered face. Sitting into the comfort of the single bed, he opened the rucksack and gazed at the wad of greens inside, untouched by human hand and weather. “You didn’t return my call.” Cooper looked up shocked; he zipped the bag up immediately, and gazed into the shadowed visage of Mary Hume. Mary Hume was twenty three years old and beautiful, a country girl at heart that had married young and into the Hume Oil Corporation. He found her easy prey and had worked up a con to steal her husband’s money and get out of the relationship quickly, but this woman’s loneliness was proving to be an irritating itch that wouldn’t go. “You look shocked to see me, Allan,” Mary mumbled. “You were just going to skip town on me, weren’t you.” “Nah baby, you know I wouldn’t do that,” Allan stood up, using his suave techniques. “Damn right you would, Allan,” Mary responded, angrily. “All this time you said you loved me, told me you’d get me away from Joseph.” Mary moved forward, shutting the motel room door behind her. “But that was all a lie, wasn’t it? You didn’t care about me; you just wanted my husband’s money.” They stood in darkness, their bodies lit by the fluttering red neon motel sign, its glare intrusive into the lovers’ quarrel. Cooper remained steady; he approached Mary with a swagger in his step. Cooper held her hand but she pulled it back, the small bag around her shoulder swung with the motion. Cooper stood silent a moment and decided to play her emotions. “Alright baby, that was the original plan, but you know I love you,” he said, softly. He could see he was getting to her, the thunder above them erupted, the rumbling cloud that was dreary and dark only moments ago, had blasted into a mass of thunder and rain. It echoed around them, drowning their dispute. “I haven’t seen you in three months, Allan,” Mary announced, coyly. “You abandoned me and our child. My husband knows of my affair and our son.” Allan’s eyes lit with fear, he couldn’t have a blood line leading back to the con’s he’d pulled. Cooper grinned and turned his back on Mary, grabbing the rucksack and suit case. “You know I can’t take you with me.” “But you said you loved me …” Mary bellowed. “That was before I knew you got pregnant, you whore,” Allan enunciated. He shoved past Mary and went for the door but paused. He heard a click and turned to see Mary holding a gun pointed directly at his chest. Allan let the bags drop to the floor and froze, hatred burning in her eyes. “I won’t let you do this to us, Allan,” Mary said. “I just won’t.” “Ok, look sweetheart, let’s just take it easy,” he ordered with a slow and quiet tone. Allan moved closer toward her and saw her hand shaking, the gun unstable. That’s when he lunged, grabbing the gun, they struggled. Mary tossed and turned in his grip, he pulled her toward him and … BANG Allan couldn’t hear, slightly deafened by the gun blast, he froze as he felt Mary go limp in his arms. He looked toward her stomach, blood oozing out and over her white summer dress. He dropped her body to the linoleum floor and grabbed the gun; shoving it into his coat he grabbed the rucksack and suitcase. The rain pelted against his skin, he couldn’t believe what had just happened. Mary Hume was now dead in his motel room; Allan Cooper ran to the road and stopped, looking to hitch-hike his way out of Kansas and to use a different name. Allan Cooper was no longer safe; he’d committed a murder, in self-defence, but once the jury stepped into the room, his con and retaining of the firearm would see him doing twenty-five to life. He stuck out his hand as a bus pulled to a screeching halt, he slid into the bus, shoving his bags into an overhead compartment, and he cast his eyes onto a free seat beside a beautiful looking young woman. In her hand she held a plane ticket and a small rucksack; she was leaving Kansas for Philadelphia. “Excuse me, do you mind?” he asked pointing to the spare seat. The young woman looked up at him with a smile, beautiful blue eyes. “No, not a problem.” He thanked her and sat down. Cooper looked toward the storm outside them; he couldn’t bring himself to look back at the Sunshine motel as it faded in the distance. He swallowed hard and buckled his seat belt. He would never come back to Kansas City, not as Allan Cooper. “I’m Marie Bernstein, nice to meet you,” she said, softly, stretching out her hand. He smiled toward her and took it, shaking it gently. “Bradley Lavelle, nice to meet you.” ---- Washington D.C., 2006 May 11th 2007 Washington, D.C. Inquisitor New Room Despite the high humidity and the chatting of voices, Pierce was aching from his match against Jonathan Winters. Although he’d lost to Winters, he had a sense of accomplishment as he’d gone out there and given all he could give, despite his accident. The sense of loss afterward seemed to have dissipated over the last week and he enjoyed being home, working at his part-time job. That morning he’d arrived at the office, always the same routine, the world ever circling and the events within the globe never ending. Today was no ordinary day, while his colleagues worked the big cases’, drugs possession or a senator murdered, he took cold cases and the ones the press tended to circle on page 28. His editor-in-chief, Joseph Wallace, belted his name across the room and it was Pierce’s turn to go and meet the executioner. Rising from the chair with a slight hobble in his step, he plastered a smile on his face and broadened his shoulders. Joseph Wallace was perched behind his desk, cigarette butts cluttered into a silver ash tray. A lone cigarette dangled from underneath his grey beard, his brown eyes gleamed with satisfaction as he puffed on the cigarette. Looking up at Pierce, he straightened his glasses. “Good morning, boss,” Pierce said. “Don’t be a kiss ass Lavelle, nobody likes a kiss ass,” Wallace replied with a husky voice. “Yes, boss,” Pierce noted, sitting in the chair uncomfortably. Joseph Wallace flicked his cigarette ash into the ashtray and leafed through a couple of documents, his thin grey hair fluttered in the wind. Brushing it back he turned to Pierce, holding one piece of paper. “See Pierce, despite you being part-time here, I like your style.” “Thanks … I think,” Pierce declared. “You ain’t like the other hot rollers outside, buzzing like bees to the best plant in the garden. You delve deeper; you go for the stories that matter. I like that about you Pierce, you’ve integrity and you remind me of myself at your age.” Pierce nodded his head and folded his arms, deciding not to reply to that comment as Mr. Wallace didn’t like a kiss ass. Pierce remained silent, adamant and couldn’t help the cocky grin that laced his lips. “I got some news down the pipe line of an assault, husband came home drunk, and I want you to write this piece. You tell the unsung victims stories better than anybody I know,” Wallace enunciated and handed Pierce the description file. Wallace rubbed the finished cigarette into the ashtray and lit another, “Go and get them Pierce.” Lavelle nodded and exited Mr. Wallace’s room, his private office awash with cigarettes and coffee, putrid against his taste buds he grabbed a small paper cup and poured himself some water. As he sipped the water, he read the file. Mrs. Lexton, age 32, lived with her husband and daughter in Arlington Drive. Monday morning, May 11th, at approx. 5.24 am, Mrs. Lexton had been brutally beaten by her husband, and was found by the nanny at approx 6:30 am. The husband, Mr. Lexton had come home from his night shift, having been fired spent a couple of hours in Rosie’s pub. Upon arrival, it is believed that in a wild rage, Mr. Lexton unleashed on his wife with the pastry rolling pin. The child had witnessed the entire ordeal from the behind the bars of their wooden stairs, and is inconsolable at this moment in time. Mrs. Lexton died from her wounds at approx 8:04 am after arriving at Mercy Hospital. Their child Anne Lexton is being taken care of by Mrs. Lexton’s sister. Mr. Lexton has received a sentence after pleading guilty to the case, he received a life sentence, possibility of parole. Flicking his pen against his jotter, there was a chilling image that began to resurface for him, a similarity to this child’s ordeal; he tossed the cup into the trash. Pierce moved away from his editor’s news room and began to think about the case and a memory that sent a nauseous sensation up his throat. As he strolled toward his cramped desk, cluttered with items and pages, post-its fluttering on the side of his laptop he sighed and found the nanny’s statement on his desk. “Thought you might like some structure for your cold cases,” Hailey said with a smile. “Shouldn’t you be finding out which actor is in trouble now?” he replied, sarcasm evident. “They usually don’t come out till night,” Hailey answered. Pierce smiled and looked toward Hailey, quite the business woman since Jessica’s death. Her work was a distraction from her loss. Hailey looked at him, one eyebrow rose, “Have I got something in my teeth?” Pierce smiled, “Nah, thanks for the statement, Hails. I won’t be home this weekend, heading up to New York. So you got the place to yourself.” “Good for me then, party time!” She turned to him with a pat on the back and plodded off toward the elevator. Hailey worked two floors up at the Washington Post, the more exquisite branch of the Inquisitor. Inserting the ear plugs of his ipod he began his work. Minutes turned to hours, song after song, he read and typed. The room began to empty, people coming and going, and as darkness swept across the majestic skyline of Washington D.C. he found himself alone. Their mother, Marie, stood in the kitchen doorway a delightful smile laced her lips. Her eldest, Pierce, looked up toward her in his blue pajamas holding his favourite bear, with a smile. “Mommy, come pway.” Marie moved forward, Rebecca her youngest by a couple of minutes, gazed at her with her loving blue eyes. “Mommy, Pwerce won’t let me pway with the teddy.” “How would my two angels like some apple pie before bed time?” This brought a joyous gaze to their little faces, jumping up Rebecca forgot about the teddy bear and ran circles around her mother’s skirt. The sweet scent of jasmine trickled up her nostrils. Pierce rose up, broadening his shoulders, pretending to be his favourite superhero, Superman. “Mommy, can we have a story tonight?” “We’ll see.” Rebecca pleaded with her mother, wrapping her little digits around her mother’s hand. Marie lifted Rebecca up and kissed her gently on the forehead. Almost a serene silence surrounded them, she adored her children. Rebecca looked toward her mother, curiosity glazed her blue eyes. “Mommy, you have a boo-boo, can I kiss it better?” Marie tried to smile but she couldn’t, despite her attempts to hide the black eye it seemed they were frivolous. Pierce had managed to pull the chair out and sit at the table, claiming to be a “big-boy” now. This brought a smile to her face. The kitchen was filled with aromas, homely in its style, a country kitchen inside a modern house. The radio played a soft tune, Tony Bennett’s “Rags to riches” in the background. The apple pie steamed in the over head light as she drove the blade into the soft crust. Marie’s heart stopped when a familiar noise echoed like nails on a board. SCREECH The tires roared as the car pulled into the driveway, she turned with fear in her eyes and checked the time. He was early. Marie placed the knife down and removed her apron, looking toward her children, fear swept through her. The sound of a car door closing made her jump. “Kids, time for bed, come on.” “But mommy -” Pierce argued. “No, Pierce, not tonight. Take Rebecca to bed, hurry.” Marie watched them saunter off the chairs, listening to the pitter-patter of little feet as they vanished into their bedroom. She breathes a sigh of relief; her little darlings would be safe tonight. The door opened and a stumbling of feet entered. “Honey, I’m home!”. Bradley Lavelle entered drunk and depressed, eight years ago on this day he’d murdered a woman under a different alias. He had married Marie as cover for his new alias, Bradley Lavelle, but he was poor. All the money he’d taken from Mary Hume to lavish on himself had gone into the husband role and family life and he despised Marie Bernstein for giving him children and a wife. He strolled into the room, the rain pelting against the hall window. Pierce remembered the scent of apple pie as he slid into bed. The cooled night breeze that blew in through the opened bedroom window ruffled their bed covers. Rebecca slumped into her bed, the pink covers ruffling from the cold breeze. Pierce sat in his bed, his feet dangling off the edge, not yet tall enough to touch the floor. “Were we bold?” Rebecca asked, turning in her bed and holding her baby blanket close to her. “No Becca,” he responded, his blonde hair soft and graciously illuminated by his superman bed light. “I’m hungry,” she mumbled and looked at the door. Rising from the bed and landing on his feet, Pierce looked at her and then toward the door. They always went to bed, and stayed the whole night, but tonight something changed, a certain request changed it all. “I get you some food.” The darkness sheltered him as he stepped into the cold landing, his little feet waddling along the wooden flooring. He heard crying from downstairs and his father shouting. His fingers laced around the wooden banister, soft under the touch. Left foot first, he skipped down the tall steps till he stopped in the hallway, seeing his father’s shadow move about. Above him he saw a little figure emerge; Rebecca sat at the top of the stairs. “I get food, go to bed.” “Mommy will yell at you.” Nevertheless he plodded along, innocent and young, toward the sweet smelling apple pie, that single slice his mother had cut for Rebecca. That’s when he saw it, his mother fell forward, and tears trickled down her beautiful white cheeks. A crimson red dribbled from her lip and nose, dark against her skin. He wandered forward toward his mother, he felt the need to help her, her eyes met with him and she shook her head ever so slightly. “Mommy …” He felt the ground move away from him, grappled by the biceps of his father, as he laced his arms around his skinny waist, the air fluttered out of him. “You’re supposed to be in bed boy, you no good idiot,” his father said in a southern accent. He remained terrified in his fathers clutches; his breath was foul, beer and cigarettes laced with crisps. Sweet billowed from his palm, sticking to Pierce’s little hand as he wriggled. Eventually his father let him go and Pierce ran to the loving embrace of his mother, something felt safe about being clutched in her arms, hidden from view. “Come on boy, you’re a big lad,” his father slurred. “Stick up for yourself, boy, be a man.” “Leave him alone Bradley, please!” his mother cried. The sound the leather made as his father slapped his Levi belt against his calf, made him shiver; the belt buckle glistened in the flickering of the broken lamp shade. He looked at his father, his blue eyes fading in the darkness. “Daddy …?” He stepped in the way of his mother, her arms tried to force him out of the way, but her husband was fast and strong, his fist colliding with her baby boy’s beautiful face, she felt him collapse in her arms, lifeless. Her palm felt a wet substance trickle from his beautiful blonde hair, she sobbed as she held him. Her husband in a drunken state moved off to the kitchen. Images of Mary Hume filled his mind, tormented by a ghost. Marie rose placing her son on the carpet gently. In the darkness of the hall, Rebecca watched, frozen on the stairs. Marie felt the cold rim of the bottle as she gripped the Jack Daniels, its weight evident as she hoisted it. “You BASTARD,” Marie cursed, swinging the bottle; she hit her husband on the back of the head and watched him collapse on top of her freshly cooked apple pie, its contents pressed into his black shirt as he collapses by her feet. She drops the whiskey bottle, it breaks and the whiskey trickles along the cracks in the wooden floor. With a shaken hand she gripped the phone. “I need an ambulance and the police …” ---- Washington, D.C. The Inquisitor New Room 2007 The thunder and rain echoed in the darkness around him, the pitter-patter of droplets trickle down the window. He awoke in his office, running his hand over his face and through his hair. It had been a long time since he’d dreamt about that night. He sat in the confines of his small desk at the Inquisitor, empty coffee mugs splayed in front of him, his laptop’s blue glow illuminating the stubble and dreary blue eyes. Saving the rest of the file, he e-mailed the piece to the editor’s account and rose from his seat. The week’s proceedings at PRIME had become but a distant memory, his thoughts filled with memories he’d fought so long to forget. His father was a man he hadn’t spoken to or thought about since he was eight years old, and the scar a painful reminder of a night his mother always apologised for. Flicking off the light switch, Pierce strode out into the gloomy night, the rain pelting against his skin, and opened his mobile. He would be leaving town tomorrow for New York City to visit Tracy, but he would be making a stop-over. As he stood in the shelter of the porch, he dialled. “Hello?” Pierce took a deep breath, cold air filled his lungs, “Hey mom, how you doing?” “Pierce, sweetheart, everything alright?” his mother asked, sweetly. “Actually, I was wondering if I could stop by tomorrow. There’s something I need to get.” “Not a problem, sweetheart, what are you looking for?” “You still got that box of his old stuff in the basement?” There was a long pause, and he couldn’t blame his mother for her hesitation, because in all honesty he couldn’t understand this need to dig up the past. “Pierce, why do you want to look at his stuff?” she asked, curious by her son’s request. “I want to find out who he really was, mom. I want to find out whether he got justice for what he did to us. Eight years you suffered, mom.” “Pierce, you can look for him, but do not bring me into this. I’ve made my peace.” And that was all she had to say as the phone went dead. Flipping it closed he took a stride out of the porch and into the night. In the distance, covered by the dark night, there stood a man with a cigarette watching the young reporter exit the office building, with a smile. His grey hair was soaked to the bone, his suit covered in droplets and leaves.
