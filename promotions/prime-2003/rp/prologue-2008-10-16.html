Prologue

16 October 2008
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: October 16 It was probably around the 10th time Troy Douglas stared blankly out his hotel window down to the bustle of Georgetown nightlife below when he realized what a horrible idea all of this was. Looking back, asking PRIME's publishing wing to explore the possibility of a book deal seemed like such an innocuous proposal. It was something Troy always wanted to try his hand at, so when his agent suggested floating the idea out there, Douglas was ready and raring to go. Of course, as a guy who'd been a part of PRIME barely six months, he never thought they'd actually give him a green light. And now, staring at a laptop devoid of any copy and a digital voice recorder begging for the sound of a human voice, Troy was rapidly coming to terms with what a monumental mistake he had made in putting his John Hancock to that contract. He turned away from the window, leaving the college students and bar hoppers to their own devices as he moved on to the next step of the routine he'd developed over the last few hours. Gaze aimlessly out the window. Shuffle around the carpet in lazy circles, accomplishing nothing more than building up an impressive static charge. Wander to the bathroom, flip on the lights and splash water in his face. Under no circumstances whatsoever make anything that could be regarded as progress on his autobiography. Lather. Rinse. Repeat. Time passed, the pattern continued, and every time Troy reached a hand towards the recorder, something caused him to hesitate and pull back. And every time his right hand stalled, the same exact thought drifted through his brain. It's an autobiography, what the fuck is so hard about this? That was the question. The question. He was a guy who'd spoken about his career and his personal life about as much as anybody in the wrestling industry, but now that he was hemmed into a corner, forced to put out something that could hold people's attention, Troy was balking like a pitcher called up from Double-A to make his first start in the middle of a pennant race. Frustrated, Troy flopped down onto the bed in exhaustion, and a moment afterwards he heard a loud THUNK! as the TV remote wobbled off the edge of the bed to the floor. "Just keeps getting better, don't it?" Now he was posing rhetorical questions to nobody in particular. That was never a good sign. Ever. For a moment, Troy thought about just leaving the damn thing on the floor and starting to wage the nightly battle that was his attempt to get some decent sleep. Quickly enough, he changed his mind and leaned down to grab the remote and turn on the TV. What came on when the LCD screen blinked to life was nothing short of fortuitous. He'd seen the commercial for the CSWA's Best of Anniversary Anthology countless times on late-night TV, and at one point, he was pretty sure he could see a 15-year-old version of himself somewhere in the throng of the crowd. This time around, he wasn't squinting to try and pick out the familiar grey Hornet t-shirt with the prominent barbecue sauce stain he'd worn to dozens of live shows. Because he was in the middle of scrambling towards his night table and fishing around for that damn voice recorder. Serendipity and inspiration have a tendency to go hand-in-hand. In his desperate search for a starting point, Troy had found one as soon as he shut off his brain. The commercial ended, the TV screen clicked back to black, Troy smiled and finally pressed that button. **BEEP!** I was just short 12 years old when I realized what I was going to do with my life. Now, that sounds silly and pretentious, especially considering the strangely roundabout way it took me to arrive on this career path of mine, but all it took to set me on this track was a crappy looking flyer. It's become public knowledge since April of '88 that Chad Merritt and Stephen Thomas did, even given modern indy wrestling standards, a pretty damn shitty job of promoting their first wrestling show. But, taped to a bus stop on my walk back home from school was a flyer that looked like it was drawn by somebody's kid sister in fourth grade art class. I don't mean to go off on a tangent here, but I've got this thing in a box somewhere in my attic, and I'm pretty sure one of the "I's" in "wrestling" was dotted with a heart. Not exactly the best way to draw the kind of audience you're looking to pack an abandoned warehouse full of wrestling fans in Greensboro, North Carolina. But, despite the absurdity of the flyer, despite the fact that they failed to mention the name of the promotion OR the bell time, I was drawn to it. Probably because the building was only about three blocks from my house, so my dad wasn't likely to object to me going. And thus began the long, winding road that led me to professional wrestling. I'd watched wrestling on TV as a kid, I'd seen a couple shows inside the Greensboro Coliseum, but in this barely-lit, ramshackle warehouse not a mile from my front door, I was inspired. Surrounded by 150 fans -- most of them drunk -- and stuck in a building with no ventilation as the smell of stale peanuts and cheap beer built up a thick lacquer in the air doesn't seem like the normal place to find a calling, but when the man we'd all come to know and love at Rhubarb Jones pressed "play" on the giant contraption that was a late 80s CD player and Europe's "Final Countdown" started playing, it was the beginning of something that changed my life forever. History will show that a 500-pound giant named Dark Knight who fell of the face of the Earth a couple of years later won the match in question, but it was the other guy who everyone was fixated on after the bell rang. Like me, he was from right there in Greensboro, and as the stories go, he wasn't supposed to end of a professional wrestler, either. But, the guy the world would soon know as Hornet had an instant connection with the sparse crowd, and with a certain kid in the third row you've all (hopefully) come to know and love lo these past nine years. It was that connection that made me realize there was something to this professional wrestling thing, and more than a decade later when I was stuck in a dark hole and needed somewhere to go with my life, a decade of memories born in a dingy little Greensboro warehouse because of a comically ineffective flyer pointed my compass back to the squared circle. I've talked a lot about the end of the road in my time, but that, that was where I started. If you want to know the rest of the journey, it'd probably help to turn the page. **BEEP!**
