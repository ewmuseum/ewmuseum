Snow Blindness

17 June 2008
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 6/17/2008 “The recipe for perpetual ignorance is: be satisfied with your opinions and content with your knowledge.” -Elbert Hubbard June 15th, 2008 12: 02 A.M. “Nobody’s getting out of this limo until we finish those motherfucking bottles!” The limo was tight with bodies. I leaned back and nearly concussed someone with an elbow. We were packed in like sardines. Really, really drunk sardines. My back had tightened up from sitting so long, so I’d popped a single Tylox. The same voice urged us on, like the Knute Rockne of binge drinking. “If you’re not spinning, you’re not trying hard enough!” That was Nate Rotem. I had first met Nate at a Gucci after-party a couple of years ago. We had been hitting on the same bartender and after she rebuked both our efforts, we drank ourselves into a friendship. He was one of the only people from my “old life” as a model who would still give me the time of day, and certainly the only one I considered a friend. He was notorious for six-figure bar tabs. And since we were going to hemorrhage money, there was only one place to be: Los Angeles. “Chan, I don’t care if you’re going up against fucking Andre the Giant tomorrow, rip this right now!” Nate yowled at me from across the back of the limo, which was spacious enough to hold ten people comfortably. Right now, it was holding about eighteen not-so-comfortably. Already tipsy, I poured from the handle of Patron Silver and raised the shot glass towards Nate. “L’haim,” he yelled, and his shot disappeared instantaneously. He shot me a crescent grin. With the sweet indifference of a much younger man, I parted my lips and tequila seared the back of my throat. I wrinkled my face in a sour expression, coughing softly into my hand. “You’re not done, Chan! Not by a long shot.” I thought about asking Nate to turn down the music, but I didn’t want to ruin this one-night-stand with the Hollywood life. Since PRIME had become a serious investment of my time, I had found myself screening calls from the TMZ names in my BlackBerry. I didn’t have a place amongst the rich and nonchalant anymore. Most nights, I liked to slide unnoticed in and out of San Diego’s smaller chic bars. But tonight was going to be special; nights with Nate always were. Nate was the younger brother of producer J.R. Rotem. He didn’t live solely off J.R.’s success, but he’d gotten in with the right people at the right times. When J.R. had gotten into the studio with Fergie for a remix, Nate was there to explain that the Peas had just held her down anyway. When J.R. played a beat for T.I., Nate was there to explain how King hadn’t left his CD changer in years. And when J.R. went out to lunch with Britney and Paris (before things got ugly), Nate was there ordering a tasty Greek salad and a Bloody Mary. He was a people person in the truest sense of the word, and he turned that skill into a truly A-list lifestyle. “Nate, what’s this song? I think I’ve heard it ten times already.” One of the hangers-on in the car, somebody who knew somebody who knew Nate, was trying to kiss ass. Every time I went out with Nate, I had to put up with it. “It’s T-Pain’s new single. Pain’s people dropped it off to John and he burned me a copy.” Nate always referred to his brother by his first name. He explained that, even if his brother was a world-famous producer, John was still the same kid who had taken piano classes in their living room in New York. It was things like this that made Nate strangely bearable. He lived strictly in the most lavish of worlds, but he wasn’t afraid to step away for a moment and be a human. It was something I had never been able to do when I was running with the Donatellas and Giorgios of the world. “It’s off the chain, man!” The guy talking had on undeniably expensive clothes and was wearing sunglasses worth the GNP of Belarus, but it all screamed poseur. He was drunk and trying to seem young again. I self-consciously adjusted a couple out-of-place hairs. I hoped I didn’t seem as desperate as the Poseur. “Yeah, Pain’s a pop genius,” Nate yelled over the song. “Listen to this hook.” On the chorus, the AutoTuned voice had transformed Bing Crosby’s “Silver & Gold” into an ode about mixing different color tequilas. It was both topical for the limo ride and mildly clever. Everyone who wasn’t in the conversation forced down more shots. The car slid to a stop outside our destination, but Nate wasn’t ready to let his captives go just yet. “That bottle done yet?” A perfect, plastic blonde raised the bottle, whose contents were now safely under the jurisdiction of our respective livers. Nate’s wild, white grin glowed in the blacklight of the limo. “Gonna be a sick night, folks.” He clasped the hand of the female sitting closest to him. He pushed out of the limo door and into the balmy Los Angeles night. The streets out night were frothing with bar hopefuls. Seeing the Les Deux sign was like running into that unattainably shy girl from high school again. I had passed through these doors before its closure in 2003. Now, it had regained its status as the only place to drop ridiculous bills. Behind the velvet rope, two stoic, monolithic men were silently standing with their arms folded over their chests. The line itself was four or abreast, and stretched half a block. Every once in a while, someone yelled from the back of the line. “I’m with Megan Good’s birthday, you asshole!” “Pharrell put my name on the list!” I was surprised that a smile curled my lips. It hadn’t been that long ago that I would’ve been just like Nate, storming in for a one night stand with the City of Angels. But the past year had changed a lot of things. My old wingman was brain-dead at home, waiting for someone to write a crappy pilot with him as the zany suburban dad. I had clawed my way back into Number One Contendership. And, in the biggest sign of change, I was standing in front of the best nightclub in the country thinking about my wrestling career. “Don’t get left behind, Chan. The bouncers might not remember you from when you used to hang out here with fuckin’ Sinatra.” Nate was still twenty-nine and each time I’d seen him since turning thirty, he’d made sure I felt like the old guy. Swept up in the dozen and a half person entourage, I followed as we moved to the door. Nate had daps and fist pounds for every staff member at the door and because most of our party was comprised of young nymphs, we floated past the velvet rope right into the club’s interior. “Jesus…” My reaction was an automatic tic, something meant to be uttered under my breath. Marble shone off every surface and the cool mist of a fountain tickled the small hairs on my hands. “She’s pretty good, eh?” There were plants suspended in ornate pots high overhead. It was truly Babylonian in its excess. “The new owners know a little something about luxury.” The foyer looked more like the lobby of a five-star hotel than a place for celebrities to get their rocks off. “Oh yeah, Boogie’s nuts about making sure this place stays the most uptempo spot in the city.” “You know the owner. Of course.” “Chan, you’ve put me on your tab more than once” he said, curling his slender right arm around my neck. Nate was an inch taller than me, but at my current weight, I probably had forty-five pounds on him. There I went again, sizing up a friend like an opponent. “Anything you want tonight is on me. I mean that, aright?” “Yeah, yeah,” I muttered. He wrenched his arm around my neck playfully. “Follow me. We’re about to have a serious noche.” I followed Nate through huddled bundles of people, all of whom gave us the eye. The eye that says “God, do I hate you, but I’d do anything to be you.” The eye that used to stir my blood and let me know that I was alive. The VIP was dim, like an unlit room at sunset. Bottles of every drink from A-Z stood at attention in their buckets of ice. More unfamiliar faces had joined the limo crew; they all blurred together. I threw some ice into a glass and whipped up a half glass of Goldschlager. Nate was flirting across the way. “Excuse me, do I know you from somewhere?” An olive-skinned brunette had snuck up on my left and stood there gaping at me. “You look really familiar.” It was so easy; all it took was a name drop about Nate or a complete lie about someone I didn’t know. When meaningless sexual encounters were my thing, pretending to be somebody else had been child’s play. Now, I didn’t have the energy for concealing anything. “Probably not,” I shrugged. “I’m Nate’s friend from back in the day.” The young woman, whose petite ankles blossomed upward into thick, sturdy calves, was quite attractive. And her dark eyes were like scorched beads. But her slim, striking nose disrupted the soft features of her face. In Hollywood, one physical flaw is two too many, especially for a woman. “Oh.” Her enthusiasm and voice should’ve been deflated, but they weren’t. “Because you look like this guy who used to model for CK.” She knew exactly who I was, but hadn’t drummed up enough courage to call me out of my lie. I nodded politely. “Thanks, but I’m not that exciting. You can have a seat if you like.” She took the offer, twirling a straw in what appeared to be an alcoholic snow cone in a glass. Neon blue is not a color that humans have been drinking for very long. “What’s your name?” “I’m Annie.” I immediately regretted asking her, but if I wasn’t going to lie for my own gain, it seemed pathetic to make up an identity. “Nice to meet you, Annie. I’m Chandler.” Her eyebrows danced up and down, a look of confusion plastered across her face. “The guy I was talking about. I think his name is–” “Chandler Tsonda,” I finished. I scrunched my lips up, unsure of whether to apologize or criticize Annie’s prodding. “Yeah, sorry about that.” “No, it’s okay.” She looked around at the lavish playground. “But why wouldn’t you want someone to recognize you?” “Annie, what do you do for a living?” Her brow furrowed again. It was always interesting to see the response I could elicit using Dr. DiPendo’s methods. He had taught me that anything is possible if you talk around what other people are saying. “I work in a real estate office.” “Well, I’ve only had two jobs in my life. And at one time or another, I convinced myself that each job was my dream. Because people recognized me across the globe. But do you know what both of those jobs have taught me?” “Recognition doesn’t mean shit if someone doesn’t understand you. Like, I bet a lot of people in your office have recognized that you’re a beautiful young woman.” She put a hand to her face; it was a rehearsed reaction to praise. She didn’t feel flattered, but Annie knew that this was how you were supposed to respond. “But do any of them understand what you really want?” Dark, deep eyes stared back at me, perplexed. “Exactly. Someone who recognizes your face but doesn’t care about your goals or dreams is an impostor. It’s like they’re rooting for a team from a city they’ve never heard of.” Annie put a spidery hand to her cheek. In the past months, I had developed a terrible habit of immediately testing girls. I couldn’t just sit through mindless small talk anymore. I couldn’t pretend that I cared about Girl X’s budding music career. And so, this strange philosophical side had risen, like a surfacing Leviathan. I wasn’t a harmlessly indifferent pretty face anymore. While I waited for Annie to come up with a rebuttal, I chugged the rest of my Goldschlager. Peppermint spice clung to the sides of my throat. “You’re pretty deep for a model.” “Even more so for a wrestler, toots.” I flashed a grin, unsure of whether to commit. She pulled in closely, tracing my jaw line with icy fingers. “You wanna do some lines?” I recoiled, not in horror or shock. My septum could tell some tales; it wasn’t the drugs that threw me. Where I had expected depth, maybe even understanding, there was a cavernous empty space. “Sorry, not really my thing anymore.” “Suit yourself.” At least she wasn’t one of those people who insisted you take the plunge with them. She surreptitiously shook out a haphazard line onto a credit card on her lap. But it wasn’t like she scanned the room; it was etiquette, not fear of reproach, that collected the powder out of sight. A quick inhale and poof! “Just surprising, ya know?” “I don’t like coming down.” “I’m not judging. I just saw you slip a coupla pills in the limo and thought you might be down.” “It’s pain medication.” “Downers, uppers, they’re all the same. Everybody’s high on something. My psych teacher used to say that.” “It’s prescribed by a doctor, though.” Why was I defending my right to use Tylox? It wasn’t need; it was philosophy again. If I was dependant, it was because I chose that dependency over pain. “I get Aderolled before I go out. Listen, I don’t care. You seem like a chill guy. I just thought because you do pills, you’d wanna do some lines. No biggie.” But it was. The monster inside screamed that Annie was wrong. Maybe that was the reason things fell apart: because I listened to the monster, the same flimsy part of my soul that had me scrounging in my pocket twice a day. “Well, that’s not me,” I snarled. “Sorry. Didn’t know you were such a righteous addict.” The monster rose and roared. “You know what, bitch? Why don’t you take your coke whore act and blow me?” Her eyes darted around. “Nate!” My aficionado friend came strolling over. “What’s good, Channycakes?” “Get this fucking fiend out of my face.” Nate bit his lip and gave a head nod to Annie. I kept looking at her as she moved away and she flipped me the bird. “Yeah, keep walking right on down to the corner." She didn't turn around for my parting shot. "You look like a fucking gargoyle!” Nate pulled me aside, but this time his arm was loose around my neck, like I was a stranger. “Yo, Chan, we just came here to party. If some chicks wanna snort a little white, don’t freak out.” “Nate, she’s a fucking scab.” It was a royal overreaction, but it was also like watching yourself from the outside. The anger that usually subsided was now surging forward bravely throughout my veins. “Chan, you need to calm down, man.” “What the fuck? You say that anything I want is on you and then when I need that stupid cunt outta my face, you clam up.” Nate’s perpetual grin had faded, as he tried to make sense of illogical anger. “Listen, man, you take the limo back to my place. You need some fresh air or something. I’ll meet up with you later.” I didn’t say anything. That sounded about right. I needed to be away from this whole scene. “You alright, Chan? This doesn’t seem like you.” It was me, though. Something had unleashed the old me. The Tsonda who was unbearable to be around. The Tsonda who thought friendship was something you shared with anything who was willing to drink and go to the bar with you. And while I was vaguely aware of this, I didn’t even think about connecting it to my pain meds. I pushed back out to the street, where the limo was waiting. The driver nodded to me. “Mr. Rotem said you want to head back to his apartment?” “Yeah, let's get the fuck out of here." The blacklight and the emptiness made the limo feel like another planet. The air conditioning was too cold and I was drowsy from the booze. I rattled the pill container in my pocket. As I drifted off, I was looking forward to the next day. I could apologize to Nate. I could prepare for ReVolution and watch some tape; that always calmed me down. I thought it all started with keeping the pain at bay.
