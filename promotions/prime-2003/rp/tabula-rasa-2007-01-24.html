Tabula Rasa

24 January 2007
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 24/01/07 Tabula Rasa Forget what you knew, because what you knew will only forget you. I Over time, Cecelia Cohen had grown to enjoy the hour they had together once a fortnight. Last session, she’d balanced her chequebook to within 2 cents, not bad considering she could barely tell between her sevens and nines. The threes and fives were a little iffy, too. The week before that, she’d picked a nice little villa in Aruba for her summer vacation. It was coming up on 2 years since Karina Wolfenden first slumped down into the client’s arm-chair and made herself comfortable. For the first 2 months, Cecelia at least tried, out of professionalism, to bounce a ball against the brick wall she was faced with. She relished a challenge. But, to save the therapist requiring therapy herself, Cohen grew to accept the fact that she could take an hour off, and bill the time down to the young woman’s employer. “Why do you even come here, if you don‘t mind me asking?” Cecelia couldn’t help but enquire a few months into their status quo. “I charge the time down and bill it to, let me check… PRIME, every fortnight, regardless.” After 10 seconds or so, once she’d finished the page of her book, the young woman answered, without looking up. “I like the peace and quiet.” Over their first summer as therapist and, well, ‘client’, she began to eek out a little more dialogue. “Air-con bust?” started it, one May afternoon. “You should sack your secretary,” followed the next fortnight. By July, they’d actually achieved the landmark of a brief conversation, albeit one initiated with something that went a little like, “you can get a way more comfy chair than this down at that Danish store next to the Russian deli.” By August, they’d confessed their addictions to Lost, triggering the ‘it’s limbo vs. it’s Atlantis’ debate. And then, the next session… nothing. Silence returned. Dropping into the newly-purchased, Scandinavian contoured chair, Karina crossed her legs, retreated behind her fringe, and slammed the door on the outside world. For 16 months they fell back into their routine. Karina would show up, sit in silence while Cecelia administrated her affairs, leave, and the tick in the box would be sent back to her employer. Until today. 22 minutes (and, roughly, $90) into this afternoon’s appointment, she’d given up trying to figure out the last clue of the Times crossword, and had resumed flicking through brochures, still debating which school to send her son to. Just as Cecelia had ringed the address of her first choice, there was movement in her peripheral vision. Unfurling from sitting cross-legged in her chair, Karina shuffled forward, chin propped up by the backs of her hands, elbows propped up by her knees. “This time tomorrow I’ll be worth just over $1.7 million,” Karina almost sighed out, before finally looking up to Cecelia’s eye-level. “That’s pretty fucked up.” II Ringtone. Groan. Shuffling of sheets. Click. “Morning, sunshine.” “Hey, Dad.” “Happy birthday.” “Thaaaaaaank-you.” “You know I wish I was there for your eighteenth, b-” “It’s okay, really.” “Well, I felt I had to make it up to you a little bit, soooo… open your curtains.” “And how do you know they’re not open already, and your surprise isn’t ruined?” “Because it’s 9 o’clock… in the morning.” “True…” “Well, go on.” “Okay, okay, what have y-… oh my God… no fucking way…” “Language.” “That’s… MINE? Seriously… if it is, you will officially be the greatest parent in the history of ever.” “Happy birthday.” “It’s… you remembered… it’s actua-” “A Ducati 748 death-trap, ‘fierce cherry’ or whatever made-up color they gave it.” “Thankyouthankyouthankyouthankyouthankyouthankyoutha-” “You can thank me by not killing yourself on i-” “Yeah, yeah, gotta go, I hear it calling me…thankyouthankyouthankyo-” “Okay, okay, you know I can’t handle THIS much gratitude from my children. Enjoy the rest of the day, and I’ll see you next weekend. Love you.” “Love ya right back.” “Remember what I said about not kil-… and you’re gone, aren’t you… thought so.” III She only had herself to blame. The clue was in the name. Public transport. PUBLIC transport. PUBLIC transport. With her wheels having been exploded at the hands, or rather the lighter, of one Michael Kriegman, Karina Wolfenden was left firing agitated glances at every interruption in the train’s carriage. But, while it was venom protruding from her emerald eyes, her true hatred was reserved for her I-Pod’s battery, which chose the choice moment to die on her. Devoid of her sensory deprivation, her sonic barrier from the world, she was left exposed to all manner of horrors. Leant back against the train’s window, the K-Wolf had spread across both seats. A notepad rested against her thighs, which formed a 45 degree easel, while her pen was in full flow. Sensing something over the horizon of her page, Wolfenden peeked, but saw nothing. Another paragraph was scrawled down, but still the feeling lingered. Shuffling her feet forward a few inches, Kari lowered her pad, and was greeted by the sight of a curious young girl, just staring right at her. Using her pad as an eyelid, Kari blinked behind it, but the same sight was there to meet her. “What’re you doing?” the girl then asked, childish intrigue overcoming her. “Writing,” Karina brusquely answered, looking up out the tops of her eyes at the girl, before waving the pen to emphasise her words. “Why?” abruptly followed, seemingly leeching a little of Wolfenden’s personality by standing so close. “Because I have a pen, some paper, and I can’t draw for shi-…” the K-Wolf went to snap in retaliation for the feeling of being cornered by the curious kid, before she checked her words. “I have too many things in my head, and this helps get some of them out.” “Are you poor?” was the answer Karina got for her troubles as the child tilted her head to the side. “Your clothes are all torn-y, and my Mom writes her stuff on a Mac. It’s purple.” “No,” Karina stated in taken-aback response. “Your hair always like that?” the deluge of questions continued with, while Wolfenden couldn’t decide between wanting to throw her out the window, or admire her brashness. “Is writing your job?” “No,” Kari again answered, scribbling down her last thought before it was lost to distraction. “My job is kicking people in the head until they stop moving. I‘m good at it.” The aim was to frighten her off, but the K-Wolf might have just found her match, and maybe even her apprentice. “Cool,” she eagerly remarked, hopping up onto what little of the seat Karina wasn‘t slouched across. “Who’s Lawrence?” Karina snapped her pad around, out of sight of the girl’s roving eyes. “They teach you about something called ‘personal space’ in school?” Karina enquired with avid curiosity as the child spun around to sit cross-legged on the train seat, facing Wolfenden. The armour of youthful innocence repelled whatever verbal rounds the K-Wolf fired off. “Nah,” the girl shrugged, “we’ll probably do that next year. So, who is he then? He your boyfriend?” Keeping the pad held close to her chest, Kari tried to shove the girl off the seat with her glare alone. “He’s my father.” “Ew,” went the repulsed reaction. “What’s he do? He write, or kick people?” “He’s dead,” the K-Wolf distantly answered, finally sapping a little tempo from the young girl. “Oh…” she paused, “that sucks.” The world vision that’s clear to those yet to be blinded by cynicism is a refreshing one, and, although she’d deny it, Karina shuffled an inch or so closer to the window, freeing some space. “Yeah, it kinda does,” the K-Wolf agreed. “So what were you writing?” the enquiring mind again attempted to feed its curiosity by asking. “Are you writing a will? Mom wrote something with Granddad in it when he died.” “I write stories,” Kari divulged, beginning to soften. “Like Narnia?” the girl continued to probe, head sneaking around to try and take another peek. “A few less lions… and usually without the happy ending,” Wolfenden answered, seeing her own blunt edge to the girl. “There’s been stalkers, romance, mysteries, family problems, fairytales, death, heroes, heroines, loss, heartbreak, and glory. But, this is the last tale in the story. The lead character walked out on all she thought she wanted, and now she’s getting ready to move on.” “Who’s that?” naturally followed. “Karina,” answered, well, Karina. “Who’s she?” the girl couldn’t help but ask, intrigued by having jumped on at the end of the story. “That’s a very good question…” IV For 16 months, Karina Wolfenden has learned to block out the fizzing of the lit fuse. For 16 months, that flash of heat had always been there, nagging, but boxed away, out of sight, out of mind. But, today, 1 month administration period after her 25th birthday, it went bang. She got the birthday present she’d never asked for, never wished for, never picked out of a window, a catalogue, a brochure. She got her inheritance, passed down just the one generation. There was no card. Feeding the last of her notebook’s pages through the shredder, Karina brought the last chapter to a close. Then, slashing a fingernail across the envelope from her father’s will’s trustees, began the next story.
