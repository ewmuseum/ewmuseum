Saint's Town

23 October 2007
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 10/23/07 “Two days ago, San Diego was the jewel of the West Coast. But the beautiful city with blue skies, a deep blue ocean and miles of beaches has become Armageddon.” -Jonathan Gallen The building was all but gone. Its frame still stood, a lonely skeleton crumbling its way off the horizon. And here I was, at the door where Jacques used to tell me stories about his nephews and nieces every day. No Jacques. Hell, no door. I was in the middle of San Diego, but I felt like I was walking back into Chernobyl. I tried to imagine fire everywhere. Everything. All. For hours, my ex-apartment building had lived in a world of unending orange. And now chunks of my home were crackling off the walls every time I put my hand down. Bizarro apartment did not suit me well and in the burned world, there was almost nothing but silence. Outside, the firefighters made no effort to mask their own produced noise. But the interior was calm and quiet. Like the trenches once every gunner has died. Random firefighter number one had told me that the structural damage was minimal, but that they’d probably tear everything down because the cosmetic damage was almost irreparable. He didn’t say it that intelligently, but made it clear that I could poke around if I really wanted to smell like a human pack of Marlboro Reds. The stairs looked haunted, completely black with charred loss. I was hesitant to climb to my second floor room. The last thing I needed was a breakdown about losing all my shit. There is nothing irreplaceable in my life. Hard to have priceless family mementos sitting around when you haven’t really had a family in years. Sure, I bought my mom her first house, but she never really recovered from her son becoming a real American. She came here looking my father and ended up losing her son to the same culture that raped her people and destroyed her culture. Fair trade, eh? No, maybe it wasn’t the stuff I’d lost. It was picking up, starting over, and making home somewhere new. To be honest, home was really on the road. Even in my time away from wrestling, I’d felt more comfortable traveling. But this was something different. I was reverting to pioneer status, blazing trails across the San Diego real estate market, caulking my covered Mercedes wagon across the next river. And it still wasn’t enough to stop me from ascending the stairs. The rooms were windswept, getting their first feel of outside air in many moons. The building was merely the instrument of the wind, which played the gaps and fractures like a cheap harmonica. Entirely random objects fluttered out with a purpose, turning the hallway into a mangled daydream. On the sooty carpet were a loose graduation certificate, a small stuffed bear solemnly missing an ear, and a refrigerator magnet in the shape of Missouri. The wind tugged all three ever so slightly; they almost looked like they fit together. Stepping through an empty doorframe, my door now singed and in the middle of my living room’s remains, was not how I imagined my triumphant return after ReV 142. I’d been looking for a dip in the hot tub and a tall Leinenkugel’s. My actual reward for a hard week’s work was unstoppable wildfires, berserk with their own power. The news said over half a million people had been displaced by Californian wildfire, no paltry sum considering man “conquered” fire thousands of years ago. Fire is funny like that. The elemental clash of heat upon the earth spills out and makes new. A purging fire prunes the wilderness, just as this fire would change the human environ. A new structure would go up, the phoenix sky smiling brightly. New life would begin there. The flames of Lazarus would be forgotten as new became commonplace. People would move back into the same valley, where fire had always snuck up on man, and repeat this cycle. Chaos was everywhere. Old boxes, split open by fire’s whim, spilled tax forms and old hate mail. The loveseat looked exactly as I had imagined it: the left side was immaculate, but the leather on the arm was burnt crispy. With the windows destroyed, the elements following the fire had left their mark on the place. The usual cleanliness was laughable to think about. I pivoted and looked into my bedroom. There was a man there. It was both sudden and surprising, but not in a way that made me start. I cocked my head to the side, wondering if flipped furniture was playing a trick on me. No, there. He was on his knees, dipping his shoulder to reach an unseen something. “Why are you in my apartment?” I guess it was still mine until they officially condemned it or knocked it down. And it sounded better than trying to figure out another way to start the conversation with authority. He turned around, as though the roles were reversed. The short-sleeve blue-collared shirt with red tie gave him away as shitty at whatever job he had. Brutal fashion, but it also said “hey, I don’t mind if my clothes don’t demand respect”. Serious no-no. “I’m inspecting damage for the landlord’s insurance claim. Did you live here?” “It’s my apartment,” I spat, being intentionally thick. Why was some paper pusher rummaging through my material goods before me? Where was the bureaucracy supposed to stop competency like this? “Unless you’re the landlord, I doubt that’s the case. Either way, if you’re the last tenant, you’ve got to wait until I finish my assessment.” He tried to turn around, but my immediate response caught him, bringing his attention back upon my face. “How about assessing the rooms with no people in them while I pick up anything that still…” I flicked a fried alarm clock, listening for the whir of gears. “…works around here.” The insurance man sighed so hard I thought he might fall over. Glancing around the room, he made a couple more notes on his clipboard and slipped into the kitchen without a word. “I know the fridge should’ve been a dead giveaway, but that’s not so much an exit as it is the kitchen.” Not even a breath. “Scram, weasel.” He poked his head through the doorway just long enough to unpack his words. “The legal precedent to be here belongs to me. So if you don’t want those husky fire department workers to give you a very hard time, be quiet about whatever it is you have to do.” I was not ready to be one-upped in what was still, for this moment in time, my home. Snarling, I strode through the same doorway, willing myself not to put my hands on him. “You do this for a paycheck? Or is there some innate pleasure in screwing with people’s misfortune?” “Oh, there’s no misfortune involved,” he cooed smugly. “This area has one of the highest incidences of destructive natural fire in the country.” His smile was loud and abrasive, and only lasted a second. “Don’t touch any of my shit,” I grumbled, turning back towards the rest of my life. We said nothing else to each other in our time there. Restless about the ashy clutter, I tried straightening things, but only kicked up clouds of dark, thick dust. Finally, it was time to give up, with the air too hot to stay in the thick, smoggy ruins. The fire was destined to chase us, as it had all the inhabitants of this area, ever. I thought about whether it might come back, compelled by windy temperament. This wild flame had been so close to me, fingering my bedsheets and kissing the marble countertop. And now home was gone again. I would look to the wind, slipping off the Pacific. For that beautiful, blue-green water propelled fire onwards. But eventually, it wouldn’t matter where things started, nor where they ended. Only where they go when they decide to dissapear.
