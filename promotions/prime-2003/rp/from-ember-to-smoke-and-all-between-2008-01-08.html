From Ember To Smoke...And All Between

8 January 2008
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 1/8/08 December 24th, 2007 11:06 P.M. She had never been able to get me to the candlelight service, but this year I didn’t have the heart to deny my mother a small pleasure. And there I was, dressed to the nines for the Almighty and a small congregation. Tipsy from several (okay, one) martini at dinner, my head was flush with rumbling ideas. The rest of the church was cloaked in the dawning organ music, a croaking, ancient sound. Apparently, we were listening to an instrumental Christmas song of some kind; I didn’t recognize it. The churchgoers solemnly listened, with bowed heads or closed eyes. Rarely do you get to come home in this world, but I felt strangely welcomed here. Almost all of the people here were Vietnamese, many of them immigrants. I found myself struggling to comprehend the language anymore, but something about their faces was right, just a little bit. As we were urged to stand for the singing of “Hark The Herald Angels Sing,” I unleashed my mind on all the things I was imagining. Why was a structure that so perfectly embodied throwing off the yolk of human, physical limitations called the “House of God?” Why did this serene, communal tradition have to be based on the idea that a bearded white guy lives in the sky? The hum of the song passed me by; I realized that church wasn’t a religious experience for me, but it was a nice, quiet place to think, in a life full of deafening sound. I thought back on King of Kings, arguably the greatest night of my professional life. I had been so simultaneously tired and amped up that I couldn’t sleep. But for the first time in years, I didn’t look at an eerily late clock and rationalize calling a woman. I think…that I had been completely content for a couple minutes. Maybe that’s why life is hard for people like my mother; maybe the trade-off for those shimmering, escaping moments of blinding happiness is fair. I didn’t know. I just knew I wanted another one. “Chan,” my mother nudged me, as I noticed I was the last person standing. “Sit,” she hissed, ever mindful of what the congregation thought. “Sorry,” I mumbled, looking back up at the stained glass that had stolen me away into full-fledged daydream. Fidgeting in my seat, I craned my neck to see more faces. A subdued happiness had taken everyone. Sure, Christmas Eve breeds karmic positivity and proverbial good will towards all, but it was more than just the forthcoming days off from work that had these people paralyzed, eyes towards the many candles near the altar and pulpit. God or no, something was very real about the inflated happiness of those present. I almost felt it necessary to thank someone for my current good graces. For the win. For a finally blossoming career. But, before getting lost in rainbows and unicorns, I couldn’t forget that whatever higher powers might be had handed my mother the most gruesome of death sentences. The song was ending, the final note breaking in the small, gossamer flames. “Tonight, friends,” the minister started. She looked too young to be pledged towards chastity, sobriety, and all that mislabeled virtue. Did she love the shaman’s indigo life of quiet? As she started on the nativity story, I faded back out into the cosmos and my own thoughts. I liked the sights and sounds here. There was none of the grim determination of everyday life; I liked being able to sit and meditate, even if I wasn’t following the prescribed ritual. In other words, I could handle everything but the belief. The bits and pieces I’d caught from the minister’s sermon didn’t even really have to do with belief. My mother, the Congregational Protestant, was not the typical Asian immigrant Christian. This church seemed pretty okay with just being a person and believing in minimal tenets of the doctrine. It was certainly the lesser of all evils. “And so we remember Jesus’ ride to his death not with sorrow, but as a ride of triumph.” Riding to slaughter? Somebody’s always doing that in PRIME. I only hoped, right now, that it was Silver, and not yours truly. After all this, would my fate be irony or catharsis? Is it better to have seen the summit and slid back down or to have never displaced mountain rock from underfoot? I was thinking in clichéd, existentialism; church was a weird fuckin’ place. The lights dimmed without sound. Ushers slid through the aisles, their floating flames illuminating those corridors. With their long candles, they lit the ones we had been handed when we entered. Before long, the darkness was repelled by individual flamelings. The cranky organ played “Silent Night” without the hum of even one ghostly voice. The second time around, we were encouraged to sing along. There we were, as ancient as possible. Fire in front of us, their tribal songs shifting through the air. We were primal in the year’s dying days. In the candlelit chamber, I looked down at my program. The inscription was a picture of the cross, with the underlying words “It is accomplished”. Even with my rudimentary religious schooling, I understood these as alleged words on the cross. I thought about it and decided that it meant something about dying with pride. But it was late, my mother needed to rest, and it would be soon back to the PRIME grind. There was much to accomplish.
