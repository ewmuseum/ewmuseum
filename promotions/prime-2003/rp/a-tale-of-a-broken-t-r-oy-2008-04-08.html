A Tale of a Broken T(r)oy

Date: 4/8/08 The thing about the human condition is that we think we've advanced somewhere; we believe we're beyond the halting strides of our past. That where we used to be and where we are now are wildly separate ends of the continuum of life. That's bullshit, though. A human being is a human being. We still love and lose. We still catch the same diseases (when we're not inventing brand new ones), and we still tell our stories the same way. Sure, we have computers and the Internet now, just like we had television and radio before them. But when one sits down and thinks about it -- really thinks about it -- most stories today are still relayed verbally. Two employees sitting around the office, gossiping about their weekend. You and your friends at the bar. Even the omnipresence of cellphones, with their text messages and their built-in instant message programs, are just ways of shortening the process. Don't want to talk to Jim right now but have something he's got to hear? Send him a text message! He'll read it and forget it -- chances are, in a few days he'll have to delete it. Modern society, just like the caveman, is about telling stories around the fire, because we really don't want to write things down. It takes time; it takes effort. And it leaves an uncomfortable trail behind, memories of a time we might rather leave behind. Ever go back and read your journal from fifth grade when you were thirty? It's kind of embarrassing. What follows is a story of what happens when a tale is written down, especially when that story should never have been told to start with. ---- There exists, someplace in the world, a filing cabinet of almost every secret never told. The cabinet is known to some as the "Shadow Cabinet," and possession of it is something long sought-after by people whose actions rarely ever saw the light of day. Among other things, the Shadow Cabinet contains a folder, beaten and pea-green, which contains several files regarding a project that officially never existed. The handwriting on the document is neat and tight, almost too perfect. Handwriting analysis (done by the cabinet's current owner) indicates a remarkable and ever-increasing level of stress throughout the document. Further research is mandated. ---- Elsewhere, in stark contrast to the neat, meticulous notes taken by Dr. McMillan, there lies a haphazard stack of wrinkled loose-leaf paper, tossed in the deep, lower drawer of a steel desk, not unlike that which might be found in a school. The desk itself is shoved up against the gray-green wall of a condemned theater. These notes are frantic, at times incoherent and at times wildly brilliant. This excerpt is far more the former than the latter: "When you wish upon a star Makes no difference who you are Everything your heart desires Will come to you." My bitchwhore of a mother used to sing that to me every night when I was young. I would be wrapped, snug as a bug, in my Sleeping Beauty bedsheets, and she would whisper that song over me, her thin lips barely moving. It was sweet, considerate, and touching... a lovely moment between a mother and her child. I think it was meant to assure me that I could be anything I wanted to be. What a load of horseshit. I sit here, curled up, Indian-style, on a rank, knotted mattress in a badly-ventilated rat's nest of a motel room, scribbling these thoughts down now with a nub of a pencil I stole from a diner waitress. They run around in my head like ants running from the light. Is that even possible? Is it possible my thoughts might just be insects, whispering in my ear? It has been very carefully kept by its current owner, however, unmarred by fingerprints and cleaned twice daily by the maid (who was, incidentally, only allowed to touch the cabinet itself). Over the years and throughout the owners, the Shadow Cabinet had been upgraded in security, to the point that the small, three-foot-tall matte black cabinet now requires a ten-digit key code to access it. It sat alone in the room, with just a small, wheeled stool to keep it company. The current owner of the Cabinet strode briskly into the room, punching the code in by rote (this was the individual who had installed this particular number pad, after all, so it stood to reason there was no need for a mnemonic device to remember it). The bottom drawer of the Cabinet opened. A thick, pea-green folder was slid into the drawer before it was gently shoved closed with an amused sound. The owner of the Shadow Cabinet strode briskly from the room. She flicked a curtain of hair the color of clotted blood from her face as she shut the door carefully behind her.
