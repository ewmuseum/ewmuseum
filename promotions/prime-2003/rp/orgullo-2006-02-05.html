Orgullo

5 February 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2/5/06 Preface: The Forces Gravity is spiteful when you defy it. Gravity is malicious, opportunistic, unmerciful, and vindictive. When the audacity of flight is revealed to be an illusion, and the gravity of the earth pulls you back to the land, you pay dearly for your arrogance. The Latin Assassin understood this. Literally, he had spent a career exploiting the laws and the loopholes of gravity. So far, he had been lucky. Pain is more subtle. More subtle, but more controlling. It shares an incestuous bed with its twin, Pleasure. It flirts and it lies, and it pretends. Unlike Gravity, the boundaries of Pain are more insidious. Pain will cocoon itself away until the time is perfect, and then explode with a white-hot fanfare of its presence. It will linger on the edges of the mind, or bleach away all things but it. Like the sun, it can haunt the spaces it leaves behind like a sunset, or wash away all things but itself. Pain is another force that Damien Cruz had learned to tame, to control, to ignore, or to embellish. Pain was more serpentine than the behemoth Gravity, but it was just as vengeful. Chaos touches us all. It breaks atoms from molecules, turning air into poison, water into peroxide, and life into death. Cells decay and turn from tissue into tumors. The break in the blood vessel, the spontaneous explosion, the missed breath in the fetal lung...Chaos tears apart everything we fight so hard to hold together. For Damien, everything he has ever accomplished has been a betrayal of Chaos' sweeping touch. Often, it is a bargain, an agreement that Damien can achieve his will, at the cost of losing all else. Damien understood Chaos, and accepted the powerlessness it brought. Pride is the apple in Eden. It is the shimmering gold coin at the bottom of the river that swallows up the fool that pursues it. Pride is the rush of pleasure that carries with it the virus into the bloodstream. Pride is the Gambler's Law that the next roll of the die will undo a lifetime full of lost earnings. Pride has sailed ships, crumbled empires, ravaged women, exterminated races, and placed Messiahs up on crosses. Pride is also what makes the cross the symbol of God's mercy. In the eyes of The Last Son of Lucha Libre, Pride is one force that he fears the most. He didn’t remember exactly why he had the word etched into his left pectoral, but the dark ink and precise calligraphy had been there for as long as he could remember. It was a single word imposed over his heart: ‘Orgullo’. Pride. Part I: The Shifting World The old cliché was true; the dead look like half-melted wax statues of their former selves. The eyes closed, with an unnatural tension that threatened to spring them open at any moment, left to stare at the gathered mourners. The hands that used to stir meals, mend clothes, till the land, please their spouses, or wipe away their children’s tears were stiff and disembodied, clutching a rosaries or neatly resting on their chests. The mouths, the bodies, the features of the dead are subliminally wrong, disproportionate, and unacceptable. For Damien Cruz, looking at the body of his mentor, idol, and friend, the discrepancy was painful. Father Victor Morales was immortal. The body in that black oak casket was a lie. It was an elaborate effigy. For whatever reason, the life of a sinner had caught up with the duties of the righteous. Father Morales was murdered by El Nino Oro. Looking around, he saw statesmen, doctors, businessmen, and fellow athletes. He saw former students, trusted friends, and shared families that had been brought together by the work of Father Morales. In the surreal reality, what would have been absurd was acceptable, and firmly appropriate. Among the mourners were masked men. Men who wore muted versions of their colorful, gaudy mascaras. Some wore darker hues, others wore black versions. Some, out of deep respect, showed their faces in public for the very first time. Damien looked at the men who had fought before him, along side him, and after him. He saw legends and he saw prodigies. He saw faded glory renewed in the memory of their mentor and friend. Whatever these men had done in their past was forgotten, and eclipsed by the shared sorrow of losing one of their own. One man, sitting in a wheelchair after disseminated multiple sclerosis had taken hold unmercifully, wiped a tear away without shame. The man, Juan Maldonado was once El Rey Diablo, and had purposely broken Morales’ arm as a vendetta for unseating him as number one contender. This was in 1972, and it made no difference at that moment. It was a lifetime ago, and it was a part of a lifestyle. It was a lifestyle that Victor Morales had embraced as El Nino Oro. The women were his first vice. As his popularity grew around Latin America, so did his taste for the groupies. He would often joke, in hushed tones among his old friends, that there wasn’t a woman fat enough, skinny enough, or old enough to repel him. He was a man with a simple motto; take what is offered. Old photos of him always showed an attractive woman, or group of women, draped over him. Young, Gifted, and Latino was his claim to fame. Soon after, there was the extravagance. Making a little more than fifty-five thousand a show at his peak, he would spend it almost as fast as he collected it. He went through four accountants before he found one that would approve of his habits. He bought three cars to park in each of his four houses. He bought ponies for the daughters of his girlfriends, and dirt bikes for their sons. Each new girlfriend was given an apartment, and a checking account. It was also not uncommon for him to have three or four girlfriends at a time. The man had a lust for life, and a sense that what we don’t embrace whole-heartedly is wasted. This sense of liberation also made cocaine especially dangerous. His first hit was at the legendary Studio 54. Underneath the gold, the silk, and the money, he was still a small-town Mexican boy with small town naiveté. When his buddy came into the room with three women and a silver plate full of coke, it was that sense of bravado that made him dive head-first into each. Three months later, the woman became more a burden than a pleasure, and the money stopped buying as much. The drugs took hold of him, and two years later, his world was spinning out of control. Doing almost a kilogram a week took its toll emotionally, financially, and most evidently, physically. Losing muscle faster than he could build it, losing bone density and gathering injuries, missing practices, while stumbling through those that he did make, and the rapid degeneration made his downfall quick and devastating. On April 17th, 1979 Victor Morales was found dead in his hotel room of an apparent drug overdose. Not able to afford the quality cocaine he was accustomed to, he scored a bag from a man he had met in the hotel lobby. It was cut with baking soda and boric acid. Fortunately, he had only done half a line before slipping into a coma. Although the newspapers and some local radio stations ran with the initial report, it was confirmed later that he was still alive, but in critical condition. It took him three years, and all of his remaining wealth to make a full recovery. When he did, he turned his life over to God. He attended seminary, earned a degree in education, and repaid the charity of The Church with his service as a priest. He separated himself from his former life in every way he could. However, he kept two things valuable. One, his mask, was kept in a chest in his bedroom. One day, he hoped to pass it on to a young man who was willing to carry on his legacy. And the second was the sport. For all his weakness, he knew that Lucha Libre was his salvation. With what he could gather from the community, borrow from old friends, and scratch together from grants, he opened the Los Locos Lucha Libre in the local gymnasium. He then took a job as a principal at a local school. This allowed him to expand his efforts from being a federation owner to also creating a wrestling program for those students who needed an escape from the streets that tempted them. That is where he met a young boy named Damien Cruz. Part II: The Ants When he first met him, Damien was eight years old, sitting on a small mound of dirt near the playground. Father Morales saw the familiar posture of a quiet boy who socialized very little, and tended to keep to himself. He approached the child, and prepared to convince him to join in the others in their game of soccer. Then he noticed it. He saw what the boy was staring at. It was an army of ants swarming around a scorpion. No matter how hard the creature tried to run away, or how valiant it tried to fend them off with his stinger, the ants literally picked him apart. Like a rolling flow of magma, the ants devoured the scorpion. Slowly, the desperate movements of the devastated animal stopped, and the ants were left with their bounty. Father Morales watched carefully, this time turning his attention to the child who watched it intently. Then the boy spoke. “Father Morales, is that what it felt like…you know, when you were sick?” Father Morales wanted to respond, but didn’t. He simply extended his hand to the boy, and led him back to where the others were playing. In the coming years, Damien Cruz would prove to be his greatest student, and would eventually grow far beyond the boundaries of that small town wrestling school. One day, he would be The Last Son. The day before Damien left, Father Morales handed him his old Nino Oro mask. He explained to his student that while he was not his son, he felt that he was the rightful heir. Damien accepted the gift, and hoped to make the name proud. Father Morales smiled, and hugged him, then explained that Damien Cruz was the name he wanted to see honored. Part III: Cumulus Clouds The service was performed by Father Gustavo, an ironic opponent of everything Father Morales attempted to do within the community. Father Gustavo felt that wrestling taught the children violence, and that more violence was the last thing the children needed in their lives. He once claimed that teaching them how to fight was just a way of making them more dangerous to themselves and each other. Father Gustavo also made his objections to Father Morales’ ordination very well-known on moral grounds. Even after thirty-something years in service to The Church, Father Gustavo scoffed at the man’s works. However, in the strange politics of the church, he was tapped to lay Father Morales to rest. Damien sat in the back of the church, focusing his respect on the changes he made in himself due to conversations he had with Father Morales, or through the lessons he learned from his mentor. He recalled the time Father Morales ‘lost’ the keys to the car, and ‘forgot’ to lock the garage when he knew Damien needed to go to his senior prom. Clearly, a priest giving one of his students access to his car for the prom was not within the handbook, but it was one of those examples of how Father Morales balanced his life between what he knew was realistic, and what he was taught to preach. Damien had made a strict promise to be a gentleman, and Father Morales made a promise to be a friend. In the end, both held up their end of the bargain. The condom Father Morales had tucked into the visor stayed right where it was. Father Gustavo raised his eyes to the gathered mourners, and rested them on Damien. Damien knew what he was being asked to do. He rose, and walked up to the pulpit. Taking his place, he began the eulogy that he had never wanted to give. He took a deep breath, and spoke. “I will not pretend to know much about this life, or the life that shall follow. I cannot concede any philosophical understanding of why we are born, why we are placed in this world with these fragile bodies, or what we are expected to accomplish before we are ushered into the next dimension of our spiritual existence. No, I am not a cleric or a prophet, or a scholar, or a teacher. I am a luchadore.” The room full of his comrades, heroes, compatriots, and scions accept the statement with shared reverence. “Father Morales was a man who had walked between worlds. He was a man who had devoured every feast placed at his table: poisons and ambrosia alike. And when he rose from that table, he was changed forever. Today, we pay respect and honor to a man who did not curse his mistakes, nor embellish his successes. Despite what the gossipers will ruminate, or what his disciples will place as Gospel, Father Victor Morales was one of us. He was a luchadore.” There was uneasiness among the back-biters, as well as those who had bleached away the darker sides of Morales’ past. They had already begun the task of co-opting the man’s legacy, or undermining the man’s impact on the community. Without acknowledging them, Damien said his peace. “I spoke to Father Morales a few months back. He had just returned from the hospital after a mild heart attack. The prognosis, as we have come to see, was ominous. It would seem that the years spent abusing his body, despite the years he spent cultivating, had left their mark. Even after the surgeries, and the change in lifestyle, Father Morales was told that he was still a high-risk for a heart attack. I spoke to him that afternoon, and he refused to see me. At first, I was angry at the man, but I also knew that he still held a belief that a man like him should not be seen as vulnerable and scared. I understood that, and I respected it. I was about to leave when I heard the door open. He was standing there, in his robe, with a tube stemming from his chest, and another leading to a bag of urine hanging from his hip.” There is a sense of discomfort in the room, but Damien doesn’t care. “He let me into his room, and he cried as he told me that he was scared that he would die and be turned away at Heaven’s Gates. This man, who lived by the code of bravado and courage was crying to his former student, and asking me if I thought less of him.” Damien cleared his throat, wiping back a tear that he hadn’t felt form itself. When he felt the stare of those who thought Father Morales a fool on him, he found the words to continue. “After that day, and despite what he had been told by his doctor, he went back to his students, and dedicated himself to teaching them the art of Lucha Libre with a dedication and fervor that had rivaled his youth. To the day he died, in that ring after pushing himself too hard, he did so for this art, this life, this world that none but a luchadore can understand. And for that, we all owe him a debt of gratitude.” Damien bowed his head for a moment, and brought his final respects to a fitting end. “For some of us, we are returning to our homes, returning to our gyms, or returning to our lives on the road. Lives spent in hotel rooms, training centers, arenas and gymnasiums. I do so as a man who has a lot to prove among my peers in PRIME. I am returning to the cold dinners, the hot compresses and ice baths, the crappy airline food and the dull ache from that ancient match that never quite goes away. I am returning to the life that my mentor, my idol, and my friend literally gave his life for. And I do it without forgetting why I do it.” In that moment, Damien felt a warm burn over his left pectoral, where the Indian ink was fused into his skin. He stepped down from where he stood, and began his descent down the aisle, pushing his dark glasses onto his tear-filled and begins his journey back home. He gives a slight bow to those who had paved the way he now walks proudly. He offers a knowing smile to those for whom he is treading new ground, and a final farewell to the man who had not only survived the swarming ants, but had grown stronger as a result.
