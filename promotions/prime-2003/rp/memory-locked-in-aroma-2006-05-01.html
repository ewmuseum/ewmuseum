Memory Locked In Aroma

1 May 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 5/1/06 I walk through the door, my door, and I see April sitting there. My first thought is, the month is over, why the fuck is she still in my apartment? The artificial light makes her face shine and violently pulls at the sleeping bags under her eyes. She looks worn out; that reminds me, I need a new pair of DKNY sneakers next time I send a Chan-bassador to Nordstrom’s. But back in May, April is causing bigger problems than just not looking up to the standards of my kitchen table. “What the fuck were you doing in here?” She looks up, finally noticing my presence. She doesn’t try to hide anything and she knows I know what she was doing. She’s that girl; when you try and tell her she’s doing something wrong, it always becomes a raging guilt trip. Don’t get me started on the way she blows up the Sidekick with horrible text messages about the latest episode of 8th and Ocean (anyone who’s anyone knows that I TiVo it because I’m rarely home on Tuesday night and…you know what, don’t get me started on this either). “Oh c’mon, Channy, I think you know what I was doing.” “It’s not fucking cute.” She doesn’t take the hint. I get the eyes leaking with self-pity and temptation. To her, a relationship is like a big game in the first place; she’s with a guy until he figures out how to turn down her womanly guiles. And that’s not nineteenth century Southern mentality about womanly guiles–she could con a well into buying water. Her laugh creeps out, barely audible but sliding quickly right under my fingernails. Just that little giggle, that “I haven’t ever held a real job in my life and I still drive a red Range Rover, you inferior being” cackle is enough, on a bad night, to send me into green, shirt-ripping rage. “It’s not. Do you think it’s funny to make my house smell like a Phish concert?” I detest Phish. More pertinent, I hate the smell of weed and that’s exactly what she’s unleashed. Really, it’s more about the complete disregard for my shit than the actual foul smell. “Oh, don’t be such a narc.” I detest being called a narc by a nineteen year-old trust fund child. But I’ve said “get out” too many times in my life. It’s not worth the fight tonight. “Open some windows or something. I’m going to sleep.” She completely ignores me and pulls back out her Ziploc bag of sticky-icky. Like I said, it’s not even about the smell. It’s not about the weed. Even if it was…fuck, I don’t wanna talk about it; I gotta get some sleep so I can shut down Adam on Wednesday. * * * * * I felt strange, standing outside the rusty park where I saw little kids disappointedly spending their afternoons. Two childhood friends, Ben and Rory, licked gums and kept their eyes from me. Every couple seconds, Rory checked his cell phone. “Where are they? It’s getting cold out here.” Maybe my voice actually sounded nervous, but more likely they recognized my anxiety; it had been their own the previous week. Now, they were regular experts. “It’s not cold, dude. Chill out and this will be much more enjoyable,” Ben cooed. I could handle the condescending from Rory; he was barely passing freshman year, but he already knew he was going to be a musician. Seeing Ben try to coax me into toking up with them was more frightening. “Yeah, well it is late. If Ryan’s not gonna be here soon, I’ve gotta get home.” This was another lie; my mother was merely happened that I hadn’t joined up with the other Vietnamese kids in our most American of creations: the street gang. They had colors and occasionally they jumped a twinkie they thought was straying too far from his roots, but other than that they weren’t bad. They were probably the type of kids who waited outside parks to smoke blunts. I felt an intense urge to pee, but now I’d gotten myself stuck. I couldn’t actually go piss because it would look like I was pussying out. So I waited. Rory gave me a primer on the fat black kid we were about to meet; I knew him from school and that he played JV football, but had no further clue on why he was our connection into the drug world. “By the way, don’t call him Ryan. Only his aunt calls him Ryan. Everyone else just calls him DeCruz.”I thought about asking why, to combat Rory’s high-handed tone, but I decided against it, with no real reason to start a fight about nothing tonight. After sitting down, then standing back up because I had to piss so bad, Ben shouted that he saw the only recreational drug user we knew strutting down the street. In his words, he’d been off eating his second dinner. It’s now time to mention that Ryan was incredibly fat; because of this, he seemed much nicer than he actually was. “I just gotta wait on my cousin and his friend.” Now, I know what you’re thinking; this is one of those stories where you have to meet like forty different characters and each has their own bland back-story that I’ll use to fill space. Not so; Ben was Asian though, I don’t think I said that. He was supposed to have glasses, but he never wore them, so his eyes actually were very slanted. I hated him for it. The cousin and friend, known by the aliases Justin and Joe (because those were their names) arrived soon after. Justin was a light-skinned Hispanic kid and Joe was black like potted soil. As we walked from the park towards my old Little League baseball field and the shiny, lonely bridge, Justin rolled the blunt. The rolling paper was the color of oak and reminded me of the paper bags at the grocery store where my mom wanted me to be a bagger the next summer. We finally got to Point Park; it overlooked the rest of the city and was the token location for tokin’. When you were in elementary school, you heard stories about how people did drugs in the park, went crazy, and killed people, but in the stories they only killed other crazy drug people from the park. You were insanely cool if you had walked through the park at night. “Aright, it’s rolled. Who wants the first hit?” Rory gave his cool laugh and put out his hand. Justin took a second to light the poor-quality buds and the lighter was reluctant to give life to a drug habit for this particular group of fourteen year-olds. “Big dogs inhale,” he spat. His voice wasn’t even low yet and he thought he could say cool shit like that? What an idiot. He pulled the misshapen cylinder to his mouth and sucked in for a long time. Finally, he expelled smoke through his smoke, his nostrils flaring and his crooked upper teeth showing. The blunt made a full round of the circle before it got to me. At least Rory and Ben hadn’t put me on the spot for being a virgin to the prick of cannabis. I copied what I’d seen all of them do and sucked the tangy air into my lungs, albeit for a shorter period than any of them. I didn’t cough, but it seemed like my lungs wouldn’t deflate. I took two more hits as we giggled and stayed relatively silent. At one point, a group of college girls walked along, all dolled up for a night out. Joe nudged Ryan with his elbow, then called, since he had the lowest voice. “You ladies blaze?” I wanted to disappear. Surely, one of them was an undercover cop and we were going to juvie and…they disappeared over the ridge, one of them mumbling about kids or how she wished she had some weed herself, I don’t really recall. “Yo, you know that kid from State who got his ass beat,” asked Justin, taking away my cozy silence. We all nodded, perhaps afraid that our voices would be warped forever from our law-breaking. “Yo, I did that.” Yes, he was that kid who prefaced everything with “yo.” What can I say, it was the late 80’s. Everyone looked at Joe, but as usual he had nothing to add. “I don’t believe you; that kid had an afro,” I almost vomited. The smart-alecky response was part of my very being, but I wanted to disappear. He merely smiled at me, twirling one of his long stringy braids in his finger. “I did,” he said flatly. “I got braids because the cops were looking for a kid with a picked-out ‘fro.” It made sense enough to me, but this teen in front of me didn’t seem to be what he claimed. The video had made the local news rounds. Some unknown assailant had thwacked a random art student on a street corner with a haymaker. A video camera had captured the entire thing. It was eerie to watch the puny white kid crumple unknowingly as the fist met the back of his head. “Why?” It was a valid question. For a second, I thought he would punch me right in the face. Justin and Joe could probably take the three of us, and that was only if DeCruz stayed neutral. I was a pencil, Ben was uncoordinated, and Rory was athletic, but stubby. Joe already looked like an NFL player; it would’ve been a no contest. “Someone dared me to.” He smiled again, but I was wary of this smile, like when the villain in a movie smiles because he’s just trapped the unwitting hero. After my introduction to weed, we went to a burger joint and all pretended to have the munchies. The waiter must’ve thought we were mentally disabled. Later that summer, the police showed up at Justin’s house to ask some questions. They didn’t make any arrests, but he was spooked. Not knowing what else to do, he showed up at my house and asked some questions…with his fist. In front of Ben, Justin beat the ever-loving shit out of me. I hear he would’ve come back the next day to do it again, but one of the Vietnamese gang members told him they didn’t want wetbacks in their neighborhood. Not long after, I swore off the green stuff forever.
