Date: Lots He would later tell her it was the most beautiful sight he'd ever laid eyes on, but that's just because he's a guy and she was naked. She had hair like spun midnight back then, and her face was narrow, sort of drawn. He had no idea how much time she spent in front of the tiny make-up mirror in her hovel of an apartment to cover up the bags under her eyes, but, then, he probably wouldn't have cared. He'd seen her on the curbside, in a purple latex skirt that left little of her pert derriere to the imagination. Her fishnet stockings had been black, if he remembered right, and they peeked out over the tops of her velvet boots. Add in a lacy purple-and-black bra and a light cover-up to protect her from the chill Bay Area evening, and one could see why he'd picked the woman up. But all of those clothes were in a rumpled pile, strewn around his mini-suite (how her stocking -- the left one, he was almost sure -- had gotten up into the light fixture, the man wasn't sure). She wasn't even breathing hard above him, braced down on long-nailed hands, a soft smile held tight to her face that only part of him would admit was plastic. The matching rhythm of their bodies built to its inevitable, thunderous crescendo, and his hips arched upwards one last time with a low grunt. She came down crashing, lying across his torso, fingernails lightly tracing across his chest. "That was great," she murmured -- it was a lie she'd told many, many times before. He picked a stray strand of dark hair off her forehead tenderly. As the blood flowed back into his brain, he knew what the statement was intended to do (raise his confidence, make him come back, make sure he paid), but he still smiled. "You don't have to say things like that, Siouxsie," he said. She'd told him her name -- even directly referenced Siouxsie and the Banshees. The Asian girl slid up to a seated, putting her back to him as she stretched out for her discarded skirt. "Don't pretend this is more than it is," she cautioned, scooching her hips off the edge of the bed to get the tight garment on; she hadn't bothered with underwear before and wouldn't now. He rolled onto his side, watching her get dressed with a small smile. "Must you leave already?" he asked softly. "Your time ran out a half-hour ago. I'm only still here out of the goodness of my heart." "I can pay for more of your time, if that's all it would take." Andrew McFarland slid to his feet, padding to the bathroom. "The firm gave me quite an impressive Christmas bon--" A heavy-fisted banging at the door to his room attracted Andrew's attention; his thin eyebrows (part of a face that, even then, would be classified more as "pretty" than as "handsome") nearly climbing off his forehead. Only then choosing to slip on his silk boxers (it might be housekeeping, after all, and he couldn't afford to get kicked out of another hotel), McFarland stepped to the door, twisting the security lock and yanking it open carelessly. As he pulled, Siouxsie turned away -- she knew what was coming, after all, and something about the bloody geyser the human nose became when punched hard enough never failed to unsettle her. A thick, gold class ring led a veritable fort of a man, all leathered skin and bad jewelry, into the room. The ring -- and the gnarled fist it was attached to -- flew towards Andrew McFarland's face. Instinctively, he ducked (skittered, really) to the side. Andy pistoned a pair of sharp blows to the man's kidneys (well, kidney area -- he wasn't an experienced enough fighter to pinpoint the organs exactly), stepped on the back of his attacker's left knee and slammed the guy's face into the doorframe. 'Come on, Siouxsie! Let's go!" McFarland barked. What was she going to do? Tell him that Sal was there because he'd followed them? Because she'd led him there? Fuck that -- the john had beaten down Sal (albeit starting with a sucker punch) and it's not like she gave a shit, anyway. Siouxsie grabbed her clutch and she ran right alongside Andrew. And that was how the man who would come to be known as Andreas van der Wal first met the woman who would come to be known as Siena van der Wal. Not exactly "Pretty Woman". * * * * * "Shall we play a game?" The spindly redhead cracked the knuckles of his long-fingered hands as he slid his high-backed, leather computer chair behind the long, charcoal-gray desk. His name was Cambrian Cambridge, and what he may have been lacking in morals or ethics or loyalty, the man more than made up for in sheer brainpower. Cambrian (who resembled nothing so much as Beaker from the Muppets) had only one task for his employer, but it was an important one. You see, Cambrian served as gatekeeper for the information storehouse of one Alexandra Eloise Pierce. This meant a number of things -- notably, that he lived a very frustrating life, as a goodly amount of what he was tasked with protecting was information he wasn't privy to. But it also meant that, if the woman they called "Desade" was, in fact, the Spider in the Web, and her boots on the ground were her Strands, people like Cambrian were her Spinnerets. (Admittedly, Cambridge wasn't thrilled with the feminine connotations of that term.) He was one of the people who spun out the silk that Pierce plucked like the strings of a harp to play her..."music" implies something far more beautiful than the games Alexandra perpetrated. Take, for example, the Ninth Precinct of the San Francisco Police Department. The Fightin' Ninth was an altogether boring tract of land in the downtown corridor of the City by the Bay. It wasn't like people already on trial committed many crimes (though you'd be surprised -- there's something to be said for the "nothing left to lose" factor), and most of the real estate under the Ninth's purview was the area immediately surrounding the U.S. District Courthouse. Almost two years ago, now, an ambulance carrying one of the jurors from the James Kennesaw Rodgers trial disappeared from near the court steps. The day before was the last time any of his colleagues saw the promising second chair for Rodgers' defense team. Those two cases were among the two dozen or so in the precinct's cold case database, and, though no records would show it, the servers containing the database had some unscheduled maintenance work done about eighteen months ago. Later investigation into this work would prove remarkably fruitless -- the company that performed the routine check-up went belly-up fifteen and a half months ago, and was owned by a holding corporation based out of Barbados. Hacking is not as easy as Hollywood makes it out to be; it's not a matter of a few keystrokes to crack into a police database, even for the expert fingers behind the incursion on the 21st of June. In truth, these sorts of things work best on-site, but a trip to the precinct house would have involved much more work than the intruder was willing to spend (not to mention it'd be more time, and the first rule of snooping around about people is Don't Let Them Know You're Snooping). The route into the mainframe was a circuitous one; hitch a ride on a Trojan Horse one of the desk sergeants downloaded by accident as part of a cute cartoon (if most people knew how many of hitchhikers were part of their computer, they would shit bricks). From there, port the signal over to the evidence locker database, which shares its housing in the same server. It wasn't until the intruder had already brushed the electronic tripwire that she would have even noticed it was there -- it was far more technologically advanced than anything the SFPD had to offer, not to mention that it wasn't warning the sys-admin for the police, but was transmitting an encrypted signal elsewhere. It was mere seconds later that the wi-fi signal from the chip was cut off, but the intruder knew it would warn someone, and that made the search through the McFarland file much more of a cursory search than it should've been otherwise. Half a continent away, a series of relays tripped a red alarm in front of a charcoal gray desk, which caused the wiry redhead named Cambrian to check the network. "San Francisco, eh?" the Brit asked no one in particular. "Let's go for a ride, shall we, hrm?" Many information-rippers are just focused on the get-in, the get-out, and (especially) the get-paid. It takes an experienced eye to be able to take in one's surroundings, find the files you want copied, and keep track of what's going on around you. But it just takes a slight nibble for an alert intruder to notice their motions (phantom mouse-clicks, files searched, etc.) are being recorded. Cambrian's long, hangdog face crumpled into a nest of lines as his snooper program suffered a fatal error, blipping its data-mining off his screen. His fingers rocketed through his response, banging out their very own rhythm on the drums of his keys. "Take that, you nosy little git." The virus was one of his own design, a recursive, corrupting program designed to whittle another program down like someone cut a block into a pretty statuette. Cambrian adjusted his thin glasses as he watched the program's output responses, tracking the readout with narrowed brown eyes. Something was wrong. Something was wrong. Something was... "FRAK!" he shouted. The bug in the system disappeared. Ghosted from anywhere he could see it. It could have retreated or it could have masked itself as a negligible file on the mainframe. But he couldn't detect it at all. It was a bad case/worse case scenario. Bad case: his little friend got what they were looking for, dug up the SFPD's work on the McFarland and Howard disappearances, and would be able to use them to piece together answers to questions the police never thought to ask. Worst case: if the intruder managed to isolate the virus, Cambrian had coded it himself, and, just like a signature with a pen, a coder's signature can be found in the binary 1's and 0's of any program they put by hand. Which means an inexperienced hand would recognize anything else that Cambridge had coded. An experienced hand... Cambrian looked to the black phone sitting next to his computer, but he didn't call his client. No. Not yet. He wouldn't tell Alexandra Pierce he failed any more than Eddie Izzard would tell people at school he was a transvestite. He thought she'd kill him with sticks. It wasn't exactly the game that Cambrian had in mind. * * * * * Dear Diary: Andy took me to the zoo today. It was like Madagascar, but none of the animals would talk to me. But it was hella exciting. Cozie * * * * * Salvatore Fennucci was just about to sit down for dinner at his favorite restaurant (for pasta alfredo and shrimp scampi, no less) when the house phone rang. He knew without looking that the call would be about his boss -- so many of them were these days. He was still waiting for a call from the cops or the cemetery. Claudio, the manager, took the call (Sal screened all of his business calls this way -- goddamn telemarketers and fuckhead collectors called all the damn time). When the thin, balding man nodded, Salvatore lifted the cloth napkin from his lap to his lips and dabbed gently. "Who is it, Claudio?" Fennucci asked. "Says her name is Elisabeth Pierotti." "Name doesn't ring a bell." "Uhm, she says she was friends with the Don. And she says she remembers Lou Angotti." The burgundy cloth fell from Fennucci's fingertips. "Give me the phone." The restaurant manager waddled over to Sal's corner booth, passed him the cordless, and scurried away. All of his employees would mysteriously find something else to do for the next few minutes. "Salvatore Fennucci," he said. "Good evening, Mr. Fennucci." The woman at the other end of the line had a voice that could freeze the blood in your veins, even when miles away and pouring (mostly false) sincerity into her words. Perhaps especially then. "You have proven to be a very difficult man to track down." "Yeah. Been, uh... we been busy." Fuck, why was this broad making him so damn uncomfortable? "So I have heard. Your increased workload is part of the reason for my call." The quiet words held Salvatore's attention. "You are likely unaware, but my organization have been longtime supporters of your family. It is an association I have let slip since the tragic passing of those who spearheaded its inception, and one I would most like to renew. With certain provisions, and only with the right people." Fennucci blinked, unsure what to make of the offer. "Just what are you saying here, lady? Which 'people' are we talking about?" "My list currently begins and ends with just two names, alas." She did not sound regretful. "Can we meet, so you might give me other suggestions?" Salvatore Fennucci was suddenly consumed with thoughts of an old poem. "Well, shit. He's...boss is already on his way to St. Louis for some, ah, business." Sal didn't want to say his boss' name, though this Pierotti babe probably knew. "He wouldn't be able to attend until later on in the week. Maybe we could reschedule." He could hear the frozen smile in the woman's words. "Nonsense," she said airily -- like a blast of icy air blowing away his excuses. "I would really like to get this completed as soon as we can; even though my own form of that business has recently left Memphis -- where I believe Mr. Gambillini would be due next." Shit, she did know. "Neither the Grin nor I are ever likely to be the fish that swim with the school, however, and I did go through quite a bit of effort to learn where you are." The next day, when Salvatore Fennucci returned home, he would secretly be glad to be alive. He'd just be glad about other things more. But still, that line would nag at him for days.
