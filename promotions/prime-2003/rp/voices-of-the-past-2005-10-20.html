Voices Of The Past

20 October 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 20/10/05 In 1803…we sailed out to sea… A man rises from his slumber deep in the bowels of his ship and sighs. He slept despite his arms being stretched out in each direction from the chains adorning his wrists. He sighs now because as he slept he dreamt of the frontier of his home, picturing the beauty and magnitude of the mountains that stood proudly amidst the blanket of trees, swarming at their feet. He remembered the snaking rivers and overlooking plateaus, the deep valleys and snowy mountaintops, all untouched by man. He remembered even the smallest insects all of which contributed to making his land the most perfect land on earth. He reminisced of his family, his wife and two children and how the time he spent with them. How he led his wife into a clearing deep into McGregor’s woods on the day they were married and how he made love to her. Then years later, depicting every little detail of the same wood to his offspring in the hope they would grow to appreciate their country like every good Irishman. He had loved his country and he had fought for his country. But now as he looks around at the tormented faces of his fellow comrades in the prison deck of ‘The Pioneer’, he realises he has paid for his country with the most terrible price. He’s almost lost count of the time since he bid farewell to the soil of his town while deep below deck he was lying. Padraig looks to his right and sees a man sobbing across from him. This hasn’t been unusual in the 68 days they have travelled, men even begging their captors to cut their throats. Disease has taken many of them, probably between forty and forty five so far with no signs of letting up. His friend Owen died not two days ago right beside him, he even heard the splash as the body was tossed overboard penetrating the water. It was the disease that got him too. For Australia bound, if we didn’t all drowned It had been three days since he had seen sunlight now, the weather they said, was ripping their ship apart. Even when they were on the deck, the sun seemed to burn cruelly onto their skin. Maybe their minds were being warped by each and every blow they received but something told them otherwise. Some even swore it was the English who gave Dan O’Connor the fever that set the chain off. Their deep seated hatred for their wardens didn’t do much to dispel the myth of this particular theory. Botany Bay is what they would see now in twenty days or so; if any of them made it, already Padraig was feeling the warm chill tickle his bones. Even if they got there, all they had to look forward to was the successive day of a twenty year stretch, the likelihood of him surviving the abysmal conditions there minuet. But despite this, it was his son Padraig was feeling for right now. His wife and daughter were beaten to death on the day the landlord’s men cast them from their humble settlement, land that had belonged to his clan before the war had reached Donegal. Now his son would have to stand alone with only the principles Padraig had taught him to guide him through his life. Potato blight was on and off this decade, could his seventeen year old son really live through another onslaught of hunger? Padraig sighed again and closed his eyes, always trying to forget the fact that he sits in two inches of seawater. Five years he had fought for the United Irishmen to rid his country of absolutism and tyranny. Five wasted years it would seem because in that space of time, Ireland has lost her parliament and he himself now faces slavery. He was sucked in by their ideals and aims and the heroics of Wolfe Tone, then spat back out into chains. There will be no second chances now, no compassion for the man who didn’t know what he was getting himself into. This is 19th Century Ireland where escaping the noose for his treachery was seen as a blessing. Well it was 19th century Ireland, at least 68 days ago. A stiff kick to the ribs awakens Padraig from his day dreaming and he howls momentarily in pain. “No sleeping you lazy swine.” Padraig looks up with unequivocal distaste and sees an English soldier, wearing his red coat proudly amongst the ragged captives. His face is completely expressionless and devoid of any emotion but Paddy immediately assumes he’ll have a private chuckle later. Now the man smashes the back of his hand across Padraig’s cheek, and he immediately feels a warm trickle meander down his face from a small cut opened by the lieutenant’s ring. “You WILL look at me with respect prisoner.” Instead of look up again, Padraig drops his gaze because he can’t trust himself to keep the naked rage from his face. Instead he licks at the blood that is gathering now at the corner of his mouth, tasting the flavourless liquid methodically. The soldier passes on, seemingly satisfied with his minor triumph and then, mercifully, out of sight. As the mainsails unfurled, our curses we hurled, at the English and thoughts of tomorrow… He can deal with men like him; he can take their swipes and jabs, on top of their torrents of verbal abuse. He can just about accept the fact his friends are dying all around him for crimes they only did out of the desperation to live. He can let other issues slide, like the fact he will spend twenty years in jail, the fact he most likely won’t breathe the air of free men again, the notion that he will become nothing more than a slave for those earls and barons who have already robbed him of so much. But in the last week, the realisation that the pure and direct line of the O’Shea’s is almost certainly coming to an end. After all, what exactly are the chances of a seventeen year old boy, not yet fully equipped and near knowledgeable enough, sustaining his life despite being on the brink of homelessness again with no work and therefore no income and few prospects. He has faith in his son; he always has and admired his maturity and general outlook but rationality balances out this hope. So now he’s simply left in the lurch as to whether his son will live or die and the cruel fact is he will never really know the outcome. He must be strong and proud, resilient and clear thinking. It’ll be tough yes but he won’t be alone, the ghosts of his family will forever walk behind him, for that’s one thing the English will never take from him - his memories… Padraig looks up through those tortured eyes one last time as he feels drowsiness overtaking him, what joy is in eyesight when there’s sorrow in sunshine, he asks himself. Eyes that will go on to the raw climate of Van Demon’s land where the gun is the law. Padraig thinks one more time for his son and the words he poetically cooed before he left…A rebel I came and I’m still the same. No regrets. Just hope now. God please….bless and deliver my son. Not for me…for the name of O’Shea. … This is your history ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~ This is your history Paddy darts upwards into a sitting position from his bed, a cold sweat drenching the white linen sheets he lies in. He was having a strange dream of a boat and a prisoner though as his half sleeping mind comes alive, the faint echo of a memory fades into the background. Already he is forgetting the command that rang in his ears to wake him up. History it was…or something…ah forget it…then he let it go to bury its head in the recesses of his sub-conscious. O’Shea kicked the blankets away from him in frustration. It was too early, his lead heavy eyelids told him so but he couldn’t get back to sleep now, he never was the type who could. Instead he rose to his feet and stretched, a moan of satisfaction escaping his lips. He rubs his eyes, getting rid of the last traces of sleep then pulls on a pair of faded jeans lying crumpled on the floor followed by other various articles of clothing. He can’t let petty dreams break his concentration now, not after Revolution. All week he’d been wrestling with his mind to accept the fact that he was the one that was pinned, a task much easier said than done. He didn’t take losses well, in fact he took them extremely personally. And even the notion of it being that weasel Adam Dick that covered him just pissed him off even more. Petty yes but when beating the hell out of a man and hearing the crowds cheer your name in admiration is your only perk in life, you fight for each and every opportunity So now it’s back to the drawing board as they say, a tag match involving the same assailant who robbed him last week now on the opposing team. So far this week, it had been training everyday. Paddy wasn’t exactly the most committed man as far as working out went but he was logical, and enough to know that a psychological weight was upon his back, weighing him down. Only getting fully prepared in any all other possible ways could really give him a fighting chance. After a quick snack and a few loose ends cleared up such as packing his training bag and locking doors, he was off, headed towards the local wrestling gym. O’Shea walked on a pavement, outlining one side of a curving seashore, the sand dunes there rising and sinking from the ground. It was a pleasant day for late October, dry and faintly warm yet a cool breeze was present, refreshing the pores of his face. He had neglected to shave but what else was new, and his hair was a mess but who was about to see? Hell, it was probably before eight o’clock even now. As O’Shea walked the half mile journey to his destination, he outlined various strategies in his head, highlighting the importance of caution and even a touch of technicality that had been alien to him up until a few months ago. The defeat had been a blow to him after all who wants to be holding the shortest straw on his debut? But he had always bounced back from things like this before, he was an endurance man and he would go the distance. Paddy turned left now as the sand turned to stone into road and as houses begin to spring up and before long, he was dandering through the early morning atmosphere of his village, taking in the sights that were offered to him. He was surprised to see one thing though and that was an old man, out walking with undoubtedly his grandson for such an early hour. He didn’t know the man but he thought he knew the face from one place or another. The scene was touching, the boy being no more than six requiring his grandpapa to hold his hand as he skipped along in red dungarees. The old man, raised his face and smiled warmly at Paddy as he walked past and Paddy returned the welcome with a nod. There really wasn’t anything special or specific about what happened there but for some reason now, Paddy’s own Grandfather came to mind. Paddy’s grandfather owned and to his knowledge still does own land in the region of Donegal, the place where his family hails from. He used to be very close to his grandfather, so close in fact that around five years ago, he wrote his will giving everything to him upon his death ahead of his wife and only son, which is of course Paddy’s father. Why did Eoin O’Shea neglect the idea that his wife and son may have desired a share in his land? Well, Eoin’s wife was had fought off cancer twice already and he never expected her to outlive him so that idea was cancelled out. But then there was his son. The two had never really got on together seeing as Eoin was a man of authority around his parts while his wayward son came across as an aspiring alcoholic and general rogue and bane. Then one day, following a heated dispute over Danny’s choice to reject university, Eoin threw him out into the cold. Without a shelter over his head, Danny went southwards and came to Galway and in exchange for free labour for a substantial period of time, he was given a caravan and just enough money to get him by. Years later, when Danny had become a father and watched his children grow into adults; his father sent a message, begging him that even if those two could not get along, that he would not deprive him of the need to be a grandfather. Danny was wary but then the idea of an inheritance swayed him and he sent his eldest son Paddy to live with him for a few months. Those months turned to years before he knew it. The time Paddy spent with his grandfather over those three years was joyful and all the while Danny stood in the background calculating his gain. Maybe Eoin was wrong to have allowed his love to have skipped a generation or maybe Danny’s greed made this the only fair conclusion, but one day in 1999, as Paddy was digging on a nearby field, his grandfather came to him and offered him everything. There was one string however…disown Danny. Paddy was stunned at the time. He knew his father and grandfather didn’t get along, even knew the reasons behind it, but had his grandpapa really conserved so much anger over the years to make such an offer viable? Paddy had trouble with his father himself and in his youth had felt his knuckles rattle his cheeks when he was intoxicated but could he really forsake him like that? Even if he said yes, would it even be possible? At the time, Paddy said yes and Eoin, in some triumphant blaze of glory had organised a party that only seemed to deeply offend the young Patrick. The party was staged on a stormy night in mid October. Built in a scoop of the hills, the great O’Shea residence stood proudly amidst the walls of rock that surrounded it. But the walls of that house was being tested undoubtedly – the west winds were peeling at them and sodden sacks had to be deployed at the front door to prevent rain seeping through and into the hallway. The night was difficult yes but it didn’t stop the locals arriving, basically peasants who were long overdue a good time by their wealthy neighbour. Inside the home was a great hall, the lights were blazing and the glass befogged from the breath and sweat of the people dancing joyfully inside. The little village seemed isolated from the real world, not yet ready to move onto modern times. For on a table at the back of the hall was three tapped half-barrels of porter, and the clothes these people wore could only be described as rural. A fiddler and melodeon player sat behind the table, playing a merry jig that lifted all people in the hall from their feet (though the alcohol had to take some of the credit). It was nearly midnight and the harsh crash of feet on the tired wooden floor rang throughout the room above and beyond the music. The young Paddy O’Shea stood with his back to the wall watching all events before him. Unlike his father, Paddy didn’t drink heavy, barely drank at all infact and so had the honour of being one of the only few sober people in the room. He might have been better off if he was however, as a variety of emotions boiled up inside of him. He looked across the room at his grandfather, a man as tall as he, his grey hair combed back neatly over a well shaped head. He was shouting and clapping and cheering and laughing. Being happy basically because he had replaced his miscreant son with another. And Paddy felt angry. A girl put a hand on his chest and asked him to dance but he looked down, remembered to smile and shook his head. His tongue felt dry in his mouth and he feared opening it to shout. His eyes flicked back to his grandfather, allowing the rage to build once more. He was finally seeing the true man he was. A vengeful, assuming and needlessly cruel individual. Paddy squeezed his hands behind his back and then looked at them – the nails had made deep indentations in his large coarse hand. He took once more glance over at the laughing Eoin, still entertaining the crowd surrounding him, then pushed himself away from the wall, having to force his way out towards the door. The faces of all the people he should have known were blurred now so he only bluffed as he answered their drowned out questions with a cheerful yet forced yes to everything. He eventually broke into the porch and stepped out into the rain. The weather wasn’t for the faint hearted, at all times he expected one of the sheep to be washed past. The white shirt he wore and black trousers were soaked through in second. He knew what he was about to do would crush Eoin but he’d left him no choice. This was…too far. He reconsidered for a while and then walked over to the window into the wall, seeing vague images through the blurred windows yet pulled away when he couldn’t locate his grandfather. He walked back to the porch and went back in, walking past a few young men who were using the walls to hold up their drunken bodies. He tried the door to his grandfathers’ bedroom. It was open. He went in and opened a small drawer on a bedside locker and removed a red sock that stuck out like a sore thumb. He put his hand inside it and removed a wad of notes, then stuck them in his pocket. The thought of stealing didn’t enter his mind; his sudden hatred towards his father wouldn’t let him feel guilt. He hurried out of the room that reminded of him of stale spices, walking past the same drunken men who again didn’t notice him and then stepped out into the rain again. No, there wouldn’t be any reconsidering, so he pulled his collar up and grunted ruefully at its ineffectiveness. Motor vehicles of any kind were rare in this secluded village and so the huge number of bicycles lined up against the garden wall was far from surprising. He pulled the nearest bike up, freed it from its neighbour then wheeled it out onto the road. He stopped for a second, his eyes going back to the lights of the hall where Eoin undoubtedly sat still laughing and drinking. He sneered at the old fools’ presumption as he mounted the bike and allowed it to cycle freely down the path, the heavy gale at his back. The road was littered with potholes and he bobbed up and down as he flew along, the dirt, gritty from the rain, splashing at his ankles. For the first time that night, he felt free, powering down the winding road for now he was truly sovereign. He could shove his money and his land, no possession was worth the chains of bondage he had intended to strap around his wrists. He would come to stop at the railway station and leave the bicycle there. He was running away yes, and he felt good about it, no guilt and no regrets. He was his own man and his fathers son. Paddy ran back into the welcoming arms of his father and life carried on as it should have. His anger subsided and sadness at the loss of his grandfather eventually surfaced but he pushed it away for as long as he could. … Paddy walked past the old man with a sigh, the hurt caused by his actions becoming known to him. His grandfather must have been furious after that night for he didn’t hear from him again. And for a long time that was fine, until he slowly realised the cycle his life was trapped in was forever going to continue. Paddy wasn’t paying attention when he walked around another corner, a junction created by a house and he didn’t realise a brick was lying on the ground until he tripped over it. He fell hard and his head seemed to bounce off the ground. Paddy. I need you A voice had appeared out of nowhere in his head and his eyes opened wide despite him lying on the floor. He had heard it alright. The voice of his grandfather. Maybe you would say he imagined it after the dream he had followed by the sight of the old man a few metres back but the clearness of the message and unmistakeable voice told him otherwise. Paddy began getting up and felt an arm grab him around his waist, gently helping him to his feet. “Are ye okay son? Are ye bleedin’?” O’Shea turned around dazed once he was standing and focused in on the old man’s face, wrinkled yet kind. No not his grandfather but close he thought sluggishly. “Aye’m…fine…thank ye.” “Do ye need an-“ “No, no…don’ worry about it aye’ll be fine.” Paddy, behaving like he was retarded, straightened himself out then walked past the man quickly, back in the direction he had come. The old man would go on to tell his nosy neighbours about the young O’Shea boy who banged his head and then ran off seemingly nowhere. They would go on to say about how that boy was just ready to go mad like his father, and God in heaven please help that young man but give don’t forget to give me more gossip because I’m a nosy bitch. It didn’t matter to Paddy, he wouldn’t see them for a while if ever. Right now, there was only one thing on his mind as his walk broke into a jog then a run…his grandfather was in trouble, and he get to him. It may have looked like he was running away again but you would be wrong, he was only coming back. Three hours later, Paddy O’Shea was boarding an airplane destined for Donegal…O’Shea country.
