Obi is the King of Endings (GBJ's incomplete symphony part 1 of most likely 1)

undated
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: today There are places you go, sometimes, to punish yourself for becoming what you have become when your mind and body are unclean and you’ve smoked yourself into a very bright, introspective place that reminds you of how you were once a child with the potential still to grow into something lovelier and less damaged. I had smoked myself into that place, hoping to make repayment on that borrowed, wasted time; looking for a deeper sense of self to strike a candlelit hole in the dim griminess of everything I now embodied. I felt that I owed it, at least, to the child I had betrayed. That child had died as a result of my own negligence to drag with me his spirit, and I had thoroughly stifled the opportunities he had sown in him self with waterless malnourishment. His sandy hair, year by year, tinted brown with greases and oils and bearded ivy crawled over his face untended. Soon, not a shade of the child was visible through the forested surface that shadowed over his burnt and cracked and buried, outgrown shell. Instead, it sunk, uncontained, into the digestively serpentine bedding of yawning time. I treaded onward to this crowded place, where mourners gathered and immolated themselves in the most humiliating of ways; stripped naked with an ugliness that could only be scrubbed with the coarsest of materials as skin had grown again over the now embedded filth as it spread infectiously across a flat sheet between two internally mummifying dermises. I was amidst a sack dragging flock of others who had grown up to compromise their morals or abandon them completely. My peers arrived in all sorts, some much dirtier, uglier, or older than me. Others were groomed in buttoned costumes that shielded the shameful, writhing emptiness clawing at the guts. But we were all dragging sacks that represented the burdensome skeletons that hung unresolved behind curtains of nary used seasonal wear. Literally, the sacks were filled with sod or rock salt or something to give them weight. Mine was purposely filled with refuse. I had resolved to live in a bin of garbage for some indeterminable time as a practitioner of alternative medicines had advised that this would fix me right up. I was never one to question the judgment of a practitioner, and this would offer no exception. The throng, at parts, dissolved chaotically without organization into separate lines that trailed off towards different areas depending on the character of guilt contented in their bags. I too broke off into a particular line, herded forth with my dirty, haggard fellows as we beaded out across the clearing. We all walked on for quite some time until the original, unthreading crowd was at a distance so great behind us that we could no longer see it. It was then that we had all arrived at a sprawling space with garbage cans prearranged in a tight grid. We each selected vacant cans, typically by convenience of vicinity though some of the more superstitious journeymen selected their cans with other, more meandering methods. There were no differences in the cans, so I selected one that looked suitable enough and climbed in because it was so near to me. As soon as I had climbed in, however, a man approached me frantically, waving his arms in sprint or pulling his flapping beard in locks out of his line of sight. He stopped, clutching two armfuls of entangled beard to his chest. He was out of breath, and his voice was otherwise faint, but I understood he wanted the can that I had chosen. “I need this one. It’s in my lucky row.” “Can’t you stand in the next can?” I asked, motioning to a can beside me that was uninhabited. “You stand in that can! I want this one!” “But I’m already in this one,” I fended him off. I had already taken a disliking to this gentleman though, and didn’t want him standing in any subsequent can. Besides that, he was prepared to get physical over the can selection. He raised his arms at my throat, spilling his fluff of beard over his unclothed belly. And he was wearing this thing with these very tiny sleeves that made me more uncomfortable with being physically contacted. I decided that it was best to cut my losses and give up the can. I searched for another one further off, and he swore at me as he climbed into the can as he was insulted that I had chosen not to occupy the can beside him. I was able to find another can a few lengths down that was just as suitable for my needs, and I climbed in. I pulled the bag up by the sides of the opening and loosened the drawstrings before dumping the contents down my chest and into the can with me. I was buried to the waste and bent at the knees, sifting through the rubbish into a manageable squat so that the can was full to my shoulders and my head peeked out unprotected over the top. Some journeymen around me were already insulated into similar positions as we all waited for them to send the children in to swat at us with mallets. It was, in essence, little more than a strange carnival game to them, but to us, it was supposed to be some kind of religious experience of self-reinvention. Collectively, nerves were settling as the journeymen accommodated to their cans, and an optimistic apprehension swept between the cans conversationally. People were talking so fast that it was hard to hear over the clicking assonance of chatty consonants. I could barely make out what the man in the next can was trying to tell me. “This is my ninth visit,” said the pilgrim between a checkerboard of teeth. “Is this your first time in the can?” “Yeah,” I replied back, nodding. “I hear it’s supposed to work.” I hadn’t actually heard any first hand testimonials, but I never expected that anyone would so avidly pursue such an endeavor eight previous times if there was no effect. “Maybe it does. Maybe it doesn’t,” he shrugged, but he pronounced his maybes like “mebbes.” “The kids, they come out and hit at you. It’ll hurt for a little bit, but then you’ll get used to it. That’s when your meat’s become tenderized.” “I’ll keep that in mind,” I noted. “Those children should be coming anytime now. They’ll blow a horn off before they open up the gates and let ‘em at us.” He nodded and then started mumbling tallies every time he swallowed a wad of his own building salivate. “How long does this usually last?” I asked, trying to distract him from his counting. It wasn’t the counting that irritated me so much as it was that I could hear when he swallowed, and I probably wouldn’t have noticed it if it wasn’t for that goddamned counting. “It depends,” he said. “One time I stayed in the can for one hundred seventeen minutes, and then another time it was a hundred and sixty three. Three hundred and twelve is my all time best. Really, you stay in there as long as you need to.” Leaving the can was something that troubled me. Nobody really knew when to leave. My guess was as good as his, and everyone was in fear of leaving too early or too late. “You don’t want to be the first one to leave,” he continued, “but you sure don’t want to be the last.” “What happens if you’re the last one to leave?” “I don’t know,” he said. “Nothing ever really happens when you leave, but it can make things very awkward if you leave when you’re not supposed to. It’s part of the whole experience, I guess. It isn’t the best part, by any means, but you’ve got to do it sometime.” Now the talk of leaving was beginning to make me uncomfortable. I feared that if it went on any longer, I would become preoccupied with the thought of leaving, and I would be too busy thinking about it to enjoy the ensuing festivities. I quickly changed the subject. “So why do you come here?” “I came here to wipe the grime off my weary soul. I’m a dirty man. I wallowed in my own filth for so long that my legs grew numb from wading and they had to be amputated.” The man lifted himself out of his can using his large arms. “All I’ve got now are these dangling fins.” The flaps that hung from his midsection looked like a deflated ass filled partially with liquid. As his arms started losing the strength required to hold his dripping thorax aloft, his bubbling globules twittered beneath him. “I reckon that’s why you’re here too?” he inquired. “More or less,” I replied, though all my limbs were still in tact. I didn’t feel right talking to the man anymore now that I had seen his saggy, flopping stumps, so I just decided to look straight ahead and listen for the horn. But then, he went back to the goddamned swallowing and the mumbled counting. He remembered what number he had left off at, too. It was seventy nine; seventy nine and not a penny less. I began talking aloud, not to anyone in particular. I would’ve talked to the man on my other side, but he was already shirtless inside his garbage can wearing swimming goggles and covering his chest in a muddy, fragrant oil. I didn’t want to disturb his chanting. It looked like he was getting in a rhythm. So, I was talking aloud to myself out of habit as I often voiced my frustrations most powerfully when there was nobody around to listen. “When’s the damn horn going to sound?” I thought, I heard someone answering behind me. It sounded like he had said, “The horn never blows when you’re listening for it,” but when I turned around it was just a very sad looking waif poking her head out of the trashcan while holding the willowing string of a deflated balloon. To her right, though, was a man who had shoved himself in the garbage can ass first so that his legs hung out over the edge of the can from bent knees, and he was always flailing his arms because the sides of the can chafed his arm pits which barely slugged over the can’s edges. Perhaps the voice had come from him, but he was just uttering grievances against the government in some foreign language. It sounded like a dirty French. As I looked around, all of these people just started to make me feel worse about myself because we were all sinking in garbage together. They all looked so unhappy, but they all had the same self-piteous look that tried to compel everyone else to feel bad for them. I wanted to knock them over in their cans, those damn cunts! Oh, it smelled like wet onions! The seething, irritable scent of wet onions everywhere! The wet, sweaty bowels of onions! It sickened me to near collapse. I wanted to get out of the can so damned bad. That horn was never going to blow. It was waiting for me to get out of my can before it blew so that it could leave me there exposed to the children and their mallets. It wanted me to leave the can so that I could look awkward and out of sorts doing so. I couldn’t take it, so I started screaming; just belting out at the top of my lungs, all the while, the bastard next to me had finished counting and started whistling as if nothing was going on. I kept screaming and screaming, but nobody so much as twitched at it until the man next to me calmly turned. “Whenever I start to get impatient, I tell myself a little story about a little boy who wanted to grow up to be an astronaut but grew up to be a drunk instead. It was 1969. I remember sitting in front of the TV set with my parents. My father was drinking, and my mother was cross stitching. Each one of them shared their hobby with me until my father caught me cross stitching. He put a stop to that- called it faggy- and my mother argued with him. She said that cross stitching would keep me from growing up a drunk like my father, and that made my father real angry. He hit her pretty hard around the eye area, and that put a stop to her arguing about cross stitching instilling values in a boy. Anyway, we were watching the television when the Apollo space shuttle landed up there on the moon. I saw the astronauts hopping around in there gleaming white suits, so bright against the backdrop of lightless space. I wanted to be like them- to float and be like those warriors of the night sky…those astronauts. You know what my father told me, though?” “No,” I replied after realizing the question wasn’t rhetorical. The lengthy pause and frozen stare were telltale clues. “He said I was too dumb to be an astronaut. He said that his daddy was a drunk and he was a drunk and he’d be damned if I turned out any better than he did. I tried to refute, but he wouldn’t have any of it because he’d fought in the war. He told me you couldn’t argue with a man who had fought in the war, and he blamed his being drunk on his father and the war. He had been a pilot and had won several medallions for shooting down Nazi jets. He loved the medallions and he loved killing Nazis and he loved to drink because of the war and he even drank sometimes to the war when his war vet friends would come by to drink with him.” He paused again for emphasis. He cleared his throat and regained his place in the story. “All they’d do was bicker over who killed the more Nazis in the war, and they’d always sworn they’d go down to South America and start a Nazi killing vigilante group. They said that all the Nazis were hiding out down there, anyhow; down there in Argentina. And then they’d fall asleep in their chairs around the kitchen table with half finished bottles and cards and chips still splashed about.” I was starting to get bored with his story. It wasn’t much of a story, but it was killing time, and I had a feeling that he was really just talking to entertain himself. “One time, I guess one of his war buddies had gotten in his ear, and while I was in the kitchen making sure that my father was face down so that he wouldn’t choke on his own vomit in his sleep, he woke up, still in a drunken stupor and roused from a deep state of sleep. He told me he was convinced my mother was a Nazi and that if I didn’t kill her, I was a Nazi sympathizer. He handed me one of the steak knives from the counter and told me that I’d better go cut out her Nazi heart. Then he fell back asleep in his chair with his head tilted back so that his long neck jutted out, and there I was, just a boy, holding a sharp knife while my father’s neck was spread out there so prettily.” “So you killed him?” I interrupted. “No, I didn’t have the guts to do it. I just put the knife back on the counter, and I was going to head back to sleep, but I heard noises gurgling from inside of him. I turned around, and it sounded like he was coughing or gagging, and I smelled the vomit caking out of his mouth. It was thick and grainy like oatmeal and it erupted out of him like a baking soda volcano, so violently that he got up from the chair in his sleep and started thrashing around violently because he couldn’t expel the vomit as fast as it was coming out. His head swung from side to side and the bile streamed so thickly out of his mouth and nostrils that it hung like a beard from his chin. He slipped on some of his own drippings and continued to vomit, and all I could do was watch and pretend that I had never seen it. Of course, this was all in an age when so much more was taboo and the death of a veteran by choking on his own vomit was something that just wasn’t talked about. I never liked the bastard, and I wasn’t sad that he was dead, but there was something about his death that left me feeling empty and unfulfilled. I think it was his funeral and how the people who knew him least said such nice things about him while the people who knew him most said nothing at all. I hated the bastard, and I only wanted to be an astronaut to spite him. When they leveled the dirt over of his grave, I really had no reason at all to be an astronaut. I figured if you can die a hero’s death by choking on your own vomit, why shoot any higher than that?” Garbage Bag Johnny smacks a ho.
