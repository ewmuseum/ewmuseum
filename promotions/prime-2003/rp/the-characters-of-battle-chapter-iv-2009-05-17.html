The Characters Of Battle (Chapter IV)

17 May 2009

Date: 05.17.2009 The Characters Of Battle (Chapter IV) Aside from circles and squares, and the spectrum of other shapes that lines snake and bend themselves into, there is light, and there is nothing without the light. It arrived one day, long ago, dragged by chariots splitting through the groping darkness like some majestically hurled shuriken, glinting only from the reflections of the shears in dark sheets of sky. The light is the only blade fine enough to slip between the thickness of intersecting segments of shapes and sever it in twain countless times over. The tattered remains of shapes blow about in the wind, pierced throughout and pieced together by light, into massive withering structures rebuilt upon themselves. The shreds cycle through, passing through slivers of shade into the silvery, glowing beckon of shiny beacons. Arms stick out from incomplete bodies grasping and tugging at the liquid rays that drip from the stars. The light slips in between the pores, but it never makes a slice. It makes it rip its insides apart. *** "Holy shit," I mouthed, not making a sound, but an impression. The stone shone with a mighty glory, and as I stood before it, the sound of the tripped alarm disappeared in recessive waves behind me. It sparkled in the dark hall, perched on it's mount like some sublime falcon or another. It was such a marvelous spectacle that I just had to stand there and admire it, even though I knew they were coming; even though I could hear a whole herd of their footsteps, clapping against the checkered tile. I dislodged it from its perching and held it in my hands, and it was like holding a star. It's indescribable, and then your palms start melting. I dimmed the lucent stone into my back pocket and ran like mad. "Stop right there!" the leader barked out. I had looked back, but only briefly, as his men filtered around him past sleeping busts, canvases and statuettes. Armed battalions were marching up the double sets of stairs as the original flank closed in on me from the corridor. I was backed against a ledge, and the troops were linking into a semi-circle around me. "I've been banistered!" I cursed loudly, and it echoed off the amphitheatric walls of the main rotunda like a chorus of mumbling phantasms. Looking back at it now, it all seems like an unnecessary display of bravado, but I was there, and there was a fountain and not much else I could do about the flock of angry officials circling me like a thirty-headed snake, charmed by sirens, with its heads bobbing back and forth in unison as poisons foamed around their wagging tongues. Too many two-bit criminals misdirect their focuses on a clever way in, but as long as you got a solid escape, you can bust right through the goddamn doors. "We've got him trapped!" "Close in!" Needless to say, I jumped it, and it wasn't the most graceful leap, but it was good enough for me to splash hard in the water with such force that I got scraped up pretty good by the scooped blocks of marble not too far beneath the surface. I think it was marble. I can't be certain, though. I had lost it for a second, thinking that once you've fallen long enough to know you're falling, it's no different from floating . . . until you hit the ground. "Backup! We need backup in the main lobby!" The thunders descending down semi-synchronized into a sheet of stampeding noise snapped me back to my senses, and I got up, all knock-boned and drenched, to limp and drag myself away as fast as I could, through a labyrinth of display rooms in the northeasternmost wing. The walls whipped around like jilted boxes seemingly zippered together in every direction. I would have gotten lost had I not been such a connoisseur of the arts. Good paintings keep good secrets. I navigated the freestanding displays and arbitrary doorways like a wily sea veteran, clawing against the tide with masterful strokes of his oar. I hurdled a velvet rope pedantically guarding a display in the process of renovation. Rather, it was a display on renovation itself, itself being renovated, right down to the finest detail. Ladders climbing to higher ladders and sheets draped over other sheets posed in the middle of the room with its partially partially painted walls; rollers drowning face down at the bottom of trays where the sludge of paint puddled by design. This was art in its most epic grandeur. This was real, gritty and blank; immaculately looped, layered and scaled. The oneness of the objects in the room -- of the room itself as a piece of art -- it was like there was nothing there at all. There, too, was an art to the chase. It was never about jewels. They're all just a bunch of filthy minerals. It was all about pulling off as big a score in as little time as I could. What's a man to do after he grows old without something to brag about? I, too, was an artist, then, and my work hung around the museum with likes of art from the most brilliant minds of all in all mediums. Unwalled wooden beams still arched to angles over the wall's recess to the fluorescently marked exit door, red neon buzzing with electricity above as I felt the depressible steel bar that operated the slab of hollowed metal open and shut. I quietly popped the door open a crack and panned an eye across the slit of exposure it presented. The coast looked clear; quiet, despite the drowning blare of sirens and alarms interlocking momentarily and then misaligning across uncoordinated orbits. Never trust an eye to do an ear's job, they say. The wind rushed out of me as I was driven back into the brick wall, the blue of his uniform covering me like a paint. I had been painted, and then it went all dark and demobilizing, as if I was locked in a trunk. Something about his mustache, more like a grainy film than anything, reassured me. Had we not met under these circumstances, I'm not so sure it would have. *** There is value in light, but determining that value is difficult when there is no means of appropriation, and owning the light affords you no benefit that you couldn't derive by not owning it. It's all a bunch of meaningless phototropism. Every why begs another why and a why not. It splits until it's itself and the opposite of itself, and it remains itself, and though its counterpart does not likewise divide, it runs alongside itself like a shadow. There is no light without a shadow. *** I came and knocked on his door. He was waiting for me. It wasn't long before the door swung open, and a slick, oily man motioned for me to enter with a greasy, extended palm. I took a seat in front of his desk, and he circled around it into his executive chair, noticing I was looking up at the gold logo: two big F's pointed outward with an extra, hermaphroditic F performing some sort of upside-down osmosis in between them. "You like the logo?" he pointed. "That shit's got more carats than a rabbit's refrigerator." "What's the extra F for?" I questioned, pointing to about where he was pointing. He shifted his finger in response, more precisely towards where I was pointing. "Fuckin'" he curtly stated, without batting an eye before sinking into his plush chair. "Now let's not beat off on the bush, here. I understand you had a plot?" Frank Furterly (who swore up and down that that was his given name -- to the underground media and the mainstream media's underground exposes alike) had been a mogul in the industry for a good 25 years. His claim to fame was that he was the first person to spot the accidentally unbagged goods of a television actor on a popular sitcom. Though he kept the discovery from the attention of the public, it was eventually caught by some whistleblower, years and years later, his 1984 adult film -- "The Charming Stranger" -- has served as nearly indisputable proof of his claim to discovery. It's proof that remains, in large part, ignored; a very particular and fiercely adamant fringe of taboo circles notwithstanding. "I wouldn't call it a plot," I admitted. "It's more of a theme, really. I didn't know these kind of things had plots." "Kid, you seen anything from the 'Nutsacks of the 80s' quadrilogy?" "It rings a bell," I lied, trying to save face. "Well, why don't you go home and pop part three in your VCR and tell me that didn't have a plot?" he challenged. "I'd put it up there as one of the greatest stories ever told against anything ever captured on film." Frank Furterly was a very hard man to reach. I had spent months, hounding his office with calls, just trying to set up this appointment. I couldn't let it go to scratch. "Wait!" I desperately interjected through the slow death of air. "You haven't heard the title yet." "Oh yeah?" he replied with fingers against his temples, too irritated at me to want to fake interest convincingly. "'Coppin' A Feel,'" I deliberated, freezing in place in anticipation of his reply. "You've gotta be fuckin' kidding me. A cop flick?" He was irate; like a man so coked out of his wits that he thought he'd have gotten used to it by now. I was losing him. "That shit's been done so many times you can fit two dicks in it without 'em touching each other." "What about 'Calling All Units?'" "Too subtle. 'Coppin' A Feel' is better. There ain't no room for subtlety in this business." "Then why don't we just ram 'em with cops? Cram 'em down people's throats? We just have to go big." "Like I said. It's been done. If we just keep cramming cops down people's throats, they're going to stop caring. It's like cocaine. You snort enough of it, and you just get used to it after a while." "Then we'll give them the biggest cops they've ever seen. Look, I can guarantee you there's money in it," I acted fast; perhaps too fast. It was all out on the table now. "You can't guarantee shit," he leaned forward. "Now get the fuck out of my office before any more bad ideas fall off you onto my chair." "Thirty million," I played the only hand I had left. "We'll split it down the middle. You can even take all the production expenses back from my share, but let me be straight with you Frank, because I know in your line of work, things can get blurry. I'll need ten large in advance . . . and I've got to be the one directing this project." Frank was silenced, thrust forward two and a half decades from where he'd spent most of that time. His niche dwindled in appeal with each passing year. Technology had rendered his methods antiquated, but he held tight to them, set in ways he was far too bullish to change. He rubbed a gold cross that hung from a necklace and tangled in the hairs of his open-chested-shirted chest. His inconsistent decor was somewhat telling. He'd blown money on lavish things, but the gap between those articles and all else was wide enough to fit a bowling alley. He was a man who could spend money faster than he could make it, and he was showing the effects of the cleaving distance in between. "I'll want to see a script first." "Of course. I'm going to need a few of your best men to fill some of the roles, though." "My men can fill anything you need." I returned days later with the script, and we went over some lines. *** Without a point of comparison, a circle cannot rightfully call itself a circle when it's unfamiliar with what isn't a circle. The best circles make the best squares. The best lines make both. One must know all of what it is and what it is not to be entire. If it only knows what it is, it might be more. A machine is all its cogs. A cog is all its teeth. No shape is as simple as a straight line that contains space. They're all made out of puzzling cuts that somehow fit together with each other, and they must lock together to become strong and powerful. *** I had been regularly visiting the museum for months, and each time I had ignored the suggestion to donate, and with each trip, the lobby clerks started to recognize me a bit more until they gave up asking me completely. The clerks exercised politeness, but there was a hollowness to the smiles they used to greet me. Everything about the place was built on a foundation of imaginary worth. Art, you see, is a complex thing to denominate. Assigning worth to the arrangement of colors on a slate or the angles at which stone had been chiseled seemed arbitrary to me. Musems attempted to standardize valuable art by validation through inclusion. I had no idea what the selection process entailed, but I couldn't imagine that it was free from politics. There were theories that wealthy conglomerates with nothing better to spend their money on allocated worth to art based on market activity in a series of auctions and trades that had less to do with the content of the work and more to do with bending the principles of supply and demand as close to breaking as possible without snapping them straight in half like a curved line becoming two bent ones. After scoping a display of rare and intriguing minerals, I stood in one of the galleries, surveying the openings to other galleries scattered haphazardly about the room. There were many rooms like this, and I had been in them. Upon my first visit, I had wandered lost for several days before finding my way outside again, but through frequent wanderings, I untangled the inner-workings of these off-kilter rooms, trying to locate a pattern to it all. I found it, subtly hidden in the current of the designs in the floors. There had been tribulations along the way, of course, but I figured this to be my penultimate trip to the museum, and as such, took it upon myself to observe the arts and crafts that gave setting to the grazing, staring herds. I had seen the displays, from the tools and weapons of ancient man to the marveling inventions of the mind of his modern counterpart. However the artistic endeavors of the men who lived in between were the most vexing to me. I was staring at a painting of a man sitting, huddled with his arms around his bent up knees with his head peering through. on the underside of the overhang an upside-down cliff, huddled with his arms around his bent up knees with his head peering through. His head was a mess of busy features underneath an severely rounded hat. A pair of tears freshly juiced from the bottoms of his sideways eyes. Trees hung wispy on the surface of the upside down cliff, and a pit of fire waited beneath them, already catching their bottomed tops ablaze. "Ah, 'L'Homme Avec La Tête Mandarine,'" accented a husky voice behind me. "Come again?" I turned around, eying the man, stuffed in pieces of suit. He procured a monocle from his front pocket to examine the painting, walking right past me as if he was being drawn into it. "The greatest artistic scholars have long bandied over whether or not the man in the painting represents Christ during the crucifixion." He shifted to face me, stepping close enough where I could smell the must of his oaken breath. "But I hypothesize that he's apprehensive to pull an invisible lever that would set the world back upright because he's not so sure it's upside down to begin with." "I suppose," I responded, without much comprehension towards what he was saying, but I looked at the painting blankly, nodding to try to fool him into thinking I understood. "Davis O'Toole," he introduced, extending a hand I cautiously shook. "I'm the head curator here." "Nice to meet you," I said, conservatively. "I wish I could say I was just here on a meet and greet," he said, dashing his own wishes while continuing more morosely. "The fact is, this is a little more grave than that. Our security cameras have caught you straggling about the museum with no real path or purpose, and not once have you made a donation." "I didn't know they were required," I deflected. "They are indeed only suggested, but we do enforce a strict ejection policy against anyone we deem to be a loiterer. This museum is for patrons of the arts, and it's a service to our patrons to allow them to browse the museum pleasantly, without vagrants wandering about." "Vagrants?" I feigned insult. "I'm not a vagrant, dammit! I'm an artist!" "Is that so?" he chuckled with a dainty flourish. "It is. I'm just here for inspiration. If I had any money to donate, I would." "Alright, then. Since you're an artist, I'd be very interested to hear your take on 'L'Homme Avec Tête Mandarine.'" Davis folded his arms and cast a somehow downward gaze on me, though we stood about level. "I think he, uh, climbed there to save himself -- to escape from all that fire and shit." I improvised. "Or I think you might have hung it upside-down." I looked up to see if I had passed his test, but he just made a noise like he was recombobulating the inside of his chest. I didn't know whether this was good or bad. "Interesting," he chewed, and then his eyes starting chewing at me. "Don't tell me . . . you're a painter. You look like a painter." "Guilty as charged!" "Do I know your work?" "I'd reckon not," I admitted, holding to my anonymity. "It hasn't really been displayed anywhere yet." "Ah," he coughed, like he'd swallowed water the wrong way. "So you're only an aspiring artist." "I guess you could say that. I prefer underground." I looked around and then put a hand on the curator's shoulder. He looked at it like my moldy hand was staling the old loaf of suit on his shoulder. "You wouldn't be able to help me out, would you?" He grabbed me by the arm and took me aside to a corner, pinning me between the walls, so I could only look directly at him or straight upwards, where I saw a camera pointed outward over us. "I don't think you know how museums work, son, but there are only two ways two get your art featured in one." His eyes reddened with fervor. "You have to slave away for years, honing your art before waiting centuries to pass while the art crystallizes, and if history deems it significant and timelessly applicable through the spectacles of hindsight, then your work is good enough to be displayed." "I'll probably be dead in a matter of centuries," I reasoned. "What's the other way? Is it quicker?" "I wouldn't worry about the second way, seeing as how you have no money." He started peering at me under tightened brows through his eyeglass. "You'd have to buy your way in." "I'll come up with something," I dealt. "How much are we talking?" "I really only discuss numbers with serious considerations," he sucked back his amusement. "I'm good for it," I assured. "I've got a few backers." "Do these backers have one hundred thousand dollars worth of interest in your work?" "Give me some time, and I think I can rustle it up." "I'm not going to wait around for you when there are several other artists lined up, ready to pay for a spot on the wall," he conducted, businesslike. "These artists know that it's an investment. Six figures may seem like a lot of money to you, but once your work has been in a respected museum, you'll be selling your next piece for seven. Now, if the walls get too crowded, it doesn't make the museum look very good, and it floods the market, driving the value of art steeply down. So you see, we only offer a select number of spots with each new display, and they fill up fast. I don't have time to wait for rustlers." "I'll get you a down payment," I blurted, recovering from the pitch of his soliloquy. "One thousand dollars," he judiciously sentenced. "That's a tenth of the total. You have a week." I returned by the end of the week with the payment in order, and when I did, I couldn't help but notice that "L'Homme Avec Tête Mandarine" had been turned over so that the man was sitting upside-down on the underside of an overpass of a righted cliff top beneath a burning sky. *** It is hard to determine if the ultimate stages of the most skilled of shapes match the originals. The most gifted in war are also the ones which know best their enemies; the ones that can outline other shapes better than those shapes themselves. It is an art to have such command over every line. Everything thus has been a sequence of curved and crooked lines, and so is everything that shall be. Perhaps there is some vague order to it all, and perhaps there isn't. You have to assume there is some finesse to it, and if there is some, there must be some potentially endless spout of it, over time. If you're patient, you can see a lot of it, but no matter how patient you are, you'll never see all of it. Quick judgment is a necessity to success in battle. You must be able to judge the strength of a piece based on the strength of its characters united. You must look upon it as a whole rather than a construction of interlocking parts that each affect the functions of their neighbors. You must see the lines and only the lines and determine whether it is a circle or a square. The hardest battles fought are those against shapeshifting adversaries that lead you mentally askew -- the ones that are already going down in the flames of their own unidentities that'll drag you along with them. *** "What's the status on Lieutenant Cox and Officer Balls?" "They're in position." "Good," he breathed, relieved that the dominoes were falling into place. "You know the plan?" "Wait sixty seconds, bust in, rough 'em up, cuff 'em up and fuck 'em in their asses." "And cut," I said. The cameras stopped rolling. "I think that's a keeper, Jack. Now take off that uniform, get over to makeup and make sure they don't do as shitty a job oiling you up this time." *** Everything came to a halt, and I heard footsteps and crickets outside. The trunk unlatched and a mustachioed man in an officer's uniform helped me out of the trunk. Jack Dripper had been cast for the lead role of Captain Rod Hardlaw in "Coppin' A Feel," and he groomed himself for the role like he was Sir Lawrence Fucking Olivier. His tireless work ethic and his lengthy girth of a baton had earned him a reputation as one of Frank's top men. Even when the cameras stopped rolling, he lived in his character until the final cut, and then as soon as it was wrapped, he'd move on to the next one. "What was all that business about back there?" I said, still rubbing away at the sore spots. "Sorry I had to cold cock you, Johnny," he explained, and I could tell there wasn't a dishonest bone in his body. "The real cops were circling around. I didn't have time to explain, so I just needed to get you out of sight as quick as I could." He walked around with me to the passenger side and opened the door apologetically. I got in and waited for Dripper to circle the automobile and slide into the driver seat. "Are we in the clear?" I looked through the rear view mirror as Jack revved up the car. "There are only two things I do well, Johnny, and that's fucking, sucking and driving cars." "That's three things, and I really could've done without hearing about the sucking." "Duly noted. You get the diamond?" "This little mineral here?" I pulled it from my pocket to show him and quickly replaced it. "Safe and sound." "What's the status on Lieutenant Cox and Officer Balls?" "They're in position." "Good," I breathed, relieved that the dominoes were falling into place. "You know the plan?" "Wait sixty seconds, bust in, rough 'em up, cuff 'em up and fuck 'em in their asses?" "Yes . . . except for that last part," I corrected. "That'd be a great scene to add to the script, though. Think I should edit it in?" "I don't know. Maybe." "Anyway, you cuff them up and leave them there. We take the briefcases, keep the gem and get the fuck out of here." "What are you going to do with the diamond?" "I haven't figured it out yet. What the hell kind of use do I have for a diamond? Sure, it's nice, but you can't really do anything much with it, but look at your misshapen reflection in it." "You could find another buyer." "You can't run the same game twice, even if you start playing it legit." Jack thought it over, but there was no time to think, and I had to start directing. "It's time to move. Make sure you take out your cut before you give the briefcase to Frank." Jack nodded, and I exited the vehicle and prayed that everything would go off without a hitch. *** To be better than your enemy, you must be a better enemy than your enemy. *** The papers hadn't made much of the ordeal. There was just a little blurb in the blotter. The diamond had been returned in a manila envelope left at the front door of the museum by morning. I still let the air clear for a couple days after that before dropping off the full payment to O'Toole, but I figured he figured that letting the public -- and competing curators, too -- know that their museum's pieces had been stolen would be an embarrassment among the museum-going and curating communities alike. "L'Homme Avec Tête Mandarine" was still upside-down, and renovations had been recently completed on that display about renovation, successfully converting it to a display about a display under renovation being under renovation. However, the display about a display under renovation being under renovation was, itself, under renovation. The diamond sat on the perch from which it was once plucked, now guarded by a polygon of bulletproof glass a foot thick. I stood there, admiring my work as I watched it hang there for the first time. It was really just a bunch of scribbles and random lines with some vague interpretations of shapes thrown in for good measure. I thought it was pretty good. "What does it mean?" It was O'Toole. I could tell by the verbal emphasis. "I just can't seem to put it together." "It doesn't mean anything," I might have lied, so shrewdly that even I couldn't tell. "It's just a bunch of lines and shit that's supposed to look like it means something." We both looked not so much at it, but through it, and I soon came to realize that every trip to the museum was my penultimate trip to the museum. "It's fucking brilliant," said O'Toole. "Do you mind if I ask what's next?" "I'm trying my hand at film." I looked at him, smiling, and then back at the painting. "I think you might have hung it upside-down."
