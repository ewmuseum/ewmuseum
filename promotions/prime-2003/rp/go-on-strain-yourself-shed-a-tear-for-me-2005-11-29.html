Go on, Strain Yourself, Shed A Tear For Me

29 November 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 29/11/05 ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~Go on, Strain Yourself, Shed A Tear For Me It’s possible to take pleasure in a funeral. For the folks of the secluded village of Bundoran, it was anyway. Minuscule civilisations in remote places live their lives equably. It’s conventional and constant. They have very little drama so when it does come along they grasp for it acquisitively. Say if it is only a new priest to the parish, they will be on one another’s doorsteps wading through the rumour mill, often by passing facts. Their lives are serene but boring to outsiders, the limited excitement they have is the only sort available so they of course make do. Then there are the highlights, the peaks so to speak. Incidents like, weddings, funerals or Martha’s eldest has been put in jail again, all revitalise people such as those. Everybody downs their tools, peel off their work clothes and take a day off. And so, for the funeral of Eoin O’Shea, men and women of the village clad in their Sunday’s clothes, walked the short distance to the Church of the Emaculate Conception, and sat down where the Dead Mass was to be said. Of course they would wear their saddest faces, a few might even spill the odd tear but there was no getting past the fact that although they were genuinely sad, they were there because they were curious. Curious of the Prodigal Son. It’s an Irish tradition for the body of the departed to lay in the abode of his family for a number of days so they could be ‘waked’. The term ‘wake’ derived from the many unseemly incidents that had occurred. Paddy’s father, Danny, and Eoin’s only son, should have looked after the arrangements but because the two hadn’t spoken in twenty years, the task was passed down to the returned Paddy, Danny’s eldest. People felt he should have done it without objection, felt he owed a lot to his Grandfather who he had, in their eyes, forsaken him. Some even said that his flight into the wilderness deprived the ‘Old Fella’ of a few extra years of life. He should have organised things to right the wrongs they said. But he didn’t. As soon as his Father had died, he took off to the hills. They had watched him go without a word. The villagers, the bulk of whom were congregated in the large O’Shea house, knew they should have been taken aback but they weren’t…”it’s in his character now” an old man had hissed through gritted teeth. Right through the night and until dawn, Paddy had lain on the crest of the far hills, overlooking the sea, his back to the village. He’d always have his back to the town he supposed, once you escaped their invisible barriers and seen the real world, you wouldn’t ever look back. Then they saw nothing of him. A good source told them he was off again in the direction he came and the people didn’t expect to ever see him again. But two days later and he was back. One of the young lads began claiming he was a famous wrestler and that got a laugh. They saw Paddy as a builder in New York perhaps but nothing beyond that. Thinking about showbiz, well that was just irrational. On the morning of the funeral, Paddy had apparently arrived back again. They were a little surprised as he left late from the house, choosing to walk at the back of the crowd of people instead at the front with close relations. His face was stern as he walked, his hands behind his back. The villagers found him peculiar and so were chary of approaching him. He watched impassively as the coffin was carried into the Church but he didn’t go in right away. Instead he stayed outside for a while, smoked a cigarette then entered. Without beads or book, he knelt on his own at the back pew, never really focusing in on what was happening, instead just nonchalantly glancing around at how fine the place was decorated. He was a heartless bastard they decided after tallying the evidence. To add insult to injury, Paddy O’Shea did the unspeakable – he resurrected the Irish practice by which a loved one of the deceased stands at the door with a plate before the coffin is brought out for burial. Everyone then walks past, throws in what they can spare and offer their sympathies. In the old days, this was deemed necessary what with the scandalous costs to for funerals. But, well, this was the 21st century and no matter how conservative these people were, this was somehow wrong. After all, the rumours had been pumping and apparently he was a millionaire now… What astounded them more was how warm he was now being to them. A boy who had been so quiet. Some of them didn’t even recognise the sound of his voice. Being the polite people they were, they acted sociable as their dear mothers had taught them. They could utter their repugnance of him later when he wasn’t around. To Paddy, this was like finally waking from a reverie. It was as if for many years he had been in a coma, contending with ghosts, mere figments of his imagination. Now suddenly, he was awake and looking at real people, faces emerging from the past. And the faces were authentic, the recognition of every single detail, like their eye colour and bone structures, convinced him of that. “Very sorry fer yer troubles young Pat. Glad t’ see ye home, jus’ unfortunate under these circumstances.” “Thanks Tom,” Paddy retorted as Tom shook his hand firmly. Tom was older than he remembered, maybe sixty five. His hair was now a shock of whiteness, including his moustache, much malformed from the vibrant ginger hair he had associated with him before hand. Despite deep lineage and wrinkles around his eyes, he was still the same, still the rebel Paddy O’Shea had known very well from the past. They say he had fought for the republican movement many moons ago though he didn’t like to converse about it. Paddy knew what it was to feel like that. “Yer Grandfather was a great man, he lead this village through thick and thin. We need someone else now, someone t’ stop the rot so t’ speak Paddy.” Paddy alleged there was a hint in there somewhere but let it go. There were more pressing matters now. He thanked Tom and watched him walk away. Tom McNulty was a good man and he was sure his sorrow was sincere. “Paddy! It’s great t’ see ye home again boy!” Jack Cavanaugh. This was a burly man, large around the stomach but with great hulking shoulders. Jack’s appearance was misleading. Despite his size, he was friendly and always wearing a smile. “Hello Jack.” “Bejesis, ye’ve certainly aged. But don’ worry, aye still see tha’ little rascal aye had t’ scare out o’ me backyard.” Paddy beamed. It was all flooding back, the memories, the dreams, all unbreakable bondages of childhood. Tom’s wife now joined the conversation. Her thin face and deep sunken eyes were unnerving and just as scary as they were when he was young. “We’ll miss yer Grandfather, Paddy,” she said. “He did a lot fer us; more than a lot of people do anyway”. She shot a glance at Paddy which he ignored with a very gracious of smile. Most of the people came now and shook his hand. Similar to all of them, they were genuinely mournful and Paddy seemed to be taken aback. He couldn’t equate their good words of his Grandfather with his own remembrance of him but then again, these people never lived with him he thought cynically. With time he’d realised what a self-seeking man he was. When he was young, he had been naïve believing was looking out for his benefit but all those errands and all those downgrading tasks were simply to relieve an able bodied man from working. Yes, he had been selfish; it had taken a while to figure that one out. He didn’t need him anymore anyway. He felt he was gaining something else. It was the dawning understanding that he belonged to a civilization that was making him reflect. He was born into it. People there remembered him as a small boy, as a youth, then as a young adult, making his trade with his Grandfather. And then…nothing more. From the instant he left until years later and he was now back, he was a blank. As he walked behind the hearse to the graveyard, he thought all this over. It seemed to him now that his life since was a blank. He found it hard to cull into his memory the people with whom he had lived and loved ever since. They were all like cut-outs. He had worked at many jobs, in many countries. He didn’t remember being nostalgic about what he had left behind or the people or that they had meant anything at all to him. And yet at times he would find himself thinking of little incidents that had happened at various times in connexion with them, unsophisticated things that if related would draw blank looks from the people he was with at the time. Was everyone assigned to a community? If you don’t, are you a misfit? Is this why there’s Irishman you meet abroad, rebels without causes just wandering aimlessly and vitriolic because they didn’t want to belong to anybody? As they lowered his Grandfather’s coffin into the grave and prayers were muttered and people shed tears, Paddy didn’t show any sentiment at all. His emotions were perfectly normal. Unless you looked at it as being abnormal for feeling nothing. He knew how it looked so he concentrated on being sad, tried to think of all the good times he had with his Grandfather as a young boy, but every time his mind drifted back to the stab in the back of his own son. You don’t deserve my attention. No wait…is that harsh? He didn’t know, he was trying to do it by the book, trying to find loopholes that he could kick the coffin, maybe piss on it and cartwheel away. Didn’t work that way though; he was confused, one half of him was sorrowful, the other half glad. The two feelings had now neutralised as one. And, like I said before, this was nothing, no emotion at all. Acid plus alkali equals water, remember? Paddy peered up and over to the other side of the grave to Hannah. She was a girl he had loved once, back when he was eighteen. Many’s a days he had spent trying to win her affection, though all that was fruitless. She was his friend just never his lover and after a minor crush all was well again. Hannah was looking at him now fully clad in a black coat and hat, her hands clasped in front her and laced with rosary beads from which they had recited a decade for the repose of the soul of Eoin O’Shea. Thinking now, he realised he didn’t think he had parted his lips during the responses and he scolded himself for it. Hannah was pale now, her eyes and the tip of her nose bright red. She was still the pretty girl he’d fallen for, just a little older. He supposed he had never forgotten her because despite the thoughts being but a minor crush some wounds never heal. The hurt of rejection had hurt him bad back then, during his vulnerable teenage years. Looking at him, he realised he really did know her. There was a single crease apparent between her eyebrows. It was there when she was puzzled he recalled, and he supposed the lack of emotions he was displaying was what was bringing it back now. She remembered herself and looked away quickly. Sorry love, he can’t simulate non-existent feelings. I think they’re non-existent anyway. ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~ Five minutes later, and Paddy O’Shea was pulling himself into Tom McNulty’s car who had kind-heartedly offered a lift. Maybe only he, out of all the villagers, refused to stay behind and talk about him. Well all except another one. “Hello,” a voice said dryly. Paddy ducked down into the backseat of the car and sat down. It was Hannah and she was now peering out of the window, purposely away from him he thought. Tom was outside chatting to Danny from just down the road leaving the two alone in what could easily have been an uncomfortable silence. Only thing is, Paddy didn’t like hidden thoughts, and he’d pry them out with a pair of tongs if he had to. “Say it,” Paddy murmured looking at Hannah’s ear which he had replaced as the nodal point for his demand in the absence of her eyes. “Say what?” Hannah said as she looked around. Still beautiful up close too though if she keeps her face like that for much longer, it’s going to stay like that he thought about saying cheekily. “Ye think aye was disrespectful, don’ ye?” “No…ye were…just yerself.” “Do ye expect me t’ pretend t’ somethin’ aye don’ feel?” Hannah turned back around, keen to end the conversation. “Aye expect nothin’ at all from you.” After that, Tom got back into the car, started the engine and drove off, the tyres making a satisfying crunching noise as they slid over the gravel underfoot. ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~ That evening, Paddy got a call on his cell phone. The details given subsequently ended the phone simple life as it was bounced off a wall. “A Good Ole Irish Bar Fight.” So he was going to be their poster boy is it? Poster boy as in the whacky PPV matches. He’s going to be the guy we’ll make gimmick matches around. Let’s go back to the old days, back to PCW. Let’s go back to Shanghaied 2002. Back then they’d made him a fucking leprechaun. HE WAS FIVE FOOT NINE! So let’s give him a pot of gold, let’s get someone to pinch it. Then Paddy, you have to act irate because they’ve got ‘yer lucky charms’! Then let’s have a great PPV match which you have to get it back. Let’s CGI the match, let’s have fairies coming out of the trees and banshees from people’s arses. Let’s make everyone snort, but not together now, this is a tyranny, this ain’t about equality, this is about laughing at the butt of the joke. Well he’s not your butt. He left to get away from that and I can see the shape you’re trying to mould him into. Just like the little sausages you made from play do so diligently, I can see your plans. Leprechauns are so five years ago, how about we do an Irish alcoholic! We can make matches where you have to drink the most! It’ll be fun! Want a pen? Just sign here! He’ll wrestle your match, and he’ll win your goddamn contest but if the results aren’t fun, don’t blame me, it wasn’t in the contract. And when all the dust is settled, and the others are still picking glass from their heads, don’t say I didn’t caution you. Only thing is, when you put him in that arena of a bar…you may think the results will be comical but they won’t. He’s been in that zone before when week after week he fought his way in and fought his way back out. This isn’t going to be to make the people laugh; this is going to be an exhibition of sheer brutality. It’s about time he was given the chance to scale like every other competitor, whether they’re Irish, English or Asian, so he can finally, state his claim to the rewards he’s already won. Call this what you will, just don’t expect another match like this again. TO BE CONTINUED
