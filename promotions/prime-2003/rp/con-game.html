Her name is Candy, or so he has always assumed, and this cookie-cutter, feminist maddening image of the “perfect woman” greets Tyler Rayne with her sensual smile every time he flips the top of his Marlboro 27s to pull a stick of lung numbing death from within. The lighter upon which Candy’s dick hardening visage has been lovingly adhered was once used as the ignition to an inferno that left Rayne’s locker room, and thus all his most precious personal belongings, in piles of forgotten ash. It was then, some time later, used as the catalyst to an explosion that decimated Rayne’s most favorite of vehicles. His first, and still to date, possibly most vicious feud in the American market, ended with the ruination of his nemesis, one Tony Studd, and the eventual possession of this very lighter by the man whom it had victimized the most. To this day, some eight years later, Tyler Rayne carries the lighter as a trophy of sorts. A small reminder, for both himself and his eventual foes, that the Rayne man always overcomes. At the moment he fights a futile battle to overcome his distinct disdain for the city of Paris. Don’t misunderstand. It’s not a hatred sprung from the loins of an irresponsible and quietly repressive government agenda, the kind of blind patriotism that launches ridiculous crusades to rename menu items and ostracize key allies within the United Nations. No, it’s actually something quite a bit more simple and innocent than all that. There’s an unspoken agreement between those of the cultured world and the city of Paris, France. An understated assumption that, beneath the loving shadow of the Eiffel Tower, all good shall come to pass. Lovers the world over spend hundreds, possibly even thousands, to come here, to this one city, to make their futures. Make their dreams. Everything perfect and wonderful happens in Paris. A city chocked so full of peace and tranquility that some have come to label it cowardice. It is, arguably, the closest we’ll ever get to Eden. But somewhere between the Fates, Paris, and the combination of alcoholic and marijaunic consumption that led to his conception, Tyler Rayne was effectively removed from this bargain. His name, inconceivably, left off the guest list. There are a catalogue of events and histories centered in or around Paris, all involving unscrupulous unpleasantries and unsavory characters of the most stalwart decadence imaginable. Yes, a veritable cornucopia of sordid affairs all designed for the ultimate ruination of Rayne. Hatred, however, certainly does not make him blind. Even as he lights the cigarette pinched between forefinger and thumb, Rayne is aware enough to admire the architectural magnificence of the Forum des Halles. Currently, he sits upon the stone cusp of a fountain, as instructed, his inherent rage soothed in the slightest by the serene sounds of flowing water. Technically speaking, the fountain may actually be considered more of a well, the type of basin in which star-crossed lovers might donate a coin in hopes of wishing their one and only into existence. It is a tiered vessel, wrapping around a corner of the stairway leading toward the main structure. Each level drops a foot or so, creating miniature waterfalls that eventually pool around two gigantic, cylindrical stones. The stones themselves are a soft bronze, oddly colored in fluid swathes of blues, oranges, and reds. Circles of smoke puff from his lips as Rayne’s attention falls upon the various denizens of the underground shopping center. It is almost immediately evident, at least to the observant eye, that he does not belong. His wardrobe rarely changes. The requisite “rock star” jeans and military issue boots. A black t-shirt, today’s choice bearing a blood red cog and skull. The “Gears of War” emblem, as any true Locust might have told you, covered, of course, by the brown leather jacket he’s hardly been seen without. All together a rather average day in terms of ensemble. Nothing ultimately out of the ordinary or even particularly eye-catching. The masses, however, run a gambit of two very distinct sects. There are high fashion aficionados, decked to the nines in the most ridiculous designs to step off this year’s Paris runways. These social elitists may be relatively few in number, but they’re presence, an air of importance that exudes from delusions of grandeur, is more than enough to make up for that. Beside them are the European aristocracy, a collection of well-dressed, well-mannered individuals that look terribly similar to the more fashion conscious brothers and sisters of our own country. From his vantage, Tyler need not look far to see where many of our fashion trends have originated. The second group consists of the slightly less well-to-do European populace, donning a plethora of forgotten American fashions pulled from any one of the last ten years. This misguided tribe of peoples might look utterly ridiculous, particularly next to the aforementioned elitists, if not for their brethren in forgotten fashion: the tourist. Yes, an overwhelming and painfully poorly dressed majority of these buzzing bodies will be leaving Paris within the week, returning with slideshows and digital pictures that shall flood the internet like a tsunami of meaningless moments. The tourists, much closer to Rayne’s own age than he’d ever willingly admit, simply left their insistence for updating fashion in the same box as their bongs and textbooks, forgotten and useless upon an emptied dorm room floor. Fashion ended after college, which, by all appearances, seems to have been around the exact time that Denise Huxtable did the same. A different world, indeed. The problem with not having a middle ground in terms of style, as illustrated in the above examples, is that one man standing alone in the middle is far easier to spot than one ewe amongst the herd of sheep.
