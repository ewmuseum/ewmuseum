A High School Gymnasium

11 February 2009
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: February 11, 2009 “One cannot choose to suffer and have that purpose be the meaning of one’s life.” The words reverberated in Fusenshoff’s mind like an alarm clock you couldn’t turn off snooze. The newest PRIMEate considered his quandary over and over while boozing heavily. The man who saved his life just a few days ago has made him finally question his current existence. He hadn’t considered his future, or even his actions in any particular moment for half a decade. Now, suddenly, this wretched gripe called accountability keeps knocking on his door. The shots flowed like an artificial waterfall creating a spectacle in Vegas, stopping only when the money ran out. Now the ethanol had a taste that soured the mouth, like a teenager just getting acquainted with it. It carried a meaning all on its own now, a constant reminder of the path he’s chosen. It became complicated rather than commonplace to tilt his head back and open his throat. Fusenshoff imagined that he could see his reflection in the shot glass, a visage of disapproval and growing resentment. Inebriation and frustration took the reins that his self-control once kept. He gripped the shot glass in his sweaty palm and heaved it with little effort at a mirror reproducing his likeness behind the bar. “What the fuck?!” the barman shouted from the opposite corner. Nobody moved as Fuse reached into his back pocket and pulled out his wallet. He left ten bills on the bar, all hundreds, and fled the scene. He was a duck on the water, leaving as if totally composed, while inside grief was doused in flames of insecurity. Stepping out the entrance, Fusenshoff took two steps and stood still, looking around at a deserted street. His head rose to the sky as a cry to make emo’s cringe filled his surroundings. ::Three days later:: The moment he dreaded had finally reached its fruition. Over the last couple of days Fusenshoff had drank less alcohol than he had since his sister died. About six months after her death, he no longer rationalized his alcoholism. Over the years, he stopped reminding himself why he drank, as the mourning slowly became his identity. Now, every time he looked at a twelve-ounce bottle or an ounce-and-a-half shot glass he was reminded that suffering has become him. Giving it up entirely wasn’t within the realm of possibility, but the agonizing reminder that it is within his power to reclaim himself was relentless. He stepped into the small gymnasium of a local high school and was immediately flooded with a monumental urge to walk right back out. A woman sitting at a table, next to a coffee-maker, was smiling and urging him in the door. Fusenshoff gave her a vexing look before nonchalantly walking within a few inches from the table. “Hi! I’m Meredith and welcome to our group. We’re always excited to have new people to share in the healing process. Please feel free to participate in any capacity you feel comfortable with tonight.” If comfort is this group’s intention then this bitch shouldn’t be the group’s spokesperson, Fusenshoff couldn’t help but think to himself. He didn’t respond to the woman’s blatantly oft-recited words and instead looked for a chair as far away from the podium as possible. He regretted the decision to stay consciously, yet couldn’t provoke his desire to walk away now. Nobody’s going to influence him to do anything he doesn’t want to do anyway. In fact, with his anxiety and frustration reaching paramount proportion, he sort of wished one of the men in the gymnasium tried. “What’s your name sir? We all wear name tags here.” Meredith snapped Fusenshoff out of his inner monologue. “I’m just gonna grab a seat, but thanks anyway.” Fusenshoff acted exceptionally cordial compared to his usual personality. “You’ve been quite helpful.” The woman smiled and said nothing. Thank God that shut her up, he thought, relieved. Fuse grabbed a seat in the back corner, though the furthest one back was already taken. He settled for the desk just in front of a man who looked more perturbed to be here than him. He assumed the stranger’s attendance was court-ordered. Waiting for the proceedings to begin, Fusenshoff couldn’t help but notice a thin, natural blond with perfect posture in the front row. She seemed very out of place with such a lot. “Hello everyone. My name is Julie, and I’m an alcoholic.” “Hi Julie!” The chant awoke the crowd and gathered Fuse’s attention to the older woman at the podium. She had raven-black hair that was straight and well groomed. Her skin was tanned and weathered, an odd contrast to her perfectly manicured nails, sharp jacket and shiny hair. She looked like a former junkie who’d cleaned herself up. “First I’d like to thank you all for being here. We all know how hard it is to maintain our sobriety. Each and every one of you -- no matter how far along you are – should congratulate your courage and discipline for taking control of your demons and silencing their constant barrage of desperate pleas. Don’t shrug off your accomplishment. It is a feat no less significant than running a marathon or climbing a mountain. Together we’re running a marathon that never ends. We’re helping each other climb a mountain with no peak.” She had an amiable quality that Fusenshoff couldn’t quite put his finger on. She emitted an air of strength and compassion rarely exhibited in public by a stranger. Julie paused while those in attendance either patted themselves on the back or nodded in affirmation. Fusenshoff just watched with interest. She continued. “Now I’d like all those who are ready for hope and help to stand up and introduce themselves. I see there are a few of you that have never been here before. I’d like to invite you to take your first step toward a sober, happy and rewarding lifestyle.” Three others stood up and one-by-one recited their names to the rest of the group. Fusenshoff watched and listened as the first two meekly said their name and briefly explained why they were here. One was court-ordered to attend the meeting. Julia reassured him that each person goes through the process at their own speed. She asked only that he listen intently to those in the room who’ve been on his path before, and to participate when he’s ready. The last man standing went off on a tangent about how he’d lost his entire life to alcohol. It started as a way to meet and connect with people in high school and hasn’t stopped. He said that his wife partied harder than him in college, but slowly refrained from drinking until his alcoholism made her refrain from it altogether. He feared losing his job, but worried more that he’d never see his kids again. He talked about his worry that his wife would never look at him again the way she did before they were married. The way she gazed lovingly into his eyes the night he proposed. He said he wanted to be that man again and would do anything to get his family back. The men and women, sitting at their desks, leapt up and gave him an ovation. Several patted him on the back as his eyes teared up and he laughed briefly. Once the ruckus halted everyone sat down and Julia was looking expectantly at Fusenshoff. He rose to his feet as slowly as a skyscraper being erected. He stood with his arms at his sides, his chest out and his chin level. Fuse felt strangely confident. The only other time he can remember feeling like this is when he’s in the ring before a match begins. He spoke in a serene but loud tone of voice as he addressed everyone in the room, who were all looking his way now. “I’m here tonight as a spectator. My drinking has reached a point where I can no longer use it to ignore my disengaged lifestyle. I’ll watch and I’ll listen. I won’t promise anything other than that.” The room was as silent as a coffin buried six feet underground as everyone turned toward Julia, looking for direction. Fusenshoff noticed one head still facing his direction. It was the woman with the natural blonde hair sitting in the front row. She wasn’t particularly beautiful and the punishment she’d put her body through was evident in her face, especially her eyes. As she turned around Fusenshoff couldn’t help but consider introducing himself after the meeting. It was a feeling that he thought he’d long forgotten. An hour later Fusenshoff was walking briskly through the cool night air on a bee-line to the nearest liquor store. He’d left near the end of the meeting, before they’d all been dismissed. No one saw him leave but Julia, the woman coordinating the whole thing. Fusenshoff was surprised at the positive impact the night had on him. He left without making any decision yet as to whether he’d visit the high school gymnasium again.
