The Awaited

20 May 2008
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 5/20/08 “What do we do with him?” The three cloaked individuals, standing on top of a hill, bore the wind without notice. This hill overlooked a simple, organic village. “Now is the time to decide.” Impenetrable white robes would have hidden their faces if darkness didn’t. The speaker nervously toyed with a piece of string. “Lachesis,” came a calm voice from the tallest figure, “be patient. The boy is barely out of the motherly vessel and you rush to judge him.” The middle Fate sister, still fingering her white string, exhaled with a snort of indignation. “Big sister, you know she hates waiting,” Clotho sighed. “There are so many that we have to collect tonight, and it just doesn’t seem prudent to waste our efforts on-” “Waste our efforts? I want to be done with the boy as much as you, little one.” Lachesis’s voice rose above the wind. “But do not dare slip out of piety. You know what happens if we leave the humans to blow in the wind.” The oldest sister nodded her head, sending waves through the massive white cloak. “I know,” whined Clotho, “all about what happens. In the face of true liberty, real ‘free will’…” The youngest Fate shuddered before continuing. “…humans always become helplessly lost.” She cleared her throat loudly, to announce that she was done speaking. The oldest sister, Atropos, middle in height, slowly turned her head away from her family and back towards the village. Oil lamps burned with pale fire, a flickering sign of the life below. “Indeed, our neglect to their ruin. Now, to the boy. Sisters, what do you see?” Lachesis and Clotho joined the ranks on either side of the eldest. The wind was reduced to a whisper. From beneath the robes, three sets of eyes stared into a certain soul. “A bastard,” said Clotho. “Here, in the country that tightens its own noose by the day, he lives meekly as a child. Often, he wishes for a better place.” As if on a fateful cue, a M109 howitzer licked off three oppressively loud shots. The earth shook, but the Fates did not lose focus. “He grows and becomes something better. Something proud and beautiful. He does leave this place. But he cannot leave it behind. It remains, like a scab upon his unblemished body.” As Lachesis spoke, she gestured wildly. One of her scaly hands snuck out from underneath the robe to make a fist that coincided with the word <i<scab. “There is something else.” Clotho stepped forward on the hill. “His humors suggest that he pursues…earthly pleasures.” She tried to mask her giggles with a cough, but Atropos was quick to catch her. “Do you see something my weathered eyes do not? Tell me now, youngling. You know the complete triumvirate is essential for blind judgment.” “Oh yes, big sister. So many beautiful women,” Clotho said, trying to refrain from laughter. “But no love for the little one. He tries and tries, but…” “But no one loves young Narcissus,” Atropos added, with an unfeeling. Although advancing years made her eyes cloudy, and details hard to reach, it was that same experience that made her wise. It was her job to see the ends of all things. “Not ever.” “You’re looking at the wrong end, Atropos!” The eldest Fate whipped her cloak around to look upon the newly bold Lachesis. Clotho offered a remedy for the awkward moment. “She’s right. Big sister, the end of that thread is tied to a new beginning, one of…interesting possibilities.” “Possibility? Do not be coy with me, Clotho.” “If you must know, then you must know.” The youngest Fate’s furtive tone had not been lost on Atropos. “The thread, as of yet unspun, shows two roads: redemption and victory…or the slow, sweet march of total destruction.” “My vision of the space between beginning and end speaks the same,” Lachesis chimed. She said the words like she was reading from a book, as though she couldn’t believe them. “ Atropos, what do you see? Does Delphi bestow, truly, the gift of opportunity?” There was a long pause before she spoke. “I, too, see a murky set of endings. There is no error in your vision. This son of the Orient is rare indeed. Aren’t you glad we didn’t rush to any sordid conclusions, Lachesis?” The middle Fate gave no response to Atropos’s taunt. The mortars were quiet. “Lachesis. Clotho. We now must decide where the boy’s footsteps fall. The gravity of granting him ascension or condemning him to the chasm is equal. But on these occasions, when the thread does not spin itself, we must resort to our divine measures.” “The vote?” Lachesis’s voice whipped into a frenzy, light with excitement. “The triumvirate’s vote? Is it upon us?!” “Calm your tongue, Lachesis!” It was not the responsible and sage Atropos, but the oft-calm Clotho who chided the middle sister. “We are the Moirae, not a gaggle of trifling schoolgirls!” The tiff between the youngest and middle Fates threatened to push these deliberations deep into the night. Atropos wisely headed them off at the pass. “Hush! Clearly you two cannot be counted upon to do anything of import tonight.” Whenever Atropos took a decision into her hands, her junior delegates felt obligated to abide by it. “What do you suggest, then?” Lachesis steamed and a hot, dismayed breath rose from between her lips. “We unleash upon him merciless free will? Let him live without Destiny’s guiding lantern? Do you slip into madness?!” “I slip nowhere,” roared Atropos, “but into the company of fools!”. Her robes nearly erupted as she spread her arms. On the black horizon, orange sparks announced the continuation of M109 rounds into the jungle. “This boy, this…bastard human has clouded the machine of your logic and reason.” Off to the side, Clotho slumped into a seat. The hill’s natural slope let her recline. Her eyes were not visible, but she faced the streaking tracer bullets off in the distance. “Sister, what do we do? It is our responsibility to forge his being and all of his choices. Discord between us is as old as the stars. Why, now, do you let it interfere with noble duty?” “Delay the vote,” Atropos replied solemnly. “Delay it until the eve of his schism.” Lachesis and Clotho were noticeably silent. The youngest Fate ran her fingers through the tall grass. “Then it is done. We will revisit this matter when the time comes. When he steps upon the dual doorsteps of undoing and salvation, we will vote.” The other two cloaked heads nodded in unison. * * * * Clotho traced a finger against the smooth skin of Chandler Tsonda’s face. One long, untamed fingernail traced his cheekbone. “Why,” the youngest sister asked, “does he not stir?” “I have asked the Magi for a favor.” Atropos stood at the foot of his bed, surveying the boy whose fate they had yet to decide. “The Wheel of Time has been stopped, but only for a moment. Our decision must be hasty.” “Now we must rush the decision? I remember urging my sisters quite the same. Now Atropos has the idea and it is canonical?” Ignoring Lachesis’s bitterness, the senior Fate spoke, addressing something celestial. “We submit the triumvirate’s vote, as law, upon the bastard child’s road.” Her voice wavered for a moment. “Clotho, what do you say?” Clotho kept her hand upon Tsonda’s pillow. “Give him ruin.” “And you, Lachesis?” “I wish to–” “You forget your brevity, Lachesis. Cast your vote and be done with it.” Clotho’s words hung in the air. Lachesis turned towards Atropos, but the oldest Fate offered no comfort. “Our instructions do not allow for you wailing from the pulpit.” “Give him salvation,” Lachesis announced. “The weight sits upon you, Atropos.” With the proverbial score at one to one, Atropos looked upon Chandler’s face. She wasn’t moved by humanity or sympathy; she was a machine, an instrument to keep the universe sound. With a sigh, the Fate looked to the stars. “I abstain.” “What?! Atropos, what do you mean?” Lachesis snarled at her older sister, furious with her indifference. “She wishes to give him his…opportunity,” Clotho said. “Her abstention releases him. He is free.” “No one is free,” Lachesis cried. “Everyone’s thread has been drawn, and your apathy damns him to the ruin that Clotho would have suffer!” “The vote is cast,” Atropos replied. “Cry over the bastard child some other time.” “No.” The smallest of words resonated loudly in this frozen moment of time. The middle sister’s denial was palpable. “He must have a divining rod if his emancipation is true.” Clotho rejoined the conversation with nonchalant ease. “A curse,” she uttered. “Something to remind him every day that we have given him his precious freedom. A placeholder if we need to…” Clotho paused and chose her next words with careful precision. “…correct certain mistakes.” Atropos folded her robed arms across her chest. “The libertine cannot be loosed without contingency.” “A curse,” Atropos repeated. “He already has one,” she said, in the same cold voice that carried every decision. * * * * I woke up and flailed at a mosquito that didn’t exist. It had been quite a night of dreams. Shaking my neck, I sat up. Despite the heating pads, despite the chiropractor, despite everything, my back shrieked in pain. I leaned over and grabbed the bottle of Tylox, for just a moment of peace.
