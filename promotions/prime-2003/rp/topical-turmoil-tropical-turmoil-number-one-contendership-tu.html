Date: Timeless From the age he first began to understand what it entailed, Deville aspired to be one thing. It was an ambition born of the delusions of childhood, but being a child, that fact eluded him, and as he grew into adolescence and manhood, despite the stark truth manifesting itself, he clung wistfully to the desire, determined to discover a conduit that would deliver him to his goal. His boyhood peers faced the same quandary, but analogously; where they were indistinguishable in quintessence, they were vastly contrastive in principle. Others dreamed of being Prime Minister, or traversing the fabled final frontier, or hearing the raucous applause of an over-saturated stadium as they hit the game-winning home run in the seventh game of the World Series. They dreamed of occupations steeped in fame and stardom, the vocations of spurious heroism. They dreamed of being leaders, respected and revered by their fellow man. Deville shared their dream of stewardship, but the road he sought to travel was cloaked in anonymity, cordoned away from fame and stardom by the clandestine sentinels that imbued it with romanticism, making it transcend mere prominence. In terms of respect and reverence there was arguably no parallel, but public acclaim was anathema to the archetypal kingpin. While there were few who would challenge it once achieved, Angelo knew there were fewer still who would laud his longing to become a Mafia Don. Even had that concerned him, it was of no consequence. He was more doomed to failure then the uncoordinated kid with lenses thicker than his arms who sought to stare down 100mph fastballs with a grin, or the anxious asthmatic who wanted to break the tape at a marathon, of genetics a prisoner for which there was no parole. With a father who epitomized what a Don should be, un-conceived he was practically guaranteed to achieve his objective, but the Irish blood of his mother that would course through his veins instead made his birthright his Achilles heel. Destiny seemed content to toy with him, arming him with the abilities and intellect to accomplish anything, yet inherently denying the one passion cultivated beyond all else. Growing up he’d been told ad nausea that he could do anything, become whatever he wanted, and as the unjust falsehood of those promises unveiled, he rebelled against the masters who made it so. It was for his blood that they refused him, so it was by their blood he made their folly known. No matter how many of them he killed, however, the survivors remained steadfast in their resolve, upholding tradition as sacrosanct above life itself. It created a murderous impasse. They would rather die than accept him, and he would rather die than kneel; he would rather murder them and re-create their rules than exist within them, content with the inferiority they demanded. A dozen assassins were dispatched for every drop of made blood spilled, but nothing they did could quell the flood. And nothing Deville did could make them rewrite the rules. For those opposed to him, doubt coalesced like an oasis in the desert, offering a fleeting feeling of superiority and salvation that would never truly be realized. They called bullshit on the legend that grew to surround him, trying to diminish it through bitter words and hollow boasts. They claimed the things he said and did were not only implausible, but impossible, as obscene as the whispered warnings of those in the know. Even as their doubts failed them, as they found themselves submitted to a power greater than they could comprehend, still they belittled and demeaned. Even as they died for it, they clung to their disbelief. It took Angelo a long time to realize it didn’t matter whether or not they believed. It didn’t matter how their judgement fell, or for what preconceived fallacy. The only way he answered to them was if he chose to, and though he’d spent most of his life wanting nothing more, now he wanted nothing less. The life they offered wasn’t life at all, but a pilfering of it, a shallow thievery of soul to ransom for an undeserved sense of lordship. Leaving it behind him, Deville couldn’t be happier. He’d ignored the bosses long enough that they’d been returning the favour. The uneasy and unspoken truce had its occasional errant shot, but by and large he was free to pursue a real life, free to experience relationships with the people he’d always considered pawns, free to engage in love and romance without an ulterior malevolent motive. He was free of the mob, free of the emotional shackles that came part and parcel of the package. He was free to live. That’s what he’d thought, at least, until he received the phone call detailing what he’d come to understand would never be. By necessity the instructions were cryptic, but to his poisoned mind the source and the intent were unmistakable. It was them. They wanted to make him. He knew it was impossible, but that knowledge didn’t prevent him from making the trip to the suburban home they’d alluded to, an unremarkable two-storied affair with a carefully manicured lawn and no other buildings in sight. As he made his way up the path to the front door, the only light pouring from an upstairs window, the only sound the hollow clacking of his shoes on the concrete, he couldn’t shake the feeling that he was approaching a tomb. Refusal to appear would have invoked as many hitmen as before, except in this war he would have no dominion over death to thwart the attempts. They wouldn’t know that, but their stubborn pride would soon discover it, delivered to them as news and perhaps a memento of his corpse. With a sigh and a hard-knuckled rap that echoed in the silent night, he conceded that he was dead either way. In all his life he’d never once heard of anyone refusing the offer – it was like refusing the lottery, or a knighthood – but in keeping with destiny’s cruel taunts, when they finally caved and accepted him, it was no longer what he wanted. He’d finally moved beyond his childhood dream, ironically turning it to a nightmare. He didn’t know what they’d say, what they’d do. The sensible part of him said they’d laugh, then gun him down in outrage and disgust. Maybe they’d simply turn their backs on him and leave him be, or send the assassins once he’d gone as repayment for the slight. Either way, he knew he was a dead man, so the least he could do was go out on his terms, his head held high. It had taken all of his willpower to leave his guns buried in their grave, every last reserve of his resolve to arrive for this showdown unarmed. If he’d given in, he knew he may as well have turned the guns on himself. The instinct to flee was overwhelming, especially when his second, louder bout of knocking went unanswered. He could hear nothing from within, no sounds of footsteps coming to greet him, no coarse laughter to fit his mood. Maybe you misinterpreted the message, his inner voice coaxed, providing the logical excuse he needed. He knew that wasn’t the case. He’d sooner tap out to a half-nelson than miss the nuances of the words he’d heard. This was the place, and as always, he was right on time. Perhaps they were testing his resolve. The light from the floor above beamed feebly around a corner, presumably where the staircase was. Beneath him the ground opened up, drawing him deeper, incinerating him, but somehow, instead of burning, it soothed, absolving him of the pain. Warmth. ~><>…<><~ Angelo began to stir, panic immediately washing over him as he found he couldn’t move his arms or legs. Blinding light made him wince as soon as he opened his eyes, but as the fog of sleep departed, he began to relax, realizing the constriction was due to a too-tight blanket snug around him. Squinting, trying to adjust, he realized he was in a hospital. He was alive. Shock at being unshackled, at having no pain in his chest, registered on a very distant level as he spotted the two officers standing at the foot of his bed, regarding him with a grim eye. Deville opened his mouth to protest, to explain what had happened, but at his stirring both men grinned as if relieved. As Deville listened with numb shock, they recounted with childlike wonder how the ambulance had arrived only to find that none of the bullets had actually touched him, that he must have passed out from the anxiety and fell before they could. They’d planned to pin the murders on him – logically and dutifully so, they insisted – but by some miracle the teenage son had lived through the strangling, succumbing to a temporary state of suspended animation instead of outright death. Once he’d been revived and they took his statement, he cleared Deville’s name, and gave them very good descriptions of the real perpetrators. It all seemed too convenient to Deville, too contrived, but he wasn’t worried about that; it wasn’t what was important. “I still can’t believe I missed you,” one of the officers muttered as they prepared to leave. “I watched my bullet hit, I swear.” The other policeman rolled his eyes, grabbing his partner by the sleeve and giving Deville a nod in farewell, letting him know they’d need him to drop by the station for a statement. Deville didn’t bother telling them that wouldn’t be happening; he might have turned over a new leaf, but a rat was still a rat, worthy only of contempt. “You’ve got somebody watching over you, Deville,” the first officer grumbled good-humouredly, casting a suspicious glance heavenward before being guided forcibly from the room by his mocking accomplice. The Deville smiled as the door closed and conceded that he must be right. Maybe destiny didn’t have it out for him after all. Maybe – just maybe – albeit with a bit of help and guidance, he was the one in control. With a new dream dawning, analogous to the old, he closed his eyes and slept.
