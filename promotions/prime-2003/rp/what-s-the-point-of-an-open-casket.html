What's the Point of an Open Casket

undated
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: May It might have been an exaggeration to say that funerals were the thing he loathed most in life, but his level of displeasure for gatherings in the presence of a dressed-up corpse on display surpassed that of the typical uncomfortable feeling. For Kaiser Vashaun, funerals, this one in-particular, could best be equated to a bad migraine. The evening had started with a slight-headache, which had turned severe to the point that every breath felt heavy and difficult to take. Soon after, the environment had become unbearable, even the gentle lighting of the funeral parlor being a nuisance to his eyes, and with each passing moment, his guts churned further and further till they felt like they were going to trudge up his throat and deposit themselves across the beautiful flowers and wreathes that surrounded the casket. “Christ. When is this thing over?” “It’s not even 7:30 yet, Kaiser.” David Vashaun whispered as subtly as possible to his brother. “Viewing hours are 7-9:00. People are just starting to show up.” “There’s no way I’m making it till nine.” Kaiser touched the back of his hand to his forehead and quickly drug it across, soaking up random drops of sweat with the cuff of his sleeve. “Why the hell did we have to show up at six o’clock, anyway.” He knew the answer to that one. Grieving time for the family, so all the tears and wailing and general emotional breakdowns could be out of the way before guests began to arrive for viewing hours to pay their respects. It was, in theory, good for the family to have that moment of appeared weakness away from the public eye, but the extra sixty minutes it added to the event were enough to break a man’s will. Plus, it was no secret to friends and co-workers as they arrived that the family had just previously spent a chunk of their evening crying and sobbing uncontrollably, chests heaving in disjointed breaths. The evidence showed up in puffy red eyes, tear-stained shirt collars, and crooked runs of mascara down women’s cheeks, like thick black veins. Kaiser’s final assessment had been that the additional hour of grieving for the family was too much misery for one evening. “I know this sucks, man. Just try to keep the chin up and just say ‘thank you’ when people offer their condolences.” “I think I need to get away for a few minutes.” “The parlor set that room aside… just for family.” “I think I may take up that offer for a few minutes.” * * * Dr. Trowbridge recommended I make a list of all the things I think I should have done, but didn’t. He said to start as big as possible and work to the smaller things, and that I’d likely find a shorter list of regrets than I might think. Things I Should Have Done - I should have apologized to Michelle. There are things in life that a person shouldn’t hae to apologize for, but this wasn’t one of them. It was my mistake that started everything, and though I may not have ultimately been at fault, my sister’s pain came at least partly at my fault. - I should have thought twice before signing up for professional wrestling. Something on the surface made it appear far more majestic and simple than its reality. Two months and one televised match after joining PRIME and there’s a burnout that’s already set in. - I should have learned a long time ago to control my emotions. My highs and lows come too swiftly and violently, and when they do, my reactions do little to lessen the severity. If I would have learned at least some semblance of self-control, God only knows how many things in my life would have gone down smoothly without the choking and coughing. … * * * “Too much to handle?” Raymond Vashaun pulled out a chair next to him at the small table in the catering room set aside for relatives of the deceased. “Yeah.” Kaiser poured himself a cup of coffee and sat down next to his father. “Felt like it was about a hundred and ten degrees in there.” “Didn’t feel that warm to me, but I can understand what you’re saying. Funerals aren’t for everybody.” “Ha… that’s an interesting way of putting it, Dad.” “It’s the truth.” “I dunno. They just don’t seem right to me. Part of me thinks too many people treat them like a party… as if they’re hanging out at the bar with some old friends just catching up and shootin’ the shit. They bump into someone they haven’t seen in six months or a year and just start laughing and talking about where they went last summer for vacation. Meanwhile, some guy’s in a coffin and his family is standing around trying to deal with this brand new hole in their life.” “Yeah, they can turn into a bit of a social gathering.” Kaiser’s father took a sip of his own coffee. “And the other part? “Huh?” “You said that part of you thinks people are too happy about a funeral gathering. So… what about the other part?” “I dunno. I guess I can see the other side of the coin. Seems to me that it’d all be a lot easier to deal with if there was less mourning and crying and more laughter. Celebrating the life or whatever. Might not be so rough that way.” “You’ve certainly had a rough go of it lately.” “No kiddin’. Most of it my fault though.” “You’re too hard on yourself, Kaiser. A murder trial is something most people can’t even begin to wrap their minds around. That you were able to weather that and not break… I think that’s a compliment to yourself.” “Then how come I can’t even handle a funeral? Or seem to be able to handle thr rigors of life on the road?” He stirred at his coffee with a small red straw, but didn’t take even a small sip. “You’re not having regrets about joining PRIME, are you?” “I dunno. I mean… it’s not exactly what I expected, and I’m not sure it was the right move for me. C’mon, Dad, are you tellin’ me you didn’t have any doubts when I came home and said I was going to wrestle? I just think after everything that’s gone down the last year… with Michelle and the Fire Department and the trial… maybe something as public and grueling as pro wrestling was a bad choice.” “I’d see it through to the end. There’s a reason you went after it in the first place. You can’t let the last year reign in the rest of your life, so that it’s just one indiscernable drag of a day. Fight through. Stop kicking your own ass, and you’ll make something of it. I know you well enough to know that.” “Thanks, Dad.” Kaiser looked up from the black hole of his coffee and gave his father a brief smile. “Still doesn’t make me want to go back into that room and stand by that casket.” “Me neither. But we need to get back in there, no matter how creepy the whole “open casket” funeral idea is. Jesus, does that weird me out. Never did understand why people wanted to leave the box propped half open to look at the shell of a man.” Raymond finished the contents of his styrofoam cup and tossed it in the trash. With a heavy sigh, he stood from the table and put his hand on his son’s shoulder, giving it a squeeze. “C’mon, if I have to go back, so do you.” * * * … - I should have turned down some of the house shows I worked those first couple weeks with PRIME. I didn’t know it at the time, but those evenings would have been better spent with family. - I should have known that you don’t know what you’ve got till it’s gone. Everyone always says it. - I should have left Laren on better terms. I walked away too casually. - I should have stayed with PRIME and not taken the time off after the funeral. Weeks away from the ring didn’t raise anyone from the dead. - I should have handled the funeral better. - I should have been less arrogant on TV. - I should have never started this list. * * * “Keep the chin up.” The Vashaun patriarch slapped his son on the back. “And I mean that.” “Yeah, I will.” “Ok,” Raymond looked at the watch on his wrist. “Still over an hour left for ya. Sorry, bud.” “It’s alright. I can make it.” “Good.” He reached out and gave his son a hug, then turned towards the open half of the casket. “Not such a fan of the color, shouldn’t it be black? But, I guess, what say do I have?” “Sorry, Dad.” “It’s ok.” Fighting to lift his leg high over the edge of the silver, metal box, Raymond began to twist himself into the confined space. “They make these things too damn small.” “They do.” It was all he could think to say. Once firmly back in place, Raymond folded his hands across his chest and closed his eyes. “See you later, son.” As Ray Vashaun uttered the final words, Kaiser turned to look at the line of guests that stretched across the large room. To his surprise, things were fairly slow at the moment. His brother David whispered something to their sister, Michelle, then walked up beside Kaiser and cast his gaze down at their father, resting peacefully amidst white sheets. “I still can’t believe he’s gone.” “I know what ya mean.” Kaiser looked down at their father. When I was in high school, I always thought Dad was an old man. But now, to think he died at sixty… that’s just way too young.” “Gonna miss ‘im.” “Me too.” The brother’s exchanged a brief hug in front of the casket. “Dave” “Yeah.” Kaiser nodded his head towards the open half of the lid of the coffin. “Remind me to close this damn thing before we leave here tonight.”
