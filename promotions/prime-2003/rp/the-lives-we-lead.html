The Lives we Lead

Date: April 5 & 6, 2008 "...been great to come back. I love it here, I miss it here and I promise you that I'll be back soon. Thanks for having me." The applause of the 125 or so gathered at CitrusTV's annual year-end alumni banquet wasn't as loud or as enthusiastic as the roars of 20,000-plus in arenas throughout the country that Troy Douglas was used to hearing, but it meant a hell of a lot nonetheless. The nation's largest student-run TV station had been a second home to him in his four years of school, a place where unlike in the classroom or on the gridiron, he actually had the chance to settle down with peers and be the thing he'd seldom had a chance to be in his life. Normal. Television had gotten him his first job after injuries prematurely put an end to his career, and even though he'd long abandoned broadcast journalism for professional wrestling, the fact that he'd been approached to speak at the alumni banquet was something Troy considered an immense honor. The applause died down, and Troy shook the hands of the station's student management, then walked back to his table, which housed a handful of proud members of the class of '98. "You still suck at giving speeches, you know that." Those words came from the ever-sarcastic voice of Neil Isaacs, former drunken fratboy and current highly respected executive producer of the top-rated evening newscast in the entire New England region. "Well, some things never change. Remember how many times I tried to lay down that voiceover for that one fucking year in review package?" "Do I remember? I've still got those tapes somewhere, and I'll be glad to hold them against you whenever possible," Neil said. "I knew I should've killed you when I had the chance." "Well, why the hell didn't you?" came the voice of Sarah Fitch, Neil's college flame and presently a field reporter for NBC. "Would've saved me a hell of a lot of trouble." "A lot of things would've saved you a hell of a lot of trouble, Fitch," Troy said. "I'm in no position to want to make your life any easier." "Oooooh. Big scary professional wrestler talking like a big man. You going to clothesline me next?" "Did anyone ever tell you that you're SO much more fun when you're drunk, Sarah?" "How the hell do you think I ended up with her?" Neil was always the one to put an end to the conversation with the quick one-liner. Sarah was the feisty one, Ben Castor was the smart one, Jared Aldridge was the one who thought the solution to everything was a party. And then there was Troy. The 6'5", 260 pound voice of reason. In a room full of people where he was by far the largest presence, Troy had the tendency to cast the lowest profile. He stood out so much, in so many aspects of his life, that sometimes it was just fine with him to be a part of a crowd. This crowd, in particular, had been responsible for many of his favorite memories. Troy hadn't even decided to take the flight into Central New York before he'd gotten on the phone with each of the people at this table. He'd only been to one of these things in the ten years since his graduation, and with the breakneck schedule he was working around the entire planet, there wouldn't have been good reason to come back unless he knew they'd all be there. Even a guy who makes a living in professional wrestling is allowed to get a little sentimental sometimes, right? The conversation continued through the night, the old friends all pretty much oblivious to the college students handing out awards to each other throughout the evening. Finally, the night came to an end, and as the lights lowered and an infuriating mix of dance music started playing inside the ballroom at the Syracuse University Sheraton, each person at Troy's table got up without saying a word to the other, and they all filed out of the room unnoticed. Getting on the dance floor with a bunch of inebriated college students wasn't exactly in the plans. They knew where they were headed. ********** Darwin's Pub was smack dab on the corner of Marshall Street and South Crouse Avenue, a busy section of bars, stores and restaurants just a block off the university's campus. This bar in particular had a reputation for two things; a damn fine turkey chili and the best selection of beers on tap in the area. They'd found a large table in the corner, far away from the lacrosse walk-ons, the sorority birthday party and the group of architecture students who had made the foolish decision to work on their senior project on a Saturday night in a bar while the Final Four was on. Then again, even when Troy was in school, the architecture students had always been just a little bit out of touch. For what seemed like hours they all sat, working through a few beers each, sharing stories and laughing. There wasn't much talk about what had happened to them since college. They all knew what the others were up to, and pointless small talk had meant nothing to all of them since the moment they'd first joined together almost 15 years before. The night wore on, and the group slowly whittled away. Ben, cheap as always, had to drive all the way to Newark by six a.m. to catch a flight back to Dallas. Sarah had to be in Pittsburgh the next day to cover a Barack Obama rally, and Jared, the only one who had never left the area, had to pick up his kids from a neighbor's house. In the end, it came down to Neil and Troy, a pair that had been bickering about anything and everything like an old married couple since their intro to communications class with Dean Rubin freshman year. For two guys who had always been such good friends, the way they argued was almost scary. They both had an intense desire to be right, and it had an unfortunate tendency to spill over into any topic. Television, sports, mindless minutiae that nobody could ever possibly care about, the two of them had probably debated it at some point. And the one that had come up between them most since they left college was about to pop up next. "Why the hell are you still acting like a little kid with this wrestling bullshit, Troy?" He knew it was coming. Neil was one of those guys who just couldn't understand the appeal of professional wrestling, and was even more puzzled by anyone who chose to do it for a living. "You still don't get it, do you Neil?" "No, Troy. I don't fucking get it. You've got a college degree, you're a former high-profile college and pro athlete, you've got a hell of a TV presence and you've got no connections holding you down." "Thanks for bringing that up." "Sorry, but seriously, my top sports guy just moved on to Chicago a week ago, and every single applicant I've got can't hold a candle to you. Come to Boston, you'll make good money, you can sleep in your own bed seven nights a week and you can get the hell out of this bullshit child's fantasy line of work." If ever a straw broke a camel's back, it was with those words. "Bullshit? You've got a hell of a lot of gall, Isaacs. I... I... God damn it. I don't need you're fucking charity, Neil. In case you haven't noticed, I'm doing pretty well with my 'bullshit child's fantasy line of work.' Save your fucking handouts for someone that needs them. I... I've got a flight to catch. Drinks are on me, man. I'll -- I'll see you later." ********** The next day, 8:35 a.m. Somewhere over the Midwestern United State... The conversation from last night sat with him for the entire flight from Syracuse to Wichita. The facts that the plane was now circling over Wichita for approximately the 11th time due to fog and that he'd finished going through the Sunday New York Times 90 minutes ago hadn't helped one bit. He didn't want the job in Boston. He'd had enough of working in television by the time he started training to get in the ring back in late 1999. But, something Neil had said stuck with him. Just why the hell was he doing this? He probably had better options. He could make better money in other lines of work, lines of work that wouldn't involve wearing tights on national television or being kicked in the face or slammed onto mats by 350 pound behemoths on a nightly business. He didn't have to have a career that could end at any moment. If he were selling mortgages, practicing law or teaching, Troy wasn't generally going to have to worry about re-tearing his labrum or permanently rupturing the muscles in his back. And, he'd be able to have a place he called home for more than a couple nights in a row. Son of a bitch, but Neil Isaacs had a way of pointing out everything wrong with his life. But, as he finally saw the seatbelt light blink back on above his head, Troy smiled. In what other line of work could he get the chance to do what he did on a nightly basis? I really need to stop listening to that guy. The plane veered into its final descent pattern, and as Troy returned his seat to its full upright and locked position, he looked out the window and made out the structure of the Kansas Coliseum peeking out from the right side of his view. He sure as hell wasn't going to get the chance to perform in front of 20,000 screaming fanatics if he was filing taxes back in Greensboro. As he felt the rumble of the plane touching down on tarmac, Troy grabbed the e-mail from PRIME headquarters that he'd printed out before leaving his hotel that morning, the notice that he'd be teaming with Bryan Dawkins against Jason Natas and Simply Beautiful at ReV 156. That was his job, and he couldn't think of anything he'd rather be doing. The aircraft finally came to a halt, and Troy grabbed his carry-on bag from overhead, pulled a blue Kansas basketball hat over his forehead, and made his way off the plane. Rock, chalk, Jayhawk wasn't the only thing about to take Wichita by storm.
