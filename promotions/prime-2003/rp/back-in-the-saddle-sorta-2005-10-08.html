Date: Oct. 8, 2005 It’s been over four months since I’ve been on tour with my band, LATHEM. We were promoting our second album, Solemn Hypnotic, selling over a million copies - or a couple hundred thousand, I never really pay attention to record sales. Along with a couple of Big Boss Record contracted band members, Deadspot Mafia, Corcophia and Caulk Sukkur, we finished our final leg of the tour in Tokyo, Japan, where about ninety thousand fans were packed to enjoy one hell of a metal show. The Japanese wasn’t too appreciative with the all-girl thrash metal band, Caulk Sukkur, they gave Corcophia a respectable round of applause, Deadspot Mafia attempted to steal my band’s thunder (Harvey Critelli always does that when we’re on tour) and my band stole the show. Harvey always poked fun at me about how he “almost upstaged my band.” Yeah, he never could, though. I always come out on top. After doing our sets, we left the stage, leaving the fans wanting more. I had enough; I was getting tired and the next day, I would be on a plane flying back home. We left the stage, still hearing the Japanese fans chanting our band’s name, which put a smile on my face. I entered my locker room, flopped down on a bench and removed my shirt, toweling myself off with it. I leaned back against the wall, feeling the fan’s cheering reverberating off the wall. After spending a few weeks in Japan, it wasn’t so bad. I met up with a few friends I hadn’t seen in years, saw my mentor, Kenji Iwakura and took over downtown Tokyo. It’s always good to reacquaint myself with all the Japanese cultures; my wife would be ashamed of me if I didn’t. A few moments later, my band’s drummer, Steve Barlow, who also doubles as the band’s manager, entered the locker room, with a couple of Cokes in his hands. “Good show tonight,” Steve said, tossing a can of Coke in my direction. “I’ve never heard of a Japanese crowd going nuts for our band.” I pop the tab on the can, smiling a bit. “I have...several times,” I replied, taking a drink. “So what do you think of your first time here in Japan? Did you enjoy it?” “Hell yeah!” Steve exclaimed. “It’s a definite culture shock to say the least. The people here are always courteous and the culture here blows my mind.” He looked over at me for a moment, sitting down next to me. “Did you ever have that feeling before?” I nodded, remembering the first time when I came here back in 2000. I had a big match against Daisuke Koriyama, the son of Heihachi Koriyama, the originator of God of the Ring Tournament he started back in 1975. Even though it was about an hour war against Daisuke, the fans respected me. They were impressed to see a Gaijin who took on one of the greatest Japanese Superstars in...well, Japan. It was also the day I met my future wife, Yuriko Yakama. “Yeah, I was lost for the first few days, trying to find my place here, but I eventually did, and I had the greatest time. I’ve been to Okinawa, Hiroshima, Nagoya - you name it, I’ve been there. Japan is a great country.” Steve nodded, taking another drink. “Since this is our final night of our world tour, where do we go from here? What do you want to do?” “Well,” I began, drinking the rest of my Coke, then crushing the can in my hands, “there are two options: we go back to the studio and work on new material for a third album, or I go back to the wrestling world.” That had been on my mind as of late, going back into the sport of Professional Wrestling. I hadn’t been inside of a wrestling ring since May, when I had my last match against Damian at a May Pay-Per-View - the name escapes me - in DWO. What better way to depart an “Extreme Icon” than to stick him with the most sadistic man in DWO and put him in a match called a “Gore Fest” Match. It was no disqualifications, no count-outs, anything goes. The DWO fans were expected to see Damian brutally destroy me and put me away in a pine box. Boy, were they wrong. After getting the upset victory over Damian, I took a break from wrestling to work on my musical career. Now I’m at the same crossroads again - should I continue making music, or go back to wrestling after a four-month hiatus? Would I be good enough to go back in the ring? Would people actually care that I go back in the ring? After the controversies in the past year, I highly doubt it. “What do you want to do?” Steve said again, breaking me out of my reverie. I let out a sigh. “I’ll have to think about that when I get home. I want to spend time with my wife and son before I make any decisions.” “Understood. To be honest with you, I’d like a break, and I think the rest of the band would like a breather, too. We’ve been working too hard, releasing an album a year. It’s too much stress.” I nodded. “Yeah, I know. Let me think about it and I’ll get back to you. For now, I need me some sleep.” I got up, stretched a bit and patted Steve on the shoulder. “See you later, man. Goodnight.” “’Night,” Steve replied, getting up himself. “I gotta hang back and help take the stage down. Why I signed on as part of the stage crew, I’ll never know.” “’Cause you’re a glutton for punishment,” I said, chuckling. “See you later.” And with that, I took my exit from the Tokyo Dome. It’s been a long flight back home, I’m tired and I can’t wait to meet up with my bride, Yuriko, and my son, Kaneda. I’ve waited a long time to reunite with my family, I missed them gravely. I’d talked to Yuriko on the phone when I was away, but it isn’t the same without her being there. There’s more interaction if she’s in the same room with me. And my son...I’ve missed my son dearly. I’ve received pictures of them throughout the months, seeing that he’d been “growing like a weed, inflating like a balloon,” as my wife would put it, and I’ve noticed that he’s looking more like Yuriko everyday. I’ve had some striking conversations with the lad, even though he’s four months old, but he can babble like the best of him. I enter my domicile, half-expecting a welcoming committee comprising of my family, but there was no one to be found. It looked like a ghost town in the house, and Yuriko said she would be home when I got back, unless there was an emergency. As I venture through the house, I noticed the television in the living room was on, so my suspicions of Yuriko leaving on an emergency were ruled out. Perhaps she was in the bedroom, napping away after a long morning with the baby. I wouldn’t blame her if she crashed for an afternoon snooze, I’m not around much to do my part as the father. I walk into the bedroom and sure enough, Yuriko was curled up on the bed, sleeping peacefully. I look into the crib in the corner of the room and Kaneda is sleeping peacefully as well. It’s nice to enter the house unannounced, I enjoyed the brief moment of silence. I sat down next to Yuriko, watching her sleep. She woke up a few moments later as she felt an unknown weight shift on the bed, happy to see her husband looking down at her. She smiled, sat up and gave me a hug and kiss on the cheek. “Hey baby,” she said with a sleepy voice. “When did you get in?” “Just a few moments ago my dear,” I replied, giving her a peck in return. “Having a long, taxing day, I see.” “You don’t know the half of it. I rushed Kaneda to the doctor’s because his temperature rose dangerously high. I thought he was sick or something, but the doc assured me nothing was wrong, so I was pleased.” She paused, reflecting what happened that morning. She remembered when Kaneda started wailing like a banshee around seven-thirty in the morning. His temperature was higher than normal and she freaked. She scooped up her baby, got into her car and rushed down to a clinic. A few checkups and minutes later, the baby was fine. Kaneda experienced a slight temperature raised, and it scared him. She was relieved to find out there was nothing horribly wrong. “That’s good to know, my dear,” I replied, breaking Yuriko from her reverie. “He has my stamina that’s for damn sure. He’s tough like his old man.” I turned to look at my son, who was still sleeping. Yuriko looked at her son as well, watching him sleep. “And he has your good looks,” Yuriko mused, smiling cheerfully. I chuckled a bit. “Well, from what I’ve seen, he’s looking more like you than me. What’s cool about him is that he has two different eye colors: his left eye is green and his right eye is blue. You don’t see that trait that often.” “Yeah. Our baby kicks ass,” Yuriko giggled. “How was your touring with our band?” “It was good. The fans were appreciative, especially the Japanese fans. Speaking of the Japanese people, I saw Sensei Iwakura and ran into your brother and a wrestling venue. Tetsu says hi.” “I know, I talked to him a few days ago.” She turned her attention back to me. Kenji Iwakura was on a flight from Tokyo, Japan, to Springfield, Illinois via jet plane. His student, Simon, needed training for his debut in Prime against Paddy O’Shea, the Illustrious Face Eater and Tyler O’Neill. Kenji was pleased to hear that Simon was going back to the wrestling ring, the first time in five months. For Kenji, being Tokyo’s most powerful man could enter him into the doldrums for days, sometimes weeks, at a time. Being a business man wasn’t all as it cracked up to be when he started back in the spring of 1990, a few months after his wrestler career was ended at the hands of Alexander Rosewater. He did receive vindication after hearing that Alexander Rosewater would never perform in a wrestling venue ever again, only to have that ban lifted in February of 2004, to legalize his match against Kenji at the Ring Leaders Pay-Per-View that year. While going business for himself, he started training students in the fine arts of wrestling, since Kenji had been doing it since 1972, and still did to this day. In fifteen years (and counting), he had trained over two thousand students, all of them Japanese - except for Simon; he was the only American to graduate under his tutelage. Out of the two thousand students, seventy-five percent graduated, twenty perfect failed and the other five percent quit because Kenji’s tutoring abilities were “too severe.” “We’re almost to Springfield, sir,” Tohru Yoshimoto said, bringing Kenji back to reality. Kenji nodded to his chauffeur/bodyguard. “Very good. How long will it be, Tohru?” “At least ten minutes, sir. Our car should be available by the time we land. I shipped our car from Tokyo to the United States.” Kenji nodded, waiting patiently as he gazed out the window of the jet plane. In a few short minutes, Kenji would be whipping Simon into wrestling shape. Ten minutes had passed since Tohru Yoshimoto landed the jet plane. He got out, stretching his monstrous girth. He stands seven feet, two inches tall and weighed six hundred pounds (he wasn’t nicknamed the “Japanese Mountain” for nothing). He is dressed in a tailor-made gray dress shoes, a button-down black shirt and black dress shoes, size twenty-four, quintuple “E”. He has brown eyes with beady pupil and his black hair is pulled back into a ponytail. Before meeting Kenji Iwakura twelve years ago, he was a world-renowned Sumo wrestler. He was a five-time World Sumo Champion, a seven-time Sumo Champion in Tokyo and a four-time Yokozuna. But his career was cut short after inadvertently killing his opponent, Hideo Hajime, by throwing him out of the ring, dropping him on his head. His neck and back snapped underneath his weight. Even though Hideo’s death was an accident, Tohru was banished from Sumo wrestling, never to wrestle again. Down on his luck and had no money left, Tohru wondered the streets of Tokyo, trying to find his place in life. His luck changed for the better when he met up with a man named Kenji Iwakura, who needed a bodyguard. Tohru jumped at the opportunity and they’ve known each other since.
