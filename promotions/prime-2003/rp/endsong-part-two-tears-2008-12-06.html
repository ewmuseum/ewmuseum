Endsong - Part Two - Tears

6 December 2008
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 6 December 2008 My mother had green eyes, which for a Cheyenne was pretty rare, and my clearest memory of her was those eyes … so vibrant, so full of life … watching over me, reflected in the rear view mirror as we chased the endless sunsets on highways and interstates. We used to travel a lot, back then, because of my parent’s jobs. My dad was an archaeologist and my mother was an anthropologist; two people who studied history from different vantages and passed it on to not only their own People but to everyone and anyone that would listen – working for the Bureau of Indian Affair’s educational outreach programme. His viewpoint, of course, was set in stone – if you will pardon the pun – in what bones and buildings could tell him while she was always more interested in stories, legends and folklore; the things that were passed down verbally, from mouth to mouth, heart to soul. He taught me, early on, that you can tell where you are going by where you have been – that foundations, if strong enough, will never break and even hundreds and thousands of years later there will be something left for our descendents to find. She taught me that no matter how old or forgotten the legend there would always be a grain of truth, something tangible and real, in the myth - you only had to know where to look. People who knew her tell me that I had her eyes. As a kid, in those dark days when things were still new and frightening and I was trying to find myself in a World that no longer made sense, I was terrified of that thought. I took it literally, you see, and had nightmares for longer than I care to remember about her walking around with no eyes – lost, frightened and alone - because I had stolen them. I used to wake up, screaming, in my Grandfather’s arms as he tried to console me through the never ending night terrors. You have to give me a break though; I was only seven years old. Only seven years old and my mother – with those beautiful green eyes that used to watch me as I fell asleep - and my father were dead. You see they died on my seventh birthday, when the truck she was driving left the road, flipping over before bursting into flames. I was meant to be with them, that day, but as they were packing the truck for the three-day trip we had an argument … actually, I suppose, it is fairer to say that I threw a tantrum. I honestly don’t know what it was about, my memory of those days is very fractured, but I know that I started it and, when the time came to get in the truck and leave, I ran and hid in the gullies that surrounded my family’s ranch. I can see them, one of my last memories, standing by the truck and calling for me – silhouetted in front of the rising Sun, their shadows reaching out for me as if they wanted to hold me tight and never let go – before they finally got in and drove away. My grandfather found me there, only a few minutes later, and tutted his disapproval before ruffling my hair and taking me back into the house for ice-cream. It was chocolate flavour. It is weird how I can remember that taste – so sweet, so cold – even now but don’t know what my parent’s looked like without a photo. Such a vivid memory, though, and I have never been able to eat chocolate ice cream since that day. You see it wasn’t too long after that my Grandfather answered the phone. It wasn’t too long after that that I watched – confused - as tears rolled down his face. And it wasn’t too long after that that he dropped the phone, turned to me and – with his face wet against my own – held me close and nearly squeezed every drop of oxygen out of my body as he told me how sorry he was. Over and over again, like a mantra to bring the rain, how sorry. He had just lost his daughter, he had just lost the man that had been as close as a son to him and yet – despite his pain – he wasn’t crying for them or for his loss. He was crying for me. - - - - - Detroit “Please, Rick … don’t.” I barely whispered the words, reaching out to Rick’s shoulder but stopping barely an inch away from him. Even though he wasn’t looking at me – he couldn’t, I knew that – he shuddered as if my fingers had traced a line against his skin. They hadn’t, though, of course they hadn’t. It wasn’t that I didn’t want to reach out, to take him in my arms and hold him tight, to dry his eyes, to wipe the tears from his cheeks and tell him that it was all going to be alright. I wanted to be there for him, I wanted to protect him as he had done for me. I wanted to reassure him and give him what he needed. I didn’t. I couldn’t. He was my best friend, he was closer to me than family and I knew that – if I had to – I would die for him; at that moment all I could only hear – over and over and over again in the silence of my own head – the words that had shocked me out of my own despair. "That’s is the most important thing, and the most simple …you are the pillar of my life, Jay, my everything …" Rick breathed, his hand holding his friend’s face steady as his bright blue eyes locked with Jay’s emerald green ones. "… I love you." Two minutes, maybe less, since those words had been spoken. In the great scheme of things that isn’t a large amount of time. If you were only two minutes late for an appointment you were hardly late at all, if you held your breath and dived into the ocean you wouldn’t drown. Two minutes was hardly enough time to register on the mind as time’s passage at all. Normally. When someone says I love you – when someone not only says the words but means them, with every fibre of their body, heart and soul – two minutes of silence may as well be forever. Rick’s tears began to fall two minutes – maybe less – after he spoke those words. Two minutes, actually, after he spoke those words, his breath warm on my face as his blue eyes locked with my own green ones, and two minutes after I did nothing but stare at him. Two minutes of silence where, if the Spirits alone were listening, the only sound would have been that of his heart breaking. Two minutes were my silence spoke louder than any words and, by simply not saying anything at all, I said everything that he needed to hear; or, to be exact, what he hoped he wouldn’t hear. I don’t know what it took to speak those words, how long he had wanted to do so but – knowing him as well as I do – I know that he had probably spent a lifetime holding it inside; we had shared a lifetime together and I, in my selfish, egocentric way, had never noticed, never guessed, that his feelings for me were so strong, so different, so real. I could have told him that I loved him too – it would have not only been easy but it would have been true. I did love him; I knew that. He had been part of my life since I was a child and he had been the one true constant in all that time. … but while I loved him I knew that it wasn’t the same thing; I knew that it wasn’t enough. I knew that if I had said the words he would have heard the meaning. So I said nothing at all. … and watched as pain filled his eyes, watched as understanding washed over him and, finally, watched as the tears brimmed up and over his eyes to fall in seemingly never ending streams down the face that I knew so well. And I did nothing. I reached out but stopped short. I grasped for words but swallowed them down. I tried to do something, anything, to console him – as he had always consoled me – and didn’t. I simply didn’t do anything to ease his pain. Selfishly, I was too consumed by my own. - - - - - Hawaii “So, this is you then?” The taxi came to a rolling stop at the gates, the driver whistling through his teeth as he glanced at the sight of the sprawling white house that dominated the sky line, and spluttered to a stop. Rick James sighed, relief evident in the escape of breath, and scrabbled for the door handle with a haste that implied that he was afraid that the driver would start driving again; driving, of course, was not quite what Rick would term what their journey from the airport had entailed but until his mind and stomach came to a halt – something that he hoped would happen soon, now that the infernal vehicle had stopped moving – he couldn’t find a word that adequately described it any better. “Yeah,” he muttered through clenched teeth as he lurched out of the taxi and took a deep breath in to calm his nerves, “this is us.” “I didn’t even know that this place was still standing,” the taxi driver mentioned as he popped the trunk and started lifting out the suitcases from inside, “let alone still taking guests, though why anyone would want to …” Rick noticed the slight shudder that ran through the taxi driver’s body as he glanced again through the bars of the gates towards the imposing building. Handing over some folded bills he smiled as driver’s attention firmly – and quickly – snapped to attention as he noticed the money. “Keep the change,” Rick smiled, easily, “but tell me, what were you going to say about this place?” The taxi driver placed the money inside his shirt pocket and, briefly, seemed to consider what he should say. Patting the money, absently, he looked back over his shoulder before walking back to the car. “Old wives’ tales, my friend,” he chuckled to himself, “nothing more than ghost stories to scare the local children an’ here is me, old enough to know better, still believing them!” Slamming the door behind him the taxi drive grinned through the window as the car wheezed and rattled into life. “You have a good vacation, my friends,” he waved out as he spun the wheels in the loose dirt and gravel of what passed as a road. “Wait,” Rick called out as the car passed him, “what ghost stories … what are you talking about?” “Dammit!” “What’s wrong?” Rick spun on his heel, frustration evident on his face which he quickly – and unsuccessfully – tried to hide, and tried his best to smile as he saw Jay Phoenix sitting on the pile of cases that the taxi driver had left by the gate. He took in the slumped shoulders, the clouded eyes and the listless expression and sighed under his breath. Since leaving Detroit a couple of days ago Rick had tried his best to convince Jay that the trip was what he needed – what they both needed – to try to make sense of everything that had happened. He had tried to convince himself too. So far, though – while neither man really admitted it – they were both barely holding on. Holding things in, yes, but coping or dealing with everything that had passed between them? No. Not yet. “Wrong?” Rick smiled, slightly, hoping that Jay hadn’t heard the word ‘ghost’, “nothing at all Jay. Look at this place, my friend, it is just as the agent said it would be and I think that it will be perfect for us … for you … to recuperate for a while!” “Recuperate?” Jay mimicked, sarcastically, “Yeah, Rick, that is just what I need – some sea air and the fact that I was mind raped by the fuckin’ ghost of my dead twin will be washed away and things will be all good again!” Biting back the angry retort Rick tried to remember what the doctor had said. Post traumatic stress brought on by the psychotic break that everyone assumed Jay had gone through; it didn’t matter, Rick assumed, that ‘they’ were all wrong. Only a few people knew – and believed – that a spirit that claimed to be his dead brother had indeed possessed him but, ultimately, the effects were the same. Jay Phoenix might be free of the possession that had claimed two years of his life but he was left broken, haunted by the memories that he wished were nothing more than childhood terrors and left knowing that he had been violated; a shell of his former self and, for someone used to being strong, used to being the ‘hero’ he was now the victim. And he didn’t know how to cope with it. Or even if he wanted to. Two days ago, however, two people had said two very different things. He himself had asked – begged – Rick to kill him, to put him out of his misery. In return he had felt the retort of a slap across the face – brought on by fear and anger – before Rick told him that he loved him. Two things that would normally have remained unspoken but, at that time, in that place, had been voiced and lives were changed. Both men tried to pretend that they hadn’t heard the other’s words, tried to move on but, ultimately, they couldn’t. All they could do was agree to go away from it all, to go somewhere where no-one knew them, where they could try – try hard – to sort things out and move on, past everything that had been done and said. So here they were, on Maui, jet legged, tired and emotional. Finally they were somewhere where no one knew what they had been through; who they were. “Aiyana Hakan” a woman’s voice called out from behind them, her tone deep and gravely, “about time you got here!” The look of surprise on Jay’s face mirrored, almost exactly, that on Rick’s. It wasn’t the fact that someone had called out to them without them hearing or seeing her approach, they knew that they had been deep in conversation. It was the two words that she had called out. Aiyana Hakan. The name that she had called out. The spirit name that Phoenix had been christened with so many years ago. The spirit name that, apart from his grandfather only Jay and Rick knew about. The name that no one, especially on an island where no one knew them, could possibly be spoken. Jumping to his feet, knocking the cases flying, Phoenix spun to stare in shock at the small, round figure of the woman who stood behind him, hands on hips. His mouth fell open, jaw slack, as his jade green eyes - filling with tears that he couldn't control or understand - locked with green eyes – framed in a sun wrinkled face webbed with lines – that mirrored his own. “Give your mama a hug, boy!”
