First Impressions

26 March 2008
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: March 26, 2008 “…so Tony Rolo, legend or not, you will not ruin my first night in PRIME. I may not be a known quantity yet, but folks, by the end of the night, I guarantee you that you’ll all know exactly what Troy Douglas is all about… …See you at the end of the road.” The red light on the camera blinked away into nonexistence, and Troy finally allowed himself to take a deep breath. He’d cut the promo on pure adrenaline, like he always had, and as soon as that little red light faded, he felt an enormous weight come off his back. He’d had to cut a lot of promos in the past, but there had been few as important as this one. He’d made an impression in the Dual Halo, to be sure, but with 51 bodies flying around, it was easy to get lost in the shuffle. Even though he was one of the final 10 people standing, Troy knew he wasn’t the story of the night. So, if he was going to stick around, he knew he had to make the most of it the first time the cameras were focused solely on him. “Thanks for coming down, Troy,” the producer said. He hadn’t memorized all the names yet. If he had his way, he’d be around long enough that these people would all make his Christmas card list. “No problem,” Troy said. “Never a second chance to make a good first impression, right?” “If you say so.” “I’m heading back to the hotel. You guys have my number if you need me for anything.” With that, he left the backstage area of the American Airlines Center. He picked up his favorite, worn leather jacket from the coat rack by the door, slung it over his shoulder, and made the short walk to the VIP parking lot. Always on the move, Troy, he thought. Never gets any easier, does it? He slid into the driver’s seat of his rented Jeep, turned on the ignition and peeled out onto the aptly named Victory Avenue. It was a quick right turn and a few blocks to his hotel. Troy turned left. Dinner and a shower could wait. There was someone he needed to see first. ******* Radio wasn’t the ideal career for someone who wanted to settle down, and Ben Castor knew it. From the day he’d picked up his Masters in broadcast journalism from Northwestern, he’d ping-ponged around the country more times than he could even remember. Burlington. Fort Lauderdale. Grand Rapids. Indianapolis. Chicago. Home in Philly. Now, Dallas/Fort Worth. He’d taken the ESPN Radio gig just two weeks before, and he arrived in the Metroplex not 48 hours ago. Boxes were strewn across the apartment floor, creating a cluttered topography littered with bubble wrap, packing peanuts and disregarded clumps of duct tape. “This place is an absolute shithole, kid.” The voice came from the open front door, but Ben recognized it instantly. “You’re late, Troy.” “ You think I can still keep track of time after all these years?” Troy said. “I’m pretty sure that you’re STILL waiting for me to show up at Darwin’s back in February of senior year.” “Damn straight. You still owe me 20 bucks for beer that night.” “Well, I’m helping you move, so that pretty much forgives anything that I did during four years of living together at Syracuse.” At that, the man who made his life arguing over the airwaves had no response. “You got me there. Help me move this table, would you?” It went on like that for a while. Two old roommates, going back and forth, not talking about anything in particular. They had a routine, one they’d fallen into over 15 years of friendship, and they didn’t often deviate. They moved things around the apartment, both arguing over and over about the proper placement of this couch or that chair. When they’d finally finished, it came up. “I’m having the dream again,” Troy said. “I thought it stopped?” “For a while, yeah. But, there’s still times where I see myself in that hotel in Scotland, and then that damn phone rings and… …Forget it.” “You can’t be guilt-tripped for the rest of your life, Troy,” Ben said. “It happened, it sucked, but it’s been five years. You went through your fair share of shit for this, but you moved on. Don’t let this be your life.” Ben had always been Troy’s personal version of a Greek chorus, and it bugged the hell out of him. But, when he was right, he was right. “You’ve really got this whole debate thing down, don’t you?” “Talk radio does that to you. Teaches you to cut through the bullshit.” They would’ve kept going, but Troy was interrupted by the beeping of the cell phone in his right pocket. “Damn,” Troy said. “PRIME time.” “You still make the shittiest puns in the world, you know that.” “Proud of it. I got you some comped tickets, if you can make it over.” “I’ll do my best.” Troy grabbed his jacket and slipped out the door. He would have stayed if he had the choice, but in the end, he had business to attend to. How appropriate that it would start at 2500 Victory Avenue, smack dab in the middle of Victory Park. ****** “In the shuffling madness of the locomotive breath, runs the all-time loser, headlong to his death.” --“Locomotive Breath” Jethro Tull He had to work for a long time to get a song like that out of his head. Troy lived with a reputation of being the guy that nobody could count on. He’d lost his best shot at a world championship because people thought he couldn’t live up to the pressure. Inconsistency plagued him for eight years in the wrestling ring. His career was filled with low expectations. He wasn’t the guy people looked at as a star. They liked watching him, for sure, they could cheer for him, but he wasn’t the guy that the crowd was going to get behind for the long haul. They’d seen him enough to think that he would flake out before long, no matter how much Troy tried to defy that. The last 18 months he’d tried his best. The next 20 minutes were going to be even more important. That little red light that he lived to be in front of, it was about to be turned on. All the bullshit in his past, all the missed opportunities, all the loads he’d beared, he had a chance to exorcise them all with one, career-making performance. He had nothing against Tony Rolo, but that didn’t mean Troy wouldn’t take him on a one way trip to the end of the road. After all, you never get a second chance to make a good first impression.
