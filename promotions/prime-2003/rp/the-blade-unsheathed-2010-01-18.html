The Blade Unsheathed

18 January 2010
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: January 18, 2010 Nishinari-ku, Osaka, Japan. 6:15 a.m. As the windows of his spartan apartment light up with a rosy hue that brings no warmth, Mamoru Azuma, kneeling, palms flat-down on the low table, regards the gun lying in front of him with a dissatisfied expression. It's too small, he thinks. It's cold outside. It's cold inside - the thin, poorly insulated walls doing little to keep the chill from creeping ghost-like into the room where it hangs palpably around Azuma's shoulders, a wraith, an angel of death whispering all of the beautiful lies and ugly truths of the world into his grizzled ear. At 38 years old, he is no longer a young man, though in truth he never was, born into this world with an old man's heart, a hard gaze the only one he was ever capable of bringing to bear upon his surroundings. There may have been a time before that, a time of warmth, but as with most idylls, it has been lost; the harsh, cold sunlight of the years bleaching everything to a uniform white. This gun, this gun, what was it called again? He can't remember what the guy said. A Beretta, maybe, but he could just be pulling the name out of a movie he saw. He had never bothered to learn about guns, so risky to own in Japan, his fists all the protection he ever needed. This one, though, it seemed so small, so inappropriate to the task. He imagined a skinny boy carrying it through some American ghetto, tucked into the waistband of his pants. A water pistol, a child's gun. For what he paid, he expected something more. He remembers how his heart sank when the man placed the gun in his hand, how light it felt, not how he imagined it at all. He had carried with him all through his life the belief that death should be heavy, something substantial. But in the end this seemed to be just one more delusion to add to the heap. The man had smiled at him ingratiatingly, an ugly smile showing too much gum, his teeth crooked and yellowed. Looking from the gun in his palm to the salesman standing in front of him, quivering slightly with some sort of palsy, Azuma had to fight back the urge to cave in his skull with the barrel of the pistol. Not bothering to return the smile, he had paid the man and left without a word. Now that he's sitting in his apartment with the gun in front of him, however, he feels disgusted at himself for having gone through with the transaction. He wishes that he had in fact brained the man, or at the very least refused payment for the undersized weapon. It had been so expensive. Not that money was a problem at this stage, it was just the principle of the matter, he felt taken advantage of. It was not a feeling he was used to, and he hated himself for allowing his momentary discomfort to drag him down the path of unwise decisions. If only the thing had more of a heft to it, he would feel more comfortable going through with it. But one shot from this puny thing couldn't possibly get the job done, and being able to get a second shot off after the first was certainly no guarantee. No, it simply wouldn't do at all. He raises his hand from the table, running it over the stubble on his chin, up over his flattened nose, past his closed eyes, creased with early signs of age, dragging it through the hard spiky run of his close-cropped hair before dropping it back to the table again. Reaching out past the gun, his fingers close upon a open bottle of Nihonshu, which he mechanically pours into his sake cup, downing it cold, without a shiver, nor a sign of satisfaction. It's going to be cold today, he thinks sadly, before pouring himself another shot. ============================ Kita-ku, Osaka. 6:31 a.m. "So you guys are all professional wrestlers?" Kazuya Araki opens his mouth and is halfway through his next sentence when he stops, startled. He was sure he had mentioned it at some point in the night. In fact, a good part of the last three hour's conversation had surely focused on wrestling, all his plans being tied up in it these days. This was just confirmation that the girl next to him didn't understand English (though she said she did), she hadn't been listening, or most likely a combination of the two. He considers complaining to the staff, before deciding it really doesn't matter. It's not like he had ever required a willing listener to go off at the mouth before. At least this girl was better looking then the drunken bar room oyaji he typically subjected to his rants and tirades. "Yeah, we're all wrestlers. Shit, my grandfather is practically a legend in the sport. Hideyuki Araki, you know? Giant Araki, owner and president of Go-Rin Puroresu....though the more I think about it, the less I'm capable of imagining you've ever heard of it. Or care." The Russian hostess beside him nods her head, not having a clue what he's talking about but still smiling warmly as she pours him another glass of whiskey, diluting it slightly with a few squirts of the water dispenser on the table. "Your English is very good," she says, running a hand coyly through her blond hair before placing it on his thigh. "Yeah, well it better be, after eight years of school in the States," he says, knocking back half of his drink with a single motion. He reaches into his pocket and knocks a cigarette out of his pack, placing it in his mouth. He speaks around it, like Humphrey Bogart. "Plus my dad was American. Is. Who knows. I'm sure it helps, though." "Americans seem like they're friendly," she says, lighting his cigarette. "Yeah, well, everyone's friendly as long as you've got money," he says, and she nods so emphatically that he almost believes she's understood him at last. Money he's got plenty of, alright, but it's family money, and though he spends it, he's done little to add to the coffers himself: a fact his grandfather never hesitates to remind him of. He'd pissed away thousands of dollars worth of tuition in the US, dropping out of law school at UC Irvine mid-way through his first year to announce he wanted to be a pro wrestler, wanted to contribute to the company that was the family's legacy. The old man was less than pleased. He's tired of being made to feel like a freeloader, though. He's got ideas, a shit-ton of them. He wants to revolutionize the sport as it's known in Japan. He could do it, too, if the old man would only give him a chance. He wishes he could communicate all of this to the girl sitting next to him, talk out his problems, unburden himself, but in the end he decides he's better off just complimenting her on her tits. "You like?" she says, glancing down at them before giggling and adjusting her hand three inches to the left to rest gently on his crotch. Before he's able to capitalize on the situation, the bar master walks over to his table, placing the bill contained in a small leather book in front of him. Kazuya waits for the guy to head back to the bar, then waits some more. Ten seconds pass before he raises his eyebrow in question. "We're closing," the master says, clearly a man of few words. As the blond removes her hand from his genitals, Kazuya sighs and gives the guy the once over. He's a big guy, probably a former athlete, though middle age has not been kind to his figure. He looks uncomfortable in a white dress shirt with the top two buttons undone to reveal a match of gray chest hair and some thin gold chains. Shaking his head, Kaz reaches for the bill. "Three hundred thousand yen? What the fuck is this?!" he shouts, his voice going high enough to crack. He gives an uneasy look to his two companions, on either side of him, who only shrug in response, looking confused. The girls have already abandoned them, taking the glasses and bottles back behind the bar on the other side of the room. "It's your bill for the night's services," the Russian says in broken Japanese. "We accept most credit cards if that would be a preferable form of payment." "But three hundred thousand yen? We didn't even fuck those bitches!" As he stands up, Kaz feels the whiskey going to his head. He sees, now that he's vertical, that he's barely taller than the Russian's shoulder. His friends, while taller and more bulky, seem to be a great deal more intoxicated. "This is a classy establishment, Mr. Araki," the owner says, encompassing the room with a sweep of his hand. "These are classy girls. The finest in Russia." "Finest in Russia, my ass. I'll be surprised if half these girls could find Russia on a map," Kaz says, glaring over the proprietor's shoulder at the girls huddling at the counter, talking together in some language that sounds nothing like Russian. He throws the bill down on the table. "I'm not paying this." "Oh, I think you will, Mr. Araki," the Russian says, the corner of his mouth turning up in a smile, as two men who were clearly listening in on the conversation step out from the back. They're similar in build and temperament to the owner, but much younger, and lacking the older man's smile, looking as if they've never smiled in their lives. As Kazuya glances from the three men in front of him, to his friends, looking greener and sicker by the minute, it doesn't take him long to calculate his disadvantage. Three hundred thousand yen. Fucking ridiculous. He could already imagine his grandfather laying into him over this latest indiscretion. He lets out a laugh, full of self-pity. "What's so funny, Mr. Araki?" the Russian asks, allowing his smile full reign now to play out across his face. "Ah, fuck it," he says. "Eat shit, Boris." Kaz lands a right hand across the bridge of the owner's nose. The man barely blinks as his head jerks slightly back then moves forward again. A thin rivulet of blood moves slowly down from his right nostril. His smile fades. So does Kazuya's. "Oh, you will pay, alright" the owner declares, as the two men on either side of him begin to move in on Kazuya. ============================ Abeno-ku, Osaka. 9:00 a.m. He tries not to wake her as he tiptoes to the door over the scattered bits and pieces of her outfit from the night before. Before trying the knob he takes an inventory of his pockets to make sure he hasn't forgotten anything. Satisfied, he slowly opens the door, careful to avoid producing any tell-tale creak or squeak that might raise an alarum from the petite figure sprawled out on the futon on the floor. She's got enough alcohol in her system to knock out an African rhino, but then, you never know with Japanese girls, they're always surprising you. Safely emerging from the apartment building without having given himself away, Trevor Hawke begins to whistle to himself as he walks down the street, his hands in his pockets. It's a brisk morning, but nothing compared to the Chicago winters he grew up with. Two months into winter here and he has yet to see any snow, not that he's complaining. Nope, there's nothing really to complain about at all. Seven years in Japan and he's finally gotten the hang of things, can finally speak the language passably after dicking around so long, and he's recognized across the country as a premier junior heavyweight, at the peak of his career, in the midst of never-ending tag team championship run. So, as is most often the case when things seem too good to be true, Trevor, utilizing his usual furtive acts of subversion, is looking to bring the whole thing down, or if not the whole than at least a significant chunk. It's not what you would call an active undermining, exactly, but more of an operation taking place on the subconscious level. The id as diabolical farmer, operating on some twisted principle of crop rotation, sewing seeds of chaos in the hopes of preparing the fields for some richer harvest that never comes. Enacting a radical reverse fertilization, if you will, where the rows of carefully cultivated crops shrivel up, shrink into themselves, and are sucked into the ground, leaving only the empty fields fallow with shit. If only he wasn't so quick to tire of things, even those beautiful, precious ones that infuse life with meaning, perhaps he could avoid this meaningless destruction. But for Trevor, meaning was fluid, always moving, grasping, consuming. A molten flow that he needed to keep one step ahead of, less it swallow him up, eradicate his boundaries, solidify around him and crystallize into a web of relationships and high valence bonds from which there was no escape. All of which would go some way, though not nearly far enough, to explain why he leaves his Riho, his fiancée of three years home alone in order to spend his nights fucking nameless girls in miniskirts and hooker boots, luring them in, in clubs and bars, under false pretenses, and sneaking out of their apartments at the crack of dawn to avoid the awkwardness of exchanging fake telephone numbers the morning after. Ah, Riho. He had fed her the same excuse he always did: that he was out drinking with the guys from the Go-Rin dojo. It was a completely believable alibi, just business as usual in Japan, where drinking with co-workers is obligatory. Which, in fact, it was during his first years with the company, as he worked hard to prove himself and build his reputation with the guys on the roster. It was the kind of excuse that wouldn't even be questioned, not that she would even try, as trusting as she was. He wonders if she waited up for him, expecting him back on the first train. She did that a lot at first, the nights that she could keep herself from falling asleep, at least. Probably not tonight, he decides, she hardly ever did that anymore, accustomed now to his absence. He'd creep into bed to find her already asleep, and wake up at noon the next day to find her smiling at him, never a complaint about the lonely nights. He feels a brief pang in his chest, rising up from whatever nameless spiritual organ the conscience resides in. He's not completely heartless, after all. He's just another fucked-up asshole in a city full of them. ============================ Tennoji-ku, Osaka. 1:30 p.m. As the sun slowly climbed in the sky, Azuma had decided to walk from Nishinari to Tennoji, about half an hour away, the sake drunk earlier numbing him to the cold. While not technically a slum, Nishinari was about as close as you could get in Japan: homeless men and women drinking 200 yen bottles of whiskey in their sleeping bags outside of convenience stores, toothless old men shouting catcalls at women on their way to work, the stench of piss welling up rank in the walkways beneath the overpass, stronger than any animal stink, you knew it could only be the stench of mankind. The street became more gentrified the closer he came to Tennoji, the southern hub of the city: trees planted on the sidewalk, a diligent police force with too much time on their hands making sure the homeless were swept away somewhere that no one of importance could see them. He circled the main train station, stopping for nearly a minute in front of the main entrance, deep in thought, before continuing his circle. On a whim, seeing the zoo across the street, he crossed and bought a ticket to enter. He took his time walking around, saw rhinos, the lions, the too-thin polar bear. He stopped for a while in front of the monkey cages, sitting on a bench to drink a coke. They were grooming each other, picking bugs or who knows what out of each other's fur. They didn't eat them like he imagined they would, but maybe they weren't bugs after all. They were far away and it was difficult to see. Eventually he got up to leave, heading back toward the entrance. He passed the zebra. Shit was falling out of its ass. He went back to the station and went to a small udon stand inside for lunch. Surrounded by middle management types in suits and ties slurping their noodles obnoxiously, Azuma felt lonely for the monkeys' company, felt that it would be far more preferable. Finishing his noodles, he paid and walked down the stairs to get to the subway entrance. He didn't want to have to use the train. It wasn't the kind of statement he had wanted to make at all. Such a passive way of going about it. A falling out of life, when he had wanted to take matters into his own hands, had wanted to go out with a bang,. Though it wasn't as if any of it really mattered in the end. Taking his ticket from the machine, he passed through the gate and descended the staircase. ============================ Chuo-ku, Osaka. 2:45 p.m. Hideyuki "Giant" Araki, at the age of 83, is still an impressive physical specimen in the land of Japan. Even hunched and shrunken in his old age, he still hovers a good ten centimeters over the head of his grandson, who through some accident of genetics, had not inherited the man's height. Looking down on him, he glowers across his desk at Kazuya, sitting contrite in a chair. "Well, does it make you feel like a tough guy? Huh?" Kazuya shakes his head without saying anything, looking at the floor. He was having a difficult time opening his right eye, and his swollen lip gave his speech a strange slur that made him feel like the Elephant Man. "You're lucky those Russians knew who you were. Or rather, that they knew who I was. If you weren't my grandson...well, you wouldn't have walked your sorry ass into here, I'll tell you that much. They would have had to wheel you in." Though he's perfectly fluent in English, his grandfather insists on speaking to him in Japanese, trapping him into the structure of the language's honorifics that give the old man one more psychological advantage over the him. The old man leans forward, reaching his arm out from behind his back to pick up a piece of paper sitting on the desk. "And there's still the matter of this...thing." He spits the last word, causing Kazuya to wince. "Grandfather, that bill is completely outrageous. There's no reason for you to have to pay it." "Shut up! You're a stain on this family's good name. And dragging Nakamura and Tasaki with you to the red light district when they should be training. Is the only reason you've come back from America: to attempt to drive my promotion into the ground? Weren't you content with simply wasting my money? Now you need to set about destroying my livelihood? Have you no shame?!" Kazuya swallows deeply, "Actually, Grandfather, I have some ideas for the business I was hoping to get your opinion on?" "What, your plan to train that junkie pop star to be a pro wrestler?" Kaz is taken aback. He had been in talks with the representation of Japanese idol Nami Okuda for almost a month, ever since she had received her suspended sentence for possession of methamphetamines. She had confined herself to her house in an attempt to escape the paparazzi, but he had been working hard to convince her to make her return to the public eye, to reinvent herself as a wrestling star. He felt it was a sure-fire plan to increase revenue, but he had yet to mention it to anyone in the company. "How did you....?" "You think you can make plans behind my back without me finding out?" His grandfather laughs. "I won't have you turning my business into a media circus." "But...." "We won't discuss it now. I have more pressing business. A job for you that will make use of your sole talent while getting you out of my hair for a while." "My sole talent?" "Your English ability. We're entering into a talent exchange with PRIME and we need an English speaking liaison. Your English is better than anyone else in the company." "What about Trevor's?" "Mahha, he's a great wrestler, one of the best junior heavyweights I've seen in a long time," the old man says, shaking his head. "But you know the board would never approve." "He's not one of us, you mean to say." "Yes, yes. It's got to be you," his grandfather says, sliding a file across the desk. "So get ready, you're leaving in three days." "This is all so sudden..." Kazuya says tentatively as he picks up the paperwork. His head jerks up, swollen eyes fluttering open in complaint. "But I've still got business in Osaka I need to take care of!" "Business?" the old man says with a laugh. "You're a failure as a wrestler, undersized, with no natural talent, no credentials, no work experience worth a damn. What business could you possibly have? No, you can get on that train in three days time or you can find yourself another family to mooch off of. And good luck with that!" Kazuya looks back down at the file, at the bullet train ticket to Saitama with his name already printed on it. He knows from a lifetime of verbal beatings that a direct assault is the absolute worse way to approach an argument with his grandfather. Once again he'll have to just take his lumps, bide his time, wait for his opportunity. Bowing, he apologizes once again, thanking his grandfather for the opportunity. The old man, looking out the window, only grunts in response, a wave of the hand showing his grandson the door. Kazuya leaves the office, bowing again as he backs out the doorway, just in case his grandfather can catch his reflection in the window. It's been twenty-five years, this spell of bad luck couldn't possibly last too much longer. His time has got to be coming up soon. If he could just hold out a little longer, a little longer. He can feel it. The start of something great. ============================ Tennoji-ku, Osaka. 5:15 p.m. Azuma has been sitting on the subway bench for the past three hours, watching the trains come and go. They roar into the station, and as soon as the doors open, people pour out in a rush, frantic, half-crazed, as if escaping from a fire, and then just as immediately, are replaced with equally crazed masses eager to leap back into the flames. He watches expressionless men in expensive looking suits knock past withered old ladies barely able to stand, plow through toddling kids unlucky enough to be in the paths of their daily business. It's not that he's exactly scared or hesitant to put an end to things. Just that, well, it seems to be so anticlimactic. He supposes he's waiting for a sign. Something, anything would do. Even if it were just a station attendant coming over and asking him to move, telling him that he couldn't sit there all day, that would be enough to get him to stand up, walk over to the edge of the platform, and drop in front of the next passing train. Nobody bothers to speak to him, though, or even acknowledge his existence at all. Every now and then someone comes to sit in the seat next to him, but only for as long as it takes for the next train to arrive, and then they're gone. A young college-aged girl comes and sat next to him and then, catching him looking at her, silently moves away from him, shifting to the next seat. Two minutes later, she's gone too. As time has passed, he's noticed that he's not the only person lingering in the station. About ten meters down, there's another man, perhaps in his mid-twenties. Just looking at him, he seems non-descript, in jeans and a thin green and white hooded sweatshirt. The way he's jittering uncomfortably, though, moving his body weight back and forth between his two feet, almost hopping, it's unsettling. From time to time Azuma can hear snatches of his muttering, but it's sporadic and unintelligible, whether garbled by the distance or by the labyrinthine twists of a subnormal mind, he cannot tell. He watches how people come and stop, waiting for the train in the man's near vicinity, watches the realization dawn on their faces that they are not in the presence of a sane man, semi-panicking as they look for an excuse to move away, usually in the direction of the garbage cans or vending machines, some going so far as to buy a can of coffee that they don't even really want in order to contribute to the facade of their escape. Or so he imagines. He sees a middle-aged woman walk up to the edge of the platform, standing in front of the man. He waits expectantly for her to notice him, almost eager now to see the unease dawn on her face when he pops her bubble of normalcy. His amusement quickly dissipates, however, when he sees the man in the sweatshirt pull a small knife from his handbag and creep up on the woman standing in front of him. Azuma calls out, at least he thinks he does, but in the craziness of the moment, he can't tell if his voice escapes his lips, if it's lost in the sound of the passing trains, or what, but whatever the case, it's too late. The man has driven the blade hilt deep into the woman's shoulder. She spins, crying out, but he's already removed the blade and replaced it, this time in her midsection, in-and-out, in-and-out. The man is moving quickly now, advancing on a older man, slashing out at him with his knife, catching a glancing blow across his sleeve. He doesn't have a chance to get another swipe, however, as Azuma is on him, tackling him to the ground in a heap. There's a brief struggle, the man has enough time to dig the knife almost an inch into Azuma's bicep before he's over-powered by the bigger, stronger man. Pinning the wrist of the man's knife hand to the ground with one hand, kneeling on his opposite shoulder to trap the other arm, Azuma, eyes ablaze, drops a heavy hammer fist to the man's throat. A second one causes him to go limp, but there's no stopping him now. The smell of blood is in the air. All thoughts are suicide are gone from his mind, as he drives his fists into the man's face again and again. People flee from the scene in disgust, squeamish at the sight of true violence, visceral and raw, a physical presence hovering unseen in the world around us. He can hear the voices of policemen shouting at him, telling him to cease and desist, their footsteps coming closer, but he can't stop himself. Just a few more blows. Just a few more. He sees the body of the woman in the subway, like the body of the woman long ago. Another undeserving victim. Another needless death. He is no longer cold. His heart is aflame, full of hate. Hate for this world. Hate for himself.
