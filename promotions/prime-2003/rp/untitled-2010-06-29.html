Untitled

29 June 2010
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 6/29/10 It was a strange time then, but Randy Watkins was more or less oblivious to it. It wasn't no stranger then it used to be back a ways, when Randy was young, living life out of a van, but it was a different kind of strange, and there came a day when living life out of a van took on different connotations. Randy remembered the day well: March 5, 1994. It was the day his van stopped running, and he remembered cursing the weather for still being cold in March, but it wasn't too far removed from February, and it was about as cold as it should've been that time of the year according to meteorological trends and such. It wasn't no surprise neither, that a man of Randy's character would still be living out of that van five years past the expiration of its vehicular abilities, but even in the most broken down, remote places in the universe, some order exists, and for all its shortcomings, even Randy's life was not entirely without its own interpretation of order. For example, every Thursday, just after noon, Randy would wake up and walk to the laundromat with a change of clothes identical to the ones he had on, only one week dirtier, that is to say, two weeks dirty, if only one week worn and the other week left crumpled in the back of his van. While his clothes were in the wash, Randy would find a nearby store with a bathroom where he could get a little privacy to wash himself up as best he could. He usually wasn't too conscious about his appearance, except on Thursdays, when he'd meet up with his guy and replenish his stock. In truth, he was small time, far too small for anything to happen if the deal went south--nothing more than Randy Watkins, pissed off, cursing, maybe kicking a wall, or at worse, a window that didn't need to be in one piece. Randy's guy went by Joey Giordani, some mook who used too much hair gel and drove around in a muscle car. Things like that impressed Randy, though, and even though Randy hated that the younger Giordani went out of his way to be disrespectful to him, he saw what Joey had--that Trans Am and that hair gel--and he figured he could work his way up the chain. Thing was, Randy was never good at keeping his finger on the pulse, and he wasn't aware that the ice market was thinning out. More folks were cooking, and the product just wasn't market strength. It was all frozen water. Any two-bit punk with a chemistry kit was looking for a slice of pie, and most were their own distributors. Randy could've done it, but he was always a salesman before a full scale entrepreneur, at least in his own mind, cause he wasn't much anywhere else. Randy preferred liquid soap to bar soap because strangers put their hands on bar soap, and there was no telling where those hands had been. People are a dirty fucking species, and nobody knew that any better than Randy. When he finished soaping himself up--always on the face and under the armpits, only in corners below the waist in the most secure of environments or desperate of situations--Randy would dry himself off with paper towels, which were always a requirement over air dryers, even more so than bar soap. After he was clean, Randy would head back to the laundromat, by which time his clothes would be done washing, and throw them in the dryer. He'd do some waiting, a skill he'd mastered to an uncharacteristic adeptness, for there were few skills where Randy would have the interest to hone, most of which would have been impossible leftover fantasies, truly opposite to Randy's nature anyway. The laundromat had an area devoted entirely to waiting, featuring an array of donated magazines with the addresses cut off and round plastic chairs that were less comfortable than they looked, colored deceptively bright citrus flavors. Sometimes, he'd get up and burn a cigarette or two, depending on the weather, out in the parking lot, and by the by, his waiting looked like nothing spectacular. What made Randy's waiting magnificent was the natural ease in which he accomplished the feat, the rare moments that made him look genuine somehow. Thursday evenings, in clean, dry clothes, Randy would engage in a much different and solitary form of waiting. In the back of his van, Randy would sit, watching his pager until it lit up. Every time the pager lit up, Randy would jump a bit, never fully able to shake the nerves. Needless to say, there wasn't much important at stake in the grand scheme of things, but for Randy, it kept him fed, and it kept him level, and it got him by day to day in a world where it was a wonder he got by at all. Sometimes, the pager stays silent for hours, and that's when he starts to think of Lisa. Randy hasn't seen Lisa in a few weeks. She threw him out again, and he knew he deserved it this time, just like he deserved it every other time. He didn't miss her much, or at least he didn't show it, and maybe that's because eventually, whether it'd be days or months, she'd always have him back. He missed her more than usual on Thursdays, though, while he waited in that van. Randy hadn't seen Lisa, but her son Johnny had been around, and it was one of those things where he was easier to find if you weren't particularly looking for him. He knew where Lisa was, of course, without having to go out of his way to see her, and he knew where she'd look for him once it all blew over. Johnny was always around, though, here or there, and it was fine, but it wasn't always the most convenient to have Johnny around when he was with Lisa. Johnny liked to fuck around with Randy; to point out Randy's disadvantages of faculty. There wasn't anything Randy hated more than being outsmarted publicly. When Randy saw Lisa's kid hanging around, and he wasn't with Lisa, he'd ask Johnny how she was doing, and he'd engage in other such untactful topics with the rowdy teenager, often trying to build himself some credibility as a renegade in Johnny's eyes. That always failed, not only because Randy was bad at it, never the shrewd salesman that he made himself out to be, but also because the kid was too smart to buy anything that Randy would say. He could smell the words coming out of Randy's mouth, and they quite literally did smell like bullshit. One particular instance happened, in fact, the previous Thursday. Randy was standing outside the laundromat having his second smoke as his clothes dried, when Johnny came pedaling up, and in between thick coughs, Randy would say, "Kid, how's your ma doin'," and Johnny would say, "She's alright." Randy would go off on some tangent, telling some story about stealing copper wire from a construction site and selling it back to the dumb motherfuckers, only Johnny knew it wasn't true because the length of wire and the negotiated prices were never consistent, unless Randy had pulled this racket quite a number of times. Johnny doubted that, thinking it would still only warrant one telling of the story; perhaps the one with the most wire or the highest price. Thing is, Randy didn't remember he was telling Johnny the same story, more or less, every Thursday back for the last month or so, probably too busy focusing on waiting, and like the other times, Randy brushed him off and rolled him along with the same line as always. "If your ma needs a good screw, you tell her she knows where to find me, alright?" Randy sat in his van, and it was Thursday, and he didn't think that he didn't see nobody around the laundromat that he knew, because he usually ran into someone. It was a small part of a big town, and people didn't stray too far from it or come from too far to it. There was nothing there that wasn't anywhere else. Randy would say that if you remembered anything, to remember that. When Randy's pager flashed, Randy jumped a little bit, and after deactivating the tiny electronic, Randy prepared himself to meet Joey. He took a deep breath to calm his nerves back to level before peeling back a quarter of the half dilapidated mattress he kept in the back of his van. The van's paneling had been exposed to the wood planks. A small, flat board covered an exposure where Randy kept his bankroll. It grew at a slight steadiness each time he turned over, and he'd coined up enough to double his pack and push in a week what used to take him two. Sometimes he had to cut a little more off for himself to keep up with the hours, but it was worth it, financially, in the end. "The fuck's it at?" Randy checked and double checked, and he cursed and kicked the poor mattress. "Mother fucker!" He met Joey anyway in the back lot of the half-vacant strip mall, behind the giant shadow of an old hardware store husk. Randy figured maybe he could explain what happened and Joey would front him on good faith, but there was no good faith in meth. "You've got to be fuckin' with me, man." Joey patted his slick of hair and ashed his cigarette. "That's about the funniest fuckin' shit I ever heard." Randy's knuckles went white, gripping the handle of his hunting knife sheathed away in the back of his jeans, but before he needed to do anything, Joey flicked the cigarette butt to the wayside and slipped back into his Trans Am. Better luck next time. Sometime later, another time of no real concern as long as it was within some reasonable realm, Randy found himself, as he did on most Friday and Saturday nights, out there at the Rusty Pecker (which wasn't what it was called, but that's what they all called it, and the sign was busted a while back, and nobody thought much to fix it). Most people probably couldn't even tell you the real name of it, call it what you will, but that's where Lisa worked, and Randy knew her shifts, and it's where Randy drank and Lisa knew his. She brought him another one, free of charge. "Thanks again, darlin'." Neither of them were much to look at, and even plastered, Randy squinted at Lisa, trying to make her out to be more than she was, a scaly, old lizard poured into a dress a couple decades too young to fit her. "You gonna let me pay for a round? I got money." She shook her head and took his glasses, always in pairs of the scummiest beer and tequila on the menu. He watched as Lisa walked off and shook his head as Lisa, who hadn't said a word to him all night, tossed some Mexican back with one of the dishwashers. He didn't understand a lick outside of English, and none of it made any goddamn sense, but the world was laughing at him, and Randy was one of those drunks who just couldn't laugh along with it. "Your little shit took my money, didn't he?" Randy was loud, and the bar was quiet for the patrons to notice. All eyes surveyed him as he stood there, yelling towards the back to Lisa. "He fuckin' did! I'm gonna kill that little shit! I'm gonna-" The bouncer handled Randy like he had many times before, tossing him out onto the dirty pavement outside, and Randy would forget most of it but blurry spots, here and there, like there were anywhere else.
