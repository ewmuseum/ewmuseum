The Characters Of Battle (Chapter II)

22 April 2009

Date: 04.22.09 The Characters Of Battle (Chapter II) It would be a relatively easy task to look at two shapes, a circle and a square, and determine which you perceived to be which. Perhaps more relevant and complex than the composition of a shape is the motivation of a shape. Why does a circle become a circle? Why does a square become a square? It may be perplexing that circles become circles for the exact same reasons squares become sqaures, and there are many reasons that one becomes itself and not the other even if some others pursue the same motivation that led one to become itself in becoming themselves. Of course, this is a very forward and controversial idea. Many books would condemn it! Traditionally, the accepted viewpoint allowed for no fluidity between circles and squares. One was always one and the other, the other, and there was no choice involved in the matter. The perception that environment may determine -- and, as it changes, even change -- a shape. If space can change along time and time along space, then certainly, through time, a shape can change. Given the weaving of circles and squares into one giant fabric that competes to be outlined by one or the other, it is easy to see how, with careful chiseling, a circle can be cut from a square. Some steadfast believers in traditional shape theory may believe that space even expands through time, and the most stubborn zealots have even offered proof, but like any advocate of such zealotry can tell you, proof is as malleable to the shape of its argument as much as the shape of its argument is malleable to proof. This idea is further complicated by the perception of shapes by other shapes. A circle may believe itself a circle and a square a square and, over time, become a square yet still perceive the original square as a square and itself a square, or it may not. It may become a square and perceive itself as a circle and perceive the original square as a circle. In some cases, the former example, and in others, the latter, but there is no data to support any of these as realities -- at least none that can be trusted. *** Summers ago, an organization offered me a modest fee to sit behind a desk for a prearranged measure of time, and once every hour, I was to leave this post, exit through a back door and smoke a cigarette underneath a half-dome shaped carving in the building's foundation. Upon completion of the cigarette, I was instructed to drop the remains of my cigarette into a tube with a cap that opened and closed via manual operation of a fulcrum with purposely unbalanced sides. Then, I was to return to my post behind a desk and wait an hour. At the beginning and the end of the shift, I was told to write down the time I started and ended on a card with my name on it. I also tallied the number of cigarettes I had smoked by shift as a courtesy to the accounting department of my own volition. There were a couple of others the organization had hired to fill the same position. There was Shoog, who wore a black hooded sweatshirt and enjoyed tattooing and piercing himself. He brought with him a plastic bottle filled with a mixture of liquids, disguised by dilutions of color. There was also Don, who wore lively buttoned down shirts, sandals and a red face. Don was about as twice as old as either Shoog or me, and he had worked for this organization since well before either of the other of us had been there. This seniority placed him slightly higher in the chain of command, but he never held that over us. We were all climbing the same never ending stairwell that stops where the highest person stands. From that precipice, there is no more climbing, and you can only stay as long as you can stand before jumping off. If you had someone on the next stair, you had the luxury of a third option. You could wait for someone in front of you to jump off and then take another step. Of course, plenty of people jumped off before the top stair on account of that there weren't no railings. They had railings installed once, but people would just jump over them if they got sick of standing, and the higher up you got, the longer you'd have to wait because the less people there'd be who could possibly jump off. I was the newest person to be hired to do this job. I knew Shoog through various other enterprises of questionable merit, and he had alerted me of the opening. I wondered who had jumped, and what had caused them to jump. It had to have been the waiting. It was excrutiating unless you had some diversion to numb and distract you from the fact that you were waiting, but one concealable enough to preoccupy you undetected behind the desk. I ingested various agents that would slow the processes of thoughts so that maybe I could stall the inevitable conclusion that I'd one day have to jump until I reached the top. I thought none of this while smoking my cigarette. I just stared off into the distance at the road as cars passed slowly over a crosswalk, narrowly missing amateur pedestrians. "You ever see any one get hit?" I asked. "I seen people fall on ice in winter, but eventually, someone's bound to get hit, and I kinda hope I'm out here to see it." It was like I was asking myself. "I mean, the cars aren't going very fast. It wouldn't kill anyone, it'd just jostle 'em up a bit, you know?" I looked over. "Shoog?" He had slouched over and passed out again. A long binding of ash hung together where his cigarette once was. "Dammit." I lifted him over my shoulder to carry him back inside and prop him in a chair behind one of the more remote desks. *** At any point in the middle of a line, you cannot see where the line ends and where it begins. You can only see the connected points in either direction, and you must assume that there are points in front of those points and behind them. Maybe you can see a few of those points, but until you've been close enough to both ends of the line to know, it has already been to significant of a distance traveled to have charted the shape of the line, as an entirety and an entity, with any semblance of precision. Points on a line exist on too small a scale to understand their own part in composition of an arc or an angle. The two are indistinguishable as part of a shape, and while points may be represented from above -- and even on their own -- by either circles or squares, they are neither. They are just parts that make up a circle or a square, regardless of what they're considered to be, and they are equal parts in both circles and squares. For a circle to be a circle, there must be other circles to compare criteria. For a shape to be a shape, the points, as a whole, must agree to align to that shape, and points, you see, can only rely on the contact of neighboring points to form such a bias. Points that perceive themselves to be a certain shape will naturally try to convince neighboring points of the logic behind being such a shape so that the collective shape will match each point's own perception of the perfect shape. In reality, there is no perfect shape, and it's so impossible for a point to know what shape it's really a part of that geometric lobbying is almost pointless save for the all the points and all their points about shapes. From any point, you can more easily see other shapes than the shape to which you belonged, but even these shapes might easily different sections of the same shape or intersecting shapes that formed even more complex shapes. It's not really possible to tell, only to speculate about the difference between what's another shape and what isn't, and this speculation is ironically the measure that changes shapes. *** Shoog normally didn't say much, but he became sparsely verbiose at certain points of the night depending on a number of factors related to the bottle that he brought around with him. It was one of those nights. "I'm thinking of jumping," he said, looking out from under the half-dome. "Why?" I asked, surprised. "I'm just sick of all this waiting," he replied. "All we ever do is wait for the next cigarette, and the whole time we're waiting for cigarettes, it's all just one long wait until we can leave." "I suppose," I replied ponderously. "It's alright though." "I don't know, man. I think I'm done," he threw dryly. "I just don't want to wait around here anymore." "Done?" I shot back. "Say you jump. What then?" "I haven't figured that part out yet." "It's probably just more waiting," I observed. "Look around. It's not a matter of if you're waiting, just a matter of where you're waiting, you know?" "Look at Don. How long you think he's been waiting?" "Exactly!" I pointed. "If he's been here so long, he's probably got a good reason." "Yeah, man, but I haven't seen him go anywhere, and if he hasn't moved in all that time, we're not going anywhere either." He ashed. "And if there's more waiting after you jump?" "Then I'll jump again a lot earlier." "I guess that makes sense," I calculated. "Don't you ever wonder what's at the top? What it is that all these people keep waiting for?" "What are you waiting for?" he turned, like a magician, into some sort of inquisition. "I figure if I wait long enough, I'll find out," I said, unsure of how I would even know if I was done waiting. At this point, he had nearly rounded off the edge I thought I had. "Well, I'm leaving," he stood up and flicked the last of his cigarette away, disregarding the tube designated for the disposal of such things. "You can come with if you want. It's up to you." "You're just going to get up and leave, huh? Like it's that easy?" "Yep," he took a breath of air through his nostrils before he said it, too, and I just stood there dumbstruck as he walked off towards the crosswalk on the road in the distance. The bastard didn't even mark down that he was leaving on his card. He was on the other side of the street now, and I could barely see him, lost in the shuffling lines of amateur pedestrians. "Hold on a minute!" I chased after him. Damn near got hit by a car on my way across the road. *** If enough points on a shape agree that they're part of one shape or another, it'll resemble that shape, more or less. In the grand scheme of things, a circle and a square are nowhere near as different as they could be. Points on a more circular shape are more likely to view less circular circles as squares. It gets a little mushy in the middle of the spectrum, but the more circular a group of points is, the more other shapes they'll view as squares, even if those squares are part of a different segment of one larger shape. Shapes can exist within larger shapes thanks to the construction of the fabric, and from two shapes, the circle and the square, more shapes can be produced. The circle, a curved line, and the square, an assemblage of straight lines, can be arranged in layers to create other shapes, but these other shapes are only combinations of varying lengths of straight and curved lines. Thus, at the heart, there are only two shapes, each made of points which stiffen, bend and even break. There are circles, and there are squares, and they don't like each other. There are circles that don't like other circles, and there are squares that don't like other squares, and circles that more circular circles see as squares and so on and so forth. There is a mess of wild amalgamations in between, but at the root of it, whichever side perceives a wider range of that mess of amalgamations as a common shape dominates, but as shapes change shape through time, the shape regarded more commonly amongst itself does, too, back and forth, at the will of various speculating defectors. *** It had been some time, but I had returned to the building, under the sway of inebriation, to see if there were still waiting. They had put a statue of a sitting man outside the door underneath the half-dome. I sat next to the statue, laughed, and lit up a cigarette for old times' sake. I had been just about right about everything; about the waiting in various lines and the jumping, and no matter when I jumped, there would be more waiting waiting for me somewhere else. I was just as miserable jumping out of line early as I was jumping out of line after a considerable amount of waiting. I guessed you could only jump out of so many lines before you had to wait in the same one forever. "Johnny! I haven't seen you in ages," Don, though soft-spoken, was excited to see me. He was so old that he didn't look any older. "What have you been up to?" "Nothing, really," my idle chatter fell unreceived as Don lit his cigarette. "I heard you moved out of town." "Yeah," I nodded. "You going to be back in town for a while?" idly, idly, idly. "For as long as I can manage, I suppose," careful not to make any points that would make any points stir. "Anything new around here?" "Nah, not really," Don confirmed. "A few weeks ago, some amateur pedestrian got hit by a car." "No shit?" I perked up, a bit let down. "Yeah, the car was going slow though, so luckily nobody was hurt too bad," he huffed quietly, saving his breath for his efficiently smoked cigarette. "It was big news around here for a few days, though." "I can imagine," I said, staring off blankly, waiting for lightning to strike twice as if there were two slices of bread in a toaster. "Well, another hour down," Don brushed his hands off each other after having properly disposed of his cigarette. "I have to get back inside. You sticking around here for another hour?" "Nah," I shook my head. "It was good to see you again, Johnny. Feel free to stop by if you're in town for a few days and you got ten minutes for a smoke." "Sure thing, Don. It was good seeing you, too." I guess Don had figured out the whole waiting thing a long time ago, but I'm sure it took some jumping of his own to do it for him, too. The statue would still be sitting there, even longer than Don would be.
