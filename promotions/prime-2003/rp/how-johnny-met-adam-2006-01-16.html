How Johnny Met Adam

16 January 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 1/16/06 Forgive me if my recollection of the events as they took place that night are historically inaccurate, but I was on about five grams of shrooms at the time. So if any of this recollection is too far fetched to believe or physically impossible, feel free to disregard it. Otherwise, it’s all true. Without further ado, this is the story of how I met the man who taught me everything I now know about life. His name was Adam Dick. It was one of those hot cabana nights, the one where the jet stream does funny things to your brain because the sun reflects so hard off the full moon that the fleshy smell of sweat still hasn’t cooled from the air. It smelled almost as if humanity has been cooking in its own collective boil. The stench was so thick that it formed a vapor of stringy individual trails. If you pulled hard enough at one of these strings to wrestle it from the vapor’s contiguity, you could follow it hand by hand like some Incan rope bridge to find its source and his route, destination, and larger purpose or meaning. You could figure out the minutest details about a person with information like this if only the heat wasn’t so damned paralyzing. It pressed against me with a heavy, wet force and I was melting into my chair on the street-side, gated patio outside the café. The baristas were all suspiciously glancing at me from time to time as I hadn’t gone to the counter. I had been sitting there for quite some time, barely twitching. And if you listened closely, in the gaps between the motor song of traffic, you could hear me screaming violently because I thought the universe had momentarily sped up, and it was upsetting my stomach. Still, it was quite decent conversation that was going on behind the rationalist on my right hand side and the idealist on my left- even if I was too busy screaming and trying to return the ping pong shots of ambient street noise coming at me from all directions to catch all of the nuances in their arguments and rebuttals. It caught me, at the time, that all these sounds were just the bubble skin molecules bursting around me. If you listen hard enough, a popped bubble sounds just like a firecracker. “If sound is just waves traveling through the air, is sound, as a composite, the collection of those waves, or are all waves disconnected just as images are by the separation of their atomic particles?” “You can’t base your argument on that logic. What you have to remember is that the human senses are imperfect interfaces. We don’t see what is really there because our vision does not process these separations.” “Invisible science only exists in theory; outside of our physical concepts of reality. The sensory interfaces do work perfectly in that the information they relay to the mind is physically factual. We’re living dual existences and physical science tries- and hilariously futilely might I add- add crossing over to the other side.” “What about the Laws of Physics? Their calculations were conceptualized in a completely mental arena, but their effects are validly measurable in the physical concept as well. Doesn’t that defeat your outlook?” “Not necessarily, you see, because it is only when these concepts are promulgated that we agree that we have collectively observed them.” “We know that gravity has been around before it was discovered. Everything, in fact, that was discovered has existed previous to its discovery.” “How do we know, though, until after it’s recorded?” I couldn’t concentrate, so I blunted myself on the head with a stone I had picked up on my way here. I was sick of hearing the two of them go at it all night, and I had begun to think that they’d keep going forever, but I could never find the right moment to interject. I didn’t know either of them well enough to interrupt. I suppose I could have- and I had every right to do so- but they had seemed so strange and foreign to me that night. When I came to, they had both gone off their separate ways, but the chain reaction pop of traffic burst on in spite of tiny peaks and crackles every now and then. The vapor began to lift after a while, but it turned out I was just growing used to it. “Once you accept that something is there, you barely notice it anymore.” I shrugged to myself after trying to tell that to anyone around me, but nobody seemed impressed enough to confirm that they had heard me. This only served to prove my point, and for some reason I became struck with a panic to disprove my own point. They all had gotten used to me, and I feared I was beginning to disappear. Furthermore, I began trying to disprove everything I said aloud; even vocalized and self-directed assurances that “I wasn’t going crazy.” Then, a man stepped out of the café. He was overdressed, and there was something armoring about his glib attire. He passed me and smiled while checking his watch so that he was sure I noticed it. Then he stopped and cleared his throat as if he was waiting for a response. “That’s a nice watch,” I said, and he turned to me doing his best to act as if he hadn’t been trying to persuade the words out of me. “I picked it up in a little shop on my recent trip to the German highlands. Buisness. You know.” He shot laser sharp jingoistic one word sentences as me, and they rendered me submissive to the situation so that I couldn’t adequately defend myself by behaving unexpectedly deviant. “They’ve got some of the best watchmakers up there. They make everything by hand, working with delicate tools to handle and affix such tiny, intricate pieces together.” “How about that,” “There’s a funny thing about this watch, though, and that’s that it doesn’t tell time. It only tells you what time you’re going to die.” “Hmm,” I pondered, beginning to really feel kind of ill about the direction this was going. “It never really fit me right. It kind of pinches my wrist, but your wrist looks just about the right size for it. Why don’t you take it?” He took the watch off as he spoke and held it out in front of me. I opened my palm to take it as he held it a full arm’s length from his body at me. “Are you sure?” “Take it. I don’t really need a watch anyway.” He winked at me. “I’ve got one on my cell phone already.” He released the watch and it now sat in my hand. I watched him turn and walk away before I took a look at the watch. I then looked up at a digital clock on an LED screen at the intersection down the way. The watch looked fine except that it was ten minutes fast. There was a knob on the side, and I tried turning it. It only turned one way to set the watch clockwise, and it worked both of the hands. I began turning the time forward; almost all the way around to get it back to the right time. “That idiot! He just didn’t know how to set it right.” That’s how life is, sometimes, though. You get a free watch. Finally conjuring up the will to walk, I sloped back on dragging feet as I tried to push them forward like I was pivoting across the sidewalk on a broom. There was a hubbub brewing a few buildings down, and I wanted to see what all the fuss was about so I scooted over there, dragging myself through the foreboding humidity. As I walked, lights flashed and flooded past me with a siren crying frenziedly like an impulse coursing through the popping current and squashing it against the building walls. Officers and medics alike rushed through the gathered rainbow of amassers in rows stretched out taller than the row before. The two officers had pushed back an eye to arc the crowd while the paramedics descended below the amphitheatre horizon. They came back up, like a sheet of white bobbing above and below the tide. They carried a man on a stretcher through the crowd, which was now parted into halves equally bathed in the reddish sea of flashing lights as it reflected off of their pried open eyes. The man was covered from head to toe by a sheet. A dome flared about the crowd with the musky scent of blood and shit mixed. More people began gathering behind me, and soon I was surrounded; helplessly packed into a mesh of bodies. I was beginning to get claustrophobic. I heard two familiar voices and looked over to see that another unfortunate person had been caught between the idealist and the rationalist, who had returned and were still arguing amongst themselves. “I heard his company found out about how the unaccounted tabs on his business expenses were for hookers. He did a lot of traveling for the company.” “No, someone who lives in the building told me that he was a resident here. Apparently, he came home early to find his wife cheating on him when he was supposed to be in Europe.” “No, he had to have known about that. Everyone knew about that. He was just fucking prostitutes to get back at his wife, but he never thought his company would find out until his boss confronted him about it.” “Where did you hear that?” “I heard it from some go who said they worked in the same office.” I lost track of the argument underneath the megaphone blare that cut above the crowd noise and shepherded everyone away with its volume. “Everyone move on back to your homes. There is nothing to see here.” With the crowd dispersion, I was able to again move freely. I stretched my wrists and checked my watch. It hadn’t moved from when I had set it earlier. I noticed beneath the center of the watch, where the bases of the hands met, there was a date. It was for tomorrow. I tried turning the knob again to set the date back, but the knob wouldn’t rotate. It was stuck or something. I jerked hard, but nothing came of it. “Wait a minute,” I thought aloud. “If you can only set the watch once, I must have accidentally dialed in my own death. I had roughly less than twelve hours to live. This left me apprehensive and a bit incensed. “Somebody help me!” I cried out as if I were drowning. The people spread from the scene like a warm lava abreast volcanic curves. I tried to remember if it was in fact salmon that swam against the current and what on earth possessed them to do something so overwhelmingly disadvantageous. Soon enough, though, the rebounding flush of gatherers had subsided. The sudden isolating openness left me weak, and with no crowded mesh to prop me afoot, I fell to the pavement and began flopping asthmatically. “That’s a nice watch,” I heard someone say. I unclosed my right eye and saw the blurry image of legs walking towards me as they kicked dust through my unfocused view. “Funny thing…” I coughed out. He didn’t wait for me to finish. “Yeah, I knew a guy who used to have one of those watches himself. That’s the one where it only tells you what time you’re going to die, right?” Even though he said his friend used to have the watch, I saw the same gold work shine from within his sleeve. “Yeah.” “The key to figuring those damn things out is setting it way off into the future so that you can figure out a way to avoid it when the time comes.” “Funny thing...” I coughed out again. His face bent down as I lifted my wrist at him. He had to hold my wrist in place to keep my hand from wafting to and fro in the wind like a pair of moist, flimsy mittens drying outside dangled on a clothesline. “Only twelve hours, huh?” he pulled me up to my feet and then let go of my wrist. The guy had an air of advocacy about him. He was calm when giving orders and sparse between them. He had one of those voices you just listen to; one you zero in on in a crowd. “Did your friend ever figure out a way to stop the watch?” “Yeah,” he said with a savvy assurance. “All you have to do is to take vengeance on someone who’s wronged you.” “How’s that going to help?” I asked. He answered by pulling down his sleeve and showing me his watch. The date on it read August 5, 1957. There it was; undeniable proof that the date on the death watch could be beaten. That was as good an answer as any for me, and I nodded. But my memory was never strong enough to be capable of vengeance in most cases, and the only man I can remember wronging me was the son of a bitch who gave me this cursed watch in the first place, and he was dead. “His corpse! You can just take vengeance on his corpse!” It was a brilliant idea, although I don’t remember if it was the Advocate or me who had initially invented it. I didn’t have time to laud out credit for inventions; I was hailing a taxi and telling him to follow this ambulance or that. I was a tiny bubble flowing through a flood of neon as Jamaal captained the taxi through it all with a sea sickening brilliance of navigation. Time began to slow down in that taxi cab, but I couldn’t tell because I was slowing down at an equal rate. From within the inertia I could see the lights dimming of their fluorescence. I had no idea where I was, I just felt rays of red and blue passing over me. This whole taxicab business was terrifying. Jamaal was driving at an alarming rate, and I wasn’t sure he knew where the road ended. I felt like perhaps it stopped, and Jamaal and I were headed off of dry land. I could feel the car skidding off of an embankment of some sort. I was not certain of the type of embankment. I closed my eyes, but I don’t know what it was in fear of; I was sure that I would live for at least another ten or eleven hours. When I reopened my eyes moments later, I found myself sitting in the back half of a taxicab that had been severed in half by some force of God. Jamaal and the front half of the cab were nowhere to be seen. Worst of all, we had lost the ambulance. Those fuckers are tricky. With my vengeance plan derailed, I climbed out of the severing in front of me and took off looking for Jamaal. I’d be damned if some taxi cab driver was going to spoil my plots for revenge. If I found Jamaal, I’d lay my vengeance down tenfold upon him! “As it turns out, Jamaal was a Dominican who practiced a rare form of voodoo sorcery.” “You and I both know there’s no such thing as voodoo sorcery. There’s always a simpler explanation than voodoo sorcery. Haven’t you ever heard of Occam’s Razor?” “Yes. Who says voodoo sorcery isn’t the simplest answer? Can you think of a simpler one?” “Not off hand, but that doesn’t mean there isn’t a simpler answer.” “Maybe Occam’s Razor cut it in half,” the Idealist said sarcastically. “Shut up, you jackass,” the Realist returned with spite. I was left with no choice but to then blunt myself with the same stone I carried with me just to silence those damned arguing voices. Somehow, I found myself back at the coffeehouse, slumped over still in my chair. Inside, I could see the baristas cleaning things early. I remembered suddenly that they were staring uncomfortably at me earlier. For this, I could exact my revenge on them. I’d just go in and order something so that they’d have to clean the espresso machine again. Yeah, fuck them. Maybe I’d wait a few minutes. I looked at my wrist, expecting the watch to be there even though I remembered that the watch didn’t tell the time; only the time I’d die. But there was no watch there. In fact, nothing was there. The streetlights had all been shut off, and it was dark as all hell outside. The city was dead of traffic and chatter. It was dead of both its rain and its warming glow. It was just cold, and it seemed airless, and I wouldn’t have cared if I died then because that’s how dead the city was. I should have been happy to have been alive and not condemned, but I wasn’t. I missed the noise and the neon, and the part that hurt the most is that I know that when the glitter on again, they’ll never be as brightening as I remembered them being. I was still fixed on vengeance for whatever reason, no matter how petty. It was then that the stars misaligned and it became clear to me that I could live my life as a vapor lingering around humanity. Vapors needed little nourishment for sustenance. I never had what it takes to rape or murder. I’d feel too guilty. But little things build, and you don’t have to feel so bad about them; little things like ordering a tall latte that you don’t want just so some patronizing barista has to clean the espresso machine again. Little things like these, from time to time, would keep the vapor nourished and lingering. The next day I vomited on a bus to get back at a driver who didn’t greet me back when I said hello. There was some blood in it, goddamned nicotine, but it made me feel better. It gave me more confidence to hold grudges. It made me feel like less of a bum to redeem my vengeances. These unnoticed petty vengeances were my payback to the world for not recognizing and reflecting its own beautiful potential. As for how I met Adam, one day, I gathered up enough gusto to slap an on-duty police officer. Three weeks earlier, he had yelled at me for taking a nap in a large, stone flower pot in front of the post office, and I felt he could have been a little more considerate about the whole deal. Long story short, I ended up doing a stint in county. That’s how I met Adam.
