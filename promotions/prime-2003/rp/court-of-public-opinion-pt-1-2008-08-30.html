Court of Public Opinion, Pt. 1

30 August 2008
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 8/30/08 One only needed to look at the layer of dust settling on the floor in Auxiliary Hearing Room B of the Guilford County Courthouse to gather the general mood of this place. For the past 15 minutes, Troy Douglas could do nothing but stare as the shards of light cut in through the window blinds, illuminating the patches of dust as they fell from the ceiling. It had been 15 minutes of dragging his feet, waiting, wishing he could be anywhere but here. "Every six months, Terry. Every six months they drag me down here to get the same damn sworn statement, every six months this bastard requests parole, and every six months he gets rejected. Sensing a pattern?" Terry Rice, Troy's longtime attorney, wiped the dust off his navy pinstriped Armani and looked his client dead in the eye. "It's 20 minutes, Troy. You answer the questions, the judge dots the "i's" and crosses the "t's", then we go out and grab a burger." "Well, you're paying," Troy said, looking at the ornate money clip in his old associate's breast pocket. Outside the courtroom, Terry Rice was most normally found in jeans and a Harvard Law t-shirt -- even in his office, a fact often lamented by his secretary. But, when he stepped onto his stage, he had no problem playing the part. "There's three bucks and a crumpled up Netflix enveloped clipped to this thing, so unless you think I can barter Disc 3 from the second season of The West Wing for food, lunch is on you." "Fine, cheapass." Troy looked at the falling dust again, absentmindedly tracing out patterns on the floor with his right foot as the inevitable symptoms of boredom set in. "What the hell is taking so long? Clay Jeffries has never been late for a meeting in his life." "It's not Jeffries." Enter the first wrench to this little equation of ours. "You're kidding." Troy stiffened in his chair, his senses immediately heightened as the situation took on a whole new sense of tension. He shouldn't have, really. This was an open-and-shut case, had been for the two years he'd been making these bi-annual trips. A new lawyer wasn't going to disrupt things. He hoped. "Yeah, some new guy," Terry said. "Name's Dexter Carroll, hotshot partner over at Marcum & White. We almost hired him out of UNC, actually." "Why'd you pass?" Terry didn't have time to answer, because as soon as he opened his mouth, the door swung open and the parade began, Dexter Carroll at the head. He couldn't have been older than 27, but this kid had most certainly read Terry Rice's "How To Fit the Lawyer Stereotype" handbook. Perfectly tailored charcoal grey suit, starched white shirt, a tie so brilliantly red it could serve as bunting at the Republican National Convention. Right down to the matching kerchief in his suit pocket and the shoes polished to militaristic perfection, everything about this guy screamed, "Fuck yes, I'm better than you." He smiled at the seated pair, the kind of smug, condescending smile Troy had seen from far too many disingenuous people in his life. This guy wasn't so much a lawyer as he was a genetic mish-mash of every defense attorney to have ever been written into a Law & Order episode. Then came the entourage, three female staffers, each carrying a stack of papers so high their heads disappeared beneath them. Each was set down onto the creaky wooden table with a resounding THUD, and only then did Carroll mechanically pull out his chair and slide into his seat. That was only for a moment however, as the door swiftly opened a second time, heralding the arrival of Judge Linda Hollis. A veteran of the North Carolina bench, Hollis had seen this case through since the very beginning and was as used to the informality of these proceedings as both Douglas and Rice. Apparently, nobody told Dexter Carroll. Because as soon as Linda Hollis slid her chair into place, Carroll pulled off his horn-rimmed glasses and launched headlong into an argument. "Your Honor, my client, James Richter, has been at the whims of this legal system for far too long. Since he was convicted five years ago, Mr. Richter has proven to his counselors, to prosecutors around the state of North Carolina and to the state parole board on numerous occasions. He has cooperated with local, state and federal investigations of various robberies and drug-related crimes. But, still, every six months, like clockwork, your office and the office of Mr. Rice have conspired to keep my client behind bars." Despite the twinges of anger rapidly firing in every muscle of his body, Troy had to admit this guy was good. Too good. "Mr. Carroll, your client was convicted of two first-degree murders, and in five years, I have not seen a single shred of evidence to convince me that his sentence should be shortened." "That's what these are for, Your Honor," Carroll said, motioning to the three mountains of documents that flanked him. "Ever since Mr. Richter approached my office four months ago, we have compiled a rather hefty file that I'd now like to introduce into evidence." "This isn't a trial, buddy. You're here to witness my client's statement." Dexter Carroll wasn't fazed. He'd quite literally read the book on Terry Rice and knew every one of the veteran litigator's tendencies. He didn't even have to stop and think before he slid right into the next stage. "I'm not trying to do anything but strengthen my client's case, Terry. Which is why I have a few questions for Mr. Douglas, if nobody objects, of course." Troy wasn't happy about it, but after seeing Terry's assent, he profferred a curt nod and looked Carroll dead in the eye, awaiting the onslaught. ******** Three hours later... Twenty minutes. That's what Terry had said. Twenty minutes, in and out. Just like always. But, this Carroll kid, he was relentless. He had question after question, follow-up after follow-up, asking each with a rising air of smug condescension. He had Troy in a defensive position, and he knew it. This guy wasn't just relentless, he was damn good. And that only served to raise Troy's level of frustration even more. He was getting pushed at the edges, and considering the increasing amount of heat Troy was feeling underneath his collared shirt, the constant needling was working. After three hours of answering a hundred versions of the same five questions, that wasn't exactly surprising. As Carroll searched through his interminable pile of papers once again, Troy looked down to his feet, where he had unconsciously gathered dust into a rather impressive-looking mound. Not only was Troy dragging his feet, but he was dragging them in a constant pattern. He couldn't take it anymore. "Are we done yet?" That was the moment Dexter Carroll knew he had him. He'd beaten around the bush long enough that he'd taken Douglas to the point of verbal frustration, and as any good lawyer knows, that's when it's time to strike. "Almost, Mr. Douglas. Just one more question..." This wasn't going to end well, for anyone.
