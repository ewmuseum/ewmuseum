Delta Upsilon Iota: Origins (Part III)

25 March 2009
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: March 25th, 2009 Winston-Salem, North Carolina July 15th, 2002 "Henry Jackson Cobb, you’ve done lost ‘yer cotton pickin’ mind. And boy, you better find it before ‘yer pa comes home and sets you straight himself." Standing every bit as tall as her slight sixty-two inch height will allow, Savannah Joyner Cobb sets her bare feet firmly against the cool linoleum floor and continues her verbal assault. "And if you think for a Beale Street second that I’m gonna let you run around the country drinkin’ and smokin’ with that…that…that no good scoundrel Duke Williams, then you must think ‘yer momma’s every bit as crazy as you suddenly seem to be." Sitting forward on one of his family’s handcrafted kitchen chairs, whose birch wood legs strain visibly under his massive three-hundred and ten pound frame, Hank Cobb rests his elbows on knees and shakes his lowered head in obvious frustration. "Momma, I just don’t know why you c’aint look at this from my point of view. My football career is over. There’s just no gettin’ back on the field after an injury like that. You know it. I know it. My coaches know it. And every single doctor we’ve seen has known it too. They’ve all said the same thing: I’ll have to work hard in rehab for the first six months after surgery, but once that’s over, the rest is up to me. Not you, momma. And not dad, neither. Me. And only me." Savannah Cobb, despite her petite stature, possesses a trait found within the vast majority of rural southern housewives—the ability to command the kind of attention and respect most often paid to heads of state and military commanders. Then again, many would argue that the role of a mother stands as a hybrid of the two, albeit with infinitely more responsibility and importance. That being said, even she was finding it difficult to stand in the way of her son’s undying devotion to athletic competition. "Henry, I don’t know what else to tell you. ‘Yer daddy and I have spent the last four months agonizin’ over this day, knowin’ the once you turned eighteen it was ultimately goin’ to be ‘yer choice what you did with ‘yer life. But I’ll be damned if I’m goin’ to stand here and let my baby walk out this home we’ve made and right into the rasslin’ world. A girl doesn’t grow up in North Carolina without learnin’ a few things, and that is not the life I want for you. Don’t you see I’m just tryin’ to protect you, son?" With great agony, thanks to his still heavily wrapped and swollen right knee, Hank rises out of his chair and stands a good foot and a half taller than his righteously indignant mother. "Now momma, you know I love you. I love you and dad both, more than anythin’ else in the world. But I’m a man now, not just because of my age. I’m ten times bigger than others my age and ten times more athletic than others my size. And you know why? Because God gave me a gift. And it ain’t a gift designed for stackin’ hay bales or plowin’ fields. I’ve never met a single soul who works as hard as you or dad, but this just ain’t the life for me, not now anyway. Maybe one day, when I’ve taken this body as far as I can take it. But that day is along way off, and I have a lot left to go and do between now and then." As her son’s words hit her like artillery fire, Mrs. Cobb slumps against the kitchen counter at her back and covers her mouth with both hands. Although she mounts a final effort, the realization of inevitable loss begins to set in. "You can’t do this, Henry. You…you can’t leave me like this." With two long, yet labored strides, Hank moves across the kitchen to his grieving mother. Placing a gigantic hand on each of her shoulders, his tone and demeanor soften accordingly. "I’m not leavin’ you, momma. You just c’aint look at it like that. This is what I have to do, and one day you’ll see that. One day, you and dad will both see that. But until then, you’re just gonna have to trust me." "And I do trust you, Henry. But I don’t trust Duke William and I don’t trust that business." "You don’t have to trust it, momma. But trust me when I tell you this, one day I’m going to stand on top of that very same business. And when I do, that Duke Williams is going to be right there with me." "I love you, Henry. Always remember that." "I love you too, momma. Always will."
