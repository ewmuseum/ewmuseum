Date: Dual Halo RP I don’t remember when I started fearing boredom, I just remember why. When I’m bored, I just begin to think about how tedious life is, even the times you didn’t think you were bored. When I was bored, that’s when the thoughts would start to creep in; the ones about running blindfolded across the freeway or seeing if the radio still gets halfway decent reception underwater in the bathtub. I was staying in a house with four other squatters at the time. I could tell that they all knew what I was thinking when I was bored because they all thought about the same thing. None of us ever mentioned it, of course, because it was one of those taboo things that you’re not supposed to talk about, but every now and then, when we were too drunk to remember, one of us would let it slip. It got me to thinking about at what point a thought gains life. Is it alive when it is just a thought, or does it have to be acted? Is it alive when it is spoken, or just when enough people hear it? Thinking drove me mad, sometimes, and boredom drove me to thinking. I had to numb it away with some petty distraction, so I started counting the couches. We had each amassed a significant number of couches and were looking for a place to keep them. Once we ran out of room, we began stacking the couches on top of each other, sometimes two or three high. We had acquired these couches a number of ways, and even if I did have the time to go into them all, there were still some ways that are best kept secret. We’ll just leave it at that. But there were currently twenty nine couches, no more and no less, scattered around the first floor and pouring out the door onto the front porch. I sat atop a pile of couches three high, watching the sunrise because I hadn’t gotten around to sleeping yet. In fact, I hadn’t gotten around to it for a few days at that point, and my imagination was acting up a bit. I was chiseling away at the mental divide between reality and imagination. It seemed that I was finally chipping through to one side from the other. We were all scientists in our own right, though none of us were very scientific about things. The other four and I had been working on our own individual methods of finding ways to cure our mutually afflicted cases of boredom. I figured that with such a defined line between reality and imagination within the conscious mind, a colloidal mixture of the two would fill the previously inaccessible gaps in reality where nothing happens to balance out the goings on of the imagination. I swore to myself that if it came down to it, I would chisel away at that gap physically. There would be no better way for me to die than to jam a chisel through my own skull once I’ve gone bat-shit insane; no better way than I could think of, anyway. I guess it wouldn’t be fair after all to call ourselves scientists. It wouldn’t be fair to call us doctors either, but we had written ourselves plenty of prescriptions, to be safe, for high dosages of agents to numb and titillate our brains on this quest to cure boredom. Adhering to the scientific method, you’re supposed to limit yourself to one independent variable per experimental tests, but that limit was troublesome and we handily disregarded it. Shoog, short for Sugar, came into the room eating peanut butter straight from the jar with a bent-necked spoon. I never understood the name we knew him by, just because he never seemed to eat anything sweet. He was constantly flushing out his system with grapefruit and refried bean sandwich diets. Shoog was big on the whole cyclical renewal thing. He thought that it might hold the cure to boredom. He would destroy and then replenish- mostly cheap furniture and glassware. He was eating peanut butter, because for a significantly long time, we had ran out of food except for a lifetime supply of peanut butter we had won in a mail in sweepstakes under the collective pseudonym of O.K. Sanchez. I looked out the window as he sat down, wedged between one couch and the couch on top of it. Shoog lit a cigarette. I don’t know if he saw me all the way up there on the top tier of couches, but he was sitting on the bank kitty corner from me. “If I woke up one morning, and everyone else was dead, it’d be impossible to tell from daybreak. I’d imagine the world would glow brightly and colorfully with life just the same as any other day.” Shoog wasn’t paying attention. He had a fishlike attention span. “Is there an ashtray over there?” “No.” There never was an ashtray anywhere. No matter how many we’d steal from the local bars, we couldn’t keep up with the ashtray turnover rate. Just last week we had raided a block of dumpsters outside a strip of restaurants. The anti-smoking activists were crusading on a scorched earth campaign and had just influenced a smoking ban at all food establishments in the area. We had surmounted quite a bag full of ashtrays that day, but everyone of them had turned up missing or had been hurled discus-like into the intersection at traffic; both vehicular and bicycler. Hasone swore it was the ants. He had taken their uninvited occupancy quite personally, and the insult drove him to war with their miniscule but well populated armies. It was a losing battle. The ants could reproduce faster than we could kill them. Hasone was the last one to accept his fate; one that wouldn’t be as antless as he would have hoped. I figured that the stomping that beat and rumbled through the ceiling from upstairs was Hasone stomping on the ants in one final fit of rage as their forces had finally infiltrated his bedroom. The thumping quieted as we could hear him offering up his surrender; his unmoving frame illuminated by the morning rays of sunshine as the quivering gun smoke above split into threads. “What happened to all the ashtrays we got from that dumpster?” “They’re all lost or broken.” Shoog got up from in between the couches. His body bent reflexively into an upright position once released from the hunching wedge. “I’m going to get more. Do you want to come with?” “No,” I replied. “I’m scared that if I go out, I’ll find out everyone else is dead.” I had been coping with this irrational fear I had developing over the last forty eight waking hours that one day I’d go outside in the morning and find that the rest of the world had all died by some force of God that had somehow passed over our house; perhaps because our names weren’t on any lease that would indicate it’s technically tongue-in-cheek vacancy. “Come on. I’ll need assistance with the bag if it’s a decent haul,” he said, as if we were headed for the high seas on some sort of clamming expedition. Adding the crustacean threat of danger at the pincers of loneliness, I cracked and climbed down from the couch. The boredom would have really clamped down on both of us had I stayed here while Shoog hunted and gathered ashtrays. I tread barefoot through the ashy hills coursed with ants that canalled through the room’s walkways between the stacks of couches. Sometimes I felt as if we were living in the bottom half of the world’s largest, most filtered hourglass, quite like Moses alongshore the Euphrates. Trekking across the couches that let out the front door, I felt the sun stick to me like a humid cellophane. I had grown weak from my underexposure to sunlight over the past week. My immune system was very botanical- like that of a plant’s. Slight changes in climate or phototropic variances onset pangs, but I had grown used to them. That’s why some trees can live hundreds of years. They adapt to the attack of change and learn to live with it. Ancestrally, I was part tree- not a large part, but if you traced my lineage back a few generations, you’d find a tree somewhere. There was a shady border at the end of the block where the trees stopped canopying overhead. From the shadowy, cobblestone street, all you could see ahead was blinding light everywhere. We knew from routine that beyond the light was an uglier world of consumerism and traffic. To either side, there would be a gas station. Sometimes, one or the other would have cigarettes on discount. Across the street was a large parking lot full of a lot of old, malnourished sedans. The crackled pavement chipped everywhere, which made contact with it all the more painful. The other squatters and I had a more intimate knowledge of that pavement than most. Some of us had slept there and others of us had spent bodily fluid there; swapping blood for tar. At night, the lot was mostly empty, but it was well lit which gave it the aura of an old gladiator arena. No meeting with passing strangers in the lot crossed without physical altercation. The daytime told a different story, though, and today, an even more different one. A wind puppet flopped around, terrorizing the lot with its clunky, cylindrical arms flapping against its long, bulbous thorax. It looked monstrous, like perhaps some sort of two eyed Cyclops with a big mouth that curved in one wavy line into a grin. “What the hell is that?” I asked. My vision was still adjusting to the brightness out from the leafy residential forestation. “I don’t know,” Shoog snarled territorially. “We should go throw rocks at it though.” I agreed. There was something threatening about a wind puppet flailing about like that in a place no more than ten yards from where you swear you can still make out the outlines of your own blood stains. It was something provocatively sexual that befouled the sanctity of old battlegrounds; something about the thrusting of his windbreaker midriff. We pelted the puppet with chipped off chunks of pavement, and it appeared we had gotten the best of him. He was deflated back so that his spine bent in a manner which none should. If we hadn’t killed the conspicuous bastard, we had damn sure paralyzed him. As with any injured or possibly deceased adversary, we had to poke at him with something to verify the diagnosis. Typically, if the subject was still alive, it would at least whimper or squeal if it was unable to squirm at all. Shoog and I stood on either side over the fallen puppet. What a mammoth windsock it was. “I think it’s dead. Should we take it home and grill its carcass?” It’d be a stringy dinner, lacking in flavor, but add a little peanut butter into the recipe and it’d go down like a juicy cut of steak. We were fooled, though. The damn thing was playing possum and doing a pretty convincing job. As I was too busy thinking of making a meal out of this thing to react, a gust of wind rejuvenated the windsock, and it came to life and flopped towards us with torrential force. It wrapped its stubbed, handless forearms around Shoog and began to grope him. It was a tangle of whirling limbs so that at one point I couldn’t tell which appendages belonged to whom. I was frozen in place for a moment by the spectacle of it all, but as soon as I regained my bearings, I helped my struggling friend out of the busy mitts of the wind puppet. Upon his excavation, he walked away rather quickly. He was obviously humiliated about being publicly caressed by a windsock. “We should go down to the station and file a report,” I suggested, but Shoog seemed to want to forget about it. I jogged to catch up with him; the wind puppet was gesticulating various victory taunts at us in the growing distance between us. “Let’s just forget it.” Shoog started crossing the street towards one of the gas stations. “I’m just going to buy some cigarettes and a case of booze. You coming in or are you going to wait outside?” “Wait outside,” I responded, and then Shoog shrugged and walked into the gas station as I stood by the door and lit a cigarette. I had always wondered if that thing about lit cigarette butts causing gas stations to explode was true. I had never heard about anything like this actually happening, and either something did happen or it didn’t happen. Something that could or could not happen did not exist as far as I could tell. I had always wanted to explode a gas station as a result of my carelessness. But luck was one of the many things I never had. Shoog came out before I could think about it any further, and we started heading back home. We had both forgotten why we had originally left in the first place, but it was assuredly of little importance. We got home and Shoog took his groceries upstairs as I retreated to my pile. I had a square room that was arbitrary shaped around a pile that I had collecting in the center of the room. I decided this to be an efficient shape as anything I would need to use at any given time would be available in a centrally located pile of everything I owned. In other words, I had my whole disposal at my disposal. I sat down in a swivel chair next to the pile and started searching the internet for pornographic images of nude women, but I couldn’t get too comfortable. I shared a wall with a boorish, boisterous fellow named Wayne who knew nothing of subtlety. He was bearded as a mountaineer and talked as if his voice were an axe bowing under the strain of a mighty oak. He must have come home when Shoog and I had gone out. He was always coming in or going out at the most random hours. I had heard rumors somewhere that Wayne was leading a double life. I didn’t understand much about Wayne’s attempts to dodge boredom; he explained them in such lofty terms that I had reason to suspect something suspect in his methodology. He sometimes jargoned on for hours about multiple personalities and sexual fundamentalism. “Have you seen the papers?” he yelled, approaching my doorway with a handful of disheveled newspaper crinkling highly displayed in his fist. There was something about Wayne with the way that his sweaters frizzed off and his pants lined with corduroy but mostly about his very demeanor that could be described as fuzzy. I don’t know if he was just naturally jittery in microscopic ways, but I always felt I could see his outline fraying off into space as he stood there. “What about them?” “There’s an article about Boneyard Creek in there. Apparently a lot of the creek life is dying off because of some change in the tides. It was caused because an armoire that was thrown off of a bridge blocked the flow of a creek.” “So?” “Police are investigating the issue and believe there to be multiple perpetrators.” I still wasn’t sure what Wayne was getting at. He put it out there more bluntly. “We were the ones that threw the armoire in the creek. The police are going to be after us.” I don’t remember throwing any armoires in any creeks, but I took Wayne’s word for it. There are patches of my memory of the last couple of days that had lapsed. One of these patches lasted an amount of time feasibly equivalent to the time it would take to carry an armoire from someone’s curb to throw it overboard into a creek. I carried too much abandoned furniture across town to remember specific incidents anyway. “How will they know we did it? Anyone could have thrown an armoire into a creek, and I’m sure there are a lot of people out there with more motivation to do so than us. If they ask, we’ll just say someone else did it.” “They’ve got witnesses. A cop actually stopped us on our way to the creek while we were hauling the armoire, but we talked our way out of anything serious. The funny thing is, we weren’t even planning on throwing it into the creek, but the creek was right there, and the armoire was starting to get heavy.” “Don’t worry about it, though,” I said, brimming with an experienced confidence. “We’ll just all stay around here until this whole thing blows over. I give it a week tops.” “It gets a little more complicated than that, according to the article.” “What does?” “Well, apparently, there was a body found sealed inside the cabinet of the armoire. Coroners state that the body was dead before the armoire was dumped into the creek.” My eyes lit up like a pair of bulbs. “But we had no idea there was a body in there!” “I know, but that’s kind of a flimsy defense, and by the way we look, nobody’s going to just take our word for it.” “We better assemble an emergency house meeting tonight to come up with a plan.” I couldn’t help but see the inherent humor in the situation, and as silence fell across the room like a lowering curtain, I started laughing to myself at the bulky weight of the armoire. Had we been thoughtful enough at the time to check the cabinets for excess weight, we would have been able to secretly stash the corpse on the side of the road, disassociating it with the armoire. On top of that, we would have had a free armoire. *** I heard Hasone coming down the stairs and saw that he was carrying a vacuum cleaner bag. The stairs led down to our kitchen, which was connected to the backdoor, so I walked into the kitchen to intercept him and tell him the news about the corpse in the armoire if he didn’t already know. I saw him duck under the top of the doorframe at the base of the stairs. “What’s in the bag?” I asked. “Ants,” he replied. “I’ve been vacuuming them up for hours. I think I got all the ants that were in my room.” “Hey, I’m not happy about the ants either, but they’re only going to keep coming back. It’ll make things a lot easier if you just learn to live in harmony with them.” “We have to keep their numbers down or else they’re going to build up enough manpower to take over the house and evict us by force.” “That’s ridiculous,” I countered, but even so, I couldn’t chance being evicted by any means. We needed to keep this stronghold, so I dropped my argument and the combative wrinkles on my face smoothed out in alliance. “But if there’s anything I can do to help that requires minimal time and effort, just let me know.” “Just stop leaving open jars of peanut butter all over the place. The ants will move on if there’s no food here for them to eat.” Then, an idea sparked in my brain. “Or, we could do the exact opposite.” “Sure,” grunted Hasone sarcastically. He thought I was just being difficult. “Seriously, if we smear gobs of peanut butter on the floor, the ants will gravitate towards it. It’ll be like a manmade spider web. The ants will get stuck in the peanut butter traps, and won’t be able to move. Then we can just scrape the ants off with a spatula and throw the gobs of ants outside.” “That’ll never work,” scoffed Hasone, but I still planned on proving otherwise. As soon as he was out of range, I was going to plant a peanut butter web in a discrete location and monitor stuck ant traffic. First, though, I had to warn Hasone about the danger we were in. “Anyway, have you talked to Wayne today?” “No. Why?” “Do you remember that armoire we found the other day?” “Yeah, the one we threw into Boneyard Creek?” “Yeah,” I nodded. “Apparently there was a story about that in the paper this morning.” “There was a story about us throwing the armoire in the creek?” “No. Apparently, the armoire disrupted the flow of the creek and started affecting the creek life. Scientists found the armoire and tried to remove it, but the woodwork was wet. The armoire fell apart, and it turns out there was a dead body stashed inside of it.” Hasone tried to recall the events a bit more clearly, and I could see the moment of concern on his face when he remembered that a policeman had seen us toting said armoire. “I knew that armoire felt heavier than it looked. Shit.” He started pacing around, and I said nothing. Usually, Hasone had just as level of a head as I did, but he was better at coming up with a plan of action than I was. I much preferred to sit back and let things play out, but I felt that this was a situation where his sturdy rationale would come in handy. Unfortunately, he appeared just as baffled about what to do. “I’m going to need some time and energy to think this over,” he finally chimed out. “Prepare the bong while I dispose of this bag of ants.” He left through the back door to toss the bag of ants into the garbage cans lined up outside, and while he did that, I quickly grabbed a jar of peanut butter and smeared a gob of it on the floor in the far corner of the kitchen where the counter bent at an angle between the sink and the assorted knife cabinet. I was on my way through the maze of couches and tunneled my way underneath one couch that was set up overhead across two stacks of couches. This was the way to my room, and I found the makeshift plastic tube bong knocked over in the doorway. The stale bong water dripped into the floor vent so that the dull, resilient stench of the illicit plant haunted the rest of the house like a ghost that lived inside the walls. I refilled the bong in the bathroom sink while ants crawled across the porcelain basin. The bong was just a plastic tube with a permanent bowl and a clear hole in the back instead of a slider. Because of its shape and shoddy material, it fell over often, but it got the job done without the flashy, multicolored patterns that I felt were an unnecessary feature of more expensive models. I had already taken the greens when Hasone entered the room and sat on a chair that was emerging tilted at an angle from the pile in the center of my room. He took the bong from me and sucked up a dense, ponderous hit. “Do you think we should all just lay low here until the whole corpse fiasco blows over?” “No way,” Hasone immediately replied. “I think that’s just your bomb shelter syndrome acting up again.” “I don’t have bomb shelter syndrome,” I lied. “I went out with Shoog earlier to gather more ashtrays.” “Then how come we’re still out of ashtrays?” “Shoog got manhandled by a windsock on the way, so we turned around. Don’t tell him I told you. I think he wanted to keep the whole being molested by a puppet thing hush-hush.” Hasone nodded before passing the paraphernalia. He reasoned out loud as I drew in the smoke. “If there are witnesses who can connect us to the armoire, we shouldn’t stay here. It’ll be harder to find us if we’re on the move. If we hole ourselves up in here, there’ll be nowhere to run, and we can’t stay cooped up inside forever.” “Sure we can,” I disagreed while handing over the bong again. “We’ve got a lifetime supply of peanut butter. We’ll need to stock up on all the other necessities, though, while we still have time; liquor, cigarettes, mild narcotics, and heavy hallucinogens.” “We’ll put it up to a vote later,” Hasone stated nonchalantly. It almost irked me how coolly he seemed to be dealing with the situation, but I soon realized how little I cared about it in the grand scheme of things as well. I started focusing more on how hungry I was and how silly it would be to get all bent over the shape over a measly corpse. I began to see one of Hasone’s points, though it was a point he hadn’t verbally made. He wasn’t worried about having to run from the authorities; we all just needed the available resources to flee boredom. We’d all go crazy under self-imposed, indefinite house arrest. We’d all go so crazy that I had the utmost confidence that at least one of us would survive. After initially thinking that lockdown would be the best option, I began considering some kind of compromise. Sometimes we would stay in the house, and other times we’d stay as far away as possible. Then, I realized that this was no compromise at all. It was just a continuance of our normal routines. And then I remembered how high and hungry I was and ultimately too stupefied to think of a way out of this mess. Sometimes, that’s the way things are though, and the only thing you can do about it is eat ice cream because that always puts things in perspective. “Let’s go get ice cream cones,” I said. “Okay,” Hasone agreed, and we both took one more hit to top off the zombie euphoria. We then stood up and filed out of my room, back past the couches, and out the back door where Hasone’s car was parked in the driveway. Hasone had his keys ready, but we noticed the fifth squatter, Matt had just woken up. He was already outside working on building a fire in a pit we made with large stones we had carried back from a new apartment complex with the finest landscaping several blocks away. The stones there weren’t just any old stones you’d find lying around. They were all imported; all ordered to the exact specification of what a premium quality stone should be. They were among the nicest things we owned. We also had a stack of stolen plywood and cardboard boxes for kindling, but we spent most of our time around the fire pit seeing what kind of other stuff we had that would burn well. Matt was usually the fire pit’s curator, and the flame had a transfixing effect on him. It worried me a little that Matt spent so much time with fire, but I worried most when the fire was out. That’s when Matt was most vulnerable to the boredom. “Quick,” Matt caught us. “Give me something to burn.” “We’re going to get ice cream. You want to go?” Hasone offered. Matt examined the fire for a few seconds before refusing politely. I saw the vacuum bag in one of the trash cans and decided it might be interestingly flammable. “How about this bag of ants?” I asked, holding the bag high for Matt to see. He nodded and grabbed the bag of ants and tossed it onto the fire. Hasone and I pulled up chairs next to Matt’s and watched the vacuum bag slowly catch. “Did you hear about the corpse they found in the armoire?” Hasone asked him. “Yeah,” Matt affirmed. “Wayne showed me the article.” “You’ve been out here for a while. Does it seem like we’re being watched?” “I haven’t noticed anything unusual, no. Do you think we’re suspects?” “Not sure,” Hasone leaned back as I lit a cigarette off of a burning branch I pulled from the outskirts of the fire. “Not yet.” “It’s not like the armoire we dumped in there was the only armoire in the creek. People are dumping armoires in there all the time.” By Jove, Matt was right. How arrogant was it to think that we were the only ones drunk enough to throw furniture off of a bridge. The creek was literally swarmed with armoires, and to investigate anyone guilty of the petty crime of illegal armoire dumping for murder would be on par with a witch hunt and equally fruitless. “You’re absolutely right. The dead body was probably in some other armoire.” That was a load off of my mind. I still felt we should act with caution and still thought that maybe it would be best to keep a low profile for a few days. The burning ants were somewhat nauseating, though. The sound of thousands of ants screaming in unison was clearly audible, but it was the smell that made me feel ill. “Fuck yeah!” Hasone exclaimed while wildly pumping his fists. “Burn in hell, you fucking ants!” God, Hasone hated those ants. His personal vendetta against them was somewhat excessive. We sat there until the bag had burnt to ashes, and the smell had only gotten stronger. I felt too sick to eat anymore. I could see the ants had melted together into a solid, black film. I never pitied the ants, but the echoes of their collective scream shrilled high pitched in my head. I had made a commitment, though, to eating ice cream, and I wasn’t about to back down. When Hasone and I returned fifteen minutes later, Matt had let the fire burn out and Hasone picked up the cooling sheet of burnt ant and nailed it up to the wall inside as a warning to the rest of the ants. Matt was in the living room, having retreated from the fire. I passed through the couched canyon to find him looking out the window at a squad car that was stopped across the street. The engine was still running, and Hasone was still admiring the new wall adornment in the kitchen. Matt and I stayed silent, our eyes peering out the window between the slots in the crooked shades. After a few minutes, the officers pulled away. “I bet the ants are going to think twice now!” We heard Hasone’s voice carry from the kitchen. I ignored it and questioned Matt. ”What the hell was that?” “I don’t know,” Matt responded. “One of them made me put out the fire, so I went inside. He said we weren’t allowed to build fires without a permit. Then they let me off with a warning.” “Did they say anything about the armoire?” “No,” Matt said assuredly as I locked the door anyway. Hasone walked into the room. “Those ants are definitely going to think twice from now on.” “Will you shut up about the god damned ants for a second? They’re not going to think twice. Ants don’t even think once. They just mill around thoughtlessly looking for crumbs. Besides, we’ve got a crisis on our hands.” I wished I was an ant. Ants don’t have serious problems. “It’s not a crisis,” Matt dissented. “The cops were here,” I rebutted. “They made Matt put out the fire. I think they’re snooping around for clues.” “We should show them what happened to the ants when they started snooping around. Then they’ll think twice.” “If they were going to mention the armoire, they would have, but they didn’t. You’re just being paranoid.” “Yeah,” Hasone agreed. “I’ll prepare the bong. It’ll calm your nerves.” Their logic was rock solid, but I needed further reassurance. Hasone left the room and came back minutes later with the bong cycling triangularly from couch to couch. “Maybe we should go back to the creek to see if the armoire is still there. That would really make me feel a whole lot more secure about this debacle.” Honestly, despite all the paranoia and despite my bomb shelter syndrome, I was proud of my penchant for furniture disposal and just wanted to take another look at the work I took part in creating. Hasone and Matt both agreed as the bridge over the creek where we tossed the armoire was on the way to a decent Mexican restaurant, and we all agreed that tacos really had the potential to bring people together. *** “Well, this is shit,” Hasone said from the bridge with his hands in his pockets. Matt and I had combed through the hill of armoires and other pieces of discarded furniture that were strewn about the creek bed. Needless to say, we couldn’t find the armoire that we threw in the creek. We would’ve recognized it by the distinctive woodwork. It had definitely been removed. Matt and I were covered in mud from wading in the creek and overturning furniture in the fruitless search for our dumping. We were about all waded out when we decided to climb back up the bank of the creek and rejoin Hasone. “No luck, huh?” Hasone shrugged rhetorically. A simple sideways shake of Matt’s head wasn’t enough to express my frustration with the whole goddamned thing, so I went off on a tirade. “We can’t catch a fucking break, can we? We just can’t catch a fucking break. What the fuck are we supposed to do now? The armoire’s fucking gone. The fucking cops are going to take us the fuck down, man. They’re going to hunt us down like fucking dogs. What the fuck are we supposed to do?” Matt and Hasone exchanged a look, briefly, intermittent from their awed stares cast upon my ranting and flailing about. Matt was the first to venture a response. “Let’s go get some fucking tacos.” Something about that made sense, and it was exactly the type of squared logic I needed to calm me. I stopped flailing and started breathing heavily to replenish the oxygen I had lost from forgetting momentarily that I had willed myself to stop breathing in a fit of hysteria. Then I lit a cigarette for good measure. “Yeah,” I realized. “Let’s go get some fucking tacos.” And then we went on, taking diagonals across side streets, through alleyways, and over parking lots and drive thru lanes. When we got to the restaurant, Matt and I were still sopped in mud from traipsing through the river, but the small Mexican lady tried her best to act like she hadn’t noticed that anything was out of sorts. We must have smelled like hell, and the tiny hostess was trying so hard not to notice- bless her tiny, little soul. She took us to the small room in the back. I’d imagine it was to keep us far from sight of the other customers, but the small room used to be the smoking section back before cigarettes were outlawed in restaurants by the city council, those goddamned fascists. It would have been the simplest fix, but apparently, we liked to make things as complicated as fucking possible. None of the eight of us wanted to be deemed as fabrications or imaginary figments, so we all kept quiet until I pulled a to-go container from beside the couch I was sitting on and opened it up. “Hey, you stole my drugs!” I spoke hastily though and guided my own pointing finger to another identical container on the other side of the room. I was thinking a lot about death, and it was a shame that I wasn’t really dying because of all the ways I had pictured myself dying, this would’ve been a hell of a way to go out.
