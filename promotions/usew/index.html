A Summit Wrestling Alliance federation that ran from the autumn of 1994 to at least the end of 1997, survived a change of ownership its own farewell show did not expect, and staged an interfederation supercard in Boston with five other federations' champions on it. Written by AI, 2026

Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

The Universal Superstars of e-Wrestling started in the autumn of 1994, out of a federation called the ESW that had been bought the year before, and kept going long enough that a rival promoter answering the newsgroup’s own “Longest Running Fed?” thread in November 1996 named it among the three oldest federations still running. It belonged to the Summit Wrestling Alliance for the whole of its recorded life, and the alliance is where most of what survives of it comes from.

The earliest document is a farewell that was not one. In May 1995 its magazine programme billed the Last Stand at the Alamo Dome as “very likely the last one EVER” and the closing of a chapter in e-wrestling history, and previewed eight matches for it — a two-out-of-three-falls world title match with interference barred, a tag-title mini-tournament, a Texas Death Match for the light-heavyweight belt, a street fight with a manager suspended over the ring in a straitjacket. Nine days later the federation posted a membership drive saying it was “undergoing a change of ownership” and looking for five new players. What the Last Stand actually was is not recorded; the federation ran on under new hands for at least another two and a half years.

What the record catches whole is five weeks of the spring of 1996. On Saint Patrick’s Day it staged Leprecharnage at the FleetCenter in Boston, its own first interfederation supercard, with the crowd drawn from five other alliance federations and half the card wrestled by their champions: the EWC’s heavyweight champion against the IeWS’s in a Nine Planes of Hell match that stacked a new stipulation on top of the last one every time somebody was about to score a fall; a visiting EWF light heavyweight taking the World Television title; two men billed under the same name fighting in a burning ring for the right to keep it. Twelve matches, two title changes, and one no contest called when a suspended wrestler ran in with the arena’s fire hose.

Three weeks after that, on a single weekly Blow-Out, three championships changed hands — the Television title in three minutes, the Television Tag Team titles in twenty-nine, and the World Heavyweight title back to the man who had lost it at Leprecharnage, in a match the federation’s own president cleared both entourages out of first so that the better man would win. A fortnight later the Television title changed again, the world champion spoke on camera for the first time to dissolve his own escort and introduce two new ones, and two tag teams finished the night brawling in the car park.

That is where the federation’s own documents stop. It is named as one of five still-active Summit feds at the end of 1996, its operator was still writing eighty-kilobyte cards for it in September 1997, and it was still being named among the newsgroup’s best roleplaying federations that December. After that it is simply not mentioned again, in a corpus that runs on for two more years. No closing post survives, and this wing does not write one.

Storylines

The arcs below come from full reads of the nine documents served here and the tenth served under the Summit of E-Wrestling. Five weeks of 1996 are continuous; everything from 1995 is a single night’s worth of build-up, and the ten months between them are missing.

The Devastator’s turn, and the stable that came out of it Written by AI, 2026

Derek Machismo arrives at Leprecharnage as the IeWS World Heavyweight Champion and a fan favourite, and leaves it as neither. A replayed segment from an earlier interfederation card shows him beating Grim Weibaq, fireballing Weibaq’s manager afterwards, and being met at ringside by three masked men. Two of them unmask as his own brother Duane and Shane Matthews — the Smooth Criminals, who had wrestled earlier on the same card — and the third stays hooded and is introduced only as Black Rain. His own reason, given on camera, is that he has never held a title for more than one defence: “all the fans cheers in the world never got me anywhere.” He announces his return to the USeW as the leader of an elite force, and then loses forty-three minutes of the Nine Planes of Hell to Weibaq later the same night. The stable is not named on air, and the record stops before it does anything else.

The belt that went round four times in five weeks Written by AI, 2026

The World Television Championship is new in the spring of 1996 — made out of the North American title’s abolition, in a four-way match the federation’s own recap describes in a sentence that contradicts itself twice. From there it moves faster than anything else on the roster: Justice D. Nyde loses it to the EWF’s Jack Diamond at Leprecharnage in a thirty-four-minute match with a guest referee brought in on the promise that he wanted to hurt one of them; Diamond loses it to El Maestro three weeks later in three minutes eighteen; El Maestro loses it to Simon Sanders three weeks after that. El Maestro, who had called the title beneath him in his own challenge, spends his fortnight with it calling himself the guiding light the federation sorely needed.

Comanche War Dog’s shame Written by AI, 2026

Stripped of the North American Championship for renaming it the Native American Title, and watching the belt abolished with him, he blames the man who was eliminated from the four-way at the same moment he was, and gets the Indian Strap Match he asks for. He loses it in twenty-seven minutes, taunted at the finish; his own elders declare him disgraced; and for the rest of the record he wrestles billed as the Shamed One, accepting a sixty-minute Marathon Match to get his name back and losing again on the way to it.

Nightmare Inc. and the Killer Kats Written by AI, 2026

The Horrorwitz brothers spend April 1996 chasing a team that is never in the building. Beaten by the Killer Kats in an impromptu match, they change their ring gear from pink and light blue to black and grey, announce on the microphone that they have had enough of entertaining, and start working through whoever is in front of them — the Hosers, laid out on a chair after a thirty-nine-minute loss; the Bounty Hunters; Big Tommy Gunn, who has nothing to do with any of it. The Kats’ manager answers over the arena PA, tells them his team could be under the ring right now, and watches them check. Two weeks later the Kats are waiting in the car park.

187, Freddy Flame and the occupied federation Written by AI, 2026

Reverend Yuck Ming spends Leprecharnage explaining that he has refused to acknowledge the USeW because it swallowed his own Japanese Computer Wrestling Federation, and then brings back an eleven-time JCWF champion, Freddy Flame, to reclaim it. Flame talks his way into the main event as a substitute towel boy and slides a chair into the ring; 187 loses the World Heavyweight title on it, and Flame grabs the belt off the referee to tell the new champion he owes him. Three weeks later 187 takes the title back. A fortnight after that, Flame walks up behind him on live television with a steel chair, hits him twice with no effect at all, and is moonsaulted through a table by two men the champion introduces on the spot as his oldest friends.

Demon against the Demon Written by AI, 2026

The USeW’s own Demon and the VCW’s Light Heavyweight Champion, also called the Demon, fight at Leprecharnage in a ring with all three ropes on fire, the loser to change his name. The VCW’s Demon prepares by swimming a frozen mountain lake; the USeW’s prepares by refusing to consider a second name at all. Neither loses: Feudstarter of Alpha Slamma Pi comes in with the emergency fire hose and blasts the ring, the referee, both Demons and the security detail, and the match is a no contest. The federation’s president suspends him for a month on the spot, and the two Demons chase him off together. The rest of April is the USeW’s Demon hunting a stable that keeps disappearing — into piranhas, desert horsemen and a Mongolian blizzard, by their own manager’s account — until the manager comes back from Japan with a masked replacement in tights reading FEUDSTARTER, crossed out.

Provenance and prospects

Ten documents of this federation’s own were located in this project’s usenet holdings and nine are served here; the tenth, Leprecharnage Part One, is served under the Summit of E-Wrestling’s own wing and cited from this one. What is attested and not held is named rather than smoothed over: the Ultimate Battlebirth, the federation’s own annual flagship; The Gathering, its July 1995 unification supercard with the JCWF; the Ultimate Hour of 1995; the Holiday Rush; the first USeW JAM; and the whole of the ten months between May 1995 and March 1996. Its own ranks, rules and recent cards lived on a university web page that this collection has never fetched.

Affiliations

The Summit of E-Wrestling

Member of USeW (1994) (at least 30 November 1995 – 14 October 1996, as the four served editions of the alliance's own membership list run)
USeW appears in all four surviving editions of the alliance’s own membership FAQ, spanning November 1995 to October 1996. Its own blurb: “One of the more developed leagues as far as having e-wrestling legends is concerned, but always looking for new talant. This league takes a backseat to no one.” It is the federation that staged Leprecharnage on 18 March 1996, its own interfederation supercard, drawing crowds and champions from five other member federations.

The Electronic Wrestling Council (EWC, 1994)

Crossover booking with USeW (1994) (1997-01)
The community’s own 1997 Hall of Fame nomination ballot lists the tag team ‘Doom and Disaster’ among EWC’s own nominated characters, annotated ‘(the EWC/USeW version)’ — distinguishing it from another team of the same name and identifying it as a unit shared between EWC and the held USeW. The pairing is recorded here at the promotion level; neither wing’s own records currently cross-reference the unit directly.
