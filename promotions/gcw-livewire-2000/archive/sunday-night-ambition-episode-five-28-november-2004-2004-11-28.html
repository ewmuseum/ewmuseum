(The GCW logo hits the screen and the screen fades backstage where Vice-President Brandon Murphy sits, leaning back in his leather chair.)

Charles "was dead: to begin with. There is no doubt whatever about that. The register of his burial was signed by the clergyman, the clerk, the undertaker and the chief mourner. Murphy signed it: and Murphy's name was good upon 'Change for anything he chose to put his hand to. Old Charles was as dead as a door-nail.

The mention of Charles' funeral brings me back to the point I started from. There is no doubt that Charles was dead. This must be distinctly understood, or nothing wonderful can come of the story I am going to relate. If we were not perfectly convinced that Hamlet's Father died before the play began, there would be nothing more remarkable in his taking a stroll at night, in an easterly wind, upon his own ramparts, than there would be in any other middle-aged gentlemn rashly turning out after dark in a breezy spot -- say Saint Paul's Churchyard for instance -- literally to astonish his son's weak mind.

Nobody ever stopped him in the street to say, with gladsome looks, "My dear Murphy, how are you? when will you come to see me?" No beggars implored him to bestow a trifle, no children asked him what it was o'clock, no man or woman ever once in all his life inquired the way to such and such a place, of Murphy. Even the blindmen's dogs appeared to know him; and when they saw him coming on, would tug their owners into doorways and up courts; and then would wag their tails as though they said, "no eye at all is better than an evil eye, dark master!"

But what did Murphy care? It was the very thing he liked. To edge his way along the crowded paths of life, warning all human sympathy to keep its distance, was what the knowing ones call "nuts" to Murphy.

"A merry Christmas, uncle! God save you!" cried a cheerful voice. It was the voice of Murphy's nephew, who came upon him so quickly that this was the first intimation he had of his approach.

"Bah!" said Murphy, "Humbug!"

Hours Later

The cellar-door flew open with a booming sound, and then he heard the noise much louder, on the floors below; then coming up the stairs, then coming straight towards his door.

"It's humbug still!" said Murphy. "I won't believe it." His colour changed though, when, without a pause, it came on through the heavy door, and passed into the room before his eyes. Upon its coming in the dying flame leaped up, as thought it cried "I know him! Charles's Ghost!" and fell again. The same face: the very same. Charles in his pig-tail, usual waistcoat, tights and boots; the tassels on the latter bristling, like his pigtail, and his coat-skirts, and the hair upon his head. The chain he drew was clasped about his middle. It was long and wound about him like a tail; and it was made (for Murphy observed it closely) of cash-boxes, keys, padlocks, ledgers, deeds, and heavy purses wrought in steel. His body was transparent: so that Murphy, observing him, and looking through his waistcoat, could see the two buttons on the his coat behind. Murphy had often heard it said that Charles had no bowels, but he had never believed it until now. No, nor did he believe it even now. Though he looked the phantom through and through, and saw it standing before him; though he felt the chilling influence of its death-cold eyes; and marked the very texture of the folded kerchief bound about its head and chin, which wrapper he had not observed before: he was still incredulous, and fought against his senses. "how now!" said Murphy, caustic and cold as ever. "What do you want with me?"

"Much!" -- Charles's voice, no doubt about it.

"Who are you?"

"Ask me who I was."

"Who were you then? said Murphy, raising his voice. "You're particular -- for a shade." He was going to say "to a shade," but substituted this, as more appropriate.

"In life I was your business partner, Charles."

"Can you -- can you sit down?" asked Murphy, looking doubtfully at him.

"I can."

"Do it then."

"You don't believe in me," observed the Ghost.

"I don't."

"That is no light part of my penance," pursued the Ghost. "I am here to-night to warn you, that you have yet a chance and hope of escaping my fate. A chance and hope of my procuring, Brandon."

"You were always a good friend to me," said Murphy, "thank'ee!"

"You will be haunted," resumed the Ghost of Charles, "by Three Spirits."

"Is that the chance and hope you mentioned, Jacob?" he demanded, in a faultering voice.

"It is."

"I -- I think I'd rather not," said Murphy.

"Without their visits," said the Ghost, "you cannot hope to shun the path I tread. Expect the first to-morrow, when the bell tolls one. Expect the second on the next night at the same hour. The third upon the next night when the last stroke of twelve has ceased to vibrate. Look to see me no more; and look that, for your own sake, you remember what has passed between us!" When it had said these words, the spectre took its wrapper from the table, and bound it round his head, as before. Murphy knew this, by the smart sound its teeth made, when the jaws were brought together by the bandage. He ventured to raise his eyes again, and found his supernatural visitor confronting him in an erect attitude, with its chain wound over and about its arm. The apparition walked backward from him; and at every stop it took, the window raised itself a little, so that when the spectre reached it, it was wide open. It beckoned Murphy to approach, which he did. When they were within two paces of each other, Charles's Ghost held up its hand, warning him to come no nearer. Murphy stopped. Not so much in obedience, as in surprise and fear: for on the raising of the hand, he became sensible of confused noises in the air; incoherent sounds of lamentation and regret; wailings inexpressibly sorrowful and self-accusatory. The spectre, after listening for a moment, joined in the mournful dirge; and floated out upon the bleak, dark night.

Murphy closed the window, and examined the door by which the Ghost had entered. It was double-locked, as he had locked it with his own hands, and the bolts were undisturbed. He tried to say "Humbug!" but stopped at the first syllable. And being, from the emotion he had undergone, or the fatigues of the day, or his glimpse of the Invisible World, or the dull conversation of the Ghost, or the lateness of the hour, much in need of repose; went straight to bed, without undressing, and fell asleep upon the instant.
