"Fear Nothing"

29 April 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2005-04-29 * * * * * The country air was ripe and pungent with a fresh coating of manure to help fertilize the ever-growing acres of cropland. The morning sun hadn’t yet ripped away all of the low-laying fog, which nestled itself into the valleys like the ghost ships of a scary tale told by an aged sea Captain, silently hiding between the rolling waves. The warmth of the sun slowly dissipated these white masses into oblivion, rather than some swashbuckler brandishing his golden tooth and sword. Archibald Allen MacGregor knelt at the base of his father’s grave, the empty bottle of Brandy clutched in his left hand. In his right was the mysterious note, signed by the man who died nearly four decades earlier in Vietnam. With the morning dew seeping through his jeans, Archibald was motionless for a few moments. Confusion was doing laps around comprehension in an endless race through his mind, far exceeding the speeds of which he could make a rational explanation for the unfolding events. He did didn’t weep, nor did he scream. Instead, he whispered calmly to his father, “Why did you lie to me? Why? You told me he died, Dad…you named me in his honor. You took your own life because you thought you didn’t honor him enough. And all this time, these years since you’ve been gone…I’ve loved you and tried to honor your wishes. For what?” The nearby trees danced in the breeze. An early morning flight out of Philadelphia was leaving a white contrail across the sky, hurtling towards the fading reaches of night on the western horizon. “What’s going on? Is this some kind of sick joke? There has been a statue in town for decades honoring him…and he’s been alive all along?” Archibald paused, eyes darting back and forth as he searched for questions he knew the answers wouldn’t be provided for, “Why all the years of pushing me to be great at something? Why name me after someone who has been alive all along?” The morning of April 2nd, 2005 was beautiful, but starting to pale in comparison to Archibald’s anger and confusion. As the last clutches of nightfall slowly lost their grip to the day, Archibald’s control over his anger slowly started to crack and splinter. The dam wasn’t going to hold much longer. He stared at the granite headstone with contempt. “I don’t deserve this! Not now! Not when I finally find the one thing I’m great at…all for a man who isn’t dead!” Archibald shot to his feet, his control finally giving way to anger and confusion. He threw the empty bottle of Brandy at the black granite headstone, where it shattered into hundreds of pieces. “Honor the fraudulent.” He said with a quiet animosity, spitting out the words as he stared at the sparkling pieces of shattered glass. “And that’s where you’re wrong,” came a voice directly behind Archibald, “because I remember my funeral being quite emotional for the town. The grief only amplified because your father wasn’t present to read my eulogy.” Archibald turned from his father’s final resting place, towards the origin of the voice. Wearing the same suit and bowler hat from the previous day, Archibald Matthews stood only feet away. Gently removing the hat, placing it under his arm, Matthew’s extended a hand forward out of common courtesy. “None of this makes sense…” MacGregor whispered to himself, staring at the extended hand of his namesake. A wave of uneasiness washed over him as he contemplated accepting the friendly gesture. “Believe me when I say this, I fully understand your confusion. There will be some explaining to do.” Matthews said quietly, glancing down at the grave. “Start by answering me one question. How are you here, today, offering me a handshake? You should be dead.” The wrinkled face of Archibald Matthews wrapped itself around a warming smile. The same smile MacGregor had seen the day before during his sales pitch with the bottle of Brandy. It still felt earnest, and full of honesty. “You see, young MacGregor, I am dead. Have been since 1967.” If he hadn’t thrown the bottle against the headstone a few moments earlier, it probably would have fallen to his feet when Archibald MacGregor took the hand of his father’s best friend. It wasn’t cold and clammy like most hands of the deceased would be, but rather, full of warmth. Full of life. A wave of compassion instantaneously replaced the anger, completely crushing the contempt under its weight. As Archibald MacGregor shook the hand of a man he was destined to honor, he was suddenly at a loss for words. He should have doubted this man’s identity from the start, especially his recent claim of being deceased. But something wasn’t allowing it. Even as their hands separated and both men were left staring at each other, the feeling persisted. The elder Archibald motioned towards town with a soft flick of the wrist, “Walk with me.” The young left the grave of his father, while the old left the grave of his best friend. Both men silently departed the cemetery and started walking to the nearby town of Oxford, strolling alongside a thin stretch of grassy parkland. As the sun continued to glide higher, Archibald MacGregor casually glanced towards the man who gave his life for his country. It struck him as peculiar, almost eerie, that sometimes at the right angle Archibald swore he saw a light blue reflection coming from the corners of the old mans eyes. Not a glow, but a soft radiance that reminded him of cats eyes at twilight. “So…Dad told me the way you really died. The story he told the papers and the stories the locals tell each other were his fabrication to hide what actually happened.” MacGregor paused, collecting his thoughts, “At least, that’s what Dad told me the night before he shot himself. He wasn’t just rambling, it was the truth?” They walked for a few moments as the thin stretch of grass finally came to a sudden halt, with both men left standing at the base of an aged statue. The flesh-and-blood apparition gazed up at the bronze recreation of himself, and sighed. ”Yes.” * * * * * Over the last decade, since Dad killed himself, I’ve often wondered how many of his traits I’ll inherit. He was never fond of alcohol, so obviously I found that vice on my own. But before he left for the war, everyone described him as arrogant and cocky. While I don’t view myself in such a light, I can see how my demeanor would come across that way to others. Confidence and arrogance walk hand in hand, only separated by the intelligence to discern them from each other. He undoubtedly loved my mother, and he loved his friend Archibald so much that I now bear his name. I’d like to think that I inherited his ability to love someone, something, or some idea so much and so unconditionally that I could honestly compare myself to Dad in that light. But here I am, writing memoirs a month after my girlfriend left me. Apparently my love falls to the bottle more than it should. If these ramblings and memoirs help me get out of that rut someday, then so be it. Only time will tell. What worries me, even more than the unbelievable events which unfolded over the previous four weeks, is the fact that I also have the one trait my father didn’t intend to pass down unto his son: being selfish. After all, putting a gun to your head and pulling the trigger is the most selfish thing a person can do. Disregarding the love your family and friends have for you, and ending your own life can’t be weighed against anything else in this world. It is a selfish act, and I can’t preach that enough. Go ahead, ignore the pain it will cause. Pull the trigger. Ignore the endless tears and sleepless nights your wife and son will have, all because you don’t feel you’re fit for this world. Because their love wasn’t good enough to keep you around. Go ahead, pull the trigger. * * * * * For a bronze statue cast decades ago, it was in remarkably magnificent condition. Age had darkened the bronze slightly, but for the most part it was in pristine condition. A few weeds grew at the base of the statue, which was a large square block of white granite, and a few bird droppings peppered the large bronze shoulders Archibald Matthews in a few places. Matthews gazed up at himself, and smiled, “Handsome, wasn’t I?” MacGregor matched the gaze, and immediately took notice to the face. It was exactly the same as the old man standing beside him, save for a few wrinkles. The same slightly bent nose, the same squared-off jaw, and the same downward-sloping brow that gave him an intense stare. Archibald MacGregor still felt the ominous feeling of belief, rather than disbelief. He knew that what he was experiencing shouldn’t be possible, but for some reason, it was like God Himself ripped away disbelief and replaced it with a warm, soothing feeling. MacGregor attributed the same sensation to drinking a shot of Whiskey. But rather than inebriation, he was simply in awe. If this was God’s sales pitch, he had the proper techniques for manipulating the sale. Perhaps one day God could cure him of his alcoholic sickness with the same omniscient persuasion. “Don’t mind me,” Matthews said, the smile still stretching across his face, “I’m just remembering the good’ole days before the war.” “Back when you and Dad roamed the town, huh?” MacGregor said with a surprising smirk, remembering the stories his father had told him about carousing around town alongside his best friend. Matthews’ angelic gaze was finally pulled away from the statue, “No. Not Vietnam, not that war.” Confused, Archibald’s head darted back in confusion, “What war are you talking about? You and Dad served in Vietnam together, but you two were barely adults when you left here.” Archibald Matthews, clad in his black suit and bowler hat, stood silent for a moment. He appeared unsure of how he would explain whatever he was about to explain, so instead he motioned towards the road again with another flick of the wrist, “Let’s walk back to your house. We have a lot to talk about.” Even with the constant feeling of believability being thrust onto him, Archibald MacGregor wasn’t being forced into following orders. He might take every word he hears as the truth, but he was still reluctant to listen until he got at least one answer. “We’ll go, as soon as you answer my question. What war?” Taking off his bowler hat once more, Archibald Matthews turned and stared at the eager and confused Archibald MacGregor. Once again, the soft reflective glow appeared for only a split-second, and was gone as quickly as it had arrived. He spoke softly, with a profound respect and an undying fear about his answer. “The war between Heaven and Hell, of course. Why else do you think I’m here?” * * * * * I’m also selfish. As I write these little entries, I’m currently involved in chasing a dream. Something my father tried to push me into, but something I ultimately found after his suicide. I want to be a professional wrestler. To those who will read these ramblings, hopefully years down the line and after I’m long gone, go ahead and laugh. I won’t care now, and I won’t care then. When you have a passion for something and want to be great at it, you start to become selfish. You want to be the best. Ask Barry Bonds. Ask Peyton Manning, or Dale Earnhardt Junior. They all love what they do to such a level, their passion could be mistaken for selfishness. Or their selfishness could be mistaken for passion. My passion is wrestling. Last week, I defeated a man who had claimed that he is professional wrestling. I forced him to submit in the center of the ring, making him tap out and give up all hope of victory. We shared the same goal, which was to win the match and ultimately the entire tournament. But I won, and he lost. Maybe it was because I was a little more eager, a little more selfish. He was just assuming I would be a pushover. And now he’s at home, a loser. Next week I’ll get someone different, and I guess we’ll see if he makes the same mistakes. Because here I am, my hands are painted in fading shades of black and blue. One of my knuckles is still swollen, because I was confident enough doing battle against the biggest name the business had to offer. Arrogant enough to think I could actually win. Fear nothing, I’ve been told. And I don’t. Let the world label you based off of your actions. Action speaks louder than words, after all. So when these memoirs are finally read, perhaps I will have been a cocky bad guy in the world of wrestling. Or perhaps I will ultimately be a loving, good-natured (in front of the cameras) role model to kids everywhere. You never know, do you? But don’t fear the chase. Don’t fear your dreams – reach for them. Cliché or not, we wouldn’t have airplanes, astronauts, or the Internet if people didn’t reach out for their dreams, for what they believed was possible. Just don’t push your son or daughter into a dream that you have pre-destined them for, like my father tried with me. But I guess one thing is clear in all of my ramblings. In order to fully comprehend the events of the last month – or even the last 29 years of my life – you really should know exactly what my father went through, and what horrors he saw in Vietnam in order to fully grasp the mentality behind his selfish acts. It was the fuel for his fire, and that fire burned hot and bright until a bullet extinguished it. What made an arrogant twenty-something come home a humbled man? What drove him to force his son into every sport imaginable, only hoping that he would be great and honor his fallen friend? What drove a loving husband and father to suicide? I guess it’s time we covered that. * * * * *
