Date: 2009-10-15 Maybe You Can Polish A Turd After All: The Garbage Bag Johnny Story Chapter 1: Admittance A Garbage Bag Production OCTOBER 5, 2009 They said that I could leave -- not so much that I could leave, but that I must leave. It had been three months, and some man with a briefcase full of important paperwork told them I could no longer stay there. They said I would still have to go to the meetings, but I had no intention, at the time, to do so. Aside from the rampant enforcement of sobriety, I liked it there alright. They kept us fed and, for the most part, clothed. I had a room where nobody would bother me. Every once in a while they'd shake the room down to make sure I was staying clean. It was a small price to pay to be out of reach from everything. The one perpetuates the other and the other perpetuates the one. They are each other, and I am a speck of dust trapped between two equally powerful vacuums. I had sold vacuum cleaners once. It was more of a hobby than anything else. I had gotten a stock of them from a guy named Pete who stole things out of trucks. After initial failures, most certainly due to the inferior quality of the knockoff vacuums, I had begun marketing and selling door-to-door an unvacuumable dirt. This venture was only slightly more successful. There was nobody here to solicit goods of variably questionable utility, door-to-door or otherwise. Capitalism and convenience were both dictated by a pair of vending machines, standing stoistically shoulder to shoulder. One dispensed assorted beverages and the other contained a selection of hydrogenated snack bars in hermetically sealed wrappers. There was a certain hermeticity to it all. The room they put me in had plain walls and two large windows with no blinds or curtains. They asked if I would like to hang an inspirational poster on the wall, but I declined. I considered it a moral obligation to have declined, which was quite the consideration considering my negotiable definitions of such things. It was enough to be alone and not bothered for a while. I appreciated the recognition, at first, and I always appreciated the money -- especially after it ran out -- but then it became too much. I couldn't go buy a pack of smokes without some dundering idiot running me down to autograph a receipt or a burrito wrapper. There were guys in suits from every angle telling me what to do with my money. On the other hand, there were some genuine folk who would gift me batches of cookies or paintings of me, as unflattering as some of them may have been. They were marginally outnumbered, however, by the ones who acted like I owed them something, and the more I became recognized, the more the latter appeared. I was in a room, and I was standing like I was in a line, but nobody else in the room stood with the same linear disposition. I had two briefcases at my side containing all of my possessions: four changes of clothes, an envelope holding $133.65, an expired bus pass, the leftovers of hygenic products I had claimed from my room and the AWC Transatlantic Championship belt. The uneven distribution of weight between the two suitcases titled me slightly to the right, and I became aware of how they were all looking at me, even when they weren't. "John Bagwell . . . Bagwell-Gumphries?" The question jarred me from my crooked linear stance into a slightly less improper stumble forward, towards a well-kept, middle-aged and slightly stout black woman who was standing behind a counter. "That's me," I politely asserted, having grown polite in my confinement. "Are there supposed to be two Bagwells in your name like that?" She hovered over a clipboard, facing upward atop the desk. "Because if that's a typo, we're going to have to resubmit all this paperwork." My middle name had always been Bagwell, as it was a tradition in the Gumphries lineage to give the mother's maiden name as the eldest son's middle name, but seeing as how I never had a father, I kept Gumphries as my surname and took my grandmother's maiden name as a middle name. Years later, nomenclatural coincidence found me making a minor adjustment to my name for the benefit of authenticating an angle, except when I filled out the paperwork I was high on paint, and I bungled it. I hadn't thought to omit my middle name, and that there was a separate box for the new middle name on the form was a recipe for disaster. "Uh-huh. Yeah. That's correct," I assured her, tapping down on the clipboard as a measure of certainty. "Then I'll need you to sign here at the bottom." She thrust a pen at me, "and initial here, here and here." "Wait. What is this?" I dropped the heavier of the two to snatch up the clipboard and hold it aloft. "I'm not signing anything until I've had the proper chance to read it." "It just says you acknowledge that you are being discharged and that all of your personal belongings are in order." "What? No!" I suddenly gesticulated with the hand holding the clipboard that it was sudden to me, and having surprised myself, compromised my entire rebuttal. "There must be some mistake. I don't want to leave." "It says your insurance has run out." "So? What does that mean." I neatly placed the clipboard back on the countertop. "It means you can't stay here for free," she replied sternly while retaining her composure that I wouldn't suspect her taking joy in it. "I'll pay!" I hunkered down and undid my suitcase, overanxious and overconfident in my counting skills. "I've got . . ." "You've got $133.65. That ain't going to cut it." "But that's all I got," I pleaded. "Isn't there anything you can do?" "Well," she mulled, and in a very particular way. "I bet that gold belt you got there might be worth something. It'd probably be good for another two or three months." "Fuck it." I crouched to snap the case shut and bent back up to retrieve the pen. I signed the paper and overthoroughly initialed it before turning about face and heading for the door. One of them opened it for me, but then again, they were all them. NOVEMBER 14, 2006 I was in President Harber's office, and it wasn't anything like the ones you saw on television with fake nice furniture. It was literally a trailer with all types of machines and monitors. Dave was known to skip around intensely behind three men helming the main console, keeping three separate threads of instruction untangled and evenly paced in juggling conversation. The taping was on commercial break, and he was sitting in a folding chair. When I entered the room, he stretched forward to shake my hand, and he was smiling one of those smiles that people smile when they're about to deliver unpleasant news. After the buy-rates on the last Pay Per View didn't meet investor demands, we knew the company was going to shut down after the current cycle. I couldn't really imagine what was left to discuss, or why he was grinning like such an uncomfortable asshole at me. "What's up, Harb?" I circled for a place to sit, like a dog before relieving itself, and unsuccessful, opted to shit myself standing, metaphorically speaking. "Johnny," he started, searching for the right words. "There's really no other way to say this than to say it, so I'm just going to say it. Alright?" "Yeah, sure. I guess." I was not looking as intently for words as he was. It was never really one of my strenghts. "How would you feel about dropping the belt at Triangles?" He closed his eyes and clenched his fists (albeit so subtly that an average spectator watching this situation unfold wouldn't have noticed) as if he were expecting me to hit him. Out of respect, I didn't, but I took a second to gather my thoughts. "I'd feel pretty bad about that, Harb. Last week you told us that the ship's going down, and losing the belt would really hurt my stock with the other promotions." "I know, Johnny. I'm trying to look out for everybody's interests. Can you just hear me out here?" "Who's going to walk out with it?" "Darcy." He stated, flatly. I bit down on my lip. I worked a program with Markson before my push, and he was always a class act. "But if the numbers are alright, we've got the option to run a reunion show down the line. You'll get a rematch there and we'll have you over Darcy in the main event." "I was hoping to have a contract already penned up and running by then, Dave." "Your stock's hot right now. You're ending on a strong title run, and wherever you go, they're going to treat you -- and pay you -- like a champion." He paused to gauge my response, but heavily caffeinated and impatient, broke back into persuasion. "Besides, this might give Darcy the push he really needs to sell the Crisis character. It'll help him out." "Fuck it, man." I shrugged doubling a new prong inward. "I'm not going to be a dick about it. It's just a belt. If you think it's going to help out Markson more than it'll hurt me, I'm cool with it. One thing, though . . ." "Sure, what do you need?" "I want the big spot," I said, hoping to secure prescription painkillers with an unhealthy supply of refills. "As long as you don't bang yourself up too badly," he smiled, his grin not abandoning its former attributes, rather adding new ones upon itself as I saluted and trailed off out the trailer as Harber's voice gave manually megaphoned chase. "Reunion Show!" I'm sure I grumbled something there and hoped he hadn't heard it. OCTOBER 11, 1991 I had been dubbed the "Sewer Baby" even though I was technically born at the spout of an old drainage pipe. The nickname unfortunately stuck for a while, and well through my childhood, strangers from nearby towns who had heard the tale would come to the farming community that was Arbuckle, Illinois -- a town so nondescript it was almost fictional -- seeking a photograph of the Sewer Baby. Some were disappointed that I had aged noticeably in my formative years. A handful of physical scofflaws even claimed that my unanticipated natural growth was some mutant byproduct of being sewer-born, expecting me to remain eternally infantile. My mother, Lisa Gumphries, named me John because she said that's what Jesus's best friend was named, and she wanted Jesus to be my best friend, too. As it turned out, that friendship never really blossomed like it should've. I had a bad habit of making friends who could get me high. That all started with Monster Truck Doug, but I suppose I'll get to him later. He lived in our house for a while, but that wasn't too uncommon. There was usually someone living there who didn't really belong, and the ones who treated Lisa worst were the ones that stayed the longest. She always needed a bad excuse to pray. There was Military Issue Dennis, the intensely psychotic Gulf War veteran, and Lawnmower Willie, who always smelled like gasoline, and a whole slew of them to the point that I couldn't remember most of them off hand unless I thought real hard about it. Lisa got sick of most of them, and I'd usually never see them again. After a while, things started to get volatile between us, and for my part, I didn't try to make things any easier between us. When I left, I thought she might have gotten sick of me, too. The more I look back, the more I think she just got sick of her prayers going unanswered. I can't say she never did anything for me, though, and there aren't many other people I can't say that about. I remember I was about seven at the time, and I was out on the front lawn playing in a hill of leaves engulfed by a post-rain muck. A couple of dullards strolled past, a married couple with a strange resemblance to each other, and even then I could tell they were dullards because they were both dressed in sweatclothes, and the man had a camera hanging from his neck on a string of yarn. They stared at me until they noticed that I noticed they were staring at me. "Say, are you that Sewer Baby?" He scratched himself as he spoke, but I was too shy to answer to him. He turned to his wife. "He don't look like no baby to me, Charlene. You sure that's him?" "Course I'm sure!" the unbecoming woman screeched back. "He looks just like the one in Sheila's photo." "Huh," the husband grunted, his bulbous head swelling and aching from overuse. "Maybe all them sewer juices gave him some kind of giantism." "Herb! What if he can hear you?" She stretched her husband's name into several syllables. "I guess we'll see about that," he turned from her to me and cupped his hands around his mouth, interlocking his fingers into a digital megaphone. "Hey, Sewer Baby! I'm talking to you!" I stiffened up, standing upright with leaves pressed onto me, stuck to the muck that stuck to me. "My word!" gasped Charlene, fanning her moon of a face. "Shoot! The dang camera's not working!" I noticed Herb, his hanging face masked by the latest of the era's large technologies. "Herb, you've got the cap on!" She reached to help, but Herb pulled the camera away quickly. "Don't touch!" He snapped. "This thing cost me $350." "I'm sorry, Herb," she retreated as he carefully removed the cap. "Herb!" "What?" "The Sewer Baby!" Her sleeved arm stretched towards me like a struggling hammock as I shuffled nervously to the fence. "Take a picture!" Herb zoomed into action, adjusting the focus of his lens, too busy squaring up for the perfect shot to notice that I was urinating on his shoes. The photo snapped, and with a dizzying flash, Herb's attention divided from the camera, and the gravity of this subconscious release sank down on me. I still finished, but both of us stood there for a moment in disbelief. It truly wasn't my intention to empty my bladder on the man's footwear, but years of being photographed had driven me into an unconquerable daze at the sight of one more camera; one more guffawing onlooker, too cheap to buy tickets to a proper circus. He smacked me backhanded across the face, and I went down, but I didn't feel anything. All the sound escaped, and time seemed to slow down. I started crying because I knew I wasn't supposed to have done what I had done and that there were consequences and retributions for such actions. I wanted to live in a world where there weren't and where I wasn't constantly being surveilled by the inconsiderate curious. My mother came rushing out, and Randy -- who sold replica wristwatches out of a trenchcoat -- slowly ambled to the doorway after her. He was wearing a bathrobe that was barely knotted. She saw I was bleeding, and with the devil in her eyes, she picked up a carelessly jettisoned rake and sent it flying like a javelin towards Herb. "Get the fuck away from my fucking kid!" She screamed as Herb shielded his camera, even after the rake fell just short of hitting him. "Your fucking kid peed on my fucking leg!" Herb defended, grabbing his wife by her drooping tricep. "Let's get out of here, Char." "Asshole." My mother got the last word in quietly before pulling me up off the ground. My lip was cut, and she tried wiping the blood away with her sleeve. "Are you okay?" "I didn't mean to pee on him." "I'm glad you did, Johnny." "Mom," I called her back then. "I don't want to be the Sewer Baby." "You don't have to be anything you don't want to be," she lied. "Come inside. I'll get you cleaned up." It wasn't long for me to learn that I'd have to be plenty of things. DECEMBER 17, 2006 It was cold in Columbus, and I didn't think much of the city my first time there. Little did I know, I'd spend plenty of time there years later with GCW. I was in town for PRIME's King of Kings by special invitation. I had finalized a contract that would go into effect at the beginning of the year, as did many other newly freed agents from AWC, and when I met with Chet for the signing, he had talked about teasing my entrance just slightly at the Pay Per View. We didn't have anything mapped out, but he promised he'd get back to me at the show with something more concrete. He assured me that PRIME had big plans for me. I ended the night at a brewery bar after a meeting I spent in disbelief over how Chet Worth kept a straight face on, the whole time. I saw Craig walk in, lengthening his neck to look around. I ducked my head down and took a long, slow gulp of whiskey, but the persistent bastard started wandering around, and I wasn't a hard guy to find if you knew who to look for. I had nothing against the guy in particular. I'd never met him before, and wasn't even familiar enough with him to remember what Chet said his last name was, but it was too soon and I was to drunk to handle the inevitable conversation with any measure of tact. "Johnny?" he found me. I looked up and made a sound and acted like I hadn't noticed him there before. "Chet told me you might be here. Mind if I take a seat?" I gestured towards the stool next to me, and he sat down as I sucked the last drops of whiskey through melted ice and lifted my glass up towards the bartender. "Chet said he talked to you?" Craig asked as the bartender came around with a bottle of Jameson. I had a feeling he already knew the answer. "Little bit," I slurred, sliding the glass back towards me for another swig. "Oh, yeah?" He was grinning wide, and I felt my stomach knot up fierce in a way that couldn't have been the whiskey. "What'd you think?" "It's, uh, great," I managed, just before dry heaving. "It's, yeah. It's great." "Hey, man. Are you alright?" "Yeah," I was unable to look at him straight, though. "Just a little drunk." "Maybe you should take it easy on the whiskey, there, buddy." He chuckled, and I forced out a laugh or two, though I resented his suggestion. "Ain't nothing I can't handle," I assessed. "You want anything?" "Nah, I'm good," he waved off. "I just wanted to see if you were up for talking some shop. I was really impressed by your work in AWC." "Come on," I avoided. "I'd feel a lot more comfortable if you had something to drink with me." "I guess one wouldn't hurt," he assessed, and I was already snapping my fingers in the air, trying to get the bartender's attention. "I think working together is going to go over huge." "Didn't I just pour you one?" The bartender disrupted what little attention I was paying to Craig in the first place. "My friend here's got some work to do." I pointed my thumb at Craig. "Make his a double." The bartender nodded and searched for where she had last put the bottle. "The tag scene could really use a boost," Craig thought aloud. "I think if we work on our chemistry, we've got a decent shot at a title run." "I'm shit at tag wrestling," I admitted. "Fair enough," Craig repositioned. "Dusk has always been kind of a lone wolf, too. That's probably why Chet thinks we can play off each other. You can do your normal crazy bum routine, and I'll play it straight." "Eh," I shook my head as Craig seemed not to have notice his drink had been brought out to him. "Your drink's here." "Look, Johnny. I'm really looking forward to working with you, but if you don't feel the same way, you can just tell me. I won't be offended." "It's not like that." I felt really bad about it, and tried my best to explain myself despite the influence of liquor. "I hope I don't sound like a prick or nothing. It's just that I work my ass off, but everyone wants me to be some drunken cartoon hobo, and I'm kind of sick of it. Chet was going on about sandwiches and moon rocks and a bunch of retarded crap like that. I mean, fuck. I was the fucking AWC Champion, right?" "Right." Craig hadn't touched his drink. I could see he really didn't know how to respond, and I really didn't know how to go on from there. "Look, dude. I'm probably too wasted to talk about this shit." I tried to oversell my inebriation. It would be a long hiatus knowing that this next cycle would bury me at worse and leave me no better off at best. It wasn't a knock against Dusk -- he was great to work with -- but PRIME had just picked up a crop of new AWC talent like Pierce Lavelle, Jack Murphy, Captain Suleimon and Chainz. It was difficult for any one of us to make the transition. I had advocated pushing all the new hires as a group of loyal AWC expatriates. I thought that we'd all be more comfortable working together, at least at first. The idea barely made waves because PRIME brass thought this might distract from their main angle, and by the time it gained any traction towards clearance, I felt the timing was too late and had already lost interest. On PRIME's behalf, their main angle at the time did tremendous ratings. Some of the top names in the industry were involved: Snow, Deville, Nova and Rollins. PRIME had also invested serious time building their own stars. Putting us over any of them right off the bat might have undermined some of that work. Binge drinking was also not the greatest motivator for reestablishing a persona. Both professionally and personally, I had put myself through the wringer, and none of my efforts in PRIME really stood out. I wasn't willing to take the same risks that I was taking in AWC main events. Maybe it was self-fulfilling prophecy, or maybe, eventually, everyone falls, especially indecisive megalomaniacs. "Can we do this some other time?" I slouched back in my stool and rubbed my eyes. "I can't do this right now. Too fucking drunk." "Yeah, sure. That sounds good." He got up and shook my hand. He tried to keep his spirits up while I pretended I was trying to keep mine down. "You want me to call you a cab?" "Johnny works." He didn't get it, or he just didn't think it was funny, and I didn't blame him. "Are you sure? You shouldn't be driving like this." "My license was already revoked," I explained. "I, uh, crashed a car into a boat." "Wow," he furrowed his brow, loosely picking at the logistics before abandoning the notion that there had to be any. "It's fine. I've got an endorsement deal with Martin Jetpack." I could tell Craig doubted me, but I had pioneered the usage of the jetpack in wrestling during my AWC tenure. I had purchased a prototype at a sizeable discount but was regretting the installments I owed them. I thought I'd have a lot more uses for it, but as it turned out, it was fairly inconvenient for long distance travel. "Well, um, try to keep yourself out of trouble." I laughed and went back to my drink as he left the bar. As soon as he was out the door, I claimed his untouched drink and made quick work of it. I hated the fact that I was going to half-ass it. I had earned a solid reputation in AWC for helping further the careers of the wrestlers I worked with, and I knew early on that that was the mark of a good worker. It takes a lot of work to look like you're not doing any work at all, and I had mastered the craft. It would've been a hell of a lot easier if Craig was an asshole, or at least if he wasn't so painstakingly eager to work with me, but I figured maybe I had gotten so good at putting effort into looking like I had no idea what I was doing that nobody would notice if I actually went about my career in PRIME completely unprepared. I figured I'd give it a shot. By this point, I firmly believed life was all some cosmic joke anyway. Unfortunately, the Columbus Police Department had a different take on comedy as I had exposed myself to an old woman outside before urinating over the rail of a bridge onto highway traffic. Though news of the arrest further hampered any chance of a push in PRIME, I hadn't had the foresight to book a hotel room. I guess it's true what they say about clouds. JUNE 30, 2000 I think more than anything, Monster Truck Doug let me hang around sometimes because he didn't really have anyone else. He lived with us for a few months, but my mother kicked him to the curb after she found out he was taking me out for rides in his monster truck -- The Americanator -- and getting me high. Doug also provided my first drink and a working knowledge of how to fight dirty. I was told to pack my bags shortly after Doug was when my mother found out I was still smoking pretty religiously, distracting my mind from Jesus. Maybe if Jesus drove a monster truck, things would have all worked out swimmingly. Instead, I ended up crashing on Doug's couch a few days a week, whenever it was convenient for him. In exchange for the roof, he'd send me along on errands. I'd pick up a bag for him here and there or take care of his apartment when he had to skip town and lay low for a few days, things like that. "I hate doing this bullshit," Doug groaned, flicking the roach out of the window of The Americanator. I just looked out the window as we approached the department store for its grand opening. As a local celebrity, Doug signed on to do a bunch of events to keep himself financially viable between monster truck rallies and demolition derbies and other county fair fare. I was there to snap polaroids of people with Doug and The Americanator for Doug to sign. The lot was bare, except for the store manager (who at some point later revealed he had been there since sunup) and a herd of employees. A big banner hung, reading "BULKAMANIA GRAND OPENING" in big block letters and "Get your picture taken with the Americanator!" smaller underneath. Doug piloted the mammoth red, white and blue vehicle up into the parking lot, stopping in front of the store as the manager jogged up to his door. "Look at this asshole," Doug chuckled, an exercise in preserving his sanity before opening the door and breathing the air in deep. "Doug Gruber?" The manager read from a clipboard before extending his hand. "I'm Steve Kelly, store manager, but you don't have to call me by my official title. Just call me Steve." "And you can call me Monster Truck Doug." Doug shook the manager's hand with a firm grip that almost rattled the clipboard out of Steve's hand. "Before we get started here, I wanted to talk turkey. We agreed on $200 for the day, right?" "Let's see here," Steve examined, tapping neurotically on his lower lip. "According to my notes, it was only supposed to be $150." "My agent told me it was $200," worked Doug. "You're the store manager, ain't you?" "I am, but I have $150 written down right here." Steve held up the clipboard. "If you're store manager, I know you can make it happen." Doug smiled, and Steve looked uncomfortable with his meager level of authority challenged. I couldn't help but smile, too, and wished I could take a picture of Steve squirming there, considering whether or not he should defy the clipboard. "My name's on the sign already and everything. You wouldn't want to waste a perfectly good sign, now, would you?" "I'll see what I can do." "Great. That's all I'm asking." Doug shined. "Now where do you want us to set up?" Steve directed us to park the monster truck at the far end of the lot where a table was set up. His theory was that the truck being right by the road would attract passersby without scaring away customers who were there to shop. As it turned out, most of the people there that day were more interested in bulk supplies than novelty oversized trucks. Only a handful of people stopped to pose with The Americanator, and we were there for a few hours. Doug kept a flask of whiskey for such bomb scares, and that flask of whiskey kept us company. "Fuck all these assholes," Doug said to me during what seemed to be a particularly long stretch. He lit a cigarette and gave one to me. We watched as they exited the store toting their economy sized groceries back to their minivans and station wagons. His ego visibly bruised, Doug polished off the flask and wiped his mouth. "Let's call it a day." Doug circled around and got into The Americanator, and I climbed into the passenger side as he started the ignition. "You know," he turned to me. "People don't appreciate shit these days. Don't ever do anything to go and get yourself famous. It's a great, big pain in the ass." "Yeah, right," I joked back. "Like I've got a lot going for me." "Look, I'm going to need you to watch the apartment for a few days, alright?" "I'll clear my schedule. You going somewhere?" I looked up at Doug, and he winked at me. "Yep!" He slammed his foot on the accelerator. "Fucking . . ." I couldn't think to finish the thought. Doug took out almost an entire aisle of parked cars, crushing them beneath the massive wheels of The Americanator. It caused a stir inside the store, and as patrons funneled out and spread into a disbelieving crowd, Steve Kelly pushed his way through, parting the throng with his clipboard. With plenty of film left, I made sure to capture the look on Steve's face before Doug sped away, both of us regretting that we weren't in a more discreet vehicle. JULY 4, 2009 I was excessively drunk, needless to say, and I didn't have a clue what city I was in or what month it was. My work schedule had become far too hectic to keep track of such minute details. Over the last year, I had been rebuilding in GCW, and my skills were sharper than ever. It lacked the backing to be a mainstream federation, much in part to owner Steven Caldera's pyromaniacal distaste for metaphorical bridges. He always believed that GCW was better off on its own, and the man was most likely right about that. He was most likely right about a lot of things. The man just knew how to conduct his business, and he always had his finger over the button. That's why I was pretty surprised when back in February, Steven Caldera had allowed me to sign a contract with PRIME. Lisa Tyler, who I believe at the time was their Director of Talent, had been making some very bold moves, and apparently, I had been generating enough buzz around wrestling circles to catch her attention. I was scheduled to debut as an unannounced entrant of their annual Dual Halo, and from there, I was slated to win the entire event and work the Universal Title card with Jason Snow and Chandler Tsonda at the following Pay Per View, UltraViolence. Caldera thought this would be a great way to get GCW more exposure, and I was bringing in so much money, I just couldn't refuse to double up my load. Aside from that, I was enjoying myself in the ring more than ever, and I was elated at the opportunity to work with such high caliber talent. In the end, I couldn't handle the pressure, and I burnt myself out. During my last month on both active rosters, I was reliably late and usually not sober. I made it a point to always work sober since getting back in form for GCW, but I couldn't fake it anymore. I decided it would be easier if I didn't have to put on an act, and it was easier, but if I only ever learned one thing, it's that good things never come easy. I was on the hard sauce, straight everclear, and it was getting the job done. The police blotter the morning after described me as being in an "unintelligible stupor," which I felt bordered on sensationalism, and I later wrote a letter of complaint to the editor that was never published due to its blatant profanity. After tuckering myself out earlier in the afternoon by breaking drywall shirtless and screaming while doing it, I had passed out on the floor, and I woke up hours later to the sounds of explosions and cheers for explosions. With my vision still sufficiently tilted, I didn't have the wherewithal to draw any semblance of a feasible conclusion about anything. When I came to, I thought I was at work for one promotion or the other, and a claustrophobic panic swept through me. "I gotta get the fuck out of here," I mumbled, or something to that effect. I might as well have been speaking entirely in punctuation. I opened a window, but instead of air, noise flowed in. I stumbled around as space seemed to close in around me and dance away when I tried to move into it. The sound all blended together until it sounded like the earth was screaming, and it felt like my skull was cracking. I chalk most of it now up to being so perfectly shitfaced, but at the time, I couldn't stay there, where it was loud with thousands of voices, all calling for my blood. "I'm done!" I tried to yell over the noise, but it was no use. It all just sounded like I was underwater. It felt that way, too; so thick that I could float on an anchor. "Fuck! I've had it!" I couldn't deal with it any longer, and then and there, I decided that I was going to leave and never come back. There would be no more redeye flights across the country to get from WorldWide to Revolution. There would be no more fans following me back to my hotel to bother me. There would be no more drunken cartoon hobo. For the average man, an escape as intricate and seamless as I had imagined usually took months of fastidious preparation. I had three things the average man didn't: a near lethal BAC, a terrible idea and a Martin Jetpack. I suppose there were a number of things I hadn't thought through all the way. For one, on a full tank, the Martin Jetpack could only travel more or less thirty miles. I also hadn't realized how intoxication would affect my ability to operate the apparatus. I figured that once I got out into the open sky, I'd be fine, but getting there proved difficult. Fitting through the window with that damned machine strapped to my back was no small feat. I began by crashing dizzily to the ceiling, cracking paint dust everywhere. From there, I forced my way through the window, grinding the propulsionary device against the frame so that wood chipped and support rods bent. Once in flight, above the unruly masses, I found that wobbling through the sky proved rather nauseating, and as had become a common theme in my life, ended up emptying the contents of my stomach over the crowded streets. Police monitoring the holiday festivities quickly caught wind of an intoxicated man piloting through the air, regurgitating on pedestrians. With no proper call number for such an incident, communications were slower than normal, and mobilization attempts were insignificantly impeded. First, some patrols cruised underneath me, and minutes later, a helicopter kept a safe distance behind me, but there was nothing any of them could do but yell things at me through megaphones unless they wanted to take me down by force. "Sir, this is the Chicago Police Department," one voice boomed. "Please land your craft. You're endangering yourself and others." "If you want me to come down, open fire!" I challenged back, but over the jetpack and the sirens and all else, who knows if they heard me. "Sir, this is your final warning. If you refuse to come down, there will be serious consequences when you land." He waited to see if I would heed his instructions. "You can't stay up there forever." "We'll see about that!" I shouted back inaudibly. I figured my best shot was to head towards state lines and see if wind currents and regulated amounts of drift would do me any favors. I tried to use the stars for direction, but they all looked the same like little, fuzzy dots of light. Thus began a twenty two and a half minute escapade that was about as uneventful as a jetpack chase on America's birthday could be. It ended anti-climactically, as most things do, when I heard the motor sputtering, choking for fuel. Some fumbling instinct helped me to deploy the built in ballistic parachute. The machine crushed to the ground beneath me and rolled until it collided with a parked car. The police had me surrounded as I floated down, and I landed in a parking lot somewhere, the ballistic parachute folding over me. The Kevlar chute was yanked off of me by a team of police while another, weapon unholstered, barked commands at me. "Stay down on the ground and put your hands behind your back!" I did as I was told and was quickly pat down, cuffed and led to the back of a squad car, where I was read my rights once again. By that point, I had nearly memorized them. I was almost glad I had been caught. The whole endeavor had been more tiring than I had anticipated. "Gotta say," the officer at the wheel started. "I've never seen anything like that." "Yeah," his partner turned back to me through the cage. "What the hell were you thinking?" "I think," I yawned, awareness slowly creeping back in as layers of invincibility peeled off to reveal the aching results of recklessness. "I think I might have a drinking problem." But no weight was relieved, and the road ahead was steep and treacherous.
