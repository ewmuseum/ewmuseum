Den of Snakes: Lies

8 November 2009
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2009-11-08 September 4, 2009 A few grams of hash were all Donovan could stomach, especially after a night of hard drinking. Benny was a lot better off because he didn’t touch either. Never had an interest in it he’d tell others, but that wasn’t exactly true given the fact that he was a drug dealer. But he never did the things he sold, always drawing the line because the temptation would be far too great. He always wondered how Donovan was able to keep his nose out of his own stash, but maybe that had more to say about crank than it did about his friend’s willpower. “You a motherfuckin’ saint, man,” Donovan drawled, his head resting on the leather headrest of the passenger seat. He had the back reclined as far as it would go, his lips buzzing and his hands clapping against his inner things. Benny was glad it was Donnie he was taking home from the bar instead of Jethro because all that short bastard would do was bitch worse and worse with each beer he slugged. He chalked it up to the guy’s Napoleon complex since no matter what, nothing was ever considered good enough. He could picture it now; Jethro picking at the seats of the Neon while he talked about that bitch at the QuikStop. He always talked about poor Ana Marie like she was some cheap whore with a loose mouth, but Benny never was able to see how he could say such things. “Don’t mention it,” he told Donovan, reaching out to give him a gentle nudge on the shoulder before resettling his grip. “Besides...someone’s got to put Bobby to bed. I ain’t seeing you volunteering or nothing.” “He’s your brother, man. I ain’t into that diaper changin’ shit anyways.” “Don’t say it like that.” Donovan might not have meant any offense, but Benny had been taking guff from people about his ‘retarded’ brother for as long as he could remember. But someone had to take care of Bobby after mom ran out on them back when he was just sixteen. “You know I don’t mean no offense by that, man.” There was Donovan, always quick to defuse the situation. In the month that Terrence had been gone on his little wrestling tour, both Benny and Donovan had begun hanging out and doubling up when it came to selling product. Jethro always told him that there should be a fine line between what colors a dealer sells to, but the population of Jefferson County was over half black. That probably was just Spisak’s rhetoric coming through; ever since that crusty Neo-Nazi had been sent to Parchman Farm, Jethro had all but stopped his fake white supremacist nonsense. After all, he’d told everyone that would listen that it was all a bunch of lies anyway. At the end of the day, getting together was probably for best of the business. They were mirror images of each other, low key brothers from different mothers who went with the flow. They also knew how to push without being slimy. “Just because we dealt crank don’t mean we got to play to stereotypes” Donovan would always say. Nights like this were becoming more frequent. The Stonewall Motel’s dilapidated sign flickered just ahead, meaning they were just about home. “It’s cool. Royce’s probably having himself a damn fit with Bobby.” Donovan chuckled. “Man, I can’t believe a hardass motherfucker like him would play babysitter.” “He’s probably got him cooking,” Benny joked, easing left and driving over the lip of the motel’s parking lot before pulling up to the main office. He shifted into park, getting out of his Neon and peeking to see if Oscar was in. The rent was due tomorrow, and he figured he could pay for the rooms now, before he had a chance to forget. It wasn’t as if Oscar would kick them out, but Terrence always felt it was best to be punctual with business arrangements. Benny didn’t disagree with that thought process at all, and even though the old man knew what they were doing, it wasn’t as it he had a problem with it. The glow of a television flickered across Oscar’s wrinkled jowls, the owner of the Stonewall having fallen asleep with his feet on the front desk. Everything about him was round; from his potbelly, to the cut of his white hair and wispy mustache, to his bifocals which exaggerated the size of his baby blue eyes. Benny walked up, rapping the window a few times to wake the old man up. “Hey!” Startled, Oscar nearly fell off his chair. “Wha...wha...” “Sorry about that,” Benny sheepishly winced. They tried to not give Oscar a hard time; he was still stuck in a haze, the death of his wife not sinking in even four years after the fact. “Just getting back. Wanted to pay for our rooms for the next month.” “Oh. Oh right.” “Say,” Benny started as he fished through his wallet to pull out a few hundreds, “where you go earlier? I tried to leave Bobby with you, but...” “I was out by Holmes Lake. Don’t know how much longer we gonna have good weather, you know?” Benny folded the bills, pushing them through a small opening on the screen partition as he nodded his head. “Well if you catch any more of them catfish, I can cut them sons of bitches up and we can have ourselves one proper cook off before winter comes.” Oscar gave a hearty laugh as he unfolded the bills and put them in his pocket. “Lord knows I’d like that. They say there’s set to be snow up around the northeast this weekend.” “So much for global warming.” “Yeah,” Oscar said as scratched his cheek with his left hand, his ring finger a mere stump. “So much for that.” Richard Gentry sat forward as his men waved their flashlights over Terrence Kingsley’s lynched body. He focused over their hoots and hollers, sucking smoke through the filter of his cigarette as he waited for Terrence’s face to turn purple, for his body to relax. The son of a bitch was persistent; he kept fighting the knot behind his back, his body struggling in haggard spins. It was a waste of precious energy; each flinch frittered away what oxygen he had left. Autumn’s shrieks were just behind, but he tuned her out. He had to; it was the only way he’d be certain Kingsley was on the edge. He’d heard men die before, but the sound had long been washed of any resonance, the rattle becoming as meaningless as the crinkling of leaves. Terrence Kingsley was dying. This was his last minute alive. Gentry pushed up to a stand, reaching to his side and unholstering a machete as he began walking to the birch tree suspending Kingsley. His men were becoming more rabid, taunting their target with grabs at his kicking barefeet. They grabbed their necks, taking long breaths that sounded mechanical because of the gas masks they wore. They were undisciplined. Kingsley stopped kicking, stopped fighting the noose. He hadn’t given up; for whatever reason, the harsh, airless gasps seemed louder than the yammering surrounding the scene. Gentry gripped the handle of his blade, his gloved fingers threatening to shave grooves into the wood. He didn’t have second thoughts on this. Kingsley’s father did. A good soldier never disobeys a direct order. You have no idea how lucky you are. The machete struck the rope holding Kingsley in place, the others going silent after the blow. The rope hadn’t given way. He took another hard hack at what was left. The militia men stepped away, letting Terrence fall to ground. Gentry walked towards him, grabbing at the noose and slackened the knot. “Why?” It was a voice from the crowd. Gentry wasn’t sure if he grabbed the right man, but it didn’t matter. He yanked him forward by the collar, pressing his nose into the rubber of the gas mask. “Don’t question authority.” The irony of the statement was overwhelming. He pushed him back into the crowd, turning around and making his way to Autumn. She wanted to run to Terrence just to shield him for any further abuse. At least the two men holding her arms were disciplined, doing their job without acting like raving idiots. The others could learn from their example. “We’re going to take a ride in your car. You’re coming along,” he told her, the words causing her to flinch against the tight grasp of those holding her at bay. Pushing past her, he stalked his way up the stairs to the trailer, his men’s eyes all staring at him. He didn’t acknowledge the glares, instead flicking the light switch on and looking around the living room for her purse. Taking a few steps inside, he looked over to his left, seeing it on her work table along with all her little arts and crafts supplies. I didn’t need to punch her he choked on the realization as he glanced at Autumn’s makeshift puppets. Timid Tammy Lamb had purple tassels and told the shy kids that it was okay to be shy because she was too. Greenie Bear had a heart button for a nose and spoke in a funny baritone and said that sometimes appearances could be deceiving. Gentry was a cold man, but he wasn’t that cold. Was he? Little Autumn didn’t hurt anybody; she took care of the kids in her shop and used those puppets in weekend shows for the kids at the local VFW Hall. Greenie’s just like you because you’re not really as mean as you look she’d told him. He was a bad man, and now he felt guilty for it having to be this way. He grabbed the straps of the purse, dragging it across the carpet as he stomped his way back outside. He went for the doorknob before realizing that it wasn’t there. They had broken it off with a sledgehammer. All that was left was a splintered crater, a gaping wound showing her shattered safety and security. The trees made it hard to read Autumn’s face, the moonlight blotting through branches swaying with the breeze. In reality, he didn’t want to see her face, didn’t want to see the damage he’d done. When tempers simmered, he’d apologize, and hopefully she would accept it. He went to find her keys in the purse, not noticing the machete he was still holding until he let go, the blade slipping down the faux leather and the handle banging against the stairs. He didn’t pick it up. Walking to her Taurus, he fished her keys out and unlocked the front door, opening it up and unlocking the rest of the car. “Put her in the backseat,” he ordered, but they didn’t hear his whisper. They all stood there. Staring. Needing direction. He banged his fists against the hood of her car. “Put her in the backseat now!” The two holding Autumn jerked her towards the car, her legs giving away and her shins scraping across the wet grass. She continued to whimper, to beg and plead. “We’re not going to hurt you. We’re taking you to Father Kingsley.” He hoped this would ease her mind, but it only made her thrashing more frantic. They forced her into the backseat, Gentry’s head hanging as he walked towards the crowd huddled around Terrence. “You men,” he paused, steadying his rocking emotions as Autumn’s sobs cut him to the bone, “make your way back to Rodney”. They did as ordered, taking off in unstructured groups before forming into a cohesive unit, their boots stomping in a haunting unison. Terrence was limp. Gentry crouched over, gingerly easing the rope off of his neck. He tossed it aside, taking a glove off and checking for a pulse. “You really have no idea how lucky you are,” he said as he stood up, spitting on the prone body before kicking him over onto his back. Thought he’d never met him, Uncle Fester was a moving force in Royce Valley’s life. Both were anarchistic chemists, which made him a valuable asset with the club because he had the know how to cook methamphetamine. But it wasn’t until he’d read Secrets of Methamphetamine Manufacture that he picked up a few intriguing tricks, such as lining cabinets in a meth kitchen with dynamite and grenades; that way, it was easier to scorch the earth in case the cops were coming. Fester and Valley both hated the pigs. But he wasn’t stupid enough to live in the same space he cooked. Though there was talk in the ‘industry’ that the hazmat suits people wore cleaning up meth dens was just for show, he couldn’t help but feel a little caution was for the best. The smell wasn’t nearly as strong in room 10. This room was structured differently the meth kitchen; tan walls with powder blue carpet, a pair of floor lamps with swinging arms on the ends of the back wall, a beat up olive green couch with an low standing coffee table in front the television stand along with a queen sized bed just off to the side. The meth kitchen had been gutted, the excess chairs having found homes spread all along open patches of floor in Royce’s room. He lounged back on the couch, Bobby Crowley Junior’s head resting against his shoulder. The local news was on a loop, talking about shit nobody cares about like bank robberies, diet tips, and changes in the local police force. He didn’t want to get up to change the channel; after all, getting Bobby Junior settled down enough so he could get to sleep was a chore in and of itself. He’d even caught him rummaging through the cabinets in the meth kitchen, fiddling around with a grenade as if it were a damn baseball. Boy don’t know no better Royce reassured himself, but if it had been anyone else, he’d more than likely gut them from where they stood. He was getting tired himself, truth be told. Bobby’s snores were making it hard to get some sleep, but he couldn’t help from nodding off a few times before catching himself in staggered gasps. With Clarence around, he should’ve been more worried. The son of a bitch had come up from Mexico just to give him a shotgun? Bullshit he thought as he pet the barrel of his AA-12, the drum magazine resting next to his heels on the coffee table. Outside the room, he heard chatter as a pair of footsteps drew close. He snorted as the door eased open, Donovan being propped up by that beanpole Benny Crowley. Donnie was laughing like an idiot, making enough noise to wake up the ghosts of the vacant rooms up and down the stretch. Benny, seeing that his brother was sleeping against Royce, tried to shush him, but it wasn’t until the barrel of the AA-12 was aimed at his head that he shut up. Royce tried to remain as stoic as possible, but he couldn’t help but start laughing at Donovan’s sudden shift. “Look like you’re about to shit your pants, boy.” Benny kept laughing, shaking his head as he tossed his keys on the coffee table, plopping himself into an open chair. “Sorry we didn’t get back sooner.” Rubbing his eyes, Royce nodded before looking towards him. “Nah, it’s alright. Though Bobby here was a bit busy acting like a damned fool...” Bobby woke up from a playful nudge, bolting from Royce’s shoulder and whipping his head to and fro as he rapidly blinked his eyes. “Huh?” He looked at the group for a minute, relaxing back on the couch. As he fell back to sleep, Donovan staggered to an empty chair, nearly bowling it over as he slumped into it. His arms hung limply over the sides, his head staring up at the painted ceiling. Benny was glaring over at the television, and Royce did too for a moment. When everyone seemed as relaxed, he began thinking about the message, kept going over how he wanted to tell them about his old cellmate making his presence known. “Not to burst you guys bubble or nothing, but we got a problem.” Benny stabbed a lazy finger in the direction of the gun Royce had been brandishing. “Does it got to do with that piece?” “Yeah,” Royce said as he stared down the barrel, his hand patting its fulcrum. “My contact from south of the border...he came up and paid me a visit. Gave me this thing as a present and gave me a duffle bag of ammo too.” “Looks like a beast,” Donovan said as he brought his head down. Royce couldn’t help but smirk as he rubbed his shotgun. “It is. Fully auto. The recoil is all absorbed in the chamber so it’s got next to no kick. This is what we got cleaning up sand niggers in their little huts out in Afghanistan.” Eyes wide as saucer plates from the description, Benny scratched his scalp and flicked the dandruff from underneath his nails with his palms. “I’m failing to see a problem here, Royce.” His smirk dissipated, a pensive stare at the carpet making it difficult for Benny to read his face. “Thing is, It ain’t all a gift. My guy...my contact?” he looked up, locking eyes with the lanky dealer. “He came here to lean on me. Tried to press me for a decision. He even went to take the gun back.” “What you tell him?” “What you think?” Benny broke eye contact. “Jethro’d have a fit if he knew that you turned them down.” “Fuck him,” Royce growled, punctuating the statement with a shake of his shotgun. “Motherfucking midget don’t make the decisions.” Donovan continued slipping over the cushions of his seat, his words heavily slurred. “Well, it’s all good, man.” Valley was barely able to understand what he’d said. “How you figure?” But Mackey spoke casually, his mind jumbled up because of hash and beer. “Terrence is back. Shit’s all good now, man.” “He’s back?” “Oh yeah,” Benny nodded his head, looking back at Royce as he rubbed the heels of his hands against his jeans. “We were stopping by Griswold’s on the way out, and he was beating the shit out of some guys. We tried to give him a hand, but he sort of just ran away from us.” Cocking an eyebrow, Royce glared back and forth between the two. “He’s back and nobody figured to call me?” “Well, I figured he was coming here, probably to talk to you or something.” But that hadn’t been the case at all. He double checked, fishing his phone from his pocket to see if anyone had called him. It wasn’t exactly as though he kept on top of the phone as much as he should’ve been. The screen was blank. “Nah, he ain’t been here. I ain’t got a call on the prepaid. If anything, he’s out with Autumn, I reckon.” “I dunno,” Benny started, “he seemed kind of spooked when we saw us.” “Who the hell was he beating up?” “Well, I think it was Lloyd Stevens. I couldn’t tell though. Motherfucker’s head was all busted open and shit.” It all just seemed so weird. Terrence might have been gone for a long spell, but it wasn’t as though he had a reason not to trust any of them. Benny got up from his chair, an odd sensation of worry causing his shoulders to tighten. “You think he’s alright?” “I dunno.” Royce was starting to feel the same way. “Hazard to say that I’m a bit worried.” There had to be a justifiable reason for it Benny thought, his teeth chipping away at the end of his thumbnail. He always chewed his fingernails when he got nervous. “Well if he’s with his girl...” “Even if he is, he needs to hear about this. Because if we’re getting leaned on, it can’t be good.” Royce touched the central button on his phone, cycling through contacts until he found Terrence’s number. He pressed the green button, putting the phone up to his ear and listening as the dial tone pitch changed. Whereas Benny was more into settling his emotions with reasonable excuses, Valley understood better than anyone that the drug trade changed everything. Even the most rational of explanations went out the window with drug dealers and territory grabs, and with Clarence in the county, absolutely anything was possible. The dial tone continued ringing through, but there was no answer. We’re next he thought as he punched the red button with his thumb, shoving the phone back into his pocket. “He ain’t answering.” Benny took a deep breath. “Well, he’s probably fine. Probably just having a good time with the old lady.” “No,” he cut through Benny’s sheepish laugh, “they ain’t like. I got to go talk to him about this. Watkins is a fucking snake in the grass. He might have paid those guys to jump him.” His mind was made up; he got off the couch, power walking to the dresser near the queen sized bed. Tossing a long sleeved shirt on, he grabbed his worn out black leather jacket and zipped it up, then sat on the bed to squeezing his feet into his boots. He stomped the carpet, getting the right fit for his soles. When he got up, Benny was standing in his way. “Does he even know what Terrence looks like?” Royce was too busy pushing past him, looking over his shoulder and staring over towards him. “You can come if you want.” Benny thought about it before looking towards his younger brother. Bobby was fast asleep on the couch, and more than anything, he had to make sure he was taken care of. “Nah,” he started, “besides, someone’s got to put Bobby to bed.” Royce looked away and continued towards the door. “Put him in bed too,” he chuckled as he shoved Donovan’s sleeping head to the side, stopping his snoring. Even in the dead of night, the dilapidated haunts of Rodney were faintly visible in the moonlight. The Taurus ambled over the dirt road, the wooden carcasses of this dead city becoming one with nature as trees and vines swallowed their exteriors. When Mississippi was just a territory, this place was a mere three votes away from becoming the capital. But the Mississippi River changed course, the cultural hotbed swiftly becoming deserted after the city was abolished in the 1930’s. It may have become a forgotten relic, but Rodney’s dead edifices had become home to a different sort of people. While he wasn’t there when the Kingslian faith was born, Richard Gentry had been one of Father Freddie Joe Kingsley’s best soldiers. He’d been pushed out the door the GM Assembly Plant in Shreveport when there were cutbacks, taking his meager severance package and moving back to the community he’d grown up in. Bored, and with a pocket full of disdain towards the automotive industry and the government which had sold away his innocence in Vietnam, he dedicated his life to combat the incompetent fascists in Washington. The gloved hand covering Autumn’s mouth ensured her piercing screams were be muffled. She was still sobbing, meek little cries far too clear to be ignored. Gentry couldn’t help but keep looking back to see if she was okay, the whimpers cutting through his sternum and leaving nothing but an empty cavity behind. They passed the old drug store, its windows long since shattered. Around brass poles anchoring the roof were chains holding a trio of Dobermans who were padding around the grounds, a scarecrow dressed in Army fatigues sitting in a rocking chair which was swaying. Truth be told, the Kingslian children had changed so much for him, Autumn chief among them; he watched over her like a guardian, trying to be the father he had never been toward his own daughter. He gulped hard, his eyes rapidly blinking as he tried to come to grips with hitting her. He’d blanked out, the mental conditioning disguised as militarism that had been forged in Biloxi gripping him still. He hated Terrence Kingsley because everything that piece of shit stood for was the fundamental reason why America was losing its identity. The kid was a drug dealer, an ex-con who had gone about planting the roots for a makeshift criminal haven in this quiet borderland. How long has he been perverting her mind he asked himself as he peeked to the backseat, the area around Autumn’s right eye beginning to swell. Pulling up through the clearing, he saw the candlelight flickering through the windows of the Old Presbyterian Church. The brick structure had stood for over a century, a cannonball shot at the façade of the church during the Civil War still embedded above the upper center window. On the top of the church was a bell tower encased in ornate white columns, the windows shuttered, the once copper dome over the top now corroded green. In the past, this place stood in opposition of Union forces. It now stood as the home of the Kingslians, their leader living inside. Parking close to the church, Gentry turned to the back, reaching his hand back towards Autumn to try and comfort her. She flinched away from his touch, and rather than press himself upon her, he retracted his arm, turning back around and opening the door to the Taurus. “It’s alright. Nobody wants to hurt you.” It was true, but the sensibility was far too late to resonate. She trembled from the touch of the hands of the two militia men she was sandwiched between. With little fight, they pulled her out of the backseat, making sure to keep a hold of her in case she tried to run away. Even so, it was they who had to do the walking, her heels planted to try and push away across the dirt, then across the grass made slick by dew. Gentry led the way, the iron gate creaking loudly as he opened it. The brick staircase to the church had been eroded, many of the blocks having become loose from their mortar. Some of the edges were sharp, and it caused him to stay in place as his men drug Autumn over. “You’re going to pick up your feet.” She wasn’t listening. “Ain’t no sense in you cutting your soles. Just pick them up, okay?” She wasn’t listening. There would be plenty of time to feel sorry for himself later. He reached down, trying to wrestle her ankles off the ground so he could lift them up for her own protection. He dipped down in a crouch, moving slowly. His intention wasn’t to hurt. She kicked him in the face. She’d caught him across the bridge of the nose. A cloud of dust trailed behind the blow. It wasn’t a strong kick, but it was quick. It caught him off guard. It put him on his ass. She didn’t struggle, even though the grasps around her arms had become tighter. Brushing his face, Gentry went forward, grabbing her ankle and resting it under the crook of his armpit before doing the same to the other. “Come on, boys.” He continued leading the way, tasting the crimson trickling through his mustache. Once at the front step, he lowered her legs, pushing his hand forward and opening the front door. The interior of the church wasn’t fancy; just white walls, a hardwood floor, and two rows of pews facing to the side. Candles rested on the three windowsills on the front and back of the church as well as on stands situated in the far corners of the pews. The service area had a worn metal bookstand, and to the left was a wooden piano. Though the ivories were covered, Father Kingsley still sat in front of them on the bench, his fingers rubbing the polish. When the door opened, he looked towards them momentarily, casting his eyes downward. Gentry slowly walked towards him, Autumn now using her own legs to walk in between the militia men. They sat her down in a front row pew, getting her as close to Kingsley as they could. Her face tightened up, her glare locked towards both soldier and leader as frigid tears shrinkwrapped her eyes. Freddie Kingsley turned around, tilting his head to the side to get a good look at his congregation’s prized magnolia. Seeing her filled him with warmth, easing the intense migraine which made it impossible to sleep. There were no pleasantries to be shared; just questions. He noticed the swelling around her eye, her cheek scuffed up just below. “What happened?” You lost your nerve Gentry thought as he glowered over the sanctimonious bastard. Had Kingsley just let the order stand, Terrence would be dead. He deserved to die; he plagued the streets with drugs, whittled away people’s savings as a loan shark, and had given their faith a bad name. Lord knows what other sins and misdeeds could be laid at the boy’s feet. Was it a change of heart on Freddie’s behalf? Or was it that he was afraid because Wyatt Conyers was not longer the Sheriff of Jefferson County? “I asked you a question,” Freddie started as he rose from the bench, kicking the seat back and causing a loud thud to reverberate through the church. “What happened?” Gentry refused to budge, his nose dangerously close to pressing into Freddie’s. They were sharing the same air. “I followed your order.” But then, he paused, uncoiling the disgust which had stayed trapped inside his stomach since getting into the Taurus. “The orders you changed because you-” “Don’t you start with me, Gentry. Don’t you start.” In Vietnam, commanding officers ordered their men to traverse the slopes, to throw caution to the wind as they ascended the deadly haze of machine gun fire and jungle terrain. They would take the hill, running the Viet Cong out and scoring a small victory in the growing conflict. And then, they would be ordered to leave the hill, allowing the Viet Cong to take it unopposed. That same damn hill could be fought for, over and over again, yet always relinquished without as much as a fight. All because the generals had no concept of how to fight a war. Freddie’s attitude was dangerously close to that same ignorant mindset which fermented Gentry’s hatred for the organized government of the United States. But even so, a good soldier never disobeys a direct order. “We took Terrence,” Gentry meekly stated, his teeth chewing on the inner skin of his cheek, “beat him up good. Strung him up.” “What about her?” “She fought us. We had to restrain her.” Freddie shook his head in disgust. “Then why the fuck is her eye swelling up? Is that part of restraint?” Gentry scratched his nostril as he peeked his head towards Autumn, his guts tied up in knots as he began quivering. “I did it.” Stunned, Freddie took a couple steps back. His brow was furrowed; the wrinkles around his eyes had become deeper and more pronounced in their depressions as he snarled towards one of his most prized followers. “He hit you?” he questioned as he pointed towards Gentry, the militia men releasing her arms. Pressing her arms against her sides, Autumn tried to calm herself down. She nodded her head, refusing to say anything for fear that her crumbling veneer of self restraint would cave in completely. Anything Gentry did also rested in Freddie’s lap; everyone in the congregation understood that the militia was just their leader’s left hand. “I’m not going to lie to you,” Freddie started as he knelt before her, his hand massaging her knee. The process to get down that far was hard; he’d hurt his right knee when he was a wrestler in the 80’s, before modern medicine was able to correct torn ligaments like they were today. “I ordered them to do what they did.” The revelation wasn’t exactly a shock, but she felt betrayed. She hadn’t done anything worth the Kingslians violation of her safety. Yet she didn’t flinch away from the touch. Her breathing became heavy, her eyelids trembling as she nodded her head. “My son has lost his way, Autumn. And I’m not sure if I can...” He chose his words carefully, trying to balance strength along with comfort. “I’m sorry you had to see that. I’m sorry that such a God honest good woman like yourself is got caught up in this. But you have to understand what he is.” She knew damn well what Terrence was, and even so, she was able to look past those faults. His excommunication from the faith had just as much to do with Freddie’s inability to control his own son as it did Terrence’s misdeeds. “He paid his debt,” she told him, her hand grazing the top of his. “And he’s trying to better himself, Father.” How can she be so naïve Gentry thought as he stared towards them. Freddie was always the first to tear his son apart, to tell others to learn from his true son’s mistakes. But the old bastard played along, clasping her dainty hand and bowing his head. “I’ll take that under consideration. Your instincts have always been strong. It’s been difficult to explain the good in my son to certain individuals” Rage shivered down Gentry’s spine as he heard those words. For Autumn, the news was somewhat relieving; part of her fear was situated in the militant behavior of the militia, believing that they were pulling the faith in an irreconcilable direction. At the very least, there may be an answer for the Father’s erratic behavior. Freddie was a good man, caught up in circumstances beyond his control. She wasn’t sure if she truly believed it. At least it made this monster seem less harmful. As Freddie stood up, he nodded his head. “I just want you to keep an eye out. Don’t be too trusting of him just yet. But Royce didn’t listen, instead focusing on the shadow’s face as it stepped out of the dark pool and into the road. Terrence knew that face; it was Richard Gentry. “Hit him!” Gentry’s arms were still up, his steps taking him towards the truck as it continued to creep closer. “He don’t got a gun.” “That son of a bitch tried to kill me.” “He’s surrendering.” “What the fuck do you know?” “Hey,” he yelled towards Terrence, “That man is giving up. If he tries anything, then we blow him the fuck away, but not right now.” Rolling the window down, Royce couldn’t help but hope he was right. All five of men meeting at this spot were filled with apprehension, the four members of the Jefferson County Five riding in the truck all having clear shots at the leader of the local militia. The truck stopped, Gentry making the first steps toward the open window. “I know what you boys are fixing to do,” he started as he stared through the two in the truck’s cabin. “I just want you to know that you ain’t got to deal with any militia. You do what you got to do.” Terrence pointed the gun in Gentry’s direction, the silver reflecting sunlight as his hand quivered. “Where’s Autumn?” Gentry tried to play it cool. If the situation had been the other way around, Kingsley would be dead already. He had to trust that the boy wasn’t like him. “She’s inside. We ain’t done anything else to her. She’s safe. She’s fine.” “Bullshit.” The gun rattled as it was waved, desperation sounding in Terrence’s voice. “You fuckers tried to kill me.” “I’m a good soldier,” Gentry started, the sins of Father Kingsley’s web of deceit now spreading to his son, “but I got to be honest with you. That was all your old man’s doing, Terrence. I ain’t got a problem with you. That old bastard doesn’t even know his head from his ass anymore.” The arm wasn’t as rigid, the momentary pause giving Gentry time to relax. “What are you talking about?” “He’s going senile. He’s been going on and on for months about how he’s going to kill you.” It wasn’t enough to tell the boy that his old man wanted him dead. “Said he’d kill Autumn too.” That statement made the arm straighten back up, but it was the barrel of Royce’s shotgun which drew his attention. Licking his lips, the old biker tilted his head to the side, the stench of death swirling around his words. “Then how the hell is she safe?” “Because he’s a coward. He can’t do anything on his own so he has us doing it.” Gentry looked back at Terrence, pleading to him. “I cut you down. A good soldier never disobeys a direct order but I did, because it wasn’t right.” Terrence holstered his gun, running his hand through his hair. He was sweating bullets. “That fucker told me to hit her. Told me to string her up too.” The thought was bitter, making Gentry spit on the grass as he thought carefully over his next few words. “So you do what you got to do. The militia’s going to go back to what it was before we got mixed into all this.” He drew his fingers to his hands, lowering his arms and walking down the path the truck had traveled to get here. He continued on his path, refusing to look back. He figured he wouldn’t make it five steps, that one of the guys in the bed of the truck would have popped a round off in the back of his head. Apparently, he was a better liar than he originally thought. Or maybe it was the fact that he’d fed Terrence the same sort of bullshit that his father had successfully sold Autumn, playing on the boy’s fears. “You believe him?” Royce asked as he shifted the truck out of park. Terrence scraped his teeth over his upper lip, the sweat stinging his rope wounds. “No. But he cut me down.” The doors to the church shoot open. Before Freddie Joe Kingsley even knew what it was, his son was making a beeline towards him, a pistol trained on his forehead. He’d had his hands clasped with Autumn’s, the two sitting side by side in the furthest front pew. She looked towards her boyfriend, mortified at the bruising around his neck. The gun just made her numb. His father stood up, nonplussed by the chilling glare of his son. Looking over him, he gave a wry smile as Terrence drew near. “You think that works in here?” He blinked. The nickel plating bashed into the side of his head. He dropped to the church’s floor. A pair of teeth from around his cheek had been jarred loose by the blow. He spit them up. Blood pooled around them. His son stood over him, the barrel of the gun pressed against his temple. “Shut the fuck up.” All Freddie could do was laugh as he pushed himself off the floor, running his fist over his lips to catch more of his blood. His son threw a punch at his sternum. He stopped laughing. He could feel the bone crack against his son’s knuckles. He dropped to the floor again. The barrel hovered over the back of his skull. Terrence looked at Autumn, his feral glower unsettling her. Those eyes might have been pointed at her, but his focus wasn’t on her, rather on something beyond her and the church and the world. She couldn’t help but tremble. “The truck is waiting for you out there.” “I was driven over here in my own car.” “You got the keys?” “Yeah, I,” she stumbled, “I have them right here.” “Go to the safe house by Holmes.” The air in the church was thick. It made her feel sick to breathe it. Standing up, she staggered on quaking footfalls as she made her way out. But she paused, staring down at the broken form of their Father as he lay writhing on his own church’s floor. “I could’ve left at any time.” “Is that right?” Those words weren’t his; they were from something else, something terrifying and dangerous and deadly. Terrence tilted his head to the left. His boot struck the side his father’s head. She yelped. “Stop it!” He turned around, roughly grabbing her arm and tugging her close. “I said go!” She slackened in his tough grip, her head twisting as she began to cry. He jerked her forward, her legs barely able to keep pace as he stormed out of the church. When they were outside, he violently threw her arm away. Before she could go back in, the door was resolutely slammed shut, the lock snapping into place. Freddie was beaten raw already, gaunt breaths the only thing he could manage. “We got a lot to talk about, dad.” The voice of his son was sinister; the Devil’s work coming to fruition, striking in the form of his own flesh and blood. His vision was blurred, but he could clearly see the obsidian horns cutting through his son’s hair, a red tail smacking against the pews. Tears formed in his eyes. “You aren’t my son.” The leader of the Kingslians was hallucinating; he just didn’t know it. Terrence nudged his father onto his back with his boot, his skull resting in the pool blood that had come from his mouth. “Then I’ll keep it short,” he said as he plopped onto the floor, staring into the eyes of his twisted father. “You want to kill me?” “I never—” “Shut up,” he interrupted, pressing the barrel of his pistol against his father’s forehead. “You don’t think I know how you operate? How you can’t ever do anything by yourself? You son of a bitch, don’t lie to me. Be man enough to tell the truth.” “You can’t go about your ways.” “And who the fuck are you to judge? I do what I got to do to survive.” The father couldn’t help his own wretched sobs. “Your influence is tainting my congregation.” “I ain’t doing anything to your damn faith.” “You got your hooks in on Autumn. She’s worried about you all the time. And that’s ripping her away from what she’s destined to do.” What she was destined to do? There was the fiery Kingslian rhetoric he’d grown up with ever since his father had woken up from his heart attack thinking he was some mystical being. “Your plans seem to change whenever you need them to, old man.” “God works in—” He pressed barrel into the flesh of his forehead as hard as he could. “What does God have to say about you taking strays and runaways, huh? Or what about brainwashing kids to follow you?” His father was squealing, his hands trying to move the barrel that was cutting a hole in his forehead. The grip was iron though, unapologetic in its harshness. His father tried to reason with him. “That’s not what this is.” “That is what it is. It’s all about control.” If it was all about control, then his father had lost it. Sure, he still had his children, his congregation of idiots following the dogma of a lunatic soothsayer. But the militia wasn’t behind him, and without some form of paramilitary fringe citizens to defend him, his father was emasculated. “You’re lucky.” Taking the pistol off his father’s head, he put it in the back of his pants. He could end his father’s life right here, and more than likely, he deserved it. Maybe there were worse things for a human being to suffer rather than dying. “You’re lucky you’re too pathetic to waste a bullet on.” He picked himself up off the floor, his hands rubbing the rope cuts around his neck. As his anger began to simmer, Terrence reached for the bolt lock, clicking it free and pushing the door open. He swore he could hear his father’s sobs as he limped to Royce’s truck. The thought of it made him smile.
