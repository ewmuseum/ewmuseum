Date: 2010-02-07 Maybe You Can Polish A Turd After All: The Garbage Bag Johnny Story Chapter 7: The Spiral A Garbage Bag Production "If that's a typo, we're going to have to resubmit all this paperwork." -Rehabilitation Center Desk Clerk #1 JANUARY 17, 2010 The art of storytelling was dead. This became apparent to me when Wayne Caballero, attorney-at-law, opened his briefcase to produce a tower of papers that offered the terms of my freedom. The era of documentation had been built on a foundation of pages and pages of records that printed illusions of proof that reality existed, and the past existed so man would not repeat his errors. There were so many pages that it would take lifetimes to read mere fractions, and the picture only partially painted was nothing more than a convergence of colors and lines at its most distinguishable glimpses. When the world eventually destroys itself, all that will be left will be pages that nobody will read, and anything not on these pages will have never existed. Until then, anything important enough to remember is simple enough to pass on and down. Such things need not be written, and these are the things that are life; that have no importance to anyone but the living. Had Wayne Caballero not been such a tall man, his stack of pages would have been all the more intimidating. He told me that Black Donald, who was editing my autobiography, had sent him for me. His tight suits hung off him in the same fashion as his limbs, stretched long and stubborn so that his blood circulated in long journeys and his skin was paler than it should've been. He was a good lawyer. He didn't look like a good lawyer. That's how I could tell. Some people look like they're good at something, and some people are so good at it that they don't have to look like anything at all. His hair was curly; a sign of twisted thoughts, perhaps. His stack of pages proved a worthy adversary even to him. He scratched his hair, not his scalp, but the hair itself as he sized up the pile of drafts. Wayne was an editor of events, not unlike Black Donald, though where Donnie breathed words to life, Wayne buried their corpses, stacked in mass graves, preserved so as not to decompose. "What the fuck were you thinking?" He leaned back in his chair, distancing his face from me, but all I could see were the dagger points in his eyes. I felt my hand on the plastic folding table but didn't see it there. I had seen those dagger points before, and I wasn't scared of them. I would have been scared of actual daggers, maybe, but there were sillier things to fear. "What the fuck was I thinking? What the fuck were you thinking?" I deflected his question by asking myself a question and then asking him a question. Had I deflected with a single question, he would have been prepared to bat it back to me, and deflection with an answer was out of the question. He could've returned the first question with a question, sure, or even two questions, but the second question would hit him before any of his deflections would have ever reached me. Conversational line judges would have already intervened. Wayne understood the physics. He was a man of laws. He waited on whistles. "These demands are unreasonable!" "Look, Johnny." The front feet of his chair slammed against the tile as he tilted forward towards me, collecting himself after the surprise outwitting. I could have been a lawyer. The thought was there, its shadow crossing his eyes as it swirled around in his psyche. "You know you would've lost if this went to court, right?" "Aren't you the lawyer? Wouldn't you be the one losing?" My powers of deflection were inhuman. When I was in detox, going through withdrawals, I heard a voice. After a week or so, I didn't hear it anymore. One of the doctors explained that the voice was just an auditory hallucination. "Well, you could've plead insanity, but you weren't willing to do that." He leaned back again, recoiling from me like a snake after an unsuccessful first draw at prey. "I'm not insane." I said it with conviction. This was a different voice. It was one that resided within me, no more of a hallucination than any thought could be. It told me that it wasn't going to be easy because it wasn't supposed to be. "I don't accept this plea bargain, and I'll defend myself in court if I have to." "Hold on there, Johnny." He tilted forward again, rocking like a ship in stormy waters. I wondered if I should have been rocking, too. The dull, serious room they had prepared for us was shaking, and he was rocking, and I was sitting still. "That's a terrible idea. You could go to prison for years over this. You're not taking child abduction seriously, and you're lucky Dina feels so bad for you." "I never asked for her pity." I surveyed a copy of one of the pages from the stack. "She can't be feeling that bad, though. If I sign this, I have to pay child support for a kid that ain't mine." "It'll look a lot better on your record if you play along and say that it is your kid, though." These were the invisible bricks, and my authorization would trowel the invisible mortar to fit them together into that tower towards the heavens. I was never bothered by my own lies, but I couldn't lie this time knowing how badly I wished that lie was true. "And what's this part?" I showed him the paper, pointing to the line in question. "Restraining odor? Is that some kind of jab at me?" "Ah, shit." Wayne rocked back again, and the chair cried for oil. "That's a typo. It's supposed to say 'restraining order.' I guess spellcheck wouldn't have caught that." The feet slammed down again on the floor, and all I could hear was the crack they made, the feet just slightly off from each other. I couldn't hear the voice over the words I saw on the page. I saw that they were walls and that there would always be walls. The writing said I would have to stay a thousand feet from Dina and her son. It said that I would have to visit a psychologist once a week to help me deal with my issues. If I failed to follow these requisites, they'd throw me back in jail. "Listen to me, Johnny. This is the only play you got." He stopped rocking, and the room stopped shaking, and I stopped sitting still. There was no voice but his, in spite of how deep within I searched, to tell me otherwise. "I'm telling you. Settle." The page bent flimsily in my trembling hand. It bound me, however unfortified, to within the structure it built, the history it wrote. It said that every month I would send money to a woman and her child, though I was never to see them, and beyond the constant reminders of them, every week, I would tell someone how awful it felt until I learned to fool myself into believing I could overcome it all because time would harden me into a scab to be picked off and flicked off the world. "I've already settled." I shook turbulently, willing every muscle to stop, but unable to focus on more than one at a time. If I steadied my arm, my leg would start. If I settled my leg, my other arm would start, and the first arm would start back join back in the shaking. It was like a finger trap, the biggest goddamn finger trap you could imagine, woven on me like an intricate tattoo. I had heard once, about finger traps, that if you relaxed yourself, you'd be free, but whoever said that must have been a filthy liar. "From my experiences, I can tell you that nothing ever stays settled for too long unless it's dead." "Jesus." He scratched at a patch of hair, flustered. "What's it going to take for me to convince you to sign this thing? Do you want to spend the next twenty years of your life in prison?" Some things, I had learned, just didn't make a lick of sense, and though making sense was never a particularly strong feature of mine, I had an aptness for identifying when things didn't add up. Maybe I never really had the right answers, but I had spent enough time with the wrong ones to know they were the wrong ones. Once in a while, I'd stumble across something in between; something that wasn't quite right, but it worked. "Why do you want me to sign that paper so bad?" I deflected his pair of questions with a simple, single inquiry. There weren't any physics behind it, and even had there been, I wouldn't have understood them. I just knew that somehow, through some magnificent cosmic glitch, it worked. "I don't." He stood, and for a second, I was worried his chair would float away. "Personally, I don't give a shit if you sign this or not, but Black Donald thinks you got a story to tell, and this isn't how he thinks it should end." "Then why does Black Donald give a shit if I sign this paper? And why do you give a shit if Black Donald gives a shit if I sign this paper?" My preemptive questioneering set more questions spiraling, and when I looked real hard, I thought I could see them all, unanswered, floating there like big, little molecules. "If you don't know why I give a shit if Black Donald gives a shit if you sign that paper, you don't know Black Donald." I had underestimated Wayne Caballero. He had somehow expected that they were coming and that there would be two of them. I had never had the gall to attempt three in succession. That would've been suicide. "Is that supposed to mean something?" I was intrigued. I had lost track, and that intrigued me. It was like my mind was blank, and the next thing Wayne Caballero said could have been anything. My body had relaxed, and in doing so, the trap loosened and slid away. Part of me wished that someone would have figured that out already. "He's editing you, right?" There was something about the way he asked the question. It had backspin. "Yeah." Blood flowed quickly to appendages. "My book." "You ever hear of that lunatic cult leader who burned down all those cancer kids with behavior problems? Louis Guy?" Knuckles twisted, and I watched him as he jumped out of absolutely nowhere. I nodded, unable to speak until he landed. "I was the state-appointed defender for Louis Guy. That's when I met Black Donald. He offered me a ton of money to throw the case, said he was editing some book or something, and I thought, well, shit. There isn't any way I'm winning this case. This maniac blew up a fucking abandoned warehouse full of sick kids. I throw the case, Guy gets the chair, I get my money, Donnie gets his book, and all's well that ends well, right?" I shrugged. "Turns out, Louis Guy never burnt the place down. One of the kids was a pyro; set off some roman candles that caught something on fire, and with all the old industrial chemicals still in the air from when it was some kind of unabandoned warehouse, those kids never stood a chance." He let slip a smirking quack at his own lawyer's tale. "Tough break for Louis. He got the death penalty, and all he did was drug up a bunch of kids with acid. The worst he would've gotten from that would've been life." I was no longer aware of where I was. There was something poetic about that. "And you want to know how I know that Louis Guy's not a murderer?" Wayne Caballero and his hooks smiled into me. "I got a rich fireman living across the street. He's editing you, Johnny." I had no answers. He had vacuumed them all out of me with the wizened art of his spells. I hadn't even said anything, and though I had already conceded, he kept on, trying to fashion me like an old putty that had lost its ability to maintain shape. "He wanted Louis Guy to fry, said that if the ending sucked, the rest didn't matter." It sounded familiar, maybe something the voice said before it had gone or when it had returned before it had gone again. "And now he wants you out." "Why?" It was the only question I had left in me. "Don't know." He shook his head, mercilessly. "You'd have to ask him yourself, wouldn't you? But I'll tell you what. If you sign these forms and agree to this deal, you can ask me whatever you want about Black Donald, and if I know, I'll tell you." His routine was practiced as he moved from dialog to direction, pulling a pen from his shirt pocket and clicking out the tip for me. I took the pen and pressed it against the paper, but when I tried signing my name, my name did not appear. I started shaking. "What's wrong?" He stood up and bent to hover over the folding table. "Out of ink." It was awkward for a second, both of us somehow expectant that the pen should have worked. It had no reason not to. "Try this one." He handed me a second pen and I signed the plea deal. His demeanor relaxed. He reached out for a handshake, and I did not return it until I realized he was just signaling for me to give him his pen back. "So, what can I tell you?" It was a question, alright, and I didn't really know the answer to this one either. I just sat there thinking until it became unclear to Caballero that I was still interested in answering him. I understood his impatience. I couldn't even focus enough to think about asking him a question. I was too wrapped up in deciding how many questions to ask. I'd always been a numbers guy, but I specialized in small numbers. "If you're not going to ask anything, I've got some other appointments. You're free to leave, too. I'm sure you've got a busy-" "Nah," I focused on the wall across from me. It was too plain to call white, and anything but the most vapid of empty landscapes would have distracted me. "I got a question; just one, though." He waited, and I couldn't ask him while he was waiting. It just wouldn't come out of me until he let his guard down. "How'd Black Donald get his nickname?" He shook his head and chuckled like the voice had told him, too, that it wasn't going to be easy because it wasn't supposed to be easy. His laughter grew more intense, and I was laughing along with him for no particularly good reason. He was laughing like he'd escaped, and I was laughing along with the world. I wondered how many others there were who all didn't know why they were laughing. "You should see that motherfucker dance." "Get your beard wet, and then cut against the grain." -"Monster Truck" Doug Gruber AUGUST 30, 2002 After Lisa was buried, I stayed around Arbuckle, Illinois -- a town so nondescript it was almost fictional -- for no particularly good reason. I was just dragging myself around, feeling guilty that I was too stubborn to attend Lisa's services. That's not why I stuck around, though. I was looking for something. I didn't know what it was. I just wanted to see something familiar. It took me a while to realize that. At first, I stayed away from my normal haunts. The weather was nice, so I slept on benches, mostly, and an old discarded mattress once. After I realized what the stains were, I went back to the benches. I would've realized it sooner, but it felt like I was wandering around a town that had barely survived a disaster. Nobody else seemed to notice the rubble, or they were all just doing tremendous jobs of pretending it wasn't crumbling down around them. The people who passed by looked like they could've been people I knew. Most people looked like that. They looked at me like I didn't come off the same way to them, but feeling like I didn't belong made me feel like I was finally back at home. By the time I had been there a week, I knew I was wanting to leave. I just didn't have a means. I found my way back to Monster Truck Doug's old place where I had stayed a summer when he was off in Mexico. The place used to have a spirit, and after a while, I realized it was an evil spirit. I never thought I'd want to return, but I had run out of other places to go. The spirit was gone. All the air had coughed out from within the building. It looked like a place would look if a place could be dying or, rather, sustained from it. I don't know why, but part of me was disappointed that Doug wasn't there, and I wouldn't have been disappointed if some part of me wasn't expecting him to be there. I looked through the windows, and it wasn't a big place, so I could see how empty it was. I had gone and sold off most of his stuff after he had left me to take care of it. "Johnny." I jumped back. I almost thought the house had said it, but I turned to see Military Issue Dennis standing there in camouflage pants and a white t-shirt. He carried his duffel bag with him towards me on the porch and set it down on the creaking wood planks that someone had haphazardly nailed into a table. He opened the bag and started digging through it. "I didn't see you at your mother's funeral." "I had some car trouble." I felt too guilty to remember I hadn't seen him there either. "Oh yeah?" He sniffed out my lie like a well-trained dog, and that was all he needed to do to ensure we both understood why he didn't have to bark. He kept digging, lifting a family of folded shirts similar to the one he had on, cycling his rations to more distant pockets of the bag. It made me feel younger than I actually was. "Johnny, I feel bad about your mother dying, so I thought I'd get you a little something." He had crumpled his folded shirts into a pile that stacked out of the opening and spilled over the unzipped flap of fabric. "Get me something?" I was confused, not having borne the weight of guilt long enough for it to tire me. He pulled out a battery powered shaver. It was a big old thing. I hear they make them much smaller now. "Like a gift?" "It's not a gift." He warned me, searching for the right words. "Consider it more like an opportunity." That did little to clarify things, and I stared at him, trying to solve what appeared to be a riddle. I had only talked to Doug once since he was with Lisa almost ten years back. He tracked me down in Mexico twelve days beforehand to tell me she'd died. In the moment, I was selfish and ignorant enough to think he was trying to make things up to me. When he found me in Mexico, he already knew I was there. He didn't tell nobody about it. He felt bad for how things ended between my mother and me, but I figured he could tell that I didn't feel bad. Looking back, I don't think he felt bad for me, either. He felt bad for Lisa. "I volunteered you for the Army." Dennis handed me the shaver, and I took it, not quite processing his words. "You're off to basic in two weeks." "What?" It was starting to sink in, slowly, like wax burning off a candle. "It'll teach you how to be a man." He straightened up, shoulders parallel, standing like a man is taught to stand. "You should really get rid of that hair." "It's really grown on me." I explained myself quite seriously. "I'm not taking 'no' for an answer." His voice was more rigid than his posture. I knew I didn't have the stamina to argue, but there wasn't much else I could do but resist until I went limp. "You're going to have to, because this is bullshit." My body thrashed and kicked around as the fibers of rope burned into my neck. "You can't just sign me up for the fucking army without my permission." "It's a shame you missed the funeral." He skipped over the protest, opting to wait out my fit by reorganizing his possessions and closing his duffel bag. "Your father was there." It went black there. I was no longer able to breathe, and my body constricted as my lungs tried to pull all the oxygen from my muscles. I lost the strength to fight and the wherewithal to know what I was fighting. Lisa never told me who my father was, and I pretended it never bothered me. I didn't know why it mattered, and maybe it didn't, but it mattered to me that Dennis knew and I didn't. I couldn't stand that he had it to hold over my head. "You make it through nine weeks in basic, and I'll help you find him." He looked me in the eye, and I wanted worse than anything to tell him I didn't care about finding my father. I tried to form words, but I thought if I did, I'd cry, and I wasn't going to let Dennis see me cry. "Lose the hair, kid." With that, Military Issue Dennis slung the duffel bag up over his shoulder and walked away. I still fought back tears for a while until after he was out of range, and I punched through one of the decrepit house's window panels. The glass cut up my hand pretty good, and punching through a window didn't make me feel any better, but there wasn't much I could do about it after it happened. As I wrapped my hand in the waistband of my shirt, I decided that there were certain things that just cost too much to know. I walked to Gas 'N Shit. Randy, who sold replica wristwatches out of a trench coat, was hanging around out back. He wasn't anything if not reliable. I felt bad going straight to Randy after Dennis left, but that didn't stop me. Dennis had only meant to help me while Randy only managed to help without knowing he was doing so. It saddened me there that meaning offered little more than comfort, but it also taught me that meaning had little value beyond it. I came to learn a lot of things about the weight of guilt. I learned it only got heavier and heavier still, and whether I dropped it off alongside the way or carried it until it crushed me would determine what kind of man I was. I doubted Randy would pay me much for the shaver, but he'd pay me something, and it'd be a start. "I hate doing this bullshit." -"Monster Truck" Doug Gruber JANUARY 25, 2010 Psychology always seemed like kind of a bullshit science to me.
