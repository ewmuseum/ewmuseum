Date: 2009-12-02 Maybe You Can Polish A Turd After All: The Garbage Bag Johnny Story Chapter 4: The Unvacuumable A Garbage Bag Production JULY 4, 2000 It was one of the first parties that had autonomously developed since I had acquired Doug's place, and we had all done a bunch of acid and started a bonfire out back. I didn't know any of the people there very well. Most of them were friends with the acid guy, who everyone knew as Acid Guy. I think his real name was Charlie or something, so I could see why he went by Acid Guy. It was a much better name than Charlie and pretty much any other name I could think of that he might have had. Acid Guy dropped out of school to concentrate on buying psychedelics wholesale to distribute in more manageable quantities to the everyday consumer for a modest markup. He lived in a pickup truck, and at the time, I thought that was a worthy aspiration if it wasn't for the company. Maybe there was something wrong with me, but I just wasn't enjoying the fire as much as everyone else. They were all fascinated by the way the flames danced and the light refracted off in trails, and they were all fascinated further that all of them were, in fact, fascinated. We'd bought a bunch of fireworks from across state lines, but everyone was too scared to light them off for some reason. I wanted to light them off, but I forgot we had them, because I was sitting there looking at all these strangers and listening to the strange noises they made. Their voices brewed together in a strange muck of squeaks and honks and other curious shapes that left me uncertain that I could successfully communicate with the group, and it didn't seem to be bothering any of them that the vapors from the fire were mixing with the noise. The words hung there and burned, and as I saw them, it became horrifyingly clear to me that it was all incoherent drug babble trying to outdo itself. "It's getting too crowded in here." I stood up from my lawn chair with my hands over my ears, and they were all saying things at me. "Too much fucking traffic." "I think Johnny's flipping out." I couldn't tell who it was from. "Hey." Acid Guy rose at me from his chair, but I could just see his head, and it looked like it was floating, and a large tail of smoke dragged behind the head. "Johnny. You okay, man? Snap out of it." "Stop it!" He started snapping with invisible fingers. "You're all just making it worse." I jogged off, and their voices followed behind me. "Johnny!" Acid Guy was the only voice I could identify. "Where you going, man?" "He just got up and left, man." I heard laughter, not the kind that understands a joke, but the kind that oozes out like a squeezed custard. "Did you see that?" I kept on through a patch of woods, and came out alongside the highway. I felt like I was parting a sea of flashing light as I maneuvered my way across the pavement and the mad jazz of more raging traffic hit me like tidal waves from all directions. One of them hit me hard, and I fell. A large metal beast roared hellish, and then a panel unhinged, and a tall shadow stepped out behind the flood of light. I couldn't tell what he was saying, but I knew there was an organism operating the wrathful vehicle. I knew it all along. I tried to get to my feet and realized I must have looked so alien to them. I raised my hands and tried to slowly explain myself, hoping that would ease tensions. "I'm just passing through." The words were coming out all mutilated, like they were shredding between my teeth. "There's a graveyard not too far from here, and I belong there." It all sounded like a muttering to me. The metal capsules around men growled and hooted, and the men inside them shouted along. The devil conducted music and only I could hear it, and behind the music, a jury of my peers considered my appeal. I was suddenly stranded on a median. Some of the lights flashed past again, and there were other lights. If it only could have ebbed to a clearing, I would have been fine. Instead, I was left with this shifting quilt of darkness and static in quickly knitted blocks. I must've been in space, and there were stars, and it was all getting so amplified that I figured there was no way to right myself from it. I didn't really know how I was going to get where I was going, and I didn't know why I needed to get there so badly, but I knew it was a graveyard, or somewhere quiet that wasn't a library, which pretty much narrowed it down to a graveyard. I thought about the ease at which I could dismiss this urge and learn to appreciate that I was on an island that separated one collection of whirring lights from another. I had just about resigned to my islander fate when something beyond this realm pushed me, as if something was pushed behind me and the air that was displaced had nudged me forward into the black. It was lucky for me. The bright, furious eyes of the mechanism veered away from me, and the behemoth jumped to the median. Had I still been there, it would have eaten me alive. The conductor left his cabin of levers and buttons, and the rushing lights stilled around and hushed. The conductor hulked up in size, and his mouth started to expand, baring sharp, carnivorous teeth. The noise was deafening; the most ungodly profane siren I had ever heard. I zipped off across the pavement and didn't stop running or screaming until I lost my breath. Running on LSD, everything went by so fast that by the time things settled, I had lost sense of the direction I was moving. "The wind is the whispering of lost ghosts. The wind is the whispering of lost ghosts." I repeated it over and over again, trying to regain my bearings. I was in some kind of field, and it smelt like dirt, but not the kind of dirt that people get buried in, so I trudged through it. I needed the air, but I also needed walls close on all four sides, so that I couldn't get away from any of them. I wanted nothing more than to lay there in an open grave and let dead air pass by over me under dead, drifting clouds while I came down. It was all part of this creepy thing I did whenever I was freaking out on acid. I knew I was getting closer. I could see it off in the distance, but I couldn't tell whether it was getting further or closer as I walked towards it. It had somehow sneaked past me the first time I reached it, but I eventually found my way back. I had learned from an old Navajo riddle that the best way to spot an open grave is to look for a mound of dirt. As mounds of dirt were never lauded for inconspicuousness, I was able to locate one without much struggle, and I climbed in and lied down on the dirt looking up out of the hole. "Close your eyes and go to sleep, so things at night that spook and creep can do their dirty deeds in peace, and the sun will rise in the morning." I recited a lullaby that my mother used to sing to me. I don't know why it stuck with me and found its way there with me in that grave. "While the butcher prepares your meat and soldiers die across the sea, the angels pick which souls to keep, and the sun will rise in the morning. " Then I laid there in the quiet and began to count out the passing seconds by the least standard of measurements. The concept of a second was foreign to me beyond the speed at which I could recall numbers in sequence. I was counting out how long it would take for it all to go away. "Johnny?" It had been 517, and then I heard my name. It was a soft voice; the kind of voice I wouldn't have minded the angel of death having. "Johnny, are you out here?" I'd let it find me. I knew it would if it was supposed to anyway. "I saw you come in here!" I heard dirt shifting beneath light footsteps. Then a figure graced the miles of rectangular sky. "Johnny? What are you doing down there?" "I was just, uh." I searched for an excuse, any excuse really, but I drew a complete blank. "You're not the angel of death. Who are you? And how did you know my name" "I'm Dina," she introduced herself, and as far as introductions went, I had done much worse. "I was, um, at your bonfire." "Right." I couldn't place her. It was probably all the acid. She might have been the one that looked like a talking spider to me. The observation was in no way meant to be unflattering. "I just thought I should tell you that Acid Guy and Scott Finch are throwing some of your shit in the fire." I stood up in the hole and looked at her standing there, all big, like some giant talking spider. "Which one's Scott Finch?" "The kid with the bowl cut." I nodded, for the simple sake of nodding. I had no idea what she was talking about. "Did you want to do anything about it?" "Nah, fuck it. I'm going to stay here for a while." I remembered my manners. "You want to lay in a grave with me for a while and look at the sky?" "That's kind of weird." "Come on. It'll be fun." Dina brushed a wisp of hair away and looked out the sides of her eyes before sitting down at the edge. I lifted her by the sides and helped her into the grave. We looked up. "See? It's not so bad." "Yeah, I guess not." She looked confused. "So what are we supposed to be looking at?" "Nothing in particular." We looked up for a while, and the sky was black, blacker than the dirt. "I don't get it." I wondered if what we saw could have been interpreted any differently. We were looking at the same thing, and it was all we could see. "I don't think you're supposed to." I saw her smile, and I could tell from her eyes that she didn't know why she was smiling, but it made me smile. I felt calm, so distant from all the turbulence on either end of time. A burst of green popped across the rectangle of sky and then blues and reds. All the colors exploded against the black, and it made the black disappear. I whispered something to her, a mere suggestion, and she laughed from disbelief. "No way! They're going to bury someone here!" "And you don't think that it'd be cool?" She pursed her lips tight, and I could tell that she was struggling hard not to agree with me. "Don't picture it as a grave. Just pretend someone dug a hole for some other reason, and we just happened to be lying in it together. It's just a dirty hole until someone stuffs a stiff into it, right?" "I can't believe I'm even thinking about doing this." She rolled over onto me and looked down into my eyes. "You know we're both going to hell for this, right?" "Eventually." It was the night I met Dina, and I remember it as clearly now as it happened then. AUGUST 18, 2009 It was my birthday, and I wasn't excited about spending it in a rehabilitation facility. I wasn't very excited about spending any of the other days there either, but I was getting used to staying there on that island. Some times new people would come in, and other times old people would leave, but for the most part it was just a bunch of addicts passing through so there were always just enough. It reminded me a lot of what Monster Truck Doug's place would have been like if I could have envisioned some way to control it, except there weren't any drugs allowed here. Most of the people there were genuinely uninteresting. A lot of them traded stories about their highest highs and lowest lows, but none of it was any good. They were all just trying to outdo each other in a contest with no criteria. I kept to myself, mostly, and I found that if you didn't respond much, other people would generally leave you alone, and you could learn things about people if you were so inclined. We were all in a room, all of us drunks, sitting in folding chairs that faced a podium. They were still telling stories to each other before the discussion leader got there. Some of the drunks were young and some were old, and they came in all sizes. The one that was talking went by Jackie Triggerfinger. It was the nickname he earned for always being the first to draw his firearm back before he got kicked off the force. He was relatively young and clean, and out on the street, nobody would've ever guessed he had a drinking problem by the looks of him. There are two types of drunks. You got the ones that look like drunks and the ones that don't. Here in Shady Acres Rehabilitation Center, you mostly saw the latter kind. On the outside, not too many people cared about the former kind enough to help clean them back up. "So, I'm chasin' the mook down this alley, see? And I'm drunk off my ass on the clock again, citizen's tax dollars hard at work and all, and this mook, he decides to jump over the fuckin' wooden fence!" He made cuts and slices with his hands to demonstrate as his armpit folded over the backrest of the chair. "I'm too off my ass to be climbing any fences, and I'm about to let the mother fucker get away, but then I hear him swearing, and this fuckin' guy, he surrenders! Turns out, the backyard he jumped into was doing some gardening, and this fuckin' guy, this fuckin' guy! He lands in a huge pile of fertilizer and compost. I have to cuff him, and he's covered in shit and garbage. Can you believe that shit?" "Everybody please settle down." The discussion leader was an older, tubby woman with glasses and a man's haircut. She claimed to have been a recovering alcoholic for over ten years. "Alright, then. I hope all of you are doing well on this blessed day. Now, before we open up with the topic of today's discussion, does anybody have anything they'd like to share with the group?" An unassuming, smallish woman in the front row of seats raised her hand. "Hello, my name is Minnie, and I'm powerless over alcohol." Her voice was nervous, but her words seemed sure enough. "Hello, Minnie." There was a smattering of applause from those who elected to clap. "Alcoholism got in the way of my life to the point where my children were taken away from me and turned over to the custody of my loving sister, Adrienne." She stopped and played with her hands. "I've been sober for three months, now, and if I make it another three, my lawyer says he can probably get me full custody back. I'll be a mom again!" "Thank you, Minnie. That's excellent news." The discussion leader directed everyone to clap again, and some joined in. It didn't last long, and it sounded more like wood blocks falling off a cart than applause. "Is there anybody else?" I looked around at everybody looking around and looked away from people looking at me. It was like a collision of ill-meaning atoms, inexperienced in the art of fraud. "Anybody?" She looked around from the podium at us looking around at each other looking round. "Then we'll move along to the topic of today's discussion. Whether it be in our careers or our personal or spiritual lives, alcohol stunts our ability to grow. Think of a time when your growth was stopped by your need for alcohol and what it cost you, and reflect upon what you can learn from it." "Any volunteers?" She looked around with eyes like an owl's and spotted a younger guy, chewing on his pen cap. "Yes?" "So, like, you're saying I would've been taller if it wasn't for alcohol?" A few people in the room laughed. He seemed pleased with himself, though he looked like he was still trying to figure out his own joke. "No, that's not what I mean." She dodged the shoe and straightened the course. "If there aren't any other serious volunteers, I'll call on someone who hasn't contributed in a while. Let's see. John Gumphires?" I was just getting used to the name, and I looked up. She locked onto me and nodded, raising her arm to will me standing. I unfolded my hands and followed orders. One of the best ways to convince someone to do something is to surprise them. I had once won an old portrait of a Portuguese Naval Captain by running through an auction house screaming and on fire. "Hi, my name's Johnny, and I'm an alcoholic." "Hi, Johnny." It sounded like I was a speech impediment rally. Some of them didn't even get my name right. "I been sober a little over a month now." A couple of claps. Most had lost their enthusiasm for clapping. "I guess I can't really relate to the topic. Sure, I got drunk and fucked a lot of shit up, but I've had enough where it'd be greedy to want more." "What about wanting more for others?" The owly leader's suggestion left a sour film in my mouth. I had never asked for much, but I hadn't given out any. "I'm sorry. I don't really have an answer for that." I sat back down and stared up at the puce colored ceiling, unaware that it was puce, but even if it wasn't, puce would have been a fitting name for the color it was. "That's what this is about, John. It's about learning from your past mistakes and using that lesson to better yourself." She ushered to someone behind the curtain. "Now, I've got a special treat for you all today. It is John's birthday, and someone he was close to growing up has volunteered to speak to us today." Initially, I was feared with dread and confusion, but it didn't take long for the narrow list of possibilities to settle on the only person I ever really cared for that was still alive. But it wasn't Dina. "Please, everybody give a warm welcome to Randall Watkins." Some rested clappers reentered the mix, and Randy walked out. It was him, but it wasn't. "Randall is also a member, and he's been recovering from alcoholism for over three years now." He stood there smiling as the owl lady gave up the podium. His hair was trimmed short, and his mustache was gone. It was like he had nothing to hide. I had never seen him outside of a trench coat, but he wore a sweater and fucking khakis. I hated Randy, and even though this man wasn't quite Randy, I couldn't help but hate him, too. "Hi, everybody. My name is Randall Watkins, and I was powerless over alcohol." He adjusted the neck of the podium microphone. "Hi Randall." "I've been clean for three years, and I've had time to reflect on all the mistakes I made under the influence of drugs and booze." He cleared his throat. "I used to be nothing more than a two-bit criminal, taking money unfairly to support my addiction, and unfortunately, I recognize that on my road to what I thought was easy money, I was hurting other people. One of them is here today. John, I know you probably don't think much of me, and you shouldn't, but I read in the newspaper that you'd admitted yourself here, and I wanted to apologize for how I have wronged you in the past." He paused and looked at me, and I didn't know what to say. However, a man once surprised is twice careful. I didn't say anything. "Instead of conning people out of money, I was able to work my way up the chain at a retail electronics store, and I'm now proud to go to work everyday as the assistant manager at Big Deal Electronics. I've found that it's a lot easier to earn an honest dollar through hard work than it is to make a quick one neglecting your conscience." Randall pulled the microphone from the podium, and he began walking towards me. It was all so awkward and surreal, and then the wire went slack and he couldn't come any closer. "I have found success and happiness, and I want to give that same feeling to others who are in the same position I was in. Johnny, I'm offering to be your sponsor. What do you say?" Now I had been asked a direct question, and I felt that they would wait longer. The opposite of surprise is anticipation, and strangely enough, it is equally effective. So shall the meek inherit the earth, the patient shall inherit its problems. "I don't know." The eyes that watched also levitated, and I rose like a baked bread. "I guess." The microphone dropped to the paneled floor, and a loud pop burnt through the speakers as Randall Watkins, new and rejuvenated, ran over to me and wrapped his arms around my body faster than I could do anything about it. At the time, I was mortified with discomfort, but looking back over the years, as far as birthdays went, I could have done a lot worse. DECEMBER 12, 2006 It didn't take them long to find me for crashing that car into them boats, but I got off easy thanks to compromises I was willing to afford thanks to my celebrity status. I was standing behind the curtain, and I was even more nervous than I usually felt behind curtains. As part of my community service, I was to go around to high schools before winter break and speak about the evils of drinking and driving. Since no respectable wrestling company would want to be associated with a man who was most recently spotlighted for such betrayal of character, I elected to mask my identity by wearing a gray wig and thick glasses. I also went by a different name. Still, I insisted on entering gymnasiums to an entrance theme that I performed myself. That insistence was swiftly denied. "Students, please join me in welcoming our special guest speaker, Herb Sellers." I resisted the urge to showboat as I jumped through the slit in the curtain, but I was drunk. I had to get drunk for these things, or I'd never get through them. Adolescents were always the toughest crowds; all that puberty going on inside them and shit. I didn't have to wait long for the kids to stop clapping. There was nothing uplifting about listening to some sad stranger lecture you on the dangers of having fun. I grabbed hold of the sides of the podium to keep me upright. "Hello students of James Warren Jones High School, home of the James Warren Jones Madmen. My name is Herb Sellers, and I am here to tell you how alcohol ruined my life and how it will ruin your lives, too." I actually felt like this had been one of my best introductions yet, even though most of the others could have gone a lot worse themselves. "There are as many alcohol related deaths in America as from toxic agents, falling off ladders, and adverse reactions to prescription drugs combined. Every year in America, one out of 3500 people dies of alcohol poisoning. If you take everybody in this school, that means by the time you finish high school, if you're lucky enough to finish, alcohol will have already probably killed several of your classmates." I could hear the students talking to each other through my pauses, but I went into the community service sentence feeling that if I could scare just one child, it would have all been worth it. "Look around you!" I commanded, pointing like a drunken Moses at faces in the crowd. "Will it be you? Or will it be you? Or maybe even you!" The staff members all looked worried behind me, but none of them yet could summon the courage to stop me. I knew then, though, that I was close, and there would be no other way to end it. Needless to say, I didn't take my service too seriously. It takes a fool to argue with a statistic because statistics don't argue. They're just numbers, and if numbers could argue, they'd be unbeatable. "I was young once, too, and I thought I was invincible. I thought that bad stuff never happened to young people, and I got behind the wheel. Big mistake!" I paused for effect, but there weren't too many people paying attention. Young people didn't appreciate the importance of a good, effectual pause. "Why, just the other day, I had a few too many, but I remember I was supposed to fulfill my community service obligation for a time that I had crashed a car into some boats while under the influence of alcohol. I should've rescheduled, but I told myself I'd be fine. The next thing I knew, I was driving on a field, and this gigantic psychopath wielding a big foam hatchet popped up right in front of me. It was the biggest god damn person I'd ever seen, and I hit it pretty hard. Luckily, it turned out to be a costume and not an actual person, or I'd probably be in jail right now." Several of teachers immediately stood up and ran out the double exit doors in the near corner of the gymnasium. This was about as far as I'd gotten in these things before I was physically stopped, but I was feeling something special about this one, and I figured I'd push my luck before the cops got there. "Now, I'd like to take some volunteers from the crowd, if you don't mind. Who out there has alcoholic parents. Raise your hand!" I craned my neck to look around. "Come on. Don't be shy!" At this point, I was stopped. A man in a tie who turned out to be the vice principal shoved me away from the podium, and I was so drunk that I fell. Luckily, I knew how to play ball with men in ties. "Students, back to your classes!" He bent over the podium and watched as the teachers ushered students back through the doors in one reluctant stream. He put his hand over the microphone and looked back at his faculty. "Did someone call the cops? Yeah? Okay, good." "Shit!" I winced. "I think you sprained my ankle." I paused for effect and rolled around, clutching my leg. Men in ties appreciated pauses for effect. "You son of a bitch! I'm going to sue everyone I can." This made him readjust his tie, and I knew it was a good sign. "Somebody call me an ambulance." The remaining staff members looked around at each other and then back at the vice principal for permission. "Please! I can't walk!" I saw the vice principal making calculations in his head. He was thinking about the budget cuts and the court case fees and the residual effects of the negative publicity on future funds issues and district reputation. Numbers. They're fucking unbeatable. "Please! I think it might be broken!" When it all was said and done, the school agreed not to press charges. They even signed my form saying that I had completed that part of my community service without incident. Up to that point, that assembly and my willingness to compromise were among the most charitable gestures I had ever made. DECEMBER 1, 2009 After I was released from rehab, I took on a few dates because I needed the money. I didn't want to get back to wrestling so soon, and I wasn't sure I wanted to get back to wrestling at all. It was a life that swayed from rough to rewarding in fell swoops. I had entered this competition, and I overheard some of the guys who were in it talking about how there was a big market for wrestler autobiographies. I felt like the story I had to tell was interesting enough, so I told it to some guys at a book company, and they said they'd find me a ghostwriter. I didn't think that a ghost would make such a good writer, though, and it was just one more knife taking a cut of the kitty, so I sold them the idea of letting me write it myself. As long as I adhered to strict, fast deadlines and wrote competently enough that they could still sell it, they agreed to consider it. They told me I had to write it so fast because time was money and the time for wrestler autobiographies might pass, and that'd cost all of us. I had been writing for a while, hopped up on a mixture of things to keep me awake, and I realized that I had been writing a lot about Dina. I hadn't really considered how deeply the way things ended between us had stuck with me, but writing about her reminded me fast, and I realized that hadn't had much luck with women since. I'd never apologized to her either, and there weren't many people left that I could still make things right with. I needed a break from the autobiography anyway. I was scared that they wouldn't think it wasn't any good, and I felt a long drive could help me think things through. It was a few hours of road, and I thought about how I'd tell her I almost hadn't had a drop for six months and about how I only left her at a gas station in the middle of nowhere for her own good and I was sorry about it. I'd kept tabs on her once every so often, and I knew she was still living in the same town, but I had no idea if she was still living in the same place. If not, I still had the drive. It went by quick, despite all the anticipation. I had the radio on, and I didn't pay attention to the songs as much as most people, but they kept me going, and I had a pack of smokes that kept me going pretty well, too. The thing that kept me going most of all, though, was imagining Dina back in that passenger seat. I wasn't too hopeful, but if the night ended with us on our way to California or Colorado, I would have been pretty happy about it. Fuck all else, I would've been happy about it. I wondered if she'd look the same, if she'd still have that pretty face that scrunched up when she tried to be angry and the same haircut. I worried most about whether or not she'd have that same glow in her eyes. I didn't want to have to blame myself for taking that away from her. I blamed myself for enough; for too much to take back in a single lifetime. When I started getting closer, the apprehension set in, and I was feeling far worse. I hoped she had moved, and I wouldn't recognize anybody in the place. I could explain politely that I was an unvacuumable dirt salesman, and I could give them a demonstration. I even stopped before I got into town and filled my inside coat pockets with dirt. I don't think I was as scared of seeing her unhappy as much as I wondered how she'd see me. I pulled into the parking lot of her small complex, and I picked out the window on the second floor, right side. It was hers, and I could see through it to her living room. There was a Christmas tree in the far corner of the room. I turned off the ignition and waited there. Light from a television reflected on the wall, but nobody was in there watching it. Then, I saw her, and it had to be her. The hair hadn't changed, and as frightened as I was, I thought she was still so beautiful that I couldn't help but get out of the car, quietly taking a few steps closer, just to see if I could see that glow. She was talking to someone, and she looked happy; happier than I had ever seen her. I felt bad for coming. I thought that maybe I really didn't make her that happy. Maybe she'd barely remember me. Then, there was a head, slightly clearing the windowsill from the angle I was at. I stepped back. It was a kid. A boy. He looked to be about the right age. I swallowed hard and walked up to the buzzer. He could've been mine. Her last name was written next to her apartment number on the buzzer. I looked past the names: Schwartz on 2L, Johnson on 3R, Rodriguez on 3L. I felt good about 3L. "Hello?" Rodriguez answered back over the intercom with as thick as an accent as I had hoped. "Hi, this is, uh, Jim Schwartz. I'm in the unit below you, and I came downstairs to throw out my trash and forgot my keys." I sold him the dirt. "Can you buzz me in?" Without verbal response, the lock clicked and the speaker buzzed. I walked up the stairs as quietly as I could and had to concentrate on my breathing as I stood in front of her door and stared at her address. I reached into my inside coat pocket, and leaving crumbs of dirt, dug out an envelope with her name on it. A check for as much money as I could spare was inside of it. I tucked it under the door and felt bad about getting dirt everywhere. The cleaners would have a hell of a time getting it out of the carpet. And then I left, slowly, quietly, hoping that maybe she'd open the door. But she didn't. I didn't know what I'd do if the kid was mine, but she had that glow in her eyes, brighter than ever. I didn't want to take it away. She had that glow, and maybe the kid was our son, and we'd be a family. But maybe it wasn't my kid. Maybe it was someone else's. When I got back in my car, I was smiling. I don't know why, but I was, and I wasn't happy. I smiled, and I don't know why, because I was sitting there thinking that it hurt me more to see an ex-girlfriend alive than it did to see my dead mother two days prior. NOVEMBER 30, 2009 I had settled down from the outburst in front of the graveyard, and I was back home after driving three hours back from Arbuckle, Illinois, a town so forgettable it could have been fictional. Still, it was my hometown nonetheless. When I woke up, I found myself lying next to a half empty bottle of cheap whiskey. At first, it bothered me that it was Cutty Sark, but after the sleep got out of my head, I realized that I had drank half of it. I hadn't had a drop in almost six months, and I couldn't believe that I had fallen off the path. I felt shame, and I still wasn't used to feeling it so much.
