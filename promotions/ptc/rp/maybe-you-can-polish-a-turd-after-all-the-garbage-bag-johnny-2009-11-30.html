Date: 2009-11-30 Maybe You Can Polish A Turd After All: The Garbage Bag Johnny Story Chapter 3: Reflections A Garbage Bag Production SEPTEMBER 3, 2006 I had been continuously drunk and without sleep for two whole days, and I had become so far gone that I'd been cunningly deceived into a conversation with an investment banker. I was in Bermuda, at the time, for Atlantic Wrestling Club's premier pay-per-view event, Coast To Coast, and I had just won the AWC Transatlantic Title in a main event that was either hailed or damned as avant garde by pay-per-view main event standards. I was still wearing the damn thing when I stumbled into The Fiddly Winkle, another one of those backlit bars where everyone dresses like they're at some television awards show. I think it was supposed to be British themed, too. One of those old, gritty country greats was on the radio. That kind of stuff about romanticized lives for vicarious living was catching on. I think he was singing some song about the hardest piss he ever took. I was feeling good, though. I had caught my second wind after camping myself in a bathroom stall for a while, twisting my body around the toilet like a gymnast, flushing intermittently while trying to minimize spillage. I didn't even mind that the drink prices were outrageous and bought the bar a round. I wasn't even considering that I'd have to pay for them. There was a lot of yelling going on around me, and I assumed that meant that everyone was just having a good time. I thought everyone in the bar was still there to celebrate me, but that didn't last long after the bell sounded. People were back too busy commiserating themselves to be celebrating, even had I been championing their commiserations. Matthew Cairney, Jr., had a polished pitch, but I had no idea what it was about or why I was talking to the guy. I was endowed with a tremendous ability to compensate for this common phenomenon where I could always tell if someone had any idea what they were talking about whether I did or not. Matthew Cairney, Jr., did not have the slightest idea what he was talking about. The fact that I knew that he had no idea what he was talking about allowed me to talk about whatever the fuck it was we were talking about with no idea what I was talking about. "I'm like a Burmese Tiger, man." I had no idea what I meant, or what he said to provoke it. Part of me thinks that he might have requested that I say it. "I move too fast." "I hear you. I hear you, buddy, and that's why we'll set up several accounts for one main pot to funnel through." He readjusted the sleeve of his gentleman's jacket. "We'll just have to make sure we cover the drops if we automate it, but if we look over some numbers, set a confidence interval and chop it up, we can send small amounts very often. You following me?" "No." I had no business with confidence intervals. "But keep going." "Alright, so we got one main pot that filters off to twenty or so accounts here in Bermuda." His hand gestures drew a confusing diagram in the air like he was some wizard, imagining what it would be like to imagine to be a wizard if he wasn't a wizard. "Then we tie each of those bank accounts to a few of about ten or so American bank accounts in less frequently automated deposits in various amounts that you control manually." "To throw off the scent," I interjected into the pause. "Exactly!" He reached into his inside vest pocket and pulled out a business card. "Here, take one of these." I looked over the card and thought about how strange a practice it was to hand out contact information on tiny cards to practical strangers. Still, I wished for one of my own, just so I could see my name on it, because nobody ever figured I'd be important enough to have one. "So are you interested?" He warded off hesitation. "Yeah," I nodded along double positively. "You know where I can get one of these?" "Huh?" He saw me looking at the business card. "Oh. I was talking about the, uh, offshore investment opportunity." "I have no idea what you're talking about," I admitted. "I like this card, though. Did you make it yourself?" "No," he sighed. His shoulders slumped and his hair looked just slightly messier. "You know where I could get one done with my name on it?" "I don't know." He looked deflated, retreating. "There are tons of companies who print these." "You got any of their cards?" I watched as he turned and floated to disappear back into the blur. "I can keep this card, right?" I readjusted my positioning on the stool to tuck the card away, and the collage of fuzzy colored shapes of The Fiddly Winkle swirled around me as the walls rocked back and forth. "Another whiskey?" The sound warbled slowly at me from behind the bar, its tender sliding larger towards me. My entire body nodded, albeit not by my own accord, and the bartender refilled my glass. I reached out for it, but it wasn't where my vision was telling me it was, or maybe I was just aiming my arms the wrong way. Eventually I got a hold of it, and the whiskey had long had a hold of me, but I was still feeling like a champion. "You alright, buddy?" "Just trying to get me some pussy." I stumbled away from the bar bowlegged, holding the glass like I was balancing an egg on a spoon. I had no idea what I was doing, not just when it came to talking to women, but in general. I did, however, know how to spot a drunk, and I knew how drunk someone had to be to lose all their standards. After all, I'd lost all my standards. By this sound logic, I was able to spot my target attempting hopefully but unsuccessfully to sit down somewhere peripheral to my meandering path. I don't remember her name or much about what she looked like beyond that she might have been blond, but I was certain I had won at least that round of alcoholic roulette. I stumbled over and helped her onto her chair, and it wasn't graceful, but it didn't need to be. Her jaw hung open, smiling at how unbelievably wonderful things were this far detached from the gravity of knowing the intricacies of consciousness. "I like your belt." She pointed, nearly tipping herself over. "Oh yeah?" I tried to arch an eyebrow, but I couldn't decide which one, so I kept raising them both. "You see the match?" "Match?" She held her face up with her hand, digging an elbow into the table to keep her body somewhat upright. "The one that was made in heaven." I poorly recovered. I shot her a smile and let out a few hiccups as she continued to hold her face. She made a noise like the lovechild of a laugh and a hot air balloon deflating. "You ever been with a champion before?" "Do you know where my friends went?" The question slurred as her face slid down her arm. I looked around, though I couldn't see much of much, even if I knew what I was looking around for. "They were supposed to remember how to get back to the hotel." "I can keep you company until they get back." I helped myself into a chair and took her lack of response for a lack of reluctance. "The name's Johnny." I extended my arm and she reached out to shake it while muttering something that sounded like it could've been her name. We never actually touched each other, but as far as introductions went, I had done a lot worse. "What are you drinking?" "I don't know." She spoke into the bend of her arm and I saw her drifting off, her forearm struggling upright like a loose lever. Every time it looked like it was going to fall one way, it jerked back up and started leaning the other way, and every time she jerked her arm back up, it frustrated me more and more. For some reason I wanted to see it fall, and the longer it didn't, the more excited I'd get when I thought it would. I must've watched this go on for at least half an hour, and I was beginning to think she didn't have any friends when it finally happened. I had almost missed it, but it happened. Her hand hit the table, and her eyes shot open. She looked around and then at me. "I think my friends left without me." I appreciated that she wasn't much of a conversationalist, and I made my move to seal the proverbial deal. "Why don't you come back to my suite with me, and we can sort this out in the morning?" How sly, how sly a devil was I. "That sounds like a good idea." She hadn't half said it, and I was already wrapping my arms around her waist, helping her out of her seat. She wrapped her arms around my neck for support, and her drunken weight anchored me down so that my torso ran parallel to the floor. I felt like the mythological guy who dragged that stone up some hill as I tugged her limply along, all the way to the car I'd rented. I sat her on the sidewalk, propping her up against the front tire so I could catch my breath. After a brief survey of the automobile, I determined that I was in no condition to be operating any vehicle. My determination, however, belied my determination, and with some resolve, I poured her into the passenger seat. Once I had situated myself, I checked my mirrors to make sure they were still there and scanned the dashboard to get reacquainted with all of the meters. There was something familiar to it all. I felt it as I cautiously merged into the lane and accelerated off without the slightest idea how to get back to my own hotel room. Bermuda was a tiny island, though. I was sure I'd recognize it eventually. In fact, I wasn't even sure if I was in the correct lane, so I stayed in the middle and figured I'd find out soon enough. The rules of the road were always a roll of the dice in foreign countries. As I drove around, my mind wandered from thoughts of which lane I should be in to thoughts of the girl in the passenger seat performing various automotive misdeeds while I wore the belt to the darker ones that oftentimes walk hand in hand with long sojourns away from sobriety. I sat there, haunted, not sure where or when I was again. "Where am I?" She stirred and then sprawled out. "Who the hell are you?" "I'm sorry, Dina." It was all I could say, and I knew that wasn't her name. I wasn't even talking to her. "I'm so sorry." "My name isn't Dina." She looked around in a panic. "Where are you taking me?" "I don't know." The car zigged across the road, feeling around in the pitch black. "I don't know. I don't know. I don't know." "Stop the car!" Her voice sounded wounded. "Stop the car and just let me out here." I drove on, convinced the car wasn't moving at all, and if it was, it wasn't moving through space. "Stop the car and just let me out of here, and I won't say anything." She was nervous and frightened, and I was barely aware she was even there. "Please." Still, I drove on, and she kept pleading with me for a while. I was too busy seeing what was before me to see what was ahead of me. Then, it ended. I couldn't remember anything else but driving alone and sobbing, and I looked over, and she was there. I stopped the car. She looked over, her breathing heavier with fear. "Go on," I ushered. "Get out." She didn't say anything. She just got out and stood there as I drove away. I lit a cigarette, figuring it would set me level. She shrunk to a speck in my rear view mirror, and that's the last thing I remember, because for a while, it's the last thing there was. AUGUST 11, 2000 Randy, who sold replica wristwatches out of a trenchcoat, had this truck with makeshift rails on the sides so he could collect scrap metal and other random knick-knacks that people would throw out or leave unattended. We were driving around town in it, and Randy had been running variations on a scheme with me. "Hup!" Randy jerked. He rolled along slowly, steering with a knee, invariably veering across the yellow line. Every time he noticed, he'd bark like that and spin the wheel as if he'd been suddenly hypnotized to believe he was a game show contestant. Then, he'd retreat back into his seat and hum a low note to himself when he wasn't talking. "You know what this is?" He shook the bottle like a tiny, medicinal maraca. "Hup!" "No idea." I looked out the window, but I could see his reflection and his vile, green teeth. "I just grabbed it." "You gotta start reading the labels. Hup! It's Groestra. Helps old, bald ladies fight hair loss or some shit." He wiped some junk that had accumulated around his mouth. "Hup!" "There wasn't much else to take." The lady's cupboard was mostly full of tubed creams, petroleum jellies and a reserve of decorative soaps, all of which were of little interest to a guy like Randy. "I'll find a use for 'em yet. Hup! Just you watch." Randy spat on the exposed floorboard and started humming as the truck bumped along. "Alright, now here's how we're going to play it. You let me do the talking and you just dance around uncomfortably like you got a growth on your bladder that's making you piss a lot. Then, when you get in there, clear out anything good in the medicine cabinets: aspirin, pills, cough syrup. Cough syrup especially." "I know," I groaned. Randy usually had trouble putting sentences together, but once he had one constructed, he'd repeat it a lot so he wouldn't forget it. "We've hit like nine houses already." "Hup!" He spasmed. "Hup! Hup!" "How much longer are we going to be doing this?" I was tired of driving around with Randy. He smelled like the inside of a heavily worn boot. "'Til we find something real good, I reckon." He sniffed and brushed his mustache off with his finger. "You gettin' tired already, boy? I been up for three days, and I'm still wide awake." "We're not going to find anything real good," I counterreckoned. "If I had something real good in my medicine cabinet, I wouldn't let a stranger piss in my house." "Somethin'll turn up. Hup! I can feel it." Randy was the worst kind of idiot, the kind that did not know he was one. "I can feel it in my teeth. My teeth been tingling all mornin'. Yep. Hup!" I looked at Randy, and he was tapping on his rotting teeth with his long, dirty fingernails. His wide open, bloodshot eyes tried to make sense of the roads in front of them, picking out whichever one looked the easiest. It scared the hell out of me that I was in his passenger seat now. As I watched him, scratching his teeth and steering short, sharp cuts across the road, I could see myself sitting there. He didn't know what he was doing, but he was happy to be doing it. He hummed to himself, probably dreaming about some big jackpot that he'd never win. I couldn't blame him for trying, though. Sometimes the search was enough. It kept him busy. It was probably the only thing he had. I hated Randy, but I couldn't bring myself to argue with him over whether or not he had anything at all. I played along. It didn't make me feel good, stealing from people who were trying to be generous, but I played along, telling myself that I'd make up for it later. "Blue house." Randy stopped the truck and rubbed his hands together like a fly. "This'ns gonna be the one. I can feel it, man. Right in the teeth." "If you're right about this one, can we stop stealing medicine from old ladies and go home?" I was always thick with Randy, but he was impenetrable, like his mind was the heavily guarded decoy of a less heavily guarded actual fortress. "Oh, I'm right about this one. I just got a feeling." He turned and tried to ruffle my hair, but I shied away, and he withdrew his arm. "It's a victimless crime, boy. If they really need that medicine, they can just go get another prescription. Now this time, don't call me Randy. If I tell them I'm your old man, they might get suspicious of you callin' at me like that." "Fine." I slid out of the truck and slammed the door. The rusty frame rattled. "I just won't call you anything." "This doesn't make us bad people, you know." Randy followed behind me, scraping his feet against the pavement all fidgety and aching for another fix. "Guys like you and me, it's the only way we know to get by." We made it to the porch, and as Randy's crooked finger pushed on the buzzer, I did my best to pretend like I was the son from Randy's story, seconds away from soiling himself due to an unfortunate growth pressing against his bladder. SEPTEMBER 12, 2000 I hit a few snags in Texas. I was out of cash, and the light that indicated I was low on gas had been on since Muskogee. I pulled into the lot of the next gas station off the highway, and being that it was a pretty long stretch of road until Tatum, there was a trucker friendly diner called The Rusty Buckle connected to the gas station shop. I sat at the counter for about three hours, and the waitress had been kind enough to provide me some free coffee and a couple of day old bagels they were going to throw out anyway. Her name was Meredith, and she kind of reminded me of Lisa without all the unpleasant history. The countryside was wide open and beautiful. The sky was wide over the dusty, jagged earth, and the sun burnt bright around drifting shadows, but I was too distracted to notice any of it. I still felt bad about leaving Dina back in Missouri with a random trucker, and being stuck in Texas without the means to continue did little to ease my disfigured conscience. I was too beat to feel the gravity of the immediate weight that saddled me. Meredith noticed I was running low and refilled my cup. "You know, I got a son about your age, and I'd be worried to tears if he was stranded halfway cross the country." She put the pot down and picked up the creamer. "Then it's probably a good thing I'm not your son." I took a sip, and it burnt down my throat. I never much took to coffee. Caffeine usually fucked me up too much. "You could at least give her a call and let her know you're safe." Meredith walked to the register, opened it up and broke open a roll of quarters. "Here. We got some pay phones by the restrooms." "I can't." I looked down at the change on the counter. "It's complicated." "No, it ain't. You put the quarters in the slot and then you dial." I tried to stifle back a laugh, and she smiled when she noticed. "Tell you what." She leaned over the counter. "You go give your mom a call and you can have anything on the menu. I'll buy." "Yeah, alright." I took the coins and slid off the stool, walking as slowly as humanly possible towards the restrooms, which were situated in a hallway between The Rusty Buckle and the convenience area. I stopped and turned. "Hey, Meredith?" "Yeah?" She stopped whatever she was doing. She was always doing something, buzzing around behind the counter, but it made me uneasy when she stopped and gave me her full attention. I knew I was about to lie to a woman who had been nothing but kind to me, all for a subpar cheeseburger with fries so undercooked that they could be better described as mashed potatoes with skin. "Say you'd done someone wrong, and you know they wouldn't forgive you for it." I shuffled on my feet. "Any sense in apologizing?" "I don't see how it could make things any worse, sugar." She tried to comfort me with a grin. "Besides, if you really mean it, she'll forgive you, and if she doesn't, maybe at least you'll feel a little better about it." Instead of saying anything, I looked down at the coins in my palm and kind of nodded at them. I thought more about Meredith's tone than her words because neither of us were talking about the same thing. She served truckers, travelers and fugitives at an all night diner that was attached to a gas station. Kindness and hospitality were the two things she knew; two things that kept her and hers fed. I wondered how people like Meredith could exist. It all seemed like the long, hard road to nowhere for me. With all the folks out there like Randy, who sold replica wristwatches out of a trenchcoat, I didn't understand how anyone could get ahead, or even get by, without cutting every corner. Meredith smiled and waved as I ambled off towards the vestibule that separated the gas station's shop from the restaurant and housed the bathrooms and pay phones. I dialed the number from memory and stuffed in more coins after the operator had calculated and relayed the charge for a long distance call. The phone rang a few times, and I realized I wasn't going to say anything. I just wanted to make sure she'd made it home safe. "Hello?" Dina answered. I sat there staring blankly, wrapping the coiled cord around my finger. "Hello?" I waited there, holding back all the questions I wanted to ask and all the things I wanted to explain. I waited there, and I listened. I could've stood there forever, listening to her repeat that simple answering question over and over as the sound of her voice broke me down piece by piece. "Anyone there?" Her voice grew louder and more agitated. "Guess not." The line went dead on her end, and I smashed the phone's handset against its keypad, and the attendant behind the register craned his neck over the counter to catch the commotion. I noticed his disdainful brow angles and caught in my outburst, gently placed the phone back on the cradle hook. Defeated and at ends with myself, I walked back to the counter, unable to hide my dejection. Still, Meredith smiled on. "How'd it go, sugar?" She tilted her head at me, and I knew I didn't have to answer her. "That well, huh?" I plopped myself on one of the stools and rested my elbows on the counter so I could hold my face in my hands. "Let's get some food in you." She failed to hide the concern in her eyes behind a layer of makeup and optimism. "What'll it be?" "I can't." I mumbled refusal into the creases of my palms. I was too drained to lie to Meredith, but I felt so low that being honest did nothing for me. "I didn't call her. I called someone else." "I insist." A tall, lanky man startled me as I felt his shadow loom. He extended his arm for a handshake, and without regaining enough of my bearings to return his firm grip, my arm flailed like a noodle that ended with trapped fingers. "Boyd Ficklin, at your service." "Johnny," I returned, my hand retreating after I had slipped it out of his clutch. "Couldn't help but overhear that you ran into a bit of a snafu." Boyd wore a cowboy hat low over his eyes and had a tanned leather jacket with plenty of frill hanging off the sleeves. He looked out the window panel and pointed at my quadrangular, olive green car. "Those your wheels stuck out there?" "Yeah." I looked through the glass over at the ugly ton of metal. "I understand you don't got the money to fill it up." I nodded, and Boyd noticed Meredith eying him suspiciously. He didn't let it distract him from his pitch. "I can fix that for you if you'd like." "How?" "Darlin'," he lifted his head towards Meredith. "Why don't you scoot on back to the kitchen and fetch us a couple of steaks. Cook 'em a while, you hear?" Meredith nodded and backed into the kitchen. I could see her reflection keeping one eye trained toward us off a spherical security mirror that hung from the ceiling and distorted the objects within itself. Boyd pulled an ashtray towards him from the far end of the corner and lit a cigarette before taking the stool next to me. I could tell he noticed Meredith in the mirror, too. He only talked when he was shielding his mouth. "Smoke?" He held the pack out and flicked the bottom corner so that the filtered end of one of the cigarettes poked out at me. I took it cautiously, and Boyd struck a match and lit the tobacco before shaking the match dead. "You heard of coyotes, kid?" "I think so." I could tell it was a trick question. I debated whether I should answer with the obvious and look like an idiot or not answer and all and look like an even bigger idiot if the obvious answer was correct. "They're like wild dogs, right? Only bigger?" Boyd shook his head and laughed, a little prematurely, too, as if he was expecting me to say something like that. I didn't appreciate being set up, especially when I was damned either way, whether I did or did not. "I'm in a bit of a jam myself." I saw the side of his mouth curve upward, and I could tell he was going to weave something together before he told me what he was getting at with the whole coyote bit. I was never much of a storyteller myself, but I could tell when someone thought himself one. "Got a call from one of my drivers last night. Guy had a little bit too much to drink, took offense at something, and has to dry out in a holding cell for a while. You don't got a drinking problem, do you?" I shook my head. Though my drinking problem hadn't yet developed, I didn't have any problems with drinking either. "Good." Boyd looked back over at my vehicle. "How many people you think you can fit in there?" "Three in the front," I surveyed. "Three in the back, maybe four if everyone squeezes together." "How many in the trunk?" The question kind of caught me off guard, and I didn't really know how to respond. I hadn't even opened the trunk yet. "I don't know. Two?" It felt like Boyd was quizzing me, and I didn't like it, but he settled for my answers, even if they were questionable. "Let's see." He calculated. "That makes eight. Any way you can do more?" I shrugged. I was still waiting for him to come back around to address the coyote reference. His mannerisms led me to believe that Boyd had this whole roundabout thing going where it'd all come together in the end. "I'll give you a hundred bucks a head if you can fit ten, and I'll knock it up to two a head if you can fit fifteen." He kept one of his eyes on the security mirror, watching Meredith's eye check on us. She saw him and looked back to the grill to tend to the steaks. "Anything less than ten, and you're throwing away too much potential money. Can I count on you for ten?" I nodded, trying to do the multiplication in my head, positive that I'd find a way, even though I was no budding mathematician. Necessity always opened possibilities that otherwise wouldn't have existed. So did lies. "Good, then." He reached into his breast pocket and pulled out a sheet of folded paper. He handed it to me, and I opened it up. "The first one's where you pick up. The second one's where you drop off. It's simple, really." "What am I picking up?" "Illegal Mexicans." He said it like he was asking me to pick up some eggs from the supermarket. "One word of advice, though. These guys you'll be working with are going to be armed, and if anything seems fishy, ain't one of them who'll hesitate to fire. You see anything you don't like, I suggest you turn a blind eye." AUGUST 19, 2002 I didn't have any valid identification, and even my fake ID was expired, so I was turned away on my first attempt to reenter America. At first, I figured I'd better just go back to Monterrey and sort things out, but I dismissed the idea. When I had found out about Lisa, I knew I had to stop ignoring everything I had been running from or soon enough, I'd have nowhere else to run. I couldn't go back to Monterrey, anyway. Gordo, the kindly luchador who had been training me, did not take well to finding out I had been lying to him about my identity for close to two years. I ended up in the limbo of an unkempt rest area, staring at my reflection in the mirror, the razor in my hand battling the fondness for my caterpillary facial hair. I had never shaved before, and I really didn't know how to go about it. The only reason I had a razor to begin with was so I could cut myself open for matches, so there were certain subtleties to grooming that were lost on me. My thickets of beard proved a much more difficult match for the razor than I had expected, and with the patience of a grizzled country veteran with an overactive bladder in the middle of a two hour set at a prison, I started hacking away more carelessly until I drew blood. "Fuck!" I ran my hand under the sink and splashed water against my cheek as pink hued deluge streamed off my chin. I barely noticed the door swing open, but I noticed the man stop when he saw me. I looked up at him, and I recognized the man immediately. He saw it in my eyes, turned his head to the side, and coughed nervously. "You mother fucker." They were the first words that came to mind, even though I hadn't intended to put forward such a literal assessment. "Johnny." Monster Truck Doug didn't look towards me, but he tipped his mesh trucker hat in some direction. "What the fuck, man?" I slammed the razor on the rim of the sink. "You just left me to take care of all of your shit." "Give me a chance to explain, alright?" He put out his hand and walked with his back against the wall, shifting to reach open a stall door behind him. "Mind if I take a seat? It's a long story." I didn't say anything. I just confirmed it with a nod. Now that I had him there, I really didn't know what to say to him. He hadn't really done anything to inconvenience me by leaving. I guess I was just sick of being abandoned. I heard porcelain clinking against itself behind the door. "I thought I'd lay low down in Mexico until that incident at the Bulkamania blew over. Figured I'd come down here, do a little drinking, take a hard earned break, you know?" I went a bit more gently at the other side of my face, making slow progress while trying not to focus on the cracks in the stall where I would catch a sliver of Doug squirming there as his insides pushed through him. "One thing led to another, and the next thing I knew, I was getting into it with a hooker." He paused and sighed uncomfortably. "We both liked it a little rough, so I was choking her a little bit, mind you I don't speak a lick of Mexican, and then she stopped screaming and went limp. I didn't mean it. God, I didn't mean it." "You killed a hooker?" I stopped shaving, the razor paused against my cheek. "Jesus Christ, Doug. You killed a hooker?" "Yeah, I know." His voice stopped, and I really didn't know what to say, but I could hear him still moving in there, and that was even more uncomfortable. "Then what happened?" I was wondering more so how this subject would have been handled in conversation between any two different people in the world. It played out much differently in the scenarios I imagined. "I did the only logical thing a man could do." His boots tapped nervously against the cracked concrete. "I rolled her up in a rug, stashed her corpse behind the seat of the Americanator, and dumper her off in the ocean. At least, I think it was an ocean." "Jesus, Doug." As it turned out, it wasn't. "So you're on the run?" "No, they caught me. They found a dead hooker and threw me in jail. It was hell, but I got out." "Apparently so." I began to wonder if Monster Truck Doug was craftier and more dangerous than I would have given him credit for. I considered sneaking away while he was preoccupied. "But to recap, you killed a prostitute and then escaped from Mexican prison?" "Escaped? No." Doug was defensive but paused to finish before finishing. "They let me out. That hooker they found was a different dead hooker. Apparently, that kind of thing happens all the time around here." The can flushed, and Doug emerged from the stall as if an immense weight had dropped from him. He walked over to the sink next to me and washed his hands. "You said you still got the Americanator?" "It's out in front." He reconsidered. "It ain't the Americanator anymore, though. I gave it a new paint job and renamed it the Mexican Murder Trolley." I liked the name. Though the murder part was a bit too recently autobiographical for comfort and the trolley part was a complete misnomer, it had a ring to it.
