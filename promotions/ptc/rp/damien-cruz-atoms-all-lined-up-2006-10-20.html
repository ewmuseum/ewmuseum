Damien Cruz: "Atoms All Lined Up"

20 October 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2006-10-20 Damien Cruz was born, but not yet complete, in Cielo Azul twenty-five years ago. The ring was better than the ghetto, and far better than the mortuary, where he would be faceless, empty, and statistically invisible. To be young, Mexican, and burdensome on the federal government was to be nothing special. So Damien understood full well what he was being offered when his mentor, Father Morales, handed him a box, and he opened it to find a black and gold luchadore mask. While he was not the only one to train with Father Morales, Damien was the only one to be asked to take the training beyond just an after-school diversion. He was being invited to carry on the tradition of the Luchadore that had come before him. Men who swore allegiance to a legacy, a culture, a way of life. And although he did not choose to wear the mask that the former Nino Oro offered, there were no hard feelings. It was, beyond all else, loyalty and respect that Damien did not accept the gesture. As Damien had made clear, the mask belonged to his mentor's own blood. Instead, Damien would wrestle without a mask, under his birth name, and he would do so with Father Morales' blessings. At the age of fifteen, in front of a lightly populated gymnasium, Damien Cruz stepped through the curtains for the first time. He walked down the walkway with his head down, his heart slamming in his chest, and the desert sand crunching under his feet. He walked out, after earning his first win, the same way. The time between, however, cemented Damien as one of the most innovative, talented, natural athletes Los Locos Lucha Libre had ever seen. While he lacked the experience that would carry him above and beyond all others, his debut saw him moving around the ring like a seasoned veteran. Every lesson taught to Damien as a young boy was remembered and exercised by the young man he had become. He moved faster than anyone could follow, hit harder than his opponent could counter, and with a natural ease and instinct that no one had ever witnessed before. In his debut, he gave a raw look at the unmatched talent he would become. Three years, seventy-five matches, and seventy three break-through nights later, Damien Cruz had grown larger than the L4 could contain, and Lucha Libre had its Last Son. It was a random videotape left in the locker room VCR that changed the course of who Damien would be, and where his talent would take him. No one knew who the tape belonged to, or how long it had been in the library, or even where it came from, but Damien Cruz would never forget it. It was Japanese. On it, three men traded blows until each was a bloodied, broken, inhuman wreck of a human. There were metal pipes strewn around the ring. There were barbed wire-wrapped tables set aflame on the outside, and the gathered audience was frenzied with excitement. As the snowy video played, Damien Cruz's decision grew clearer. Staring at the digital snow long after the footage ended, Damien understood where he wanted to be. He knew, like he had the day he stepped through the ropes for the last time in the L4, that he was looking at the threshold to the next stage in his personal evolution. If he would have to travel to the other side of the world to usher into reality his future, than he would. Three weeks of teaching classes, borrowing money, and skipping meals came up short. He would need almost three times as much money as he held in his hand just to buy the ticket. And then, he would need three times that much to survive long enough in Japan to get himself a try-out with a decent company. Damien Cruz sat in his basement apartment, staring at the promo tape he had made from old footage his best friend had taped for him. He stared at the footage of himself doing things in the ring that kept the audiences on their feet, and money overflowing in the box office. Tourists, locals, and even American agents had come from far and wide to see the man who had become as large as folk lore. He watched as he literally aged on the footage- from a thin, insecure luchadore to a strong, powerful, confident innovator. What he had become was far more than Morales had ever taught him, but far less than he knew he wanted to be. Damien had changed the face of the sport, but had reached a wall within himself. The knock at the door was unexpected, but Damien was not surprised. He knew it would come, and felt relieved and torn. Damien did not hear the roar of a 1927 Ford E-liner, the car that Victor Morales had dreamed of as a child, and purchased as El Nino Oro. It was a car that had remained even after the house, the women, and the drugs blew away. It was the last remaining icon of innocence that Victor Morales held onto to remind him that his corruption had not been complete. As Damien sat on that plane, he swore that Father Morales would have his car back, and would never again have to sacrifice so much. Damien had, as it were, completed his promise four years later, three days after winning his first title in an American federation. But at that moment, as he sat on a Trans-Pacific flight to Someplace Else, he closed his eyes and made sure that the man's gift to him would be rewarded ten-fold. His debut match: The Last Son of Lucha Libre never bled that much before. He watched in morbid amazement as blood trickled from his forehead, and pooled under him in the center of the ring. He watched, with a pain-induced drunkenness, as the crowd around him leapt to his feet, and the referee lifted him from his knees, and raised his hand above his head in victory. A far-away smile spread across his mouth, and the blood tinted his teeth with a sick red as he grinned through them when he realized that he had learned as much about himself in that moment as he had ever in that dusty Mexican gym in four years. The next three years, from Last Son to Latin Assassin also taught Damien about pain, dedication, sadism, masochism, limitations, thresholds, evolution, inspiration, sacrifice, adulation, heresy, isolation, chaos, doubt, and all other things that flash through the mind right before pure adrenaline takes control. From glass cages to thumbtack turnbuckles, to iron ropes, to barbwire lynchings. Damien sat in crystal white palaces that turned black and pulsating as audiences filled them and moved in a single, unicellular organism of bloodlust and admiration. The Last Son of Lucha Libre fought for his life, fought for his honor, and fought to keep the crowds guessing. He fought keep his blood pumping, and keep himself from being lost in the onslaught. Billion-dollar stadiums blurred into loose-organized fight clubs, million-dollar events were no different from underground gambling in corporate parking lots. Whether the blinding lights were from the cameras overhead or the pale blue headlights of the cars that surrounded him, Damien Cruz fought harder, more desperately, and with more drive than he thought he was capable of. And when the honor was achieved, the wealth was cultivated, and the fame made him an icon, the tug of Something More brought him back to that airport, and he was buying a ticket to that Someplace Else that called him. He landed in Las Vegas, Nevada at 3:28am. He had almost three hundred dollars in his pocket and nothing more than his suitcase on his back. The money he had earned had been sent back to Cielo Azul, and he kept only what he needed to stay fed, stay clothed, and stay healthy. All else, he realized, was better spent by those with true necessity. Although his reputation carried him this far, being a blood sport sideshow in Japan was not enough to get him recognized. So he did as he knew he would have to do; he wrestled in any company that would have him, and knew that he would have to start from the bottom. Once again, he took fights from whoever would videotape it. He fought for whoever would earn him lunch money, and he performed for whoever craved unrelenting violence. And almost two weeks after landing in the US, Damien Cruz was given a chance to become a new addition to the US Wrestling Circuit. From that day, five years ago, he had been breaking barriers, changing minds, and re-inventing himself better, faster, stronger, and more dynamic than anyone placed before him. Once again, billion-dollar arenas faded into basement fight clubs, high profile PPVs blurred into high school exhibitions, and personal evolution far out-weighed fame and vanity. Today, the wealth was there. The accolades were there. The respect, the credentials, and the credibility were there. From company to company, circuit to circuit, and household to household, Damien Cruz had nothing left to prove. He had done it all. But the quest for Something More did not waver. He grew hungrier as speculation of a new challenge grew louder. His eyes grew wide with the chance to do it all again in pursuit of something far larger than himself. The Latin Assassin could feel the adrenaline dripping into his bloodstream, and his muscles twitched and danced in response. He was, as the old adage goes, smelling blood in the water. Standing on the rooftop of a Brooklyn property that he had made his home, he looked out over the New York Harbor. In his back pocket were an acceptance letter and a plane ticket to that Ever-present Someplace Else. Whatever the logo on the canvas, whatever the opponent standing across the ring, whatever the name of the event, the location of the venue, or the numbers been tossed around in Vegas, he was going to enter that ring with one singular purpose; to change everything.
