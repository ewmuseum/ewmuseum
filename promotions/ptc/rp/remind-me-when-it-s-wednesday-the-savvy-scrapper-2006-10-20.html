Remind Me When It's Wednesday: The Savvy Scrapper

20 October 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2006-10-20 "My name is Evangeline Elizabeth O’Connor," a young woman declares, as though it is a name important to remember. While her tone may assert such an assumption, her clothing does not. She wears a ragged old pair of jeans, with natural holes in the knees. She also wears some type of sporting jersey. What looks like beautiful, straight brunette hair is somewhat obscured with a dusty old baseball cap atop her head. She lies on a black couch, her head propped up on the left armrest. Her attention diverts briefly to a high-definition plasma television hanging from the wall of her living room, but then back. "I am no one that you’re interested in. I’m just a boring twenty seven year old woman who has accomplished so little." Her beautiful, piercing green eyes hold little emotion. Her expression reveals nothing. She speaks in riddles but gives no clues. "As so often happens in life, we meet very important people by nothing more than chance. When I reminisce, I often ponder what the odds were that his breakdown would have occurred on that specific date, and what the odds were that his boss would have blackmailed him, and what the odds were that it was our clinic he came to, and the odds that he was assigned to me. Undoubtedly, if any of these details would have been even slightly altered, I would not have met, admired nor appreciated this diligent, remarkable man. But such is life. Every course of events has an equally slim chance of happening, but this is one of the few such sequences that I am grateful for." She hesitates for a moment, as though aware that her words may eventually hold a significant meaning. "It was February of this year, 2006 … " and her voice is drowned out by the murmuring of guards and a commanding voice. February 19, 2006 I was just sitting in the room, awaiting this special assignment my supervisor had decided to give me. The door swung open, my supervisor walked in with a small, balding man in a dark suit. The other man looked tired, bothered. His eyes revealed that he had been burdened in this task. But he was only a man of interest; my patient was dragged in by three guards. He looked ragged. His blue jeans had holes and his t-shirt looked like it might have been older than he was. The first thing I noticed about him was his dark hair. Of all the things he had at the time of his arrival, it was the only thing that showed that he cared. Many of the men that I have dealt with have matted long hair. This man’s was long, but stylishly so. It came down to his shoulders. His eyes were an emerald green. His goatee showed little care. As I always did when faced with such a situation, I wetted a rag. "Please put him on his bed," I told the security guards. They knew what to do, but for some reason they were hesitating, as though something was bothering them. They eventually placed him there. I walked over to the bed and placed my hand on the man’s forehead. It was already warm. I placed the rag on his forehead, and then moved to his cheeks. "I’m sorry," I said, "but you’re in for a long battle, Mister … " I stumbled, I had not yet bothered to learn his name. "Gwynn," the balding man finished my sentence. "His name is William Gwynn. There was a time when he was a respectable man, stood for ideals and principles. He still claims to, but he’s a shadow of who he used to be, a burden to his family and his company. I’m still shocked I managed to get him to come here." "You did the right thing," my supervisor told him, reassuringly, "he will benefit greatly from sobriety. We appreciate your concern, but once the patients are in the room, we generally ask for some time to get them settled, and no visitors for the first few days. In your case, though, we might make an excep – " "There’s no need," the balding man declared, "I’ll go. Just remember what I told you. No press, don’t let anyone know he’s in here. I’ll make sure those that need to know are aware of his location. He just needs to get better." I was surprised. Who was this man? Why was this patient so important? I did not recognize him. The balding man immediately took his leave, and the patient, this William Gwynn, my supervisor, Scott Robertson, and I were left in the room. "Here’s your special assignment, Evangeline," he said, "but you probably already guessed that. He’s a very important man. There are a lot of people that are depending on him getting better. And the sooner that happens, the better. The longer he’s in here, the more questions are going to be asked. Do you understand, Miss O’Connor?" "Yes," I said, "I’ve dealt with celebrities before, Scott." Scott just laughed. "Celebrities? Oh no, Evangeline. This man is not a mere celebrity. I also have to look out for your safety. This man packs quite a punch, you know the safety guidelines. I want you to use them. Keep yourself safe." "Is he dangerous?" I asked, feeling that I had the right to know. "Dangerous? Absolutely!" he said, stressing the point. "Harmful? Absolutely not. This is a good man, but a love for alcohol makes all men dangerous when they need to have it." I nodded, agreeing. "Who am I kidding?" He asked, apparently to no one, "you know all of this, probably better than anyone. You’re the best caretaker I could ask for, just keep the press out. Get him better. That’s our job. Don’t treat him any differently than you would treat anyone else, he has the same disease." My grit convinced me that I had no reason to treat this dark-haired man any differently than any other. He was an alcoholic, and it was my duty to bring him back to respectability. My supervisor decided at that moment to take his leave. "If you need anything," he started, "please let me know." "Yes, sir," I answered; he nodded and left the room, leaving me with my patient. For the first time, he acknowledged me. It was silent, simply an eye contact deal. It was then that I got the first indication of real sentience, real knowledge and wisdom. This man was not like the average person I have had in my care. He’s something a little different. It struck me that it was no wonder he was so successful. But I dismissed it as soon as it came to my mind. What can you really tell from just a look? Nothing was the answer that arose in my head. Regardless, I smiled at the man. I was his caretaker, but not his friend. And yet I must be friendly, because sympathy is something that I’ve found I need to give these men. This William Gwynn did not need it just yet, but I knew that he soon would. And perhaps as the withdrawals set in I would get to gauge the true character of the man, and see if my assumptions had been correct. I looked at his chart. It stated that in all likelihood, he had been sober for a couple of days. His symptoms supported this. It seemed as though he had already been slightly affected by the lack of ethanol in his system. His headaches seemed almost debilitating. He was weak and uncaring, sweat slowly swept unchecked down his face. I wiped it off for him, and returned to his chart. His drinking was not entirely characteristic. He did not necessarily drink heavily alone. His heavy drinking he reserved for when he was in small groups. He did drink alone, just rarely excessively. His boss, this balding man, Matthew Lanier, had said that William’s drinking had gotten in the way of his work. And yet it was Lanier who brought him here to get help. Why? Why does this irritable, commanding man have forgiveness in his heart for this William Gwynn? Why not just fire the drunk? This, I determined, was something I should endeavor to uncover. February 20, 2006 The night had been harder than I had anticipated. With the record this man had, the withdrawals would certainly be terrible, but I was still unprepared for the violent shaking and the whimpering. I watched him undergo this torture, and I wondered if he deserved it. He certainly brought it on himself, drinking as he did. He surely knew that he eventually would have to go through detox, which would undoubtedly be the most trying occurrence in his life. Didn’t he fear this consequence? Expect it? Only he knew, and he would not be in the talking mood for quite some time, so I would not have my answers. He laid there, almost peaceful. I knew that it was simply a marching point in the war. Both armies were just now recuperating after a battle, but certainly not a large one. The war was yet to be waged. And Gwynn would be equipped with the best generals and technology that could be given to him. He would have the best opportunities available, and if they were squandered, he would have no one to blame but himself. Unfortunately, I reflected, that was true to begin with. February 22, 2006 I watched him. I was tired. I’d spent so much time here. I’d cared for him for several days. He had yet to say a word to me, he just recognized me through motions and gestures. This was not uncommon. The symptoms were obviously excruciating enough to bring a man like this, so muscular and proud, to his knees. Suddenly there was a great noise outside. It startled William; it startled me. I was just beginning to nod off, I had realized. But there would be none of that now. The racket just got louder, as though it was getting closer. I looked out the window, and saw vans with television station logos. I also saw members of the press, many of them looking like they wrote for journals or magazines. And then, in the middle of the group, I saw my boss. I told William, "I’ll be back." And I swept out the door toward my friend. As I reached him, he seemed relieved. "What’s going on?" I asked him. "They’re trying to get a comment out of William," he whispered to me, so none could hear. "He can’t speak," I said. "That and the fact we’re not allowed to have him talk to the press," my boss answered gruffly. Suddenly he made a great shout, and all turned to listen to him. "Listen! You are disrupting the business of this center. You are correct in the fact that you have indeed come to a detox center. You are incorrect in saying that this man you’re looking for is here. This … William … " "Gwynn!" someone shouted from the audience. "This William Gwynn is not in this center. And even if he was, I couldn’t let you in. This is a highly protected area for very sick people. So please, be on your way. Maybe hassle some of my competitor detox centers," he mentioned, "maybe your William Gwynn is there." Some of the reporters laughed, but a great many of them also looked serious about going there. The two of us went back in the center and made sure to lock all the doors and windows, and to close the blinds, as to let no one see in. "You did very well," I told my boss, still somewhat amazed at how he dealt with the press. "You managed to get them all to leave." "I knew it would happen," he said, "your boy is pretty famous. And I learned to deal with it when Scott Stapp was in here." "Scott Stapp? Really?" "Yes," he said, "I guess it was a couple months before you. Anyway, he was pretty cool. You’d better get back to William." I listened, walking back in the room. William was on his back, staring at the ceiling. He turned his head slightly toward me. "What’s your name, darling?" "Evangeline O’Connor," I said, choosing to ignore the ‘darling’ statement, "but you can call me Eve, William." "Most people don’t call me William," he said softly. "What do they call you then?" I asked, somewhat curious, but looking down at what I was doing. "Avatar," he declared, with a chuckle, "but I suppose that time is long past." "Then I guess we’ll have to find something new for you," I thought for a moment, "the Savvy Scrapper? It’s amazing, you haven’t even asked for your first drink yet." "I’m not an alcoholic," he answered. I smiled. Some people chose this method, but I must admit I didn’t expect it from him. "Very well," I said, "nevertheless, you’re doing quite well. Keep it up." "Do we get television in here?" The question surprised me. "Yes, I suppose we can. Why?" "I’d like to watch something on Wednesday. What day is it?" "Sunday," I answered. "Please remind me when it’s Wednesday," he asked. "I live for Wednesday." And with that he fell into a sleep. I watched him for a moment, amazed. I leapt up and grabbed my laptop from my bag. I turned it on. As soon as it was possible, I clicked on the internet, went to google, and typed in "Avatar" William Gwynn. Nine-hundred and seventy-two results. I was shocked. I moved to click on the first one … October 19, 2006 "How could a man so famous," William Gwynn asks the camera, "become so forgotten?" He smiles, it was rhetorical. "Here I am, a man that went all the way to the sweet sixteen in this tournament a year ago, now I’m apparently up against the ropes, already about to be eliminated. Considering my past year, this might not be a surprise. After the last tournament, I lost the Legacy Championship in Core. I started drinking heavily when I failed to get it back for so long. I was forced into detox and Core Wrestling closed before I could get out." He sighed. "And all I’ve done is lift and spar since. And now I’m up against this Phantom Republican in the first round. I’m not the favorite, but some seem to remember me as a great technical wrestler. I must admit I know little about this Republican, though I certainly admire his political ideals, if he is in fact, a Republican. To someone, he means something, I take it. He’s popular somewhere. That’s where I think he and I separate. My following has disbanded. My fan club died with Core. And so when I beat him, there won’t be much notoriety. There won’t be any bragging rights. It’ll be a quiet sputter. And as such, I will be just as forgotten in the second round." And then he rises from his chair. "That which does not kill me only makes me stronger," he says, "and over this past year, I have gone through more than anyone. You can claim that I’m nothing because I’ve been out of the game, dismissed by my own company. Lost, dazed, confused. But I contend that I can be anywhere tomorrow, and I can be everything today. Don’t count out William Gwynn, Republican. He’s got an old-fashioned Republican October Surprise for you." Fade.
