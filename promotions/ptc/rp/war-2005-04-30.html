"War"

30 April 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2005-04-30 * * * * * When you see your own father crying, you pay attention. Witnessing someone who has given you direction in life, sometimes a place you may or may not have wanted to go, suddenly break down with uncontrollable emotion, it pulls at you. It yanks you, actually. Clear off of your feet. My father was a Vietnam veteran, with numerous years buried deep in enemy territory. I had constructed such a high image of him that I never really had even entertained the thought that he might suddenly break down. Seeing someone you love, sitting in his favorite chair, with tears tracing down his cheeks and soaking the front of his shirt is a memory I’ll never forget. I’ll also never forget the story he told me, something he had been keeping to himself since his days in Vietnam. It was the truth about how his best friend, Archibald Matthews, was killed in action. For years, I had read stories and been told lies by townspeople about what had happened. Because when my father returned from active duty, he had spent years in Vietnam without Archibald. He spent years creating a story to tell the masses, to hide the pain he had endured. In my research on the topic, it seems to be a common case with war Veterans. They create alternate versions of what happened – far less emotionally scarring – and pass them off as valid. But for some reason, as he sat on his recliner, my father wept. And the walls that he had built up over the years came crumbling down, brick by brick. I tried to console my father, and had I known that the next morning he would dress in his uniform and put a bullet through his head, I would’ve never left his side. Perhaps you will never hear the version of the story that the townspeople of Oxford, Pennsylvania believe in. It doesn’t matter, because you’re about to learn what exactly happened to Archibald Matthews, and how he died. The truth is that my father was arrogant and cowardly. And it cost him the life of his best friend. * * * * * Both Archibald’s continued the short walk from the bronze statue back to the MacGregor home. It was only a few blocks, but it might as well have been a thousand miles to Archibald Allen MacGregor, because everything he was hearing made absolutely no sense, and he needed all the time in the world to make any judgment of it all. “So, if there is a war between Heaven and Hell going on…why are you here, talking to me?” MacGregor asked. “Because we can’t lose you, dear boy, the way we lost your father.” “What do you mean ‘lose me’, lose me to what?” “Hell.” Matthews replied, dryly. The morning breeze stiffened slightly, sending a few leaves scuttling along the ground. Both men continued to stroll. “I don’t understand. What makes you think I’m going to Hell, and furthermore…what makes you think that I’d even fight in this War you speak of?” Archibald Matthews stopped and turned towards the 29 year old. He opened his mouth as if to speak, but paused for a moment. The words just didn’t seem to come easily to him, or perhaps trying to explain a war between Heaven and Hell to a mere mortal was too much for Matthews to convey. Finally, he found the words, “What I’m going to explain to you might not make any sense.” ”Try me. This entire day is something that will drive me insane for the rest of my life.” They continued the walk towards Archibald MacGregor’s home. Quickly turning off of Main Street, both men continued the walk down Hope Street. The house was only two blocks away. A passing car honked and waved, not because the occupant knew either man, but because that is the way Oxford is. Friendly by nature, a quaint throwback to the mid 1950’s. “There is a wrestling tournament coming up later this month, a tournament in which you’ve been thinking about entering. The Cruiserweight 2 Tournament. What I’m here to do is to show you that entering that tournament will be the best thing you could ever do for your life.” “Go on.” “You see, you have no direction. Your father tried to find one for you, but ultimately you ended up pointed in every direction except the one you were supposed to be facing. You ended up finding wresting, and finding a passion. What we need you to do is to go after that dream. Seize it…achieve it…and not waste away your life in this little town, being someone who wishes he was someone else while mourning the loss of others.” “Like my father.” “Yes, like your father. I miss him too.” Archibald MacGregor didn’t know how to respond to that affectionate comment, so he mildly ignored it, “Why is this such a concern? It’s only a wrestling tournament.” MacGregor said it with a slight chuckle. Matthews’ gaze held the weight of the world, and Archibald MacGregor knew how serious the situation must be, “Because this is where your life turns for better, or worse.” “And why won’t you let me take the darker path? Does God really want me that badly?” Matthews didn’t respond for a moment as both men finally reached the MacGregor home. They walked the small pathway towards the front door, and came to a stop at the head of the steps while MacGregor unlocked the door. “No, he doesn’t. What’s more important to Him is that you don’t fight against him. He fears your presence in Hell much more than he approves of your place in Heaven.” “Wait, wha—what?” Frustration washed across Archibald Matthews’ face. For the first time, his earnest smile was hollow and emotionless. Whatever he tried to do in order to calm the son of his best friend, he couldn’t find the phrase, nor could he find the emotion to convey it. ”Are you familiar with horse racing?” Matthews asked, finally locating an analogy that would fit. “Yes.” “Then you understand the concept of thoroughbreds and breeding. A winning horse is sold to a stud-farm, where he is mated with a winning female. Their child should bear the superior traits of both parents, and hopefully carry those traits onto the racetrack as well. A child, bred for winning. And if he succeeds, the same process will befall him. Each generation in the bloodline becomes pure and powerful.” ”I’m following.” ”Entry into Heaven and Hell follows the same concept. Except a little more malevolent, especially for Hell. Should you feel you are a failure and end up taking your own life, you will enter hell as a normal foot-soldier. There are millions and millions of these soldiers down there, doing the Devil’s bidding. But if someone in your immediate bloodline was already present, and you follow in his or her footsteps, you’ll enter Hell a little more powerful. Rather than a foot-soldier, you enter as a General. Third generation…and you can’t even imagine the power and abilities you’re granted.” The world’s best magicians couldn’t hide the look of awe across Archibald MacGregor’s face. He didn’t know how to react, so he just continued to listen. Both men entered the house, and made their way towards the study. ”I’m here to send you on the correct path through life. Because I was granted permission to re-enter this world, to hopefully prevent a fifth-generation of your bloodline from going to Hell.” Matthews stated, determination shining through his grayed eyes. ”Fifth?” MacGregor asked, his eyes blinking in shock. He quickly traced back through his family tree and tried to figure out what happened to his male predecessors. ”Yes, fifth. I’ll let you find out the details on your own, because the only thing that is important is that you don’t follow your father into Hell. Have you ever heard the phrase ‘The right hand of the Devil’?” Archibald MacGregor nodded. “In reality, he has nearly 100. You’re one of the next in line. Where a normal person would enter Hell as a foot-soldier, you will be nearly as powerful as Satan himself. And we can’t allow that. We won’t allow that.” Archibald Matthews took off his bowler hat, and reached into the top drawer of the cabinet against the back wall of the study. As the 29 year old Archibald MacGregor watched, Archibald Matthews produced the same pistol that had taken the life of Christopher MacGregor at the base of his bronze statue. * * * * * Christopher MacGregor, my father, and Archibald Matthews had been in Vietnam almost two years when the incident occurred. Their unit was coming under heavy fire, well into enemy territory, and became separated. They had to lay low and hide from the constant packs of Vietnamese soldiers surveying the area – but at the same time, they had to find their way back towards the checkpoint in order to survive. Two US soldiers, alone, would be killed immediately. Their bodies would be hung from trees, where comrades would easily find them. So my father and Matthews had to avoid detection, and manage to head back towards the checkpoint. And this is where my father’s first error surfaced. They got into an argument over which direction they should go. Not a shouting match, but the kind of argument best friends have all the time. You disagree, and you continue to disagree until one person backs down and finally just decides to go along with what the other is saying. My father’s arrogance the foundation for his argument. He cited examples of previous trips out, and how he had been correct in the direction before. Ultimately, Matthews backed down and decided to follow my father. Oddly, my father had chosen the correct direction. They just didn’t know that Vietnamese soldiers were waiting only a half-mile in that same direction. While telling this story, my father continually wished that he had listened to Matthews and taken the wrong direction. At least then his cowardice might not have been exposed. After a half mile of quietly hiking through the jungle and riverbeds, Dad needed a refill of his canteen. They found a freshwater stream, and decided to top off their supply. Running out of water wasn’t really a fear, but fighting dehydrated and exhausted was. So they filled up. Archibald Matthews was a little quicker, and decided to keep on trekking towards the checkpoint my father had been directing them towards. Dad was filling up his canteen, when he heard a quick two-toned whistle coming from the brush. It wasn’t the Vietnamese soldiers, but three others from their mobile unit. They were hiding in what appeared to be a hole dug into the side of the riverbed. Think of a foxhole, just large enough for a few grown men to fit into, but not large enough to stand out from behind the tall bamboo that grew in Vietnam. My father was ready to call for Matthews when he heard the first shots. He sunk into the water as he watched his best friend take a round in the shoulder, sending him tumbling back down into the shallow river. In war, silence is as close of a friend as the man holding the gun next to you. The element of surprise, or the benefit of the hidden. 15 Vietnamese soldiers closed in on Matthews, and my father could do nothing but watch. He slipped down into the river, and silently slid over towards the foxhole with the other soldiers. He watched in horror as they lifted up his best friend, put a gun to his other shoulder, and fired. My Dad’s tears only stressed at how emotionally distressing this situation must have been. You, nor I, will hopefully ever have to make a call like my father had to make. “They’re killing him, Chris!” one of the soldiers had said to my father. Another shot, in another limb. Archibald screamed in agony. “What should we do?” another soldier whispered. My father remained silent. He didn’t know how many guns his comrades had, and he knew that there were at least 15 armed Vietnamese surrounding Matthews, gleefully torturing him. Another shot echoed in the jungle, and Archibald’s screams were steadily weakening. Undoubtedly from the loss of blood. The four men sat in the foxhole, but only my father could see what was happening. Apparently the leader of the Vietnamese troop took out a strand of piano wire, and used it as a garrote around the neck of Archibald Matthews. He didn’t put up a fight, and was dead seconds later. The four soldiers, my father included, waited in that foxhole for a half day – until they were sure that the Vietnamese were nowhere near. They exited, and my Dad carried Archibald’s body back to camp by himself. He made sure the other men kept watch as they made the journey through riverbeds and forests of bamboo. He told me that he could have ordered the men out of the foxhole, to try and save Matthews. But he didn’t, and he could only discern one reason why he couldn’t give that command. I tried to tell Dad that instinct overruled his judgment, because he knew they would all be slaughtered if they had left that foxhole in Archibald’s defense. But he didn’t believe it. He called himself a coward, and told me that the actual reason was that he was afraid to die. My father, arrogant and brash, was a coward. Selfish in that his own life overruled the life of his best friend. And that’s it. If it lacks any emotion, I apologize because I’m not exactly the world’s best scribe. My father’s bravery was non-existent – at least until Archibald’s death – and he’s mourned and felt ashamed of the loss ever since. I am named Archibald because my father felt that the memory of a man who shouldn’t be dead should live on, and what way to do that than name your son after him? I was pushed to be great at anything and everything, because my father couldn’t surmise the courage to save a friend in need. ”Honor the fallen” was beaten into my psyche because, quite simply, my father could not. Christopher MacGregor was hoping that his son would bring some ease to the pain, but ultimately my failures at everything he pushed me towards ended up killing him. He killed Archibald Matthews because of inaction, and I killed my father because of inability. * * * * * ”You won’t be needing this old relic, so I’ll be taking it.” Matthews said, holding the pistol in his hand. “I don’t want to give some symbolic advantage to Satan.” “So what do you want me to do? Enter the tournament? What happens if I lose, how do you know that I won’t be so depressed that I drink myself into an oblivion?” Matthews paused a moment, then responded, “That’s a risk we’re willing to take. You’ll have to find your own strength on that front. However, what we can’t allow you to do is kill yourself, which we know you’ve been thinking about.” MacGregor clenched his jaw tightly. So they do hear everything. Matthews reached up and put a consoling hand across the shoulders of Archibald MacGregor. His grip was firm, yet welcoming, “I know how confusing this must be for you. And don’t think that you’re the only person that must make a decision like this. There are others like you, ready to become a powerful pawn of The Devil in The War. Right now, there are others like me trying to show them exactly how much their life means in the overall scheme of things. After all, a war can’t be won without its soldiers.” ”And a soldier isn’t a soldier unless there is a war.” MacGregor replied, dryly. “Perhaps. But we need you to show the strength we know you possess. Be strong! My time here has passed, and I must be leaving you. But I’ll return. We need you to do what you feel is right,” Matthews walked towards the door, pausing at the threshold. He turned towards MacGregor and flashed the warming smile once more, “But I must warn you about something. Something inevitable, as its part of the rules.” “And what’s that?” “The other side is going to throw their sales pitch your way. Its inevitable.” Matthews said, and stepped out through the doorway with a noticeable spite stressing one word in particular. * * * * *
