Ceilings [Author's Note: Yes, the prologue is very similar to what I used to get into the match. However, it's a huge part of the character I'm trying to build/introduce, and, I think, necessary to the story/roleplay.] Prologue Everyone calls me Gavin. How will I be remembered? As an ordinary, under-the-radar boy that never showed any real talent or ambition. Never seemed to want attention; always just “got by” and feigned happiness in that state. Some people dread this – others embrace it – but I did neither; instead, I never found a niche and became insignificant, unaware of the black hole I was headed towards. I was semi-intelligent but never worked for top marks; athletic but never tried out for the sports team. At twenty-something, it's time to find my feet. I see all of my past friends doing things. Succeeding or failing; but trying. And they see me doing none of those three things, nor making a bid to change that. Lack of attention has a strange effect on the mind. It makes you feel things you never thought you'd feel; makes you think things that you never thought you'd think. The extremities come and go, weaving in and out of your consciousness, but always waiting for the opportune moment to pounce on vulnerability. It's a disease waiting to show symptoms; a catalyst for actions you never thought you'd act. It's a terrible, terrible thing, and the long and short of it is ... I'm afraid I've become a victim of this social construct, and even more terrified it might lead to that one grand, evil act. Dying for attention. Sometimes I tell myself it's all a figment of my imagination. And the truth be told, it might just be. I've never had insomnia, but my Mother sent me to therapy and now I'm on drugs for it. I've met my father before, but Mother keeps telling me that he abandoned us before I was born. She's wrong. She's a brainwashed, faithful Catholic who turns to the wrong person for all the answers that never existed. Tells me to ask forgiveness for sins that I've never committed. And through all of that, I think we still get along. Maybe it's because I have to. Maybe I don't even know. I told her the other day that I was going to try to become a wrestler. She says she doesn't remember when I told her the same thing at fifteen years of age, but she does. I think that it's a dream I never acted on, and now is as good of time as any. I tell myself that I'm right to think that. I'm not an optimist. I know that my chance of stumbling into an elite wrestling match with little experience is low. But I'm also forcing myself to believe that sometimes, you just have to try. Sometimes, you might get lucky; and even if I fail, I at least tried, for the first time in a long while. I hate to say it, but maybe I just want some attention.. Sometimes that's all a guy has -- his own self and a desire to be something. Ceilings Observing a ceiling, even a stylistically-designed one, is only momentarily entertaining for most people. As I don't align myself with most people, I found the mechanics of a ceiling quite interesting. Again, unlike most people, I found the ceiling not only inescapable, but unreachable. Even intimidating. As I stretched my arm toward it, effortlessly, it only moved further away – evading my reach – a ubiquitous reality of everyday life. I felt trapped by its presence; forever expected to make ends meet. I didn't know the whole truth – but I knew the importance of the ceiling, imposing a limit on all those below it. Exceptions do not disprove the rule: we are set up to fail. A voice: “Before we start, I would like you to close your eyes and rid yourself of every thought – every worry – a blank page so we can work with ease.” It wasn't my own, but its reach invaded what I deemed my personal space. At that point in time, I'm not sure if I liked that. Soft and caring as it was, I didn't feel inclined to follow its request. I was more concerned about my current state of discomfort, an experience that would frustrate anyone who was intent on observing a ceiling. Seconds passed. Minutes. My eyes remained unmoved, but I wasn't making any progress in my observation. Someone shifted in their chair. A sigh; again, not my own, and I heard the voice: “Your mother tells me that your father left the family before you were born. Would you care to tell me what that was like ... growing up without a fatherly figure?” Though I was ready to talk, I didn't want to take the time to comment on her very loose definitions of 'family', 'mother', and 'father', but instead responded, “She doesn't think I have ever met him. But she's wrong.” “Oh?”. They didn't sound convinced, but willing to hear more. I remember the details of the day I met my father. I was old enough to stay home by myself, so it was sometime in middle school. It'd be best if you don't tell Mother this; she didn't know, and still doesn't, what happened. Other than missing school, that day was fairly ordinary. Guess I just didn't feel like going, even after waking up at the same hour, eating the same toast-and-peanut-butter breakfast, orange juice on the side. Mom usually left a note; same place, same handwriting, a pointless heart scribbled next to her signature. This time it read: 'I'll be home late tonight, probably around 7. Make supper, please – Mom'. I was reading a book when the doorbell rang. Normally, Mother didn't like me answering the door when I was at home alone; whether it was a precaution for my safety or to prevent her reputation as a 'good' Mother from being damaged, I never knew. Doesn't matter, anyway, because I usually went against her will and did it anyway. I found pleasure in the feeling of holding dominion over the entire house. A simple, 'I'm sorry, she isn't home right now'. Even if she was home, out of sight in her bedroom or the bathroom, that's all I needed to do. In return, a smile, an 'Okay, I'll come back sometime else', then the refuted visitor would go back to their boring, meaningless life. Sometimes I wondered what they had come for, even though I never asked to take a message. Maybe I thought they were too predictable. Hey Mom, Shelly was here earlier and wants to meet for coffee tomorrow at 11; I forgot to tell you that Gail stopped by, said that the church committee meeting was rescheduled from the 3rd to the 5th; Mark wanted me to remind you to pick up a card for Mavis, who is suffering from breast cancer – keep her in your prayers! This time around, though, wasn't quite that ordinary. I opened the door to someone I didn't know or recognize. “Hello, son.” At the time I didn't know he was being literal. I said, “My Mom isn't here right now ... so if you want to come back another time ...” “I'm actually not here to see her.” Before that day, I didn't know much about my Dad. Never seen a picture, never even knew his name; Mom thought that it was best for me to be left in the dark, and for the most part, I agreed. It's better to forget, completely, about someone that betrays you instead of hanging off of those little details; the threads that, just barely, connected us. It wasn't hate – it was indifference. I didn't know of him, but of his existence. So, when I heard that this man was not here to see Mom – well, it was easy to deduce what he was here for. I probably looked a little surprised, even scared, and at fault for words. I said, “I'm sorry, you'll have to come by another time,” and began to close the door. I heard a murmur of protest as the door closed and I locked the door. Turning to go back to my book, I heard one more plea: “Gavin! Please open the door. It's ... it's me, Gavin. It's your father.” Dramatic as it was, it wasn't enough to stop me. I sat back down on the couch, straight-faced, my heart starting to pick up a little bit, breaths becoming more frequent. Even at the time I felt how anti-climactic that event had turned out; even I realized how easily that could have escalated out of control. I was incapable of handling such a huge epiphany, and buried myself back in a book, even though I had lost the page. Mother came home at 7 o'clock like she had said. “How was your day?” “Okay.” “Anything exciting happen?” “No.” Our conversations over supper almost always went like that, so as usual, she didn't push the subject. And that's how it happened. As I finished my story, I felt something. A sense of relief. I shifted my position on the couch and looked at my therapist. “That's an interesting story, Gavin.” A pause. “However, your Mother and I think that, in either case, that this trauma has continued to impose a negative effect on your life. At this point, it's starting to be a serious cause for concern.” “I don't think so.” “Well, what do you think?” “I don't know.” I think that's the best explanation of my situation – I simply don't know. Everything that society expects of a 20-something year old man like myself – I don't see the importance of it. The anxiety associated with these circumstances has, almost ironically, become the cause for my inability to succeed. I asked my therapist if that might be the case. She didn't know. I left before she could utter a word of protest. Maybe that was her job: silence. Paid one-hundred dollars an hour to listen. Whatever.
