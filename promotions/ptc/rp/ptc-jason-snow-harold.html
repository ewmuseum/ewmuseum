Harold

24 April 2007

November 4, 1984 10:30pm The memory would grow foggy through the years, like trying to hold water in your hands. There would come a time, when he was ten, that he held his eyes closed tight for most of a night, trying to picture his father’s face, but already, the memory was fading. Dripping away. Water through his hands. Harold’s left hand shook on the wheel; his right shook next to his mouth, holding a cigarette between his index and middle fingers. Jason sat in the passenger seat, staring at his father through burning red eyes. He hated him, and yet he loved him still. He hated him for the things he’d seen - for the way he’d listened to his mother scream for as long as his five year-old memory served him. He hated him because he couldn’t understand why he did things he did - why he said the things he said. And yet he loved him because he was his father. He would always be Jason’s father, and there was the constant yearning that maybe someday the man would change. Maybe someday they’d all be happy. “You know I don’t mean to...” Harold’s voice broke the silence under the heavy glare of his five year-old son’s haunting blue eyes. They were driving at night without destination. “She just... she finds the buttons to push. She always finds the fucking buttons, and I... Fucking bitch!” His temper was a legendary thing. Someday I’m going to get big, Jason thought, staring at his father in the dark. And then I’m going to make you pay for this. Someday I’m going to be bigger and stronger than you could ever be. “I never meant for you to see it,” Harold’s voice shook as much as his hands. “You know I love you, right?” Jason didn’t say anything. “Right?” Harold repeated, putting his hand on Jason’s chin, not softly, but not overly harsh. The cigarette still burned between his fingers, and Jason could feel the heat on his cheek. There was something desperate in his voice, and Jason’s jaw shook in the cup of his calloused hand. “I know,” he finally sniffled. “Good,” Harold took his hand back to his own mouth. “I just need you to know that. It ain’t that I don’t love your mother, it’s just that she...” his voice trailed off. Jason felt tears burning down his cheeks again. “You’ll understand when you’re older,” Harold went on. “When you have a wife of your own.” I’ll never be like you, Jason told himself, his lips pursed and his icicle blue eyes hard as steel. “You’re just like your father,” his mother would tell him countless times in the years to come. “You hungry?” Harold asked, turning his head sharply to face his son. The headlights of an oncoming car illuminated his features. He did not look unlike the man that Jason would grow up to be, though his loose shaggy curls were a few shades darker and his eyes were green, rather than blue. He wore a thick beard that gave him a fierce look. “We can stop to get something to eat.” He was trying to sound gentle now - trying to apologize for another night of terror without actually apologizing. Even at five years old, Jason knew this game well. Jason shook his head, but they still stopped at a small diner and ordered take out. Jason ate grudgingly, and then they returned home. Harold parked the car on the side of the street rather than in the driveway, and they sat there a moment, staring at the house. The lights were still on, and Jason supposed that Grace was waiting inside to continue their fight, her eye likely purple-black by now. He cried again when he thought of that, and his father noticed from the driver’s seat. Harold Snow was a hard man, not prone to affection, but in that moment, he reached over and stroked his son’s bright yellow hair. “I want you to treat your mother good, Jason,” his tone was deep, as always it was - as Jason’s would be when he grew to a man’s age. “Go on in.” “What about you?” Jason asked. Harold hesitated, and finally removed his rough, callused fingers from his sons silky blond locks. “I’m just going to drive around for a while,” he said finally, softer than Jason was used to. There are moments in time that feel like the last. Moments when time seems to stop, because you know this is the last time you will ever do the thing you’re doing. Perhaps it’s the last time you see an old friend. The last tender kiss of a lover’s lips - the last whispered word. The last taste. The last touch. The last time. Jason, even at five years of age, had such a moment, as he stared at the shadowed image of his father. I’m never going to see him again, he told himself, and suddenly, he remembered the good times. Early morning drives listening to the radio. Watching sports far past his bed time, sitting beneath his father’s arm. In the years that came later, he often wished he’d said something in that moment. Maybe there was something that might have made his father stay. But instead, Jason did as he was told, and got out of the car. He never saw his father again. March 23, 2007 11:50am Grace turned slowly to face him, and he could see for a moment that she was shocked by the news. Her hand trembled slightly, as his own had when he’d first opened the letter. He looked down at his hand now to find it steady. There was a small scar on it - the right one - that had been there since he was a child. It had faded over the years. “Dead?” Grace’s face couldn’t hide that the news had struck her. “How do... how did you find out?” “I got a letter,” said Jason, who was unwilling to tell his mother that the letter came from a prison. He didn’t want to give her the satisfaction. “Are you ok with it?” she asked, and he was surprised by the sincerity of the question. He didn’t know how to answer it. “I didn’t know him,” he said. “But I came here to tell you that I forgive you. For everything.” He saw a flash of anger in her eyes, but perhaps she thought better of it, for she held her silence. And then Jason added: “And I forgive him too.” “He had his good qualities,” was all Grace said. “You know, I’d really like it if you came down to stay with us sometime,” Jason went on, scratching the back of his head. “Raven always wanted that - or to bring Haley up here. She’s five years old now... I think she’d like to have her grandmother around more often.” “That’d be nice,” said Grace, but her smile was a phony one, and Jason had seen right through it a million times before. He bit his lip and looked away from her as his heart sank. She won’t come, a voice inside said. “I’m just going to drive around for a while,” said Harold. He recognized the feeling instantly. But in truth, he didn’t come here for Grace; he came for himself. Grace turned back to her coffee, oblivious to the bolder in Jason’s stomach. “So how is that wife of yours anyway?” she asked. Jason leveled his eyes on her for a long moment. “She’s fine,” he replied. March 6, 2007 1:25pm James Harmon pulled a file from a his briefcase and leaned back in his chair. He tried to look empathetic, but such men were not made for empathy. Jason could understand. He sat across from the middle aged case manager in his cramped, makeshift office that appeared to be a modified interviewing room. Like the rest of the prison, it was bland, and utterly without color - grays, whites, and off-whites. James himself was much the same, with graying brown hair and a simple golf shirt. “Your father...” his voice trailed off as he tried to choose his words carefully, “came to stay with us about six years ago. He-” “What was he in for?” Jason interrupted. “Armed robbery,” James replied with a swallow, but without hesitation. “Your father had a lengthy criminal record.” Jason nodded. “I take it that doesn’t surprise you,” James cocked an eyebrow. He went on: “We found out about the cancer maybe a year ago. It was already too far... If we’d found it earlier, maybe... I wish I could tell you that your father went peacefully, Mr. Snow, but that just wasn’t the case.” Jason leaned back and ran his fingers through his long, golden locks. It was a strange sensation - a hollow feeling in the it of his stomach. Even if it was prison, this was the building his father lived in. His father had probably sat in the very seat he was sitting in now. He’d talked to James Harmon, as Jason was now. His father. Harold. “He always said his son was Jason Snow, on television,” chuckled James with a small shake of his head. “We didn’t find out it was true until after he died. We were only able to find your address because he had it.” “My father had my address?” Jason perked up. James nodded. “We found an envelope addressed to you in his cell after the body was removed.” When he saw Jason’s face, he added: “There was nothing inside - it was empty.” Jason was too stunned to speak - too stunned to think. His father had addressed an envelope to him. How close had he come to writing the letter? Had he written one and then thrown it out? It all seemed so trivial, and yet to Jason, the information was earth shattering. “We also found this,” James reached back into his briefcase. “Maybe you’d like to keep it.” He produced a magazine, creased down the center, and on the cover, there was a large image of Jason, as he was moments after the end of GTT6, with blood and sweat streaming down his face and chest - his hair soaked back and away from his face. His eyes upturned toward the lights. PRO WRESTLING ILLUSTRATED. “He was awfully proud of you,” James said. “He told all the boys about how you won GTT6 and about the title match coming up. At least he lived to see GTT6, right? For that we can be thankful.” “Thanks,” Jason said as he raised the magazine to read the caption. “The Greatest?” it read. His father had read these words, not so long ago - had touched these pages with his own, callused fingertips. “Again, I’m sorry for your loss,” sighed James. Jason wasn’t naive enough to ask for pleasant anecdotes or amusing facts. It was obvious to Jason that James hadn’t liked Harold much, and was struggling to find comforting words. Jason didn’t blame him - he had no illusions that his father had been a likable man. “I don’t suppose you have a picture or something,” Jason looked up from the magazine. “You see, I really... I don’t have any. I haven’t seen him since I was a kid. So I was thinking...” “Yeah,” James reached not into his briefcase this time, but rather into the right-hand drawer of his desk. “Of course.” After some searching, he pulled a photograph out, and before passing it to Jason, he warned: “it’s only a mug shot, but it’s the best we can do.” “That’s fine,” said Jason, and then he took the photograph and peered on the face of his father for the first time since he was six - that was when his mother got rid of all the pictures. As soon as he saw the man, holding a number up to his chest, he realized just how much his memory of his father had been distorted throughout the years. It had become almost a cartoon. And now he was looking once more on the flesh and blood face of the man who had given him life. His hair was short and receding in the front - darker than he remembered. The beard was about the same. Most striking were the man’s eyes. They weren’t blue, like his own (he got those from his mother), but the hardness, he’d obviously inherited from Harold. There was an intensity to him that was uncommon - perhaps unnatural. There was fire in those eyes. “Is there anything else?” James asked. “No,” replied Jason, still staring at the image of his father. “Thank you.” November 4, 1984 11:20pm Above the rim of the metal trash can, flames licked at the walls of Jason’s room. He’d found a book of matches left carelessly on the kitchen table. He took them because he was curious. He couldn’t say why he started the fire. It crackled and spat before his eyes, tickling the tips of the navy blue curtains that hung from the room’s lone window. In the years to come, he would use that window to escape - to run to Raven. But for now, he was five years old. There was no where to run. And in truth, there had never been anywhere to run. Not really, for it wasn’t the fighting he was running from. It wasn’t the sounds of men beating his mother, or her hysterical cries. It wasn’t the shouting or the violence. It was the demon in his stomach being born of all of those things, as if they mated in his intestines and left their spawn as his cross to bear. At five, and at twelve, and at fourteen, and nineteen, and twenty-five, and twenty-seven. A man can’t run from himself. And neither can a boy. He felt the heat of the flames against his face as he leaned closer. Wooden. Stone. Dead. They looked as if he might be able to reach out and pluck one out of the air - hold it in the palm of his hand. He even put his hand out, his tiny fingers spread in some delirious desperation, trembling. Too close. It burned - and yet Jason refused to pull his hand away. Perhaps there was something soothing in the pain. The burn scars would fade over time, but they would always remain. Eventually, the curtains would catch fire, and had a neighbor not noticed while on a midnight stroll, the entire house would likely have burned down. But for now, Jason stared into the flames - into the heat - as if staring into the pit of his stomach. Now, and tomorrow, and forever.
