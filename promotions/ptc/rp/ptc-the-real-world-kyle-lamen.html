The Real World (Kyle Lamen)

3 April 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
A hand comes across Kyle Lamen’s handsome grinning face with an audible ‘slap.’ Reality is not all it’s cracked up to be. The grin drops for a moment, but the almond-eyed Japanese woman opposite him with the killer smile and killer right still smiles slightly. The grin rights itself on Lamen’s face, his eyes glinting against the backdrop of the Red Bank night sky. She tilts her head to the side, and gives him a look out the corner of her eye, still smiling. Her crimson lips are parted only a crack, and even that doesn’t last too long as Kyle seals the crack with his own lips. This time, he keeps his hands on the back of her neck and the small of her back, and she doesn’t slap him. We as Americans…no, scratch that. We as humans...we have this fascination with what is “real” and what is “unreal.” It’s the morning now, and Kyle wakes up in his messy bed, covers strewn about everywhere, facing the wall and a poster for NOFX. Turning his face away from the cartoonish figures mooning him, he turns and looks at the woman sleeping next to him, her jet hair spread across the pillow, framing her head. He smiles, turns away to check the time (9:12), turns back, and Miho’s not there. She’s in a hotel room over at the Molly Pitcher Inn, her hair’s tied up, and she’s actually awake and having a continental breakfast. For me, the line’s a little blurrier, and it gets worse every day. Kyle frowns, and pulls himself out of his bed, past the alarm-clock-wrecking-area on the nightstand, and makes his way to the restroom. He pulls a toothbrush from the four-holed toothbrush holder, hastily put some Crest on it, and jammed it into his mouth. Kyle stared into the spotted bathroom mirror, and the face that looked back at him was not his own. It was older, weathered, and lined with crow’s feet on the corners of his eyes. His hair was still blue, but pulled back along a widow’s peak, and the creases on his forehead did not disappear when he’d stopped raising his eyebrows. Kyle blinked, and it was back to normal. Fresh-faced and twenty-five. I mean, after all, people have their perception about wrestling and its perceived reality. And well, the way I perceive reality... He plopped himself down on the gray leather couch, in front of the television, having dressed in his track pants and blue Electric Six t-shirt. He fumbled for the remote in the cushions and flipped it on. He inspected it carefully, as he flipped from station to station. News, blonde babes, a stand-up comedian, commercial for more toothpaste… Me? I don’t watch much TV. He clicked the TV off, stands back up, and wanders off. See, on television, the most watched programs tend to be “reality television” where people have their “real lives” taped for the camera. Now Kyle is standing next to the window, tuning up his classic Fender Stratocaster ’94 which is black and decorated with pink sakura cherry blossoms. He took care to pluck a few quick strings, as he lost his gaze to the street below. Below, he caught the glimpse of blonde hair and green eyes, and thought he saw a glimpse in his direction, but the image faded as quickly as it appeared. Or there’s the other type of escapism. The dramas. Stuff like Grey’s Anatomy and CSI. Dramas about good-looking professionals with money and angst. Kyle shook his head, shook it off as he launched himself into a series of power chords and pounding rhythms. But, there’s more to it. Now, Kyle’s worked up a good lather, working himself into a frenzy as he ground music out from his guitar. It sounded great for a moment, even though the low G string had snapped, and one chord had gone sour. These types of shows are, quite obviously, not real. We cut to a show, specifically PRIME’s ReVolution…episode 91. Kyle is sweating heavily, as he steps through the curtain, into the back, behind the stage. There, a stagehand awaits with a large jug of Dasani water and a towel. We the people, we’re not as clever, as good-looking, or as successful as the people on these shows. So the television shows invent flaws for their hyper-characters. Kyle takes the water, opening it up, and splashing a little in his mouth and a lot on his torso, washing off the sweat of the match. We see a few bruises having risen purplish and red around his torso, and Kyle prods one carefully with a finger. The marks are compliments of Kenjiro Ito, one of the two Osakans in the PRIME locker room today. Kyle’s least favorite of the two. He’s the one who fought him, not dated him. They have illegitimate children, uncomfortable sex, and drinking problems. The same problems as we do. Except a little more sexy, a little more clean, a little easier. Miho Kurosaki, the young Japanese woman of Kyle’s dreams, is waiting around the corner, her miniskirt far, far too respectable for most of the Playboy TV “starlets” that typically hang around the backstage area of PRIME’s ReVolution shows. The cancer patient just dies, no struggle. No loss of dignity, no questions about honor. Kyle looks her up and down, a vision. He idol good-looks haven’t diminished with age, as she still has a body built for Saturday night and a face for Sunday morning. The kid is given up for adoption, or kept, and everything goes on as normal. In her arms is a little, half-breed baby. If it’s a boy, his name would be Kairu, a Japproximation of the name “Kyle.” If it was a girl, it’d be Mikiko, after Miho’s mother. They never had talked about it, back in Japan, but Kyle knew. He knew. He knew because he knew Miho. Even still. The sweat dripped into Kyle’s eyes, and blinked his vision black. When his eyes reopened, Miho was there, cradling Kyle’s gym bag in his arms. Chances are, when you watch a piece of television that you love, you love it because it touches something within you. Later, they sat at a table at a Village Inn, and they talked like no time had past since their relationship in 2003. When he spoke, she either laughed, or she smiled. When she spoke, his eyes locked to her and his mouth slightly parted as if she was taking his breath away, by the moment. It makes you feel like “Hey, that person’s like me. Sure, they’re better…but they’re like me.” He ate eggs and cheese and potatoes in a skillet, and she ate white toast and coffee. They talked about a dozen different things under the pale fluorescent lights. They talked about what music was good, and what wasn’t. Asian Kung-Fu Generation was still good. Flow! wasn’t. (Kyle’s opinion) The Delgados were still underrated, and James Blunt should be put in front of a firing squad. (Miho’s opintion.) They talked about Miho’s idol career, which collapsed right after Kyle left. She said it was due to the fickle nature of popularity, but in truth, it was because she was pregnant with a gaijin’s baby. They talked about Kyle’s tumor, and how he should get a second opinion, and not just go with the doctor that his buddy hired, and the inadvertent CAT scan that “lucked” him into a cancer diagnosis. They talked about how he wasn’t sure what he’d do yet, but in truth, he knew he’d have a surgery to have it removed at the last possible minute, at the end of the year. And he knew he’d die on the table, the way you know before you check the box score that your team has lost the game, and it wasn’t even close. It’s a lie. The people on television, they’re not like us at all. They’re not real on the reality shows, and they’re not real on the unreality shows. They’re nothing like us. They’re lies. And then, when they felt exceptionally brave amidst the smiles and jokes and “honesty,” they tried to talk about why Miho disappeared, and why Kyle stopped looking for her. It just wasn’t the right time for her. He knew she didn’t want to be found. Lies. It’s magic. Back in Japan, Kairu Kurosaki lay around on the floor of his grandmother’s house, while his father and mother wandered the backstage halls of a wrestling show. And this is why the real world is better than any fantasy, better than any faux-reality television program. To the other people in the Village Inn, the college kids, the older folks, and the truck drivers with their black coffees and pie…they look like any other mid-twenties couple in love. Because that’s still what they are. The people and places are legitimate. And it’s honest. It’s not pretending to be something that it’s not. Miho Kurosaki looks down, breaking her gaze from Kyle Lamen…wrestler and cancer victim. She’s scared, too scared to talk about their son. Too scared to talk about the future they’ll never have. They’ll talk for days and days. She wants to be back with Kairu. She wants to be with Kyle. And when he talks to her later about the cancer, and the fact that he’s thought about her every day for the last three years, she looks away for a moment, but she never stops smiling. It’s truthful.
