Derrick Ramsay

15 October 2009

Date: 2009-10-15 I let that rock fly and watched it careen into the lake making a dull plop, a sound that fell flat and silent against the autumn forest scene. The collage of red, yellow and browns signalled the coming frost and another winter. It seems like just yesterday I was shovelling that white sand. How cruel it is that summer slips by ever so quietly and quicker as each year past. I dreamt about living on some island where a toque was nothing but a poor fashion statement and wondered when I let myself become chained up in this small town. And then I remember; it was when that beautiful girl smiled at me and shook away the stoicism I harboured with her compassion. Blowing past my shoulder ran a jogger absorbed in himself and the music he pumped into his head: Stones, I think. I wondered if Mick and boys would ever be confined to one spot like I had let happen to myself. I settled that money (more so the lack there of) and my superior moral code was what kept me here. Besides that, if I ever was to be true to myself, I had grown quite comfortable in this town; letting loose the cowboy for the proud parent. It just offered such great scenes of tranquility, tucked away from the world, all to myself to enjoy in moments like this. It all seemed such a mysterious discrepancy between the boy that thought he would die trying to get somewhere better and the man who thought it might kill him to have to let go. In the mists of time lays a young adult, swallowed whole, just another missing link. Time seemed to stand still as I cast an empty gaze to the humble body of water and thought of how great today was, but a quick glance down my wrist to the fabulous looking, five dollar watch scientifically proved that sentiment false. It read 2:55 pm and indicated I was to leave one beauty for another. This village provided a great convenience: that everything you might need would be a walks distance. I walked the dusty trail back to Dundas St and the roar of the far too congested traffic way. The stroll was brief and enjoyable. I took notice of the porches along the way and the orange mass of vegetation (for calling them simple pumpkins would be far too modest) that sat proudly on display, waiting to be carved. Thanksgiving had passed by too, and now Paris Ontario was gearing itself to celebrate the next festival: All Hallows’ Eve. Ours was a stable if not mundane habitat and the tales of terror and the unexplained jived imaginations and spurred childlike excitement in most. I stop at the local think-tank of our next great thinkers and occupy the bench, waiting. The great yellow caravan of buses pulled into place, waiting too. And someone inside of Sacred Heart Elementary the children sat ignoring their teaching, looking at the clock and waiting. The time dragged from the view of all us waiting, but like clockwork (well, not like, but actual clockwork) the hammer happily banged against the bell. The buses howled back to life and the children piled out the front door making joyous conversation of being free for another weekend. And I, I sat waiting, discerning child from child until I found my own. I was waiting for some while after the initial wave of youth exited. Gracefully, before my patience expired to fear and concern, the messy ponytail and the little girl beneath it that held my heart walked out and up to me, arms open. June tells me I should cherish these moments, when she is still young enough and unashamed enough to give her father an embrace in public. I take heed completely. When we broke from the bearhug/headlock, the quisitive face of a four year old poked up. “Dad,” starts Rosie, “what does punk mean?’ I set her down and took hold her hand, beginning our walk back and searched for some favourable explanation. “Well, it means... well, where did you hear it?” “Didn’t hear it, read it. In the washroom, some kid had scratched it in. Simon Dep’ter is a punk.” Her innocence was adorable, and selfishly I wanted to keep her four forever. “Dempster?” “Yeah daddy, that was the name.” “Oh, well,” still not finding what I wanted to say I scratched the back of my head with my free hand. I looked into her expecting eyes and said, “A punk is something people call a bad person. Someone they don’t like.” With home in sight that little hand pulled tighter on mine, and it was shortly thereafter that she broke into a sprint while she sang “Punk, punk, punk, punk...” Up to the steps and a gentle giggle of the keys saw us in, and her straight to her toys. Simon Dempster, now that was a name I had cast aside for some time. But like I told you, this town loves a ghost story, especially if it was about a local boy and his mother. But what is surprising is just how fast and far away from the truth the tale had grown since the eighteen years that it took place; a twisted continuation of the bullying of Simon Dempster. Sid was a real quiet kid that grew up just a few houses over from me. Proximity is often all that is required for young boys to become friends, and our case was not so different. We shared many fantasies of scoring the goal that won the Stanley Cup for the Canadiens, tossed snow when it was present and balls when not. But for reasons of silly pride, that friendship failed to extend itself to the school yard. The rules of the school yard that governed us made it clear that a mixing between age groups was simple impermissible. I was three years older and to be caught with a third grader might have seen me die of embarrassment. I hid behind such stupid reasoning when Sid would come to me looking for protection, and boy did that kid ever need it. Like I said Sid was a quiet kid, and that simply rubbed some people the wrong way, so they started to pick on him. Simon did little to defend himself, and played the easy target. He became the quick ticket for a child to skyrocket his popularity for the day. Tongue lashings tired quickly and then it involved shoving. Shoving lead way to games of keep-away and all summed to one very lonely child. Sid didn’t really find any friends in his own peer group, what with he being a magnet for the wrath of the older ones. It was only maybe a month’s worth of fun had at his expense, but our actions carried heavy, and scarred the boy some. After that, when the recess bell rang Simon would simply go find a quiet corner of the yard and sit there to himself, moving to find another secluded area if anyone happened by. And it all happened because the kid was wearing long sleeves in twenty eight degree weather. So than another month passed by and nothing much thought of, and nothing significant sticking from my childhood recollections. Then came the juiciest piece of gossip the town had in years. It was a fucking tragedy, but everyone gobbled it up. On morning of October 22, 1991 I walked past the Dempster’s house on my way to school, only to notice the perimeter covered off by police tape. That day in school, no one made much mention of it, and I don’t think anyone recognized that Sid was truant. But that next morning the paper read, ‘On the night of Oct. 21 Paula Dempster had been assaulted.’ The article later went on to say that they police were looking for the attacker and leads were welcome. The story read more with its lack of information, and circulated with each person’s guess as to had really happened at 24 Warwick St that night. And ultimately the town discerned that Sid’s mom was a rape victim, and all it took was one ill told taunt by some kid looking to become the next star of the day by picking on poor ole Sid. He did what he thought to be his best impersonation of the clowns in town for the fair. He jested something to the tune of his willingness to be the next or perhaps even the current perpetrator of the crime. He then proceeded to have a few teeth knocked out of his mouth by a kid five years younger than him. Little did I know that the spectacle of Simon Dempster finally stepping up to his own defence, and now being lead away quite literally kicking and screaming, would be the last time I laid eyes on my neighbour. The news was out a few days later, ‘Local boy goes missing.’ Sid ran. The search wore on, and yielded nothing but strained hopes. The police gave up on the search after two weeks, declaring that the boy could not have survived in the cold November nights, and gave the case to their provincial counterpart much more removed and slow to act any further. Everyone thought he was dead, but I knew he must have been stronger than that. I hoped. His mother, however, struggled to keep the same hope as I did. The victim of two great injustices that no one should ever incur in the span of a month had left her in a pit of despair and dismay. Three weeks later she was rushed to the hospital after a heart attack. She died of a broken heart. That was the misfortunate reality that spun a ghost story of Mrs. Dempster seeking revenge on the children of Sacred Heart who tortured her poor, beloved boy to the point he would want to leave her. From what I could gather of overhearing the story these days is that every Hallowe’en she would grab boys walking past 24 Warwick St, demanding to know if they have seen Simon. And while I didn’t believe that ghost story, the same as I don’t believe any tale of souls still lingering, I knew that that residence has a great sadness hanging above it. Today it had become desolate and boarded up. No one who heard the story would ever want to live there. “Daddy?” her tiny voice pulled me out of my darker thoughts. “Yeah. What is it sweetheart?” my response, on my feet and following her voice. “I broke my doll!” sobbed my little girl. I grabbed her in my arms and hugged her tightly. I asked her, “Rosa, sweetie, you know that I love you right? And I want you to promise me that you won’t ever run away.” ---- He may have been the first to wish his grizzly ghost story was true, had he ever heard it. Instead, the hulking man stood snugly confined with a phone booth sliding quarters in. He pulled out the crumpled piece of magazine from his pocket, unfurled it and dialled the number it stated. “He-Hello. Primetime Central, how may I help you?” the other end greeted. “Yeah, uh, hi. I am responding to the ad for wrestlers.” “Okay sir, are you referring to the Global Title Tournament?” “Yes, Ma’am.” “Okay, and your name please sir.” “Ah, just call me Atreus.” “Real name sir.” “Just call me Atreus.”
