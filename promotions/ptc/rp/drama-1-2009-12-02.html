Drama 1

Date: 2009-12-02 For someone so concerned with conceiving great thoughts and doing great things, Buck Fledger has made some questionable life decisions. Signing a contract to unknowingly have his life videotaped for a six month period. Sleeping with his friend's girlfriend. You get the picture. He was flawed, but it was his awareness of those flaws that defined him. The difficulty for Buck was reconciling how he imagined himself with his actual self – his intellect with his actions. What kind of person was he to make a statement about humanity that he himself could not fulfil? This is what he was always talking about: substance behind the words substance behind the words. Let's call out others on their sophistry. Chastise someone for being shallow. Tell others that they should care more about people and ideas that exist outside of themselves, but not actually do it. Buck's mind was like Sammy. Always running and reeling. Here he was as a writer, in Hollywood, too, but not actually writing anything, instead just jockeying for position, looking to be liked, chasing the dream, climbing the rope that doesn't end but it seems worth climbing anyway, befriending people that he thought could be useful in advancing his stories, spreading ideas as if they were currency, all the while trying to understand what it meant to be human in the modern U S of A where information flows like water, operating everywhere, all at once, simultaneously, from the East Egg to Sitka, from the midwest to Gordita Beach, from the road in Tennesse to the Mississippi River. There he was, Americanized Canadian, not sure where to look for meaning, not sure if he quite understood what it meant to be American or why he found himself always coming back to it. He really tried to like Brandon, a small city in central Canada. He gave an honest effort there. At being liked. But the payoff wasn't the same. He didn't feel as validated. He needed more. He wouldn't find it in LA, but at least he would find people willing to deceive themselves into living the same lie. Brandon was like that too, but not in the same way. In Brandon it was the mighty farmer; in LA, the mighty filmmaker. Or so Buck thought. He was enthralled with this idea of self-creation. Buck. Fledger. Not a real name, a real name likes James, or Shmelka. And a girl named Jane. A Jane who hadn't smoked since that rebellious stage in high school. A girl who preferred to stay home instead of going out. Ambitious and independent, but not really sure if that's how she wanted to be seen. That's defining yourself against something, and that's akin to letting other people define you. Not so with her. She was real. Really real. The kind of real that you never knew was there until one morning you wake up beside it. That's what Buck experienced, anyway, as he opened his eyes to a world that he was never quite attuned to. A world where people act without obsessing over the consequences of their actions. Is there a world not like that? He would consider that question later. He had more immediate, logistical problems. The phone was ringing. He didn't know what time it was, couldn't find his clothes, and needed to go to the washroom. He ran around, trying to deny where he was, and who he was with. He could hear The Mom on the other side of the door, chastising him for what he had done. He could hear The Assistant, laughing at him. An angry laugh, the kind that makes everything into a cruel joke that the world plays on you. The cynical laugh in a world where there is no meaning beyond titles or income. Is this a cruel joke? Am I here? Questions unanswered. Jane looked at him with the dissatisfaction of a disgruntled lover, post-coital, as if that's how she's supposed to look. It looks like she's looking like she's supposed to look. Making a concentrated effort. But, she is also a genius at seeming unwatched. She tried her hand at acting, once, until she realized she didn't have the patience or social sensibility for it. Jane had an aversion to the drama, the power game, even though she was good at it. The game that is inevitable but she tried to reject anyway, deciding that it was better to push back at an abstract authority that she had no control over than to submit to it. It made no sense to her why The Assistant would be so concerned with a raise or an upcoming promotion. And yet, it felt good when the editor of her magazine asked her to cover a topic of importance, one that catches people's eye when it's on the cover, even if it's juxtaposed by a story of Tiger Woods being unfaithful. Oh my God, a celebrity cheats on his wife. Page 16. Oh my God, a wrestler sleeps with his friend's girlfriend. You can not make this shit up. The perverse irony that hangs over the whole situation is too thick to ignore. It's even a topic of discussion. “This was unexpected.” “I know. I can't believe we just did that. I mean, I can, but ...” “But it's too cliche to be real.” “Yeah. Too cliche to be real.” “Cliches come from something. Usually real things. Real things that happen.” “Stop getting all writer on me. Help me find my clothes.” It could have been the same voice. Either one of them. Both at once. Not scripted, but not authentic. Ironized irony. The Assistant did not burst into the room, but they were both expecting it as they looked for their clothes. The conversation was nothing special. How did I get home, how did this happen, did we use a condom. The usual pillow talk. Let's just forget about the whole thing. Okay. -- Buck was pacing outside of the Staples Arena just after the second round of GTT. Not smoking, but wanting someone else to come along and have one just so he could have a conversation with them. A conversation that existed in speech, not just internal dialogue. Not the dialogue of a movie, or a comments thread, or a forum topic, where there has to be a focus and not just a minor theme or interest that made sense to talk about at the time. The person that came long was, in fact, a smoker, though he knew the dangers of it and in all likelihood wanted to die before he got too old anyway. He worked as a stage hand, setting up and tearing down equipment, the kind of tangible labour where shit actually gets done rather than the mental masturbation of writing and philosophizing. Which Buck, at this time, had been doing for too long, which is why he just craves this conversation, like this guy who was craving a smoke. He needed a human voice like the smoker needs nicotine in the bloodstream. “Do you like smoking?” Buck asked. “Not really,” the guy responded, taking his first drag. “But I like the smoke breaks. Just to get away from all that shit in there.” Confused, Buck tried to make sense of it. He wondered if there was a philosophical basis for smoking a cigarette. As a kid, he tried it largely because adults didn't want him to. The adult, smoke-filled breath, yells at the kid for smoking. Like the adult who beats his son because he was violent at school. Smoking was a reaction when he was younger, but it couldn't possibly have the same meaning for a middle-aged stage hand. He took another drag. “I don't understand it either. It's nice to get some fresh air though.” The irony of the statement struck Buck as genuine. He couldn't tell if this guy were being serious, but there was something about him that Buck liked. Just living in the moment, really. Doing whatever feels good at the time. “I can't believe I'm working here, it doesn't feel good at all.” Buck replied: “Well, you know what Emerson said ... labour is good for the soul.” “I'm not sure if Emerson actually said that. I think it's from a song.” “Yeah, I know, but ...” Buck had lost, and it was irrecoverable. “Are you a wrestler?” “Sort of.” “What do you mean, sort of? You either are or you're not. It's not a hard question.” “Okay, well, I guess the answer is yes then.” The man took another drag. “Good for you. I remember I was a kid I dreamt of being a wrestler. And I end up in the business, only dragging around wrestling canvases. Do yourself a favour, kid, don't do what I did.” Buck didn't get it. “What did you do?” “Took up smoking.” One more drag, then he stomped out his butt. “Take care.” And he was gone. -- Over coffee the next day, Buck told Jane about this encounter that he had. He seemed passionate – very involved and animated about a simple conversation. Jane was paying attention because she was fascinated with Buck's fascination, and related to him on that level, because human situations, as is said, are a writer's food. And she had something really human to tell him, as soon as he gave her the chance. But he was enthralled by The Smoker and what he had to say about life, and Jane just listened. Jane wasn't much of a talker. She was an introvert. Buck considered himself an introvert too, but when there was stuff to talk about, he was like an automatic typewriter with unlimited paper. Most of it might as well be labelled for the garbage because it had no long-term value, but nonetheless the right nowness of this moment undermined the idea of a long-term anything. But long-term has a tendency of kicking people in the ass, or, as might be more appropriate for this situation, in the stomach. Long-term is a test of someone's mettle. When-push-comes-to-shove, that kind of philosophy. Buck was always talking about it in his usual way, but he had never really been pushed. He only pretended to know what it was like to be hard done by. But the realities of life are a little bit fucking harder than a professor not giving an extension he needed to perfect his short story about boys and girls in America. Buck was finally finished his train of thought. His story about the smoker and his importance. He could hardly contain himself he was so excited. Jane was smiling while gripping her coffee cup in a fidgety manner. Buck did this too, and tapped his foot, and ruffled his hair with his hand, and brushed his nose, thinking that he might have something running out, like a child who has a bit of dirt on his face and his mature sister is looking at him strangely. “Buck, I'm pregnant.” Page 19.
