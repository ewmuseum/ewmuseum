The Lost Art of Communication, Part 1

18 December 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2005-12-18 Communication is important. At the time of writing, the human race has not developed the ability to read each others’ minds. Of course, there are those who believe they can sense the feelings of another, but mostly that’s because they know what to look for; the tilt of the head, the tone of voice, the way someone’s gaze tracks across another’s face or avoids eye contact altogether. If you pay enough attention to what’s been said instead of planning your reply, sure, you can sense the emotion behind the words; find the incongruence and perhaps work out their meaning. Communication is very important when you share a life, a home, a dream, a reality. Sitting in different rooms, watching different but equally bad reality TV shows doesn’t promote communication. Letting tears fall without sharing the cause of the pain with the only ones who will care will not cure Doreen of the indecision that plagues her. Confining all her happiness to the pages of a locked diary will not get Sharifa the sisterly support she could so easily have. Speaking about his fears and confusions only to the bathroom mirror will never get Coach the help he so desperately needs. ……………………………………………………………………………………………….. Doreen lay on her bed, knees bent, staring in the middle distance, content. After a moment or two, she giggled and sat up, grabbed a well thumbed magazine from the floor and quickly opened it to the pages she had by now read a dozen times. There, in the bottom left hand corner was a small piece, barely three hundred words long, detailing her victory over Butterfly Hamada. She didn’t need the article as a reminder of the night, she remembered every second of the match and it was burned into her brain as the brightest and happiest of her memories so far. But seeing it in print somehow made it more real, and made her smile. As a hugely overweight teenager with thin, mousy hair, dishwater coloured eyes, and acne so advanced it had its own government, there had been very little in Doreen’s life worth celebrating. But now she was a professional wrestler, an entrant in the prestigious GTT5 tournament. Other kids could make as much fun as they wanted of her, but it didn’t matter. The teasing wouldn’t hurt so much because Doreen wouldn’t take it as truth anymore. She wasn’t a loser! She wasn’t as slow or thick or ugly as they said. When she was in that ring, she was powerful, graceful and a winner. No one could take that away from her. As she re-read the article, she marvelled at how it had all come to pass, how strange it was that her happiness rode on the back of one mans madness. If Coach hadn’t be so… unhinged, would she be where she was now? She doubted it. Before his mind was marred with psychosis and depression, he had been a fairly successful coach, moulding scores of beefy young men into fodder for the smaller wrestling federations. He was good at what he did, encouraging those he knew could make it far and being frank with those who most likely would only ever wrestle low on the card. Each Christmas, he would receive gifts from a handful of stars who owed their careers, in part, to him. Back then, he would never have considered training the likes of Doreen Pepperpot. She lacked the confidence a good wrestler needed. She had no drive, no hunger for success. All she had was a massive body she was uncomfortable in and a grace of movement that belied her size. But that was then, and this was now. Coach had been out of work for almost a year and out of hospital barely a month when Doreen’s mother first met him at a conference. Always eager for a bargain and constantly concerned about her daughter’s ever increasing weight, she engaged him as Doreen’s private personal trainer. That had been just over three years ago. He had quickly seen that Doreen was never going to be a slender wisp like her mother and sister and had insisted on sending her to the Endocrinologist who would make the diagnoses that would change everything. Doreen had an excess of growth hormone and an imbalance of other hormones that had led her to be shaped like a small mountain. No amount of dieting or exercise would change the way she looked. Instead of making Coach’s role in the family redundant, this discovery just made him all the more important to it as it drove a wedge further between mother and daughter. Doreen’s mother was Gillian Pepperpot, the famous author who went by the more sweet sounding and acceptable nom de plume of Gillian Gervais. She was a perfectionist who wanted the whole world to listen to her and adore her. She believed with an unshaken certainty that human beings were in total control of their own lives and destiny. She believed that all of humanity’s faults could be blamed squarely on laziness. She believed completely that the body was a temple and should be treated as if it was the cell of a penitent monk. She became the country’s best loved Dietician slash author slash TV personality. Was it any wonder that she found it so hard to accept her daughter? Doreen stood for everything Gillian despised and soon the book tours and personal appearances got longer and longer, further and further away from home. And so Coach stepped in more and more, becoming trainer, father and friend. He cooked, paid the cleaner, helped with homework, and ultimately, taught the girls to wrestle. Then there was… the accident. A tear slid down Doreen’s pocked cheek. She didn’t want to think about the accident, there was nothing she could’ve done to change what happened, and so she shook the thought of it from her mind. What was important was what she could do now to help fix her friend. So far, she had just done as he asked, certain that by avoiding conflict and letting him live in his deluded reality, he would eventually get better. But now she wasn’t so sure. He was convinced he once again was the great coach he used to be and that he could mould Doreen into a star. In fact, he believed that she already was. In his mind, another’s image overlaid Doreen’s at times. She worried that he would push things too far. Push her into a match where she would be seriously hurt. She had met Mick Foley once at an anti-Bush rally and marvelled at the scar where his ear should have been. She didn’t want to end up like that. Given that her next opponent was Brian Arson, the most notorious Bad Boy in a sport filled with the baddest of bad boy types, injury was almost a certainty and victory only a vague possibility. But more than her own safety, she worried about Coach himself. Maybe being without medication for so long wasn’t the best thing for Coach himself? Her mother had always preached that chemicals, even those prescribed by doctors, were wrong and destroyed the body. But what if she was wrong? Doreen sat on the bed, conflicted. If Sharifa had known that at that very moment, Doreen was contemplating bowing out of the tournament, she would have been furious. All her life, she had quite literally lived in the shadow of her big sister. She was never "Sharifa, the pretty blonde in the gymnastics team," or "Sharifa, the lead in the Christmas ballet." No, she was "Sharifa, her sister is the huge ugly one over there eating off of two lunch trays." She hated being associated with her sister. At home together, Doreen was alright for a sister, but in public, she was nothing but an embarrassment. Until now. Doreen was not just a local celebrity for her size, but for her wrestling, and in a small town that was ninety percent redneck, that was something special. And then there was the night of the match against Butterfly Hamada. Sharifa’s life had changed forever that night. It was the last thing she’d expected, but then, these things generally are. She’d gone along to the match partly because she cared what happened to her sister, but mostly because she knew she’d be able to stay up late, maybe convince a vendor that she was old enough to buy a beer or two. The atmosphere had been electric and the arena crowded. Being family of a competitor, Sharifa was glad of the ringside seats. If you’re going to do it, do it in style, right? Hyper aware of the cameras, she sat sipping her Bud-lite, skirt hiked to mid thigh and her small chest sticking out as far as she could without seriously distorting her spine. Fluttering her eye lashes and concentrating on smiling sweetly, she let the first few matches pass her by without having paid any attention. Then suddenly, she saw Him. This absolutely gorgeous guy stepped into the ring. He was magnificent. Sculpted and arrogant looking, a bad boy to be sure. Sharifa felt an alien rush of excitement running through her body. Others might call it her first crush, but she knew it was more than that. She was in love with Brian Arson! Every moment since then had been spent planning and scheming. Somehow, she would get him to notice her. He’d be there at the next match, when Doreen wrestled… Sharifa shrugged. Who cared who it was she was wrestling, just so long as it was on the same night as Brian Arson next appeared. Coach would have been horrified if he knew what Sharifa was thinking. Maybe it would have been enough to snap him out of the delusional world he had shakily constructed around his fragile mind. It was getting harder to convince himself that everything was fine. He had to sing louder inside his head to drown out the voices, distract himself with as much as possible to be able to ignore the blame and condescension in the voices. He had been in a similar state a few years ago and got through it with support and therapy, but it had been easier; the voices didn’t sound so much like himself back then. It was a vicious cycle. The more he denied the voices, the harder it became to function. But if he concentrated and listened to not just the tone, but the words they were saying, he would cease functioning altogether. A voice, smaller than the others, perhaps his internal dialogue, told him he should seek help. It counselled him to pick up the phone, call the nurse, take the tablets, sink onto a hospital bed and wait for the chemicals to rebalance reality. But on another level, it recognised that he had a responsibility to the Pepperpot sisters. If he went away now, they’d be alone. Mrs. Pepperpot… sorry, Ms. Gervais, wouldn’t be back for weeks. He couldn’t let the girls fall into the care of the social services. He’d survive a bit longer. He’s see the girls though Christmas and then, if he could hold onto a shred of insight, would seek the help he so desperately needed. Communication is important; never more so than when it’s seriously lacking.
