Date: 2009-11-08 Maybe You Can Polish A Turd After All: The Garbage Bag Johnny Story Chapter 2: Higher Powers A Garbage Bag Production JULY 8, 2009 I never trusted doctors. If they all did their jobs right, you'd never need to see any of them more than once. They were like drug dealers, except they kept all your records, and it was a lot tougher to get what you wanted. If you were hard up for some pills or something, and your normal guy was out of town, I suppose you could try to get a doctor to prescribe you something good, but that ain't like shooting fish in a barrel. It's more like throwing a fish at a line of barrels moving on a conveyor belt and hoping that somehow it gets you nice and fucked up. Doctors also kept inconvenient hours and made you wait, and after the receptionist called your number, you'd have to endure mild molestations, proddings of unlikely orifices, with an assortment of cold instruments. When it was all said and done, all you'd get would be a slip of paper with an illegible hieroglyphic on it assigning you a random vial that was more often than not useless. From there, you'd have to bring that slip of paper somewhere else to get whatever it said you were supposed to get, and you'd have to pay them on both ends, each before you knew what kind of chemical enterprise you were signing up for. With a dealer, there's no middle man, no paper trail and no uncertainty. It's a flawless system. You ask a guy for what you want, and he gives it to you, no questions asked and no holes intruded by unwelcome devices. I had refused all of their attempts to medicate me, but there was something unrelenting about the place that didn't agree with my faculties. I'd get random bouts of the shakes, and my brain swelled against the consistency of my skull. The first few days there, I slept. The place hadn't started to grow on me, and it wouldn't for a while. I had been clean for three days, but it was not at all how I had imagined sobriety would be. There was a knock, solely out of courtesy, and the door opened. I tilted my head just enough to examine the latest in a string of strange visitors. Most wore green scrubs, but he wore the clothes that men of god wear. I hadn't seen but a handful of clergymen up this close, and whenever I did before, it was in the context of death. "For in that he himself hath suffered being tempted, he is able to succour them that are tempted." He pulled a chair to my bedside. "Hebrews 2:18." His names and numbers held no resonance to me, but I made a sound of some fashion to acknowledge that there were names and numbers exchanged. I wasn't shaking so much anymore. My body was too sore, and even the slightest movements were too taxing. "John," he looked at me before sitting. He had younger features than I expected, and they curved sympathetically upon seeing my condition. "Your doctors tell me you've been refusing to take your medicine." I nodded, feeling the persistent headache shift forward and sink down over my eyes. For the past couple of days, I watched molecules bead together and melt down the air. I had never seen such weather indoors, even in the most altered states of consciousness I had worked so diligently to achieve. "It takes a lot of courage to admit you have a problem." I looked at him with one eye and grunted. "That's only the first step, though, in a long and trying path. The medicine will help you, John, but you have to accept that help. You shouldn't try to do this alone." Argumentative blood coursed through my veins as I tried to distinguish his voice from the other; the one that would come and go, even when nobody else was there. It certainly wasn't mine. At least, it didn't sound like mine. It was there now, reminding me how I was better off alone because bad things don't happen when other people aren't around to acknowledge them. "Maybe," I started, with a conviction that lacked any strength to defend it. "Maybe this was all a mistake." "What was that?" The reverend leaned his head in closer, turning an ear to me. "I shouldn't have come here," I quickly concluded, but I was just talking, and not even necessarily to him. I certainly didn't belong anywhere else. "You can leave if you choose." His tone contradicted his message. "Overcoming addiction is an endless battle, and without choice, there would be no victory." If I had the strength to get up from that bed and walk out right then and there, I probably would have, but lately I had been feeling too weak to stand for long bouts. I couldn't make it to the bathroom without getting dizzy. I'd been attacked with more pieces of furniture than the average man uses in a lifetime. I'd fallen off ladders so tall I had to climb other ladders to get to them. I'd dropped from crippling heights off of structures forged in madmen's dreams. No matter how much I was hurt, I always got up, and it never seemed like I had a choice. That's just how it went, and there was something about it that made me feel alive. Everything else would shut away, and for a second, there was this clarity that couldn't be synthesized or attained by any other means, regardless of how hard I sought to find something that could take me there whenever I needed to escape. But there I was, taunted by that phantom of choice; that I could leave if only I could put myself together just enough to get up, and I couldn't get up. "I hope you don't mind if I'm frank, John, but are you a religious man?" I felt like I was talking to an actor with very little patience for research, and someone must have hired him the to portray one of my grandparents for reasons of personal amusement. "I don't mind, but if you're going to call me anything, could you please just call me Johnny?" "Sure," he smiled, and then he waited for me to answer, but I'd already forgotten what he'd asked me. His smile wasn't patient, though, and while his lips still bent, his cheeks strained to lift their corners. "You mind helping me out?" My upper half wriggled while my legs were pinned under a knot of heavy blankets. "That's what I'm here for." The muscles in his face relaxed. "I was being literal," I corrected myself. Apparently it hadn't worked. "I just meant I wanted to go out for a smoke." "That's fine, Johnny," he remembered, like the appendage was being dragged by loose tendons. Then, as if bestowed with some unearthly power, he casually freed my legs from the blanket so I could swing them to the ground. WIth hesitation, I pulled myself to my feet, and sensing my weakness, he jumped to my side to keep me from toppling forward. "Thanks, Frank." He looked confused as he soldiered me out of the room and confused again as he watched me compulsively look back towards the confined noise battling after us. I thought he had done an excellent job pretending that it didn't bother him, but eventually, I was able to drown it out, too, and wasn't as impressed. It was almost as if it had never been there at all. I doted along, distracted by the nobility I felt in being assisted about the dull halls, like I had been wounded in battle, undoubtedly pierced by exploded shrapnel, too resilient or afraid to ask how bad it was. Frank assured me, in ways, that I was going to live. I couldn't tell if things were moving fast or if I was just seeing them in slow motion. It had to have been a combination of the two, as is often the case when urgent matters are at play. It was making me dizzy. They always tell you not to close your eyes after they tell you you're going to make it. Despite it all, eyes always closed, and people always died. I didn't know yet if I was fit for such a noble death. Making me dizzier still, it had been some time since I last had a cigarette: three hours, maybe four. I wasn't shaking anymore, but I broke out in a sweat. I had only been out of bed to smoke cigarettes, to inspect the room when the voices seemed outside myself and, oftentimes, to relieve myself. The tempestuousness of movement wrought itself against the craving pangs, and I'd like to say that I tried to aim more cautiously, but by all accounts, I vomitted all over the reverend's clerics and my own long worn garb. He recoiled in disgust, holding his arms, the foodless, yellow bile dripping down him as time reoriented itself. I suddenly felt better. "Jesus!" I shouted, completely disregarding my audience. "I'm really sorry about that." I could tell that he didn't want to forgive me, but he really didn't have much of a choice, it being his job and all. "Here," he looked around for some staff, arms hanging down and away from his body as he spun like he was doing ghost impressions. Unfortunately, there wasn't anyone or anything around to help him before the stains would start to set in. "Let's just find a restroom and wash ourselves off." I followed him down the hallway as he forged forward, and we didn't exchange any words or glances until we found a restroom. As we stood at sinks on opposite ends of the row, I was too embarrassed to face him, and he was still too angry to face me. I could just hear his sink, turning on and off and on and off, conserving water while he excessively tore away at paper towels so that it was on and off and rip and on and off and rip. I had done a pretty shoddy job of cleaning myself up, and to be honest, by the time we got to the sinks, I couldn't tell which spots on my shirt were from the incident in the hallway and which had accumulated since I had admitted myself. I looked in the mirror, and it brought back stinging memories of looking in mirrors backstage, looking roughly the same as I did now. This time, though, there weren't thousands of fans waiting for me to emerge from behind a curtain. It was just me and the reverend, and he was still wiping my vomit off of himself, and there was no crowd to cheer me on the outside of the washroom. There was something empty and depressing about all of that, but on the other hand, there was something free about it. There was nobody expecting me to win. There was nobody expecting me to look good losing. The only people around me now expected me to fail miserably, and if somehow I didn't, it would be a miracle of god's merciful hand. "So, you never answered me before." The voice shocked me from thought, but I still looked ahead, into the reflection of my own eyes. They had looked more damaged on several occasions, but I could trace those occassions back to a baseball bat to the head or a stiff, wild forearm that caught me just right. I couldn't attribute this to any particular instance. Eventually, I just permanently became Garbage Bag Johnny out of spite for having to pretend to be him. He was always part of me, but there were always other parts, too. I wished I could remember what they were like. "What did you ask?" I looked away from the mirror, but I couldn't look at him. I looked at the speckling on the ceiling and thought about how easy it would've been for me to knock him unconscious from behind, change into his clothes and leave the facility unchallenged. My conscience reminded me that I had already put him through enough, and besides, the easy road was only easy until it ended, and they always ended sooner than later without obstacles. "I asked if you were a religious man." "Well, Frank," I swallowed hard. I didn't quite know how to answer him because I didn't quite know the answer. "I've seen a lot of things that I couldn't even begin to explain, but there ain't never been evidence to indicate that there was any rhyme or reason to any of it." "What if I told you there was a reason for everything?" From my periphery, I could see the back of his head and the reflection of his eyes in the mirror, pointed back at the harrowed lines above his furrowed brows. "You think all of those things would be here without some kind of plan in the first place?" "I don't know, and I'm not about to rule that out. I'm just saying." I looked down at the floor. Ugly green tiles bordered by black grout surrounded each other. It was the only safe place to look. "I've told a few people my take on everything, but none of them ever listened with any intention of leaving confused." "I'll admit I'm a little confused now." "I take it you've heard a saying about how god helps those who help themselves?" The tiles held no answers. "I don't know what book or number that one's from, but you know what I mean?" "I don't believe that quote is in the bible, but yes, I've heard it." "You think that's true, Frank?" He paused and thought about the question as I fumbled to explain fumbled explanations. "That's a tricky question, you know. It depends on where you're going with this," he assessed correctly as he assessed, and in doing so, was incorrect. "And why do you keep calling me Frank?" She was 43 years old. I had been there for about 45 minutes and was halfway through my second joint. I think I recognized some of the people who were there. I think I saw Lawnmower Willie and maybe a few great aunts or second cousins I had met a handful of times when I was small. There weren't that many people there; just enough that I didn't recognize most of them. I figured there was no reason to hold anything against her anymore, but I still couldn't bring myself to go in; not in front of all those people. I thought I had run a gamut of emotions in the preceding five days, but I really hadn't felt much and never much differently than I had felt when Military Issue Dennis gave me the news. He wasn't there. I guess he couldn't bear it either. I think mostly, I just didn't want to see her lying there in that casket. The last time I saw her, she was furious at me, but she was alive, and I wanted to remember her alive. Sometimes she wasn't furious at me, although I was considerably good at doing things that set her off, so those times weren't too often. She never had much, and she did good with teaching me what that was like, but she once had hope, and she once had faith. I never had very much of either. I'm sure there was something about her life that was beautiful, most likely before my time, and maybe I missed it. She never asked to have me, but she could have done worse with what she was dealt. I hoped that the ceremony was nice, on one hand, and on the other, I hoped it wasn't. I didn't want her funeral to be the nicest thing she ever had. I had zoned out, and god knows for how long, when someone started tapping on my window. It was about the last thing I wanted to hear. I hurried up and snuffed the joint out in the ashtray and then hid the roach under a tissue. I reached across and cranked the window open. There was a smiling face full of crooked, stained teeth on the other end. It belonged to a tall, rail-thin fellow with a big beard and long, stringy hair that had been tied back into a ponytail. It didn't look like he was dressed nice enough for a funeral, but it looked like the black jeans and tan cowboy cut work shirt were about the nicest things he could find. He bore a strange resemblance to some of the pictures of Jesus I had seen, enough so that he could've been Jesus's underacheiving brother. That's probably all they'd send me if I needed a miracle anyway. "Excuse me, sir, but are you going back into town?" "I don't know." I hadn't planned very far ahead, and I hadn't thought about if I was. "I guess I could." "I couldn't, by chance, catch a ride with you, could I?" He held his hands together, fingers interlocked, and shook them up and down. "I parked my rig there. Someone was nice enough to drive me out here. I didn't feel it was proper to bring it to a funeral." "Hop in." I couldn't turn him away, knowing that he was kind enough to not bring a semi-truck to my mother's funeral. "Much obliged." He hopped in and extended his hand for a handshake. "Zeb Feiten, at your service." "Nice to meet you." I closed the ashtray and started the car. "Johnny." "You cut out early, too?" "Cut out early?" I didn't understand, or I probably wasn't paying much attention. "From the funeral," Zeb explained. "I don't remember seeing you, but I'm lucky I caught you." "Oh, uh, no." I was nervous, and it felt like it was getting hotter in the car. "Sorry for your loss, man." "Thanks, kid." He made a noise that would've been a chuckle had it continued any longer. "It wasn't nobody close to me. Fact is, I can't even remember the last time I saw her. Had to be at least twenty years." "Hey, you said you were a truck driver, right?" I didn't really want to hear anything more about the funeral. "Yep, most of my life now." He nodded vigorously, affirming his being as if it were his place to drive trucks. "You ever pick up a hitchiker named Dina?" "Doesn't ring a bell." He was almost still nodding as his motions were switching longitudinal to latitudinal courses. "It would've been about two years ago. She's kinda short, brown hair, black hoodie?" "Sorry, kid. I would've remembered if I had." He arched an eyebrow at me. "Why you so interested?" "Doesn't matter," I bit down on my lip and steered on. "Just a girl." "Ain't no such thing as just a girl, son." He chuckled again, for the appropriate length of time for it to be considered as such. I smiled out of the right side of my mouth at him, and figured I'd just let it be at that, but without conversation between strangers, unnoticed things came to light. "It's awful smoky in here." It was coming through the vents in waves. His truck driving instincts kicked into high gear. "If you got engine problems, I can probably fix 'em." "Oh, shit!" The console was on fire. I tried pulling out the ashtray, but it was so hot it burnt my fingers. "Fuck! Run!" I opened my door, Zeb opened his, and both of us ran in opposite directions
