IG 36: Sleight of Hand Title: Sleight of Hand Wrestler: The Illusionist To tell the story of how I was first introduced to my craft, I must take you back to the night the voices came to me. They started faint as whispers, and for a time I mistook them as breezes through the chimney draft, or mice in the halls. But I remember in great detail first night the demon appeared, for only a second in my mirror, before returning to his hell. I saw him in other places after that. I heard him enter my room one night, his cotton soft footfalls rendered useless against the creaking of century-old floor boards. He took a seat on my bed that night, I know, even though I did not see him. Within weeks, I found him lingering in the front yard, and I would stare at him for hours through my windows. And of all the lessons that he’s taught me over the years, that first one remains most important. There’s no such thing as magic. I make this known at every opportunity, as I’m doing now with you. Magic is a dying man’s game, you see. In years past, a man could attribute what could not be explained to magic. But now, everything is explained for us. Science has been magic’s bane, for it has left no stone unturned; there are no more questions waiting for fantastical answers. So allow me to reiterate. There is no such thing as magic, but the illusion is an art form. I make my home in a mansion jutted from a hill, in a location that I prefer not to disclose. I was born here, the only child to Alexander, for whom I was named, and Gretchen Pavlinksi. I come from very old money. So very old that I have no use for it. My parents died together when I was very young, and for the last several years I’ve lived alone. “There’s no such thing as magic,” a voice came from my fire place on one particularly cool January night. I’d been staring into the flames for an hour or more by that point, for I knew he was lurking in there, my demon. I'd by then grown accustomed to his tingling presence, like a spider on the underside of the skin. “Would you like something to drink?” I asked him, and immediately, the flames in my hearth were sucked into the wood that they burned, and I was left alone in the dark. And yet to say I was alone was not quite true. The demon stood behind my chair, a plush, velvet old thing, though I did not turn around to confirm his presence. “Is it my soul you’re after?” I asked him. A moment passed, or perhaps more than that, before he replied. “I have no use for souls.” “So you’re not Satan then?” I heard him laugh behind me, and then my floor creaked beneath him as he rounded my chair and came into full view. He looked not like a man, but rather like a cartoon devil, with translucent skin that took the shade of whatever light he was standing in. There was only moonlight tonight, and it seeped in through the room’s lone window and bathed him in shimmering blue-white. While he had no horns, I could see the spots on his forehead where they might come out if summoned, or perhaps they were grown seasonally, like the antlers of a moose. The nails on both his hands and his feet were long, and they were pointed, as were his teeth, and he was naked as any animal, covered only by a few patches of fur. “No,” he said finally, and he took his seat next to me in an identical chair to my own. “I’m not Satan.” “Then who are you?” He thought on that for a moment, easing himself back into the chair and folding his sinewy arms over his ribs. “Your father called me Justice on the night he killed your mother,” he turned to me snake-like, “and then Guilt, hours later as he killed himself.” “And what will I call you?” “Loneliness,” he shrugged. “Boredom, perhaps.” “And why have you come?” I asked him. “For the same reason I came to your father,” he replied in half a whisper. “I’ve come to offer you power.” “Power?” “Power,” the demon nodded, and rubbing his middle and index fingers together, he drew the flames back from the logs in the hearth. “I can give you the power to shape the reality around you. To make the atheist offer his soul to Christ or to make the Christian denounce his God. I offer you, my friend, the art of illusion.” “You said there was no such thing as magic.” “There isn’t,” he told me, “but the art of illusion is to make your audience, if only for an instant, believe that it might exist.” “And what will I do with this power?” I asked him as the light of the flames seeped into his skin. “Whatever you will,” was the answer. “Your father used it to destroy his enemies, but his ambitions were different than yours. He had a lust for money. But you...” He breathed me in for a moment, and a smile came over his wicked face. “The son, as they say, is not the father. So I offer you my gift to use how you like. You can orchestrate marvels that will have you known throughout the world. You can cow great men. You can entertain children. It makes no difference to me.” “And in exchange?” “I've already told you that I’m not Satan,” the demon chuckled. As I looked at him closer I could see that there were scales on his flesh, and perhaps it was those that caused his color to change the way it did. And over the next several years, this demon of mine, whose name changed as often as his skin, taught me my craft. And now, as a man full grown, I say with confidence that I can, indeed, make the atheist offer his soul to Christ. I can make the Christian denounce his God. And all of it that he taught me, quite literally or by metaphor, was nothing more than sleight of hand. “How will you use this power?” he asked me, years later, after we’d come to know each other quite well. He already knew the answer, and when I said nothing, a grin appeared on his thin, dark lips. “To cow great men,” he said. “I can craft you a mask of my own skin. And the world will know you only as The Illusionist.” I nodded and sipped from my cup of cider, and then, I asked him once more the question that I’d asked him many times over the years. “And what will you have in exchange for this power you’ve given me?” His smile remained unwavered on his wicked face. “Tell the world, Illusionist,” his snake’s tongue flicked, “that there is no such thing as magic.” Now it has long been said that the devil’s greatest trick was to convince the world that he does not exist. And perhaps my demon, if he was indeed Satan, was using me to continue his work by convincing the world that magic does not exist either. I can read your thoughts, my audience. You are asking, how is it that a demon can exist if magic does not? If science has left no stone unturned? If there are no more questions waiting to be answered by the fantastical? And for the answer to this, my friends, I must remind you that alas, magic, like demons, indeed does not exist. But the art of the illusion is to make you believe, if for only an instant, that it might. My name is not Alexander Pavlinski. I am everything and I am nothing; everyone and no one at all. I am whatever I choose. I am the Illusionist.
