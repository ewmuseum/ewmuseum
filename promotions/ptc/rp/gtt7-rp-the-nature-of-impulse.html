Primetime Central Presents: GTT7 Story GTT7 a Primetime Central production Home Mobile Rules The Field The Seeds Promos Discussion PTC GTT7 at PTCPedia The Draw at UStream Login Title: The Nature of Impulse Featuring: Impulse Date: 13th Oct 2009 Location: New York - from JFK to Washington Heights to the Bronx and back. ”Folks, this is your captain speaking, uhhh… we’re currently descending into John F. Kennedy International Airport, our final destination. Local time is ten thirteen PM, uhhh… temperature is a crisp fifty – seven degrees Fahrenheit. I would remind you to remain seated until the plane comes to a complete stop at the terminal, and, uhhh… thank you for flying American Airlines.” The voice was calm and even handed, with the slight southern twang that always signaled a pilot who knew what he was doing. It woke me up, but not with a start, and I sensed the plane descending. I checked my watch; man, I just slept for four straight hours. I can’t remember the last time I did that when I wasn’t home. Either I’m getting used to travel, or my last three matches were all sloppy and shoddy blurs of exhaustion. “Well it was nice to meet you,” said the man sitting next to me. I’m almost sure he said his name was Bob, but I don’t want to assume, “and I’ll certainly look for you on the television next time I’m flipping channels.” Thank you, I said, but I thought to myself, no you won’t. This was small talk on a six hour flight that I slept through most of. What do you do for a living, you asked me, and I told you I was a wrestler. Immediately you went into a dissertation of all the guys you watched when you were growing up, and the only name I recognized was Mickey Benedict. Considering how much of a history junkie I am, that surprised even me. “So who’s coming to meet you?” asked Bob, as he busily put his belongings back into his carry – on bag. I don’t think anyone is, I said, My girlfriend is working and the rest of my friends either live upstate or don’t have a car. “That’s a shame,” said Bob, “My wife meets me at the airport every time I come home.” He patted his knuckles against my chest. “There’s nothing in the world that beats that,” said Bob. There wasn’t really anything to say in response, so I just smiled and nodded. The plane landed without incident, and the people all around me gathered their things mostly wordlessly, and we exited the plane. I waited my turn and let most of the people around me go first; my friends are all night owls so it didn’t really matter what time I got back to the city. I looked at my watch – it was ten twenty nine exactly as I stepped off the plane and weaved my way through the crowd of people and plugged my iPod into my ears. Complicated crush of mine, esoteric valentine. Sirsy refilled my mind, picking up right where I left off. Oh, how you make me… Crazy… Oh, how you make me… Crazy… I made my way to the baggage claim, keeping my head down. It’s a rare gift, I think, to be on television and command the attention of thousands of people, but manage to be invisible when I want to be. “Excuse me, Mr. Impulse?” Maybe I’m not as invisible as I thought. I looked to my left at a little boy of maybe eight. Yessir, I said, with a smile on my face. “Mr. Impulse,” said the boy, “Can I have your autograph?” Sure, I said, what’s your name? “Jeremy,” said the boy. I took the piece of paper from him and wrote ‘Jeremy, be the message. Randall “Impulse” Knox’ and handed it back to him. Here you go, my friend, I said to him. “Could I trouble you for a picture?” asked a woman that I assumed was his mother. Of course, I said. I crouched down next to my new friend Jeremy and put an arm around his shoulders and held up my hand with the stereotypical rock ‘n roll horns, but she didn’t complain. “One… two… three!” said the woman, as she snapped the picture, “Thank you, Mr. Impulse,” she said. Randall, I told her, Randall Knox, and I shook her hand. It was nice to meet you both, I said. My flight’s bags had just started coming out of the feeder, and I kept an eye on my new friends as they walked out of sight. Cliché though it is, I love getting to meet fans. It proves to me that my message isn’t falling on deaf ears – that there is a place for decent people in professional wrestling. C… O… N-T-I-N-E… N… T-A-L And with that, my shuffle switched to ‘Continental’ by Queen V. Wanna ride and make you slide, get along without your pride. What do you think that you’d decide when I come around, when I come around? Bags in hand, I left the terminal without any subsequent delays. I thought a few people looked at me like they recognized me, but none of them said a thing, and none of them approached me so it could’ve all been in my mind. A ton of yellow cabs were waiting outside, like vultures. Then again, it’s not like there’s anything wrong with waiting where the fares were. I just don’t like getting descended on by six different guys looking to drive me back to the city. As always, I picked the guy hanging out leaning on the hood of the car. This is the guy who isn’t gonna beg for a fare. “Where to, buddy?” asked the cabdriver, as he opened the door for me. Manhattan, I said, Washington Heights, Dyckman Street. Thanks. We sped off as the song faded out. My name is Randall Knox. My friends call me Knox, and my girlfriend calls me RK. Most of you have known me as Impulse. This is my story. When I was a kid, my father and I had pretty much nothing in common, except professional wrestling. He was always working, I was always skating. Privately, I thought he must be a miserable man, since his life revolved around working, coming home, and watching television. Privately, I always assumed he saw me as a slacker, a skateboarder who could’ve done better in school if I’d just applied myself. That’s how we got through our family life until I was about fourteen, as he slowly detached himself from Mom and me. Except for wrestling. We would go to every event that would ever come to Madison Square Garden. It was kind of rough on the household budget because of the sheer number of events that were rolling through by the millennium, but he always made sure to get us into every event we could. We would endlessly debate over who was the better wrestler: he liked the Windhams, and I insisted on Mike Randalls. That’s beside the point. The most significant wrestling show I ever saw as a kid was at a dingy little gym in the South Bronx, a gym that no longer exists, across the street from an auto shop that closed down after a gruesome murder that nearly broke my heart. An old man named Terence Cooper, who was probably one of the last true Hookers in professional wrestling, had been putting on monthly shows under the moniker ‘Independent Wrestling of the Bronx’ or IWB, for probably close to fifty years. Coop, as everyone called him, used the monthly show to let the kids under his wing show their stuff. He taught hundreds of people the ins and outs of professional wrestling over the years, and while most of them never amounted to anything, status – wise, over and over again they would return to Coop’s and tell him that his training and his regimen gave them the focus and discipline to succeed wherever they ended up. On this particular night, the most famous wrestler that Coop ever trained was wrestling in the main event. I was to learn that Eli Flair, at the time, the CSWA World Heavyweight Champion, was teaming with the best wrestler you’ve never heard of, Johnny Fizzbin, against a newly created team of Craig Miles and Eddie Mayfield. Two years later, Craig and Eddie would redefine tag team wrestling forever as the Professionals, and start up a small, regional promotion called New Frontier Wrestling, but at the moment they were just a pair of old school geniuses who owed Miss Ivy a favor. That’s Ivy McGinnis, also known as Poison Ivy. But these were a bunch of big time wrestlers who had come to this little gym in the Bronx for – I assumed, and later learned was actually true – the love of the sport. Immediately, I decided that I wanted to be a professional wrestler. My dad told me that it was a foolish pipe dream, and that I was wasting my time, but I somehow found the guts to wait for Ivy McGinnis after the show and tell her what I wanted to do. She listened to me and was awesome and encouraging and told me that if I was serious, I should talk to Terry Cooper. I did, despite my dad’s insistence that I focus on my studies and enter the business world. And my dad and I started to drift apart. I’m not naïve enough to draw a correlation, but less than six months later he had moved out. Home for me was currently a two bedroom apartment in Washington Heights in Manhattan, essentially as far north as one could live while still saying ‘I live in Manhattan.’ It wasn’t too bad, the 1 train was literally a block away, and it could take me downtown in twenty minutes or into the Bronx in less than ten. Which is where I was headed, of course. I stopped home pretty much solely to drop off my bags and see if my roommate was around. The door was double locked and the lights were out, so it was obvious to me where he’d gone to. Three stops later on the 1, and I was walking down the street toward TC’s Pub, where, years ago, I learned my trade. And there was a mass of people out front. TC’s had an enclosed, open – air area behind the actual building so anyone who needed a nicotine fix could just walk outside and not have to leave their drink behind. If this many people are out front, inside must be crazy. Next phase, next craze, next nothing new. Got the pretty boy beat him up black and blue. If I didn’t already know that my girlfriend Rosie was working tonight, the fact that the jukebox was playing The Birthday Massacre would’ve told me. A big guy with a shaved head was working the door; I didn’t recognize him but he knew who I was, apparently, as he let me in without even asking if I had ID. Broke the sissy boy’s teeny toy heart in two, turned him into a video kid like you. To the left, the bar was swarmed by people three deep, and to the right, a group of people were breaking down gig equipment on the makeshift stage. I was able to catch a glimpse of the name on the bass drum - All Points West. Apparently, these guys can draw. “Knox, you crazy mother!” yelled a familiar voice, and I turned around to see my roommate, Luis, with a pint of ice cold beer in each hand. He handed one to me and hugged me tightly, spilling half his drink in the process, “When did you get in?” he asked me. Hey Lou, I said, what’s been happening? Lou was actually Luis Gonzales, my best friend and one – time tag team partner back when this was a gym. He was built like a wrestler, but it was never something he wanted to do for a living. We trained together in high school and, when I was home, through college, and we actually wrestled each other in the dark match at FWO’s Countdown 1, 2003 at Miss Ivy’s request. Seventeen years old and opening up one of the flagship promotion’s final event of the year. Unbelievable. “You missed a really great gig,” said Lou, “Place has been jam packed all night.” I can see that, I said, as I tried to look over the crowd at the bar. There were three bartenders: there have never been three people serving drinks here in the three years that this place has been a bar. Leaning as far as I could into the crowd, I saw Loriann, the second most senior employee after Rose, pouring a pair of bottles simultaneously into two separate glasses. I saw Jeff, typically the cook, restocking bottles of Dos Equis in the cooler. The other two girls, I didn’t recognize. Since Rosie wasn’t back there, I asked a stupid question. “Sally took her outside,” said Lou, his demeanor suddenly serious, “She was having trouble breathing.” Great, I said. Just great. At least there was a fairly clear path to the back door; and it was suddenly clear why everyone was out in front with cigarettes in their hands. “Hey Knox,” said Sally, sitting on a bench on the far end of the mini courtyard, “Whoever said that drunks with smokes won’t listen to reason never cleared out the back of TC’s in about six seconds.” Sally was actually Salida Arroyo, Lou’s girlfriend and Rosie’s best friend. She looked like she had gone all out for tonight – red leather skirt and black top - APW was one of her favorite local groups, which made her presence outside all the more significant. Rose had been sitting with her elbows on her knees and her head down until Sally’s greeting, at which point, she looked up at me, and her eyes lit up. She was working, not partying, so she was not dressed nearly as ‘up’ as Sally, but I have to tell you , she’s the most beautiful sight these tired eyes have ever seen. “Hey, RK,” said Rose, as she stood up and practically jumped into my arms, “I missed you.” I missed you too, I said. Rose was actually Rosalyn Callasantos, my girlfriend and the head bartender – slash – manager of TC’s Pub. She had her reddish brown hair tucked neatly behind her ears, and wore a simple sleeveless black tank top, cutoff jean shorts, and old, comfortable sneakers; as opposed to nearly every other girl in the bar who was just as dressed up as Sally. Speaking of Sally, she took the hint and left us alone. So what happened, I asked. “Nothing serious,” said Rose, “It just got way too hot and stuffy inside, even with the doors open I couldn’t breathe. Sally kicked all the smokers out of the back here and I’ve just been trying to get my wind back.” Rosie has horrendous asthma, exacerbated by smoke damaged lungs from a house fire when she was four or five years old. She also trained in the gym as a wrestler and has all the knowledge and the raw talent to have succeeded at it, but it was never going to be her career as she can’t pull enough air into her lungs. It’s just as well, I later found out that she never had any real interest in the sport, she was just trying to spend time with me. We’ve been together for about two years now, but if she’d spoken up – —or if I’d spoken up— —it probably would’ve been at least four. I had been training for professional wrestling for about a year when I started high school. That might seem like an excessively young age, but Coop didn’t put anyone in the ring before they were sixteen. He had us train with weights, swimming, running – basically anything to solidify muscle and build stamina. Most of the kids he took in – he never charged anyone – gave up after a few weeks because they weren’t, as they put it, kickin’ ass and takin’ names. I stayed because I enjoyed the workouts. I stayed because I enjoyed the atmosphere. Most of all, I stayed because I could soak up sixty eight years of wrestling knowledge from Coop. I could listen to him for hours, telling stories of the old days when the NWA and CWL ruled; about the time he actually wrestled to a one hour draw against the legendary Lou Thesz; about the six months he spent learning how to hook from ‘Golden’ Bobby Miller, who learned from Ed Lewis. I think that’s why everyone ‘adopted’ me, so to speak. Coop ended up being more of a father to me than the man who tried to pass himself off as the real article, and the other kids, the cast of characters who were constantly around the gym were the brothers and sisters I never had. When I was sixteen, I got my first taste of in – ring training, at the direction of Coop, Johnny Fizzbin, the greatest wrestler you’ve never heard of, and of Eli Flair, who had come back in to rehab an injury and figure out the next phase of his career. It was these three men who would teach me everything I know: but the most important things they taught me were how to listen, and how to have the right perspective. Several months ago, I entered the 2009 TEAM Invitational Tournament, and my first round opponent was a man named DC Stark. I didn’t know anything about him going into the tournament, and I gave him what I thought was his due respect. Almost immediately, his focus went to the fact that I wasn’t talking about how awesome I was, I wasn’t telling him how much better than him I was, and I wouldn’t disrespect his wrestling ability. Instead, I picked apart his own arguments time and time again, and by match time, had him so turned around with my non – aggressive mental maneuvering that he was reacting to just about every move I made, whether they pertained to him or not. DC Stark had put all of his ability into one basket, while I talked about how I was going to wrestle my hardest, but I knew for a fact that a victory was not assured. Of course, I always believe I’m going to win, I’m just not foolish enough to brag about it.  This time, I won. I had the right perspective, and, following that moment, I realized that it was the most significant thing I had ever learned. It’s pretty incredible how I can be a twenty three year old kid, holding the second most important title belt in the greatest wrestling promotion currently operating, and actually have the right perspective on the business. I once read an interview with one of the old school boys, whose name I’ve tragically forgotten, who said that you can’t be a big star in a town and be a local, because you live there. I don’t know if that’s true, but I don’t ever want to leave my neighborhood: not only does everyone who matters to me live there, but as a local, I know I’ll be treated like a person. My neighborhood is just as important to who I am as my friends – it keeps the right perspective. Fortunately, I got to the bar at the absolute peak of business. All Points West had finished their gig literally five minutes before, and the bar had gotten mobbed. Rosie and I hung out outside for about five minutes and she felt good enough to go back to work, and by that point the rush had become manageable. “Thanks for your help,” said Rosie to one of the bartenders and hugged her, “I won’t forget this.” “Don’t worry about it,” said the bartender. She was a young looking blonde girl who was far too dressed up to work here. I took my usual seat off to the side, and nodded at her as she left. “That’s Valerie,” said Rosie, “She’s only been working here for two, three weeks now, and she came by for the APW gig and actually jumped behind the bar without any kind of prompting because she saw we were getting slammed.” It looked pretty bad, I said. “You don’t know the half of it,” said Rosie, “I had the kitchen closed tonight because I knew that was gonna be more than any of us could handle, and even though Jeff was back there just doing inventory and general maintenance, I had to ask him to bar back. Miss Ivy didn’t get the backup PBR kegs where we needed them downstairs so I spent a good five minutes looking.” PBR, I said, I still can’t believe it. Rosie, still serving drinks, winked at me. “Miss Ivy knows her patrons,” she said, “We get everyone who doesn’t want to spend a half hour riding a subway or a cab to get downtown and spend thirty bucks on a drink, and now that school is back in session we get a ton of college and grad students who can’t afford the big drinks. PBR is perfect.” “Speaking of which, I’ll take a refill,” said Lou, sitting next to me. Sally, sitting on the other side, smacked him in the arm. “It’s her job,” said Lou, as Rose refilled his pint glass, all the while giving him a non – threatening evil eye. Rosie took a minute to say goodbye to a few regulars, and whispered something to the other remaining bartender, Loriann, before she snuck under the employees’ entrance and sat next to me. “Break time,” said Rosie, as she grabbed me around the waist and squeezed, “It’s been a long night. And I won’t stop licking my Astropop ‘till my tongue and teeth turn blue, and I continue to smile proudly. Well, that’s certainly enough to kick up your energy, I said, Lourds could power Tokyo if you hooked her up to a generator. I plant a kiss upon your mouth again ‘till your lips are sticky too, and we can stick together loudly … Rosie laughed. “Sarah was in here the other night, actually. She said to say hi when you get home.” Her head found its way to my shoulder, “Speaking of… how long do I get you for this time?” Two glorious weeks, I said, I’ve got a few shows in that time but the farthest away is Hartford so it’s going to be a series of day trips, besides, I’d like to actually see mom for a bit this time. My mother had moved to Williamsburg about a year ago. For anyone unsure about the layout of New York, we were about an hour and a half apart by subway – if we were lucky. Since she pretty much single handedly raised me – along with the guys and girls at Coop’s – since I was fourteen, I was pretty close with her and Rosie and I always did our best to visit her when I was in town – and Rose did so anyways, when I wasn’t. “No doubt,” said Lou. He and Sally were both enamored with my mother as well, she had earned the nickname ‘Ma Knox’ among my friends back in high school. “For now, though,” said Lou, “tonight is about the man bein’ back in town!” He raised his hand and I slapped it, but there wasn’t much motivation behind it. Rosie kissed me on the cheek and went back to work, while Lou and Sally and myself took a table close to the bar. “So, talk to me, sir,” said Lou, “Tell me ‘bout this last trip. Who’s up, who’s down, who’s messing up and who’s holding it together?” I laughed. Lou always enjoyed the locker room gossip, but for the past six months or so, I’ve been separating myself from it as best I can, since in my experience, nothing good can come from gossiping like a bunch of hit men in dapper suits. Not much to tell, I said, me and PC have been tearing it up on the houses, getting ready for my match with Castor at Crash. Joe’s still giving Mayfield migraines, but he’s drawing a ton of people, he’s always in condition to work, and his more colorful moments have never cost the NFW time or money. He doesn’t much like Joe with the big belt but he’s not arguing the results. “Still giving you problems?” asked Lou. Occasionally, I admitted, but he won’t do anything about it. With the way he’s keeping the feds off his back by talking about cracking down on the drug use and keeping the company full of integrity, there’s no way he’ll jeopardize one of the few guys on his roster that’s clearly not doing anything stupid, even if it means putting up with the fact that I don’t toe the company line, and speak my mind about everything that I think is wrong with it. “What about Xander?” asked Sally, “My sister still wants you to hook her up.” Xander Scott was my tag team partner in the Fans Wrestling Organization, and he and I had very little in common. Essentially, he’s a typical nineteen year old whose first break in the wrestling business is a company as high profile as the FWO, and even though his success so far has been middling, he’s got an ego the size of Gibraltar. Not that my FWO career, to date, has been anything to write home about, but of the two of us, I’m the one who understands that this business is a marathon, not a sprint. He’s actually hitting up two of the indy shows I’m doing next week, I said, so if you guys and Mia want to come, just let me know. I tuned out for a few, as Lou and Sally started talking to each other about their work schedules next week, whether or not Sally’s parents would let her (seventeen year old) sister come out on a school night, and whether they’d really be able to stop her if they wanted to. This is the chatter I’ve missed. This is reality, not a string of hotel rooms and six hour drives, pissing contests with Locker Room Prima Donnas who think they’re owed something more than an opportunity. A quiet moment with my girl, or my best friends arguing over minutiae of their family life may be boring to anyone else, but it’s what keeps me grounded when I’m here in the real world. In the two years that I’ve been wrestling professionally full time, I’ve publicly and privately rejected the notion that professional wrestlers are celebrities. Getting cheered or booed, or slapping hands or giving the fans a show while we’re in the arena, that’s just part of the job. Living the gimmick, setting hotel rooms on fire, slapping flight attendants… that’s not part of the game. To that end, I don’t give interviews. I don’t like photo shoots. I don’t throw my weight around the locker room. I know, at a buck eighty eight, there’s not much to throw. Beyond that, I will sign anything for anyone, I will take pictures with people at almost any time – I love and treasure individuals and for the most part, can’t stand the groups they belong to. That’s why I never liked when Lou called me the man, even though I know he didn’t mean what I took it as by it. I’m not the man, I’m a professional who does his job and does it well and doesn’t need an attaboy to do it just as well the next night. In New Frontier Wrestling, known pretty much as the most spaced – out promotion currently running, and I’ve remained one of the few spots on the card that have the lowest CGI component and highest wrestling content – but they also have some of the highest ratings. That’s how I roll. I don’t tell the fans what I’m going to do; I just go out and do it. When you talk about it, you run the risk of overstepping your ability and having to backtrack in front of a live audience. The key to being memorable is to always move forward, and you can’t do that if you have to backtrack. Now, I may be the single greatest pure wrestler currently in the wrestling business, but that doesn’t always translate to a victory. But, at least I have the proper perspective to take the wins and losses in stride, learn from them, and always move forward. It helps, that my ego and my sense of self – worth are not tied to whether or not I won my last match, nor are they directly proportional to how many people in the room know who I am. I leave that for the people in this business who need to be controversial to be over, because it’s their only trick. Look around your own locker room and you’ll see what I mean. Here’s a hint, though – if you can’t pick out who the king of the cheap heat may be, try looking in the mirror. At the appropriate minute, the lights flicked on. Well the clock says it’s time to close… now. I love my girl, I really do. “How ‘bout one for the road, honey?” asked a guy who looked about my age, who flanked Loriann at the bar with two of his friends. “That’s last call,” said Loriann, “What can I get you guys?” She leaned on the bar in a flirtatious manner. L.A. was a master at reeling in her customers. “Three shots of Jager,” said the head guy. His buddies all nodded and leered, “and one for you, darlin’.” Loriann moved like a ball of greased energy, pulled four shot glasses out of the shelf underneath the bar with one hand, and the bottle of Jagermeister from the cooler with the other. Take a guess. “Okay,” said one of the guys on the side, “and now I’ll have—“ “No,” interrupted Rosie, “Last call means last call, you’re done.” Loriann wasn’t about to try and override Rosie; she was the boss, so all she could do was shrug. “Sorry sweetie,” she said, “come see me tomorrow?” “No,” said the dude, “I want another drink now, and this bitch,” he gestured to Rosie. Mistake. “—ain’t gonna tell me otherwise. Where’s the manager?” “That’s me,” said Rose, “and you boys’ve had enough.” The leader laughed again. I don’t like to get involved because it impedes Rosie’s authority as the woman in charge here, but if I am here and someone implies a threat, I’m all on it. But every now and then… “What are you gonna do about it?” asked the leader. I’d gotten up in his face, though I had to look up since he was a good three or four inches taller. Coop taught me how to hook, I think, because out of everyone who he was training at the time, I was the only one with both the ability and the insatiable need to learn everything he had to teach. But when Coop taught me all the hooks he knew, his big mistake, I think, was doing it when I was only sixteen. What sixteen year old do you know that can be trusted with the technical knowledge of how to break bones? Coop obviously never watched Jackass. No, seriously, I was probably the most responsible person he could’ve taught, and I had no real reason to fight with anyone I knew. I was that guy that went to your high school that somehow managed to have his hand in every major clique, the respect of all the recognized beautiful people, and the knowledge of everyone’s first and last name, all the while having only a small handful of truly close friends. I never had reason to fight with anyone, and nobody ever gave me a reason – either by action or omission – for us to get into it. Except one time. Junior year, mid – terms. Me and Lou were leaving one ridiculous math class or another, and I heard something from the stairway. We went in and up a level, and saw this skinny freshman girl cornered by two guys from the basketball team. They had taken her bag away from her, and were shoving her between them, making inappropriate grabs, and so forth. In hindsight, and with the advantage of time, I don’t think they were going to do anything to that girl other than freak her out and let her go, but both then and now, I didn’t think it was right to take the chance. So I walked up to them and asked them what was going on. I’ve forgotten their names, but one said to me, nothing, Knox, just having some fun. So I asked the girl if she was having fun, and she didn’t say anything. Silence speaks louder sometimes, and I told the two guys to let her be. They were both six foot something and towered over me, but I wasn’t afraid of them. The worst they could do to me was beat me up; the worst they could’ve done to that girl was… a lot worse. One shoved me, and I repeated myself. Shove number two was strike three, and I grabbed his wrist with both my hands and jammed it home, just like Coop taught me. I don’t know how many ligaments I ripped, but I was suspended from school for a week. Five days might not seem like much, but it was just long enough for the story (surprisingly, the truth) to spread around school, and a phone call from the freshman girl’s mother to my mom about the true nature of the incident pretty much made me untouchable for life. Except with Coop. He never ever yelled at me, but that was the closest I think he’d ever come in the five years I knew him. I can understand his point – some kid messes up another kid’s wrist and the parents come down in droves, where are these kids learning this stuff? He could’ve been shut down, investigated, run out of the wrestling business and the neighborhood, all for the crime of giving the neighborhood kids a place to go other than a street corner. I would never have done anything to jeopardize Coop’s operation, so that remains the only fight I’ve ever been in at school, though I don’t know that it’s much of a fight. That skinny Freshman? Her name was Rosalyn Callasantos, and we became fast friends after that. Five years later, we became something more. I never sleep as well anywhere as I do at home. That’s not a unique position, of course, it’s the reason that so many of us end up on downers to sleep and uppers to wake up. I’m assuming, at least. Other than drinks at the end of the night, and the extremely rare smoke when I’m home with my friends, I don’t touch anything mind – or mood – altering. Except Rosie, but you don’t need to hear about that. She’s currently sound asleep next to me, in a black wife beater and a pair of my boxers. As she and Loriann closed the bar she was telling me how she was at the bar at two in the afternoon to receive a pair of shipments; then she opened the bar at four PM, worked a twelve hour shift, closed the bar at 4 AM, and then cleaned up. It’s seven fifteen in the morning and we got back to my place about an hour and a half ago. But I digress. The strongest temptation on the road, as far as I’ve been able to tell, is getting to the next show. We kill ourselves in the ring every night, and if we’re not, we’re stealing from the fans who paid to see us. I’ve avoided the chemical enhancements for one simple reason: it’s a short trip from just a few to wake up or go to sleep to just a few to get through the day. To me, it’s not worth it. So I suffer the road, but I also love it. Every ache, every scrape, every bruise is a sign that I’m still alive and still a feeling human being. Based on the stories the veterans have been telling me, this is a feeling I have to cherish as long as I can. And I do. I eased myself out of bed and out of the room, and made a beeline to the left to the kitchen. We got a coffee pot a few weeks ago that I haven’t had a chance to use yet, and since I’m jet – lagged and my mind is racing, why not? Yes, caffeine is a drug. Shut up. Since I was thirteen – ten years ago, all I’ve wanted to do was be a wrestler. I trained my body and my mind, and worked as hard as I could to achieve my goals. Then I got there, and found out that the show is precisely not what I thought it would be. Eli and Miss Ivy, and Coop, and Fizzy, they all taught me how to wrestle, and how to work with an opponent’s strength and around their weaknesses… but they never taught me how to deal with the politics in the locker room. They never taught me how to deal with the grind of the constant travel, and the endless hours of nothing that can crush you harder than the rigors of wrestling. In hindsight, it’s probably better that they didn’t. Sure, I had some rude awakenings when I first took people at their word, but I’m glad I didn’t come in like I knew everything. That would’ve been a disaster. But it’s true what they say – this business gets in your blood and you can’t get away from it. That’s why I’ve been wrestling full time for two separate promotions. That’s why I’ve been taking these one shot deals from the TEAM organization and the Dual Halo in PRIME. That’s why I sent in a tape to the GTT committee. Some of my detractors would say I’ve been wrestling so much to get away from Rosie, but if that was true, I wouldn’t have spent the past year trying to convince her to come on tour with me. She’ll come out on occasion for the weekday shots, but that’s all. “I have a job and a life in New York,” Rosie would always say, “and I’m a simple girl. I don’t think I’d do well in the fast lanes of professional wrestling.” There is logic to her words. Rosie has lived her entire life in New York City, but as I learned a year and a half ago at NFW’s Wrestlestock II event, she’s just a dirty, dirty hippie at heart. I just miss her when I’m on the road, and her presence would both be a boon for business, and would do well to keep me grounded. It’s an age – old paradox. When you’re on the road you end up a certain person, and when you come home you make a decision that the tour is over, and it’s time to be who you really are again. The problem, especially when it comes to the guys who are my age, is that the longer we’re on the road, the harder it is to determine who that is. When I look at the rest of the GTT field, I see a slew of men and women who have lost themselves completely, exemplified by the fact that the schedule has given me a Machiavellian Mastermind in the first round. Whatever he thinks he is in his personal life, once we’re in the ring we’re both wrestlers with a 0-0 record, and I don’t fear any wrestler. My coffee finished, I took my cup back into the bedroom, taking great care not to wake my girl. She looks like an angel right now, peaceful and innocent, and I know for a fact that the path that led me here was the right one. All I know about the future is, wherever my path takes me, I want Rosie by my side. My name is Randall Knox. My friends call me Knox, my girl calls me RK. Most of you know me as Impulse. This is my story.
