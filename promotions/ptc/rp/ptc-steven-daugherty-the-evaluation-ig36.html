'Soccer Style' Steven Daugherty - The Evaluation: A Placekicker's View of the World ‘Soccer Style’ Steven Daugherty The Evaluation: A Placekicker’s View of the World There’s a faint click as the door swings open and the man in the orange jumpsuit shuffles towards the only chair in the room. He sits himself down roughly and looks up to the man with the clipboard, who fixes him with a penetrating gaze. “Mr. Daugherty, glad you could make it. I assume you understand the reason for this evaluation?” He nods, deliberately failing to meet the eyes of his inquisitor. His mind is already on other things. Only six seconds left, and all Akron has to do is watch the kicker miss… A smile unconsciously forms on his face. The mandated therapist continues. “Good. Now, we want to get a tight grasp of your mental stability before we release you back into society. So I’m going to ask you some questions, and I promise this process will go a lot easier if you answer the questions truthfully and completely. Are you ready to go?” Steven Daugherty gives a perfunctory nod, his eyes still on the cracked cement floor. Fifty-nine yards, that’s a toughie for any kicker, especially in the wind. Plus this guy’s a freshman, he hasn’t made one over fifty-two all season… His hands grip the edge of the chair. “How old are you?” Finally, Daugherty looks up at his questioner, that dazed smile still on his face. “24.” I don’t even know why they’d cart this guy out here just so he can embarrass himself. I know this is their only chance to win, but come on! This kick is hard for a professional to make, how do they expect a man so young to come even close? I’d hate to be this Steven Daugherty now. “Okay. Now, tell me a few things about you prior to your time here. You were in college, right? Where did you go?” “San Diego State.” Here’s the snap, and Daugherty puts the boot to it…it’s up… The therapist attempts an understanding smile. It fails. “And was your college experience fun? Did you have a lot of good memories?” My God! It’s good! Daugherty did it! Daugherty won the game for SDSU! I can’t believe it! The crooked smile on the man in the orange jumpsuit grows wider. “I can think of one.” ----------------- It wasn’t even an important game. We had already missed a shot at a bowl game that year. So making that kick was essentially for nothing. But man, did it feel so good. For those few seconds, I was a hero. I was tackled to the ground by the middle linebacker, and then everyone else jumped on top of the pile. It was hard to breathe. Still, it was hard to get over how good I felt being the hero. The rest of the world didn’t have such a hard time. The next week I missed two field goals, both under 40 yards. Some frat boys made a website about cutting me from the team. The rest of the season passed, and so did the three after that. No more game winners, no more group tackles. No more hero. They loved me for those few seconds, and then they moved on. Maybe one day I will too. ----------------- “Before you came here, what was your life like? Did you have a girlfriend?” Steven Daugherty nods. “A wife, actually. We married as soon as we graduated from college.” The therapist forces a smile. “That’s nice. Have you kept contact with her?” Steven? Steven, please talk to me. I miss you, and I don’t believe you did what they’re saying you did. You’ve got to tell them you’re innocent, baby. “A little.” And that brings a frown to the therapist’s face. “Mhm. Tell me, what do you think of when you think of your wife?” I’ll wait for you, Steven. I’ll spend every day waiting for you to come home. Two years is nothing when you’ve got a love like ours. And now it is he who forces a smile. “I don’t know. I guess normal thoughts.” “We don’t define things as normal, Steven, because normal is different for everyone. What are the thoughts specifically?” Steven brings those thoughts to the surface. I’m sorry, Steven. It just got too hard. You’re gone, and I just…I’m so sorry. And shrugs. “I don’t know. Angry?” ----------------- It’s easy to be angry in my position. Kickers are the most disrespected professionals in the world. Worse than garbage men. At least with them there’s no set standard that not meeting will automatically render you vulnerable. And maybe that sort of bleeds into your personal life. I don’t know. I tried to get on to a few NFL teams. Accepted a few invites to training camps. I’m sure I was some fifty word blurb in Miami’s newspaper when they signed me to make their starter work harder. Same with the other stops I made. None of it worked out, because stability is just something that doesn’t come with the territory. If you’re good at your job, you have something called job security. You know that you’re allowed a little leeway so you can make some minor mistakes, as humans tend to do, and not have it ruin anything. There is no such thing as job security in my profession. So yeah, it’s easy to be angry. ----------------- The therapist is nodding, for the first time looking quite interested in his subject. “Do you get angry a lot, Mr. Daugherty?” Once again avoiding eye contact, Steven flickers his eyes from side to side before answering, “I…I don’t know.” “Well, do you think your anger is what drove you to the incident that put you here?” Hey, kicker, you have the wrong locker. Yours is in the corner, with the rest of the soon-to-be-cut kids. This part of the locker room is for real athletes only. “I guess so. I don’t really think about that incident too much.” Did you not hear me, kicker? Or was your education as meaningless as your position? The therapist sighs, catching the lie but not pursuing it. “Well, in order to qualify you as suitable to leave our care, I need to be sure that you do think about your incident, that you have come to terms with it and moved on.” “I guess I have.” For the last time, g-ugh! The therapist notices that Steven is wincing and pushes forward. “Are you sure? Are you sure that you’re not bothered by what happened at all? Steven Daugherty looks up suddenly with another crooked smile, his teeth shining as he says, “Why should I be? I’m the one who won, right?” ----------------- Won the trip into this hellhole. I just couldn’t take it. The constant degradation. The stereotyping. The constant badgering and questioning my physical and mental abilities. I’m not a real athlete? Then why did a kick to your face put you in a coma, star receiver? Tell me that one. It got me here, but on some levels I am glad it happened. It was my answer to the constant disrespect, the terrible treatment I got every day since I decided to drop soccer and lace up the football cleats. And you know something? It felt real good. Almost as good as that game winner. I kind of felt like a hero again. ----------------- “What are you planning on doing once you are free? I know some avenues of your profession are somewhat…closed, but maybe there are other promotions…” Daugherty interrupts with a timid voice, wanting to get his point across but almost afraid to say it. “Actually, I was thinking about something more…physical.” The smile returns to the man’s face, and the therapist masks his displeasure at the smile. “I’m assuming you’re referring to the ‘friendly match’ you and a fellow inmate who was a college wrestler had a few months ago?” I got ten bucks on Baczkowski, he wrestled II-A. What did this guy do, kick? Yeah right. This’ll last five minutes. Steven Daugherty nods. “Yeah. It was…it was fun.” Oh shit! Did that see that kick? I’ve changed my mind, I don’t know about this one anymore… “Fun isn’t the word I’d use to describe it,” the therapist says in a condescending tone. “I understand it was that incident that kept you in here a few months longer than you were supposed to be in.” He shrugs. “What’s a few more months?” Is…is Baczkowski moving? Hey, he better not be dead! That fucker owed me money! HEY KICKER, IF YOU KILLED HIM YOU OWE ME THE FIFTY BUCKS HE OWES ME! “It’s also the reason they’ve sent me here to evaluate you. They want to make sure you don’t hurt anybody anymore. They don’t want to let you free on some wrestling promotion or ultimate fighting just so you can paralyze a few more people.” Again he is met with a shrug. “I’ll be sure to take it easy on the guys I face.” ----------------- Just like we don’t get a lot of respect, kickers don’t get a lot of power, either. One time I saw the quarterback for my college team make the coach release a running back who was having sex with his girlfriend. And this was college. You think a kicker could make someone even modify the depth chart, let alone get a team member axed? But there was a lot of power after that little sparring session. And respect followed. They gave me an extended sentence, but all of the guys who sneered at me before were nodding. Getting me stuff from the outside. I smoked my first cigarette in years. And it was a pretty powerful feeling. After years of the disrespect, years of the neglect, my leg has finally given me something I’ve never had. And if using it gets a few unlucky punks knocked unconscious, I’m sure that’s something I can deal with. ----------------- “Do you know what parole is, Mr. Daugherty?” The kicker is past answering questions at this point, so he just fixes the therapist with a glare to make him move on. “Well, you’ll have to complete four months of parole, and you’ll be required to get a job, and contact Benny Covington, your parole officer, every week. Most importantly, you have to stay away from hurting people.” Steven Daugherty sighs loudly at this point, and the therapist nervously continues. “Now, we can’t tell you you’re not allowed to pursue your little dream of wrestling, but if you do anything there that violates your parole, you’ll be back in here for an extended sentence. Do you understand?” Knowing the end is near, he nods. “Yes sir. I understand clearly.” “Then leave the room, collect your papers, and good luck.” The therapist leaves without saying anything further, and the man in the orange jumpsuit smiles, stands, and shuffles towards a new life. A new power. A new respect. A new Steven Daugherty.
