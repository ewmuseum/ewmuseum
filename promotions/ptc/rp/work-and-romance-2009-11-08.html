Work and Romance

8 November 2009
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2009-11-08 LA; October One minute you have nothing to do. The next: you have too much. That was only one line among over a thousand and one sentences in a journal, somewhere, that Buck kept on account of his idea of himself as an artist, observer, recorder of thoughts, expresser of emotions: curator of the human condition. He hoped that some day his journal would be a collector's item; something that fans perused for his insights, musings, ideas. He just wanted people to understand. He wanted them to identify. He wished he was stoic enough to rid himself of that burden: the need to be validated. He once wrote, "There's something very human about this whole process. Needing to be validated. Why is it that I think what I feel is what everyone feels?" One sentence among many and one sentence among few that expressed any doubt in his understanding of others. At some point in his life he realized that his generalizations about human nature were probably self-delusional. But maybe he was onto something. He was living it, too, self-aware but unaware of the importance of actually putting himself out there. Being fearful of failure and criticism was a precursor to inaction, and he was trying push past that. If throwing himself into any action was what it took then he would do it. And so, he was in California for a short period of time, back in a place where he once lived but never called home; away from another place the he didn't call home either. The complicating factor was that, after winning the first round of GTT, he was informed of a contractual obligation he had to fight again. He told everyone that his motivation for wrestling was exclusively financial, and that he had no choice but to continue onto the second round. Unfortunately, the minutia of his contract with PTC was unfavourable to his other pursuits in the entertainment industry. He was pitching an autobiographical screenplay to someone he knew only as The Director, with very little knowledge of how to do it or what the whole process was like. A few years ago he had failed as an actor while working in the mailroom. That was his only firsthand experience, and film school seemed like nothing more than a fuzzy memory. He was just trying to Make It. He was out for a beer with a friend, The Assistant, who was also trying to Make It. They were both on the fringes of what they saw as a business that thrived off of a homogeneous art culture. The Suits were the enemy, and the boys' In was The Director, an extraordinarily dull man who had shown interest in Buck's work. Ironically, there was a certain mystery to his lack of emotion and boring demeanour. It wasn't a facade, but he was so much a product of the system that he caricatured it. Somehow, though, he wanted to redeem himself from conformity. He had a nagging conscience that told him his current pursuits weren't enough. This is what The Assistant and Buck were trading ideas over: why would The Director want to say “fuck you” to the environment that nurtured him, that was basically tailor-made for workers like him? “After a while I think you'd just get fed up with bending to every whim, y'know. It's like the kind of kid who is raised in a prison. Eventually they just want to get out and raise hell.” Buck figured this had to be the explanation. “But why now?” The Assistant wasn't convinced. “You don't know him like I do. This guy has read thousands and thousands of scripts. Many of them, no offence, that are better than yours. If he wanted to make something artsy then he's passed up a million of 'em.” “Yeah. Doesn't seem right.” Buck took a sip of his beer. “Maybe it's because it came from you.” The Assistant set his beer down and shook his head. “I know more aspiring writers than I'd like, Buck. I think I have an eye for these kind of things, and I'm not convinced on this one. I'm trying to figure it out. Something's off.” Buck shifted in his chair, slightly uncomfortable. The Assistant was a friend – one that Buck thought he could trust, but he felt undermined. Buck had already spent a number of years being an armchair critic, and he knew what it was like to look at other writers with envy. The kind of envy that isn't admitted to: it just is. It's self-involved, but only in the way that everyone can be. In the pursuit of something more than what we already have, it's hard not to look to those who have it. It can be fame or money or power or happiness or spiritual wholeness. Anything. And Buck knew that envy wasn't just about wanting what others have. It often was transformed into feelings of negativity, channelled toward those who have achieved what he wanted to achieve. Those writers aren't artists, they're plot generators – if he said it with enough of a smirk, he might get to take a young lady with an eye for bohemian artists home. Not long ago Buck was of the mind that the merit of his work was not determined by its ability to get mainstream attention; a rejection letter did not reflect on his artwork but the state of the industry. Now that something he wrote was in the machinery, things were different. He was losing the smirk. No longer was he doing the envying. He still didn't have much to show for it. Still anxious and unprepared. Buck didn't share his feelings with The Assistant; they were about six beers deep when a girl joined their company. It was The Assistant's girlfriend: they called her Jane. At 24, Jane was a writer for a magazine on arts and culture. Her favourite author was Toni Morrison. She was an introvert who resisted the unrestrained activities of the local youth culture, preferring small lunches, readings, art galleries, and, to fulfil her duties as a journalist, the occasional red-carpet event. She covered the smaller releases, mostly, and liked talking books over coffee with her small group of friends: recent grads, most who were on the lookout for work and romance. This wasn't the first time they had met, but Buck found himself drawn to her gracefulness. She had an assuring smile and a confidence about her that Buck always admired in a person, partly because he lacked it himself. “This is Buck Fledger, a friend from school. I think you two might have met?” The Assistant introduced them. Buck sensed a tension between the two, but as his eyes darted from him to her her to him, he reminded himself not to force meaning onto other people's relationships. It was not his responsibility to make those judgements. This was an exercise in self-consciousness. “Hi, I'm Buck,” he said, nodding his head. “Jane. Nice to meet you. Again,” was her response. “What brings you to our lovely city?” “Well, I'm here because I wrote a-” “Buck makes a living as a wrestler,” The Assistant interrupted. Buck frowned. Buck looked a bit disgruntled. “It's just temporary.” Jane ordered a drink, then said, “No offence, Buck, but you sound defencive. If that's what you like doing, then why be ashamed? If not, why do it in the first place?” Buck shifted in his chair uncomfortably. He had struggled with this question ever since he graduated from high school in Toronto, and was still unsure that he even had an answer for it. He figured that there was something about wrestling that kept him coming back to it, but his lack of success caused him to conclude that he found no meaning in it. Jane was a sharp, intelligent woman – the kind of friend that Buck should want. He needed to be asked difficult questions. He needed to be challenged to explain himself to others, to justify why he did what he did. The only way he had been held accountable for his actions was with The Mom; unfortunately, her idea of male success was tainted by age and place. To her, writing wasn't a viable career option because few succeed at ever making money in it; if wrestling was what Buck was good at and could make money in, then that's just the way it has to be. Jane was different: she thought people should do what they like instead of living a lie. Buck, caught in this tangled web of social and personal expectations, found himself resorting to glib sayings like, “I'll know what I want to do when the time is right.” Jane was drinking a vodka and tonic. She shrugged, then confirmed what Buck was thinking: “I've always thought that people should do what they like, and don't compromise their principles.” Buck wished it was that easy. - Next day The Director was in his office, sitting across from someone Buck and The Assistant knew only as The Suit. His attired defied any sort of description. This was a curious meeting, as both The Director and The Suit were men of little emotion. One would be right to assume that more might get done by trading e-mails rather than being in the same room. Time was of the essence. The Suit especially felt that showing emotion was a sign of weakness; that it impeded on more pressing matters, such as setting up the right deals and making sure that his company's interests were met. His neat hair and straight-face had a terrifying quality, and even The Director, a man of simple tastes and pleasures, was shook by the cold gaze that only the board member of a multimillion dollar company could muster. The Suit: “This situation calls for a strategy to ensure that we make a timely deal and our interests are met.” He was, of course, talking about money. The Director: “Wait, are we talking about money? Because I-” The Suit put up a hand. “I know, I know.” “You know what?” “I just know. I'm glad we could come to an agreement.” The Suit got up, straightened his suit, then pulled out a contact. “Sign here.” The Director signed. “And here.” He signed again. The Director: “Does this mean we're going to make a movie?” “Not exactly,” The Suit replied. “You'll see soon. We're making the announcement tonight at The High Class Bar. You and your, er, 'team' are on the list. What's this kid's name again? Fuck Fledger?” “Buck Fledger.” “Right. Good day.” The Director sat in confusion. He had, quite clearly, been tricked into something and he wasn't quite sure what. This was very unlike him, and he knew it. His desk had a drawer that he reached into for his medication. Then he picked up the phone to make some calls. Or to look like he was. What did it matter? - That night The Hight Class Bar was a high class bar somewhere in a high class neighbourhood in Los Angeles, where everyone went for high class service and high class romance. It was one of those private establishments that required a pre-booking and a large down payment. Or so the story went. Buck, The Assistant, and Jane were all in attendance. They tried, like everyone, to give the impression that they knew what they were there for. Buck was having problems with his nerves and was dealing with it in the form of a rum and enough Coke to give it colour. He figured that asking for a cigarette from a Suit wasn't very classy. Even in his Sunday's best he felt out of place; he didn't know who anyone was or what they did, but he deluded himself into thinking that they were probably important and he should act to impress. There was nothing natural about it. He didn't know it at the time but this was just the beginning of what would be months of behaviour that didn't embody him but a version of him: a version that appealed to certain business interests. The rest of the night devolved into what was a hazy experience for Buck. He remembered some kind of announcement that involved his name, a television series, and the life of a wrestling star. He woke up in the back of a car, spinning. Unable to focus on a single spot. Jane was driving the car and The Assistant was absent. He was back with The Director and The Suit, laughing over how the so-called star of the night had drank too much, got into a fight, and blacked out before people could even congratulate him. On what? On his autobiographical screenplay being delegated from biopic to a television series that might not even happen? Even if he were sentient, Buck probably wouldn't even get it. The problem, he realized, was that he had no control over what happened to his work. Was it the same way in wrestling? He gave himself a name and a face. He was a caricature of himself and the object was to make people think that that was the real him. How could it be if he didn't even know? The next morning the back of his eyeballs hurt. He wanted a glass of water and, thankfully, there was one. He tried to figure out where he was: a hotel that looked like his, and probably was. The difference from the former nights at his hotel was one of company. There was a girl beside him, and that salient fact took a moment to register. She had the same experience, and they simultaneously jumped out of bed. Blankets and pillows everywhere. The morning after, disbelief, and just not knowing what happened or what to think. Or what to do about it. In the pursuit of work and romance, things get complicated. Desires are fulfilled at a cost. But at the time it just seems oh, so, right. Lose control over one thing and to feel in control again you ... Buck always thought of himself as someone who could resist the romance. So did Jane.
