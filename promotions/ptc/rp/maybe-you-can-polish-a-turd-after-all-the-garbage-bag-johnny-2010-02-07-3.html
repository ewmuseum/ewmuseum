Date: 2010-02-07 Maybe You Can Polish A Turd After All: The Garbage Bag Johnny Story Chapter 7: The Vortex A Garbage Bag Production "Nobody wants to read a story about a guy writing a story." -Donnie "Black Donald" Wright DECEMBER 4, 2009 I had passed out on plenty of unfamiliar floors before, but the cool marble tile at the Literary Press branch in Chicago was by far the nicest. At the time, I didn't know where I was, and when I came to, I was still drunk from the night before. I was sore all over from the position I'd slept in, so sore, in fact, that it took me such great effort to shift from that position I decided against it. The papers shuffling above me sounded like animals rustling around in tree branches, and I saw trousered stumps jutting up from within shoes polished into their reflections in the marble. There was one pair of stumps colored somewhere between blue and gray and another one was sandy tan. Both pairs were freshly pressed, and the cuffs on the first pair were cut so tall as to overdecorate the ankles. The second pair had a more modest set of cuffs that better served the drape of the trousers. I'd never seen such an immaculate drape. It was like a leggy curtain, and I was much enjoying it. Beyond the trousers and the feet standing toe to toe with each other, the marble extended off past the horizon, gliding past indoor shrubbery and post-ironic stone chiselings. The man in the bluish gray stood unflinching as the other shifted around, pivoting on his inner big toe mounds. "I've already called building security." Bluish Gray started. "They're going to be here any minute." "Call them off." Sandy Tan pleaded, pleated. "At least don't make any decisions until you've read it." "There are hundreds of popular wrestlers we can use to push autobiographies." Bluish Gray was adamant. "I'm sure we can find plenty who would take this a little more seriously. I swear, though. The next one of these morons we get is going to dictate everything he can remember into a tape recorder and let one of the pros handle the writing." "There's something here, Jerry. I'm serious." Sandy Tan moved his weight from one foot to the other. "I can't put my finger on it, but I'm sure I can thresh it out if you give me a chance." "We're not trying to win any awards with this one, Donnie." Bluish Gray tried to soften his tone as he stiffened his position. "The people who buy these things are going to be wrestling fans. They don't care if it's any good as long as it's about wrestling." "You saw what I did with the Louis Guy piece, right?" Donnie bolstered his offensive. "Doesn't that buy me a little good faith?" "Of course it does." Jerry did his best to appear gracious while maintaining a firmness. "It's just that there are a number of better projects that you could be working on next. Hell, we were supposed to have one of the interns put on this one." "Let me take it. I get that you don't want to put too much into this, but I saw him lying there with the drafts in his hand when I got in this morning, and I was a little bit curious." Donnie chose his words, reconsidered them, and replaced them with words that he thought fit better. "It may not be polished by our standards, but there's just this strange appeal to it. I looked up some videos of this guy's matches online, and if you watch real closely, you can tell this guy knows how to tell a story, even if he doesn't know his way around words so well." "If I give this turd to you, and you can't polish it, all that good faith from the Guy piece goes out the window, you hear?" Jerry sterned warningly. "You really see something in this, huh? Just how captivating can descriptions of choreographed fights be?" "He doesn't write too much about wrestling." "What?" Jerry's ire grew. "This autobiography was supposed to be about wrestling." "I suppose it is." Donnie pondered. "By process of elimination, anyway." "Christ." Jerry breathed heavier in reconsideration. "If anything goes wrong with this-" "If anything goes wrong . . ." Donnie sounded assured, beheading the oncoming threat to surgically punctuate his own conclusion. "I'll right it." "You damn well better." Jerry reinforced his authority, stamping down the last word before turning off and exiting. I groaned as Donnie flipped me onto my back, and the lights from the ceiling wrapped around him brightly, obscuring any clear shot of his face as he looked down, wondering what to do to with me. He eventually decided to grab me by my arms and slide my whimpering corpse across the smooth, marble tile, and as I was dragged past reflective surfaces like pristine metallic elevator doors and glass so clean I couldn't quite tell where the surfaces were, I saw myself being dragged, but I couldn't see his reflection dragging me. "You can't stay up there forever." -Chicago Police Officer #1 SEPTEMBER 15, 2002 In the quieter stretches of the Dakotas, I listened to the can gallop loudly against the road when I had the windows open, and given that I didn't think it proper to drive without a cigarette, it was rolled down most of the way up. The border patrolman was about the most pleasant man imaginable, and he asked if we were going to Canada for our honeymoon. I told him it was something like that, and Ash took a break from looking through her albums to take a picture of him through the window frame. "I'm glad we did this." She smiled at me from the passenger seat, and I uneasily returned it. "It's going to be some ride." We both knew from the beginning that we'd gotten hitched for all the wrong reasons. The ride was one thing, but we never loved each other. It didn't matter to neither of us any, though, because we were young and ambitious. I had heard there were some good schools up in Canada, much different from the ones in Mexico. They'd teach you how to tear off any limb you pleased, to stretch a man in ways a man wasn't meant to stretch. I'd find some honest work again until I had enough money to pay for a decent education. Ashlee had her photos to keep her busy, and when she wasn't taking them, they gave her paused, emulsified people to talk to. We didn't have too much to talk about, just didn't share too many interests is all, but I didn't mind having someone around for a while, even if it was anyone. We shared moments when she wasn't on anything and I wasn't on anything neither, and the glimpses I got of who she really was made me think that there was something better hiding beneath her frail, fracturing being, but those moments were just too few and far between. Canada was wide open country, and the provinces looked much smaller on maps. We didn't have any drugs on us when we crossed the border. We had made sure to use them all beforehand, and as they wore off, I watched the eyes of her reflection as she looked out the window, wondering what the hell she was doing all the way up there in the middle of all that rolling, beautiful space. The roads began to thin out on the way to the place where Doug was. I had been able to reach Doug through some of his contacts on the monster truck circuit. He had registered a truck called the Hoser Dozer in several competitions across Saskatchewan and its neighboring provinces. I guess there ain't nothing conspicuous about a monster truck. The roads started to lose their names and turned to dirt. By the time we found Doug's address, there wasn't even dirt, just dirtless ground. I parked the sedan outside Doug's place. It was a neat, little cabin, built like a cube with a pyramidal roof. Extra logs were strewn about the yard, trying to find uses. Some of them piled together to form a makeshift parking spot for the Dozer. The truck was growing thicker with paint jobs. Other logs waited to become important. Ashlee followed behind me as I walked towards the door, but I couldn't see anything through the windows on the way. The cabin looked dark. I knocked anyway and waited. "Maybe he's not home." Ashlee said it like we hadn't spent almost a day in a car. I had driven the whole way, refusing to pull over for sleep. I knocked again, but nothing changed, and I began to think she was right. "Fuck." I moved from the door to the window and peered in, and there were no signs of life to be found. "What the fuck do we do now?" One of Ashlee's more redeeming qualities was that she could tell when a question was rhetorical. She watched on, concerned but powerless, as I paced back and forth in front of Doug's door. I sat down with my back against the place with my head in my hands, and Ashlee sat next to me. "Cigarette." I heard her shuffling through her purse, and her hand reached between the angle of my forearms. I opened my mouth as she put one of her cigarettes in and lit it. I didn't care that we smoked different brands. I liked my smokes harsh, and she liked hers minty. She fumbled around to get the flame in position while I was sitting there, unable to look at anything, but a few puffs cleared my head while I heard her lighting one for herself. "Thanks, Ash." "Sure." She massaged my knee as I calmed myself there. Her attempts to comfort me didn't expedite the process, and maybe I didn't appreciate them as much as I should have. "Let's just wait it out, huh? We can look at one of my albums." She didn't know Doug like I did. He had a tendency to disappear for stretches and never come back. I decided that the only suitable course of action was to shatter his window so we could at least get inside. The glass was easy to kick through, but I cut myself pretty bad trying to tear away the remaining shards. As soon as we got in, I had Ashlee look around for something to bandage me up with. There wasn't much in there. There weren't even any walls inside. It was just one big room meant to serve all purposes. Doug's bed was the only thing there to sit on, so I sat there, bleeding from my palm while Ashlee wrapped toilet paper around my hand until the roll ran out. "I wonder what he did this time." I winced, trying to quell the stinging in my palm. The place was barren, and it reminded me of his house back in the states after I'd sold everything off to Randy. I laid back, and it felt nice after driving all that way without any sleep. Ashlee laid there with me, watching me fade, and for a while there, I thought everything was going to work itself out. We'd just be able to set up there and forget that anything before had ever happened. She brushed some of the hair away from my face as I lied there, and she sung something pretty to me that I didn't recognize while it was putting me to sleep. I remember the dream I had vividly because it wasn't a dream at all. It was more like a memory of something I had never thought to recall while awake; a memory of a dream, perhaps. When I was younger, I had that imaginary friend I wrote about earlier. When my mother told me he wasn't real, it traumatized me for a while until I didn't think of him no more, and in time, it didn't bother me that he'd stopped appearing. At that point, I never knew anyone that died, and I figured that's what he'd done, so I threw him a little service, and it put the thought in my head that maybe death wasn't as bad as the living made it out to be. More time passed and years went by, and I got into drugs and other stuff that'd help make me so forgetful, and eventually, I was no longer as afraid to die as I was afraid to live. The first time Doug took me out to the Americanator for a joint was about six months before Lisa kicked me out of the house for smoking. It might have been six months to the day, even. In any case, it was frightening for me as it was exciting. I knew that once I had smoked with Doug, my mind would operate in a completely different manner, and with new tunnels opened, it was a strong possibility that it would alter my functions for good. Doug's description of my experience indicated that I was croaking like a bullfrog, even after I passed out in the passenger seat of the Americanator. I woke up momentarily, and the truck had been parked. Doug was gone. "I thought you were dead." I imagined a ghost. "Bad motherfuckers don't die." He slapped me on the shoulder. "You a bad motherfucker yet?" "I don't know." I spoke slow, drooling out the words into the sponge of hazy air in the cab of the truck. "Bad motherfuckers know when they're bad motherfuckers." "Maybe." The word was so particularly hard that it came out as a sentence, the unlikely transition of grungy syllables in midsection slurred off my tongue like it was trying to climb back up. I gave up on the rest of it. If it was important, I'd remember it later. It wasn't. "You high as hell." The ghost pointed at me, smiling a wide ghost smile. "Ain't you?" "Where did you go?" "Gas 'N Shit." Monster Truck Doug returned replacing the streetwise visage, plopping two plastic bags of junk food and beer on the divider. "I picked up some energy drinks. You might want to crack one open if you're gonna stay sharp." As I heard her scream, I realized that Doug was probably just passed out high or drunk in his monster truck all along. I'd just lived in that house he abandoned for so long that it felt like I'd only ever see abandoned houses. I shot up, and even though Doug had a shotgun trained at my wife, I was relieved. I could put the endless party that celebrated nothing to rest; the party that longed for Dina. "Whoa! Doug! It's alright! It's alright!" I waved my hands in frantic surrender. "It's just me, and that's my wife, Ash! She's cool." Doug turned the gun towards me, but he lowered it quickly as his braking thoughts ground to a halt. He put his left hand on his hip and just shook his head at me for a bit, but before he said anything to me, he quickly shifted back to Ashlee. "Monster Truck Doug. Pleased to meet you." "Likewise." Ash's voice floated slowly, trying to regain its volume. Doug turned back to give me a properer welcome. "What the fuck are you doing breaking my fucking window?" He modeled the glass shards that had fallen into the cabin. "Jesus! And you got blood all over my bed, too." "It didn't look like you were here." It was almost inquisitive. Doug started pacing forward and he laughed a little bit to cool himself before he spoke too brashly. He breathed in deep and composed himself. "I was out back in the shed doing some gardening." He looked out the window through the empty pane. "You're going to pay for that window and buy me some new sheets." "Yeah. Of course." I agreed graciously at his leniency as he dragged the shotgun behind him and headed towards the back of the cabin. "Come here. I want to show you something." I looked at Ash to see where she was at in all this. "Both of you. Come on." I nodded to Ashlee and sprung from the bed. Doug unlatched the back door and pushed it open, leading us down a small hill to a wooden shed, which from the outside, looked like a scale model of the cabin. Doug stopped at the door and put his hand on it. "Now mind your eyes. It's gonna be awful bright in here." With that, he pulled the door open to reveal a grid of marijuana plants drenched in light from two huge fluorescent fixtures that looked about the size of plane turbines. Doug looked upon it all, proud that he'd harnessed his own piece of the sun. "The older ones got buds the size of your fist." He led us into the shed like we were exploring a tomb, all three of us ducking and putting our hands out in front of ourselves, single file. When we got to the back of the shed, he stopped, and I bumped him with my hand and stopped, and I felt Ashlee's hand poke into my back. Doug turned, holding a ripely picked nugget in front of his face next to a clenched reference fist. "You ever smoke bud fresh from the plant?" I shook my head and our train reversed and retreated from the shed. Doug let us back up the hill and into the main building. It had grown a lot darker in there. "This stuff's too good to waste on a joint." He pulled a bong out of a cabinet under his sink, and there weren't many places to sit, so Ashlee and I settled for the bed since the blood had dried. "Nobody comes and hassles you?" "Aren't too many people around here to care much what I do. It ain't bad, I guess." I exhaled a tiny cloud and watched it float as Doug posed an afterthought. Ashlee passed the bong over, and I noticed she was toying with the film canister hanging around her neck. "Do you have a bathroom I could use?" "Oh, yeah." He twisted his torso around and pointed. "Maybe all them sewer juices gave him some kind of giantism." -Herb Byers FEBRUARY 6, 2010 I was up in a tree in Hillshade, a pleasantly quiet suburb with an anytown feel, looking across the street through a lens at a group of kids throwing around a football in the front yard. In the background, there was a big, bright yellow house. In the foreground, there was a black mailbox. The red flag wasn't raised that day, but the address was the same as the one on the photo. I had a particularly rough night coming into the day, and I don't know why I thought of the picture of Lennon that Ash had given me when I left, and I thought of her. I guess nearly blowing my own head off was a bit of a wake up call. The blast must have triggered off some kind of epiphany because I realized that I needed to put down the bottle and finally let the whole Dina thing go. I remembered that I was technically still married, and we had a photograph of a kid together. The kid might not have been ours, but Ashlee loved that photograph like it was. I didn't know what she was talking about, but I could tell it was special. She said it made Lennon more hers than anyone's, even if she wasn't his real mother. I couldn't tell which one of them was him. He was just a kid in the photo, and he could've looked like just about anyone right now, especially since he wouldn't have looked like Ashlee or me. I don't know why I thought it'd be a better idea to take photos from a tree across the street, but there I was. Times had changed, and people couldn't just go around photographing strange children like they used to. I guess getting caught taking pictures from a tree made things look worse, but I figured I was less likely to get caught if I wasn't in plain view. As I snapped shots, I flashed back to my own childhood and remembered all the local tourists who aimed their lenses at me. I tried climbing down, but I was up there pretty good, and by the time I was able to shimmy down to the slippery frost, the police car had parked next to me, and a pair of officers stepped out. "Excuse me, sir." He waited for me to get back to my feet. "We got a call from one of the residents here that you were causing a disturbance. May I ask what business you have here?" "I'm just taking some photos, officer." I held the camera up as evidence. "I'm not trying to bother nobody." "You weren't taking pictures of those kids there, now, were you?" His partner chimed in, eying me with suspicion. The kids across the way stopped and watched the mild commotion, safe behind their sidewalk. "Not particularly." I fumbled.
