It's all fate.

24 September 2004
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2004-09-24 George killed the headlights as he pulled off the road and onto the gravel drive, stopping three feet shy of the gate to Mount Pleasure Cemetery. His rear bumper just cleared the broad shoulder and hedgerow, giving him cover provided he moved hurriedly and nobody drove by. He pulled the trunk release and climbed out of the car. The moonlight spilled across the monuments, bathing them all in silver. The older stones, some as old as a century or more, cast hard, cruel shadows, while the newer, highly polished ones gleamed and glittered. The tranquility of the place struck him at once, and he realized he had never visited after sunset. It might have been nice to do so without other people wandering around or the caretaker cruising by on a roaring mower, and he wondered why he hadn’t thought of it sooner. Oh yeah, he thought. The chain on the gate. A light puff of air tickled his neck and sent a chill down his spine. Best get a move on. He instantly regretted unscrewing the trunk bulb, as the trunk cover cast a shadow across the void and masked the contents. He felt around where he thought he laid the bolt cutters, but of course they had shifted during the drive. Every murmur of the wind through the trees sounded like an approaching car as he anxiously searched. Finally his hand came to rest on a solid rubber grip. ‘Aha!’ George held the cutters aloft, partially in victory and partially to confirm their identity, then went to the gate. The clippers on the end fit neatly around the padlock’s hasp, and with a little exertion they pinched efficiently through the metal. The bolt snapped open and he swiftly removed it and tossed it in the grass. He winced as the chain slipped free and tumbled to the ground with a rattle, but reassured himself there was not a soul within audible range to take notice of it. He let it lie and opened the gate, then returned to the car. He tossed the bolt cutters in the back seat and drove into the cemetery. Once clear of the gate, he got out and closed it. Passers-by would never become aware of the absent chain unless they were purposely looking for it. The oldest part of the cemetery began on the long, flat mound atop the hill climbing away from the road and gate. The hill was steep enough that George had to give the car a little gas to make the ascend, but not so steep that area families could not lay to rest their loved ones all along its side. Some of the older headstones leaned unsteadily, though most of the families who purchased these cheaper-or, as the funeral homes put it, ‘more affordable’-plots did their greatest to take care of them. The Wollock family plot resided just past the peak of the hill on a broad, level spot with a first-class view of the woodlands to the west of the cemetery. George pulled off to one side of the gravel roadway and turned off the engine. He left the keys hanging from the ignition and picked up his corroded aged tackle box from the passenger seat. The shelves and hooks and lures accumulated over decades of fishing weekends all sat in a cardboard box in the garage back home, a box recently labeled with his grandsons’ names. A much heavier load weighed the tackle box down now, and he cradled it to his chest as he slid out of his seat. His back protested but he endured, and he bumped the door closed with his hip. The moonlight was not quite vivid enough to make out the engravings on the headstones without leaning in close to them, but he had made enough visits to the plot that luminosity wasn’t really necessary. He picked his way cautiously across the twenty yards or so of uneven ground to the left of the drive, then turned and walked two rows in, where he found the perimeter stones of the Wollock plot. His grandmother Dorothy had purchased the land in 1917 to bury his grandfather, Robert Wollock, who died in the War to End All Wars. ‘The Kaiser didn’t leave me much of him,’ she used to say, ‘but by God I’ll take care of what’s left.’ His marble monument, a majestic affair topped by a statuette of a saluting soldier, dominated the center of the plot. The statuette atop her monument, purchased at the same time but not placed until her death nearly forty years later, was of a woman looking up to the soldier, her hands clasped over her heart. The entire thing had cost a pretty penny, but she had inherited a healthy amount from her father, a wealthy Chicago banker. She put a lot of money into her church and the community, so they had been happy to parcel the land, guaranteeing space for quite a few generations. Indeed, the row in front of the eldest Wollocks had yet to be used. George paused temporarily at the mirror image grave of his parents, David and Donna. Dorothy gave birth to David, the second of two boys, a month after Robert was buried. The tackle box in his hands had been a present from Dad, and he gave the hackneyed handle a tender squeeze before moving on. He dropped to one knee on the plot behind his parents’ and set the tackle box down. Tenderly, respectfully, he ran his fingers across the name imprinted on the gray granite surface. ‘I’m here, baby,’ he whispered. ‘I’m here.’ A tear tumbled from his left eye, and he wiped it away quickly. He hadn’t expected them to come so soon. The tackle box lid grated open unenthusiastically, and George removed his old Zippo lighter from inside. He lit it and held it close to his wife’s side of the broad stone, the gleaming firelight rendering the impression as bottomless shadows. ‘Anne Marie,’ it read. ‘Born 10/8/1944, Died 9/19/1994.’ They had been setting up her fiftieth birthday party when she died. It was going to be a hell of a bash. She went for a walk that morning and collapsed. Heart failure, the doctors said. It was just her time, Father Alan said. The party became a memorial; the hall had already been paid for. Ten years back, that had been. Ten years to the day, though to George it still felt like yesterday. He shifted the lighter to the other side of the stone. ‘George James, Born 7/18/1941.’ He licked his thumb and rubbed clean the smooth patch underneath the word ‘Died,’ just as he had every time he visited the site. It freaked him out every now and then, seeing his name on the stone like that. It made him think like the stone wanted to complete itself, that it waited for him to pass on so it could after a long wait tell the full tale of its function. Tonight, though, it felt mysteriously welcoming. Like a welcome from an old buddy. Time to go to work. He fetched the candle from the tackle box and wedged its base into the soft ground, then lit the wick. The mild yellow light would give him an abundance of light to work with, yet would with a bit of luck be low enough that no one would distinguish it from the road. He snapped the Zippo closed and stuck it in his pocket. The chisel had rolled to one side of the box. It felt frozen and heavy as he pulled it out and took the plastic cover off the tip, and the three-pound hammer he purchased with it felt even heavier. He crept a little closer to the gravestone, and his knees groaned as he leaned over and placed the chisel head just underneath and to the left of the first D in ‘Died.’ The primary hammer blow landed exactly on the chisel head in spite of its trembling in his arthritic grasp. The hammer rang vociferously, but the echo was rapidly immersed into the night. He lined up for the next blow and struck, and this time the chisel head skittered across the stone face and almost buried itself in the ground. ‘Damn it,’ he muttered as he rubbed a thumb across the scrape running the span of the chisel trail. It looked like it would buff out, which would need to be done when he finished here at any rate. He leaned closer and inspected the proposed line, then frowned; the line was only a quarter inch long and scarcely deeper than the finished surface. He took a deep breath and lined up his next blows. Clang! Clang! Clang! His wrists already started to ache and he’d hardly lengthened the first vertical line to three quarters of an inch. George licked his thumb once more and wiped away the dust, and observed that his groove was barely more than an hideous scrape. He dropped the tools and rubbed his jaw. ‘Chiseling granite by hand will take you hours!’ the Home Depot guy had told him. ‘Even with a high-quality power drill and the correct bits, it’s a time investment.’ George snickered. He figured the guy was just trying to sell him something. Time for plan B, he thought, and reached back into the tackle box. The Sharpie weighed a hell of a lot less than the chisel, and as he situated the tip at the top of the chisel marks, he found he could clutch it far steadier as well. With one skillful stroke he finished the vertical line on the four, then sketched in the angle and inspected his achievement. ‘So. Another year, huh, babe? I wish I could say it was better than the first nine. My AA token’s still good, but further than that, there’s not much to tell you. I don’t see the kids much anymore. They’ve spread to the four winds. Jobs, their own families, the sort of thing. The grandkids are getting big, judging by the pictures.’ George sits back and gets as relaxed as he possibly can. ‘Do you remember those times we talked about my sting in the Army? There I was, too young for Korea by a decade, too old for Vietnam by a hair. I thought I lived a enamored life, coming home to a gorgeous wife to start a family, particularly given Grandpa’s death in World War One and Dad losing his legs in Round Two. You told me one time that there was someone upstairs looking out for me. I tried to tell myself you were right. And Dad, God bless him, figured America had sufficient Wollock blood for a while. I tried to consume that, too. A single tear falls out of George’s left eye and lightly glides down his cheek. ‘I just wish it were that straightforward. I never told anybody this, but I was horrified of going. I stayed up many a night, praying to God, and pleading with God that He not send me over there. You used to get angry with me for ignoring the mailbox, but truth be told, I was terrified to open it. I was afraid I’d find my reactivation orders within, or a draft card, or anything from the Army saying they were taking me from my family and putting my ass on a plane to unload me in some Godforsaken jungle that no one but a room occupied of cigar-smoking assholes in Washington gave two shits about.’ At that last comment, George actually breaks out a small-restrained smile. ‘When the Memorial Wall went up, you wouldn’t believe how many of them served with me, or above me, or just filled space in the duty rosters I used to shuffle in the clerical pool from time to time. I cried when I found a buddy from the wrestling team on there, and cried harder when I found the name of the kid he and I used to stuff into lockers.’ A minor mix of a chuckle and a sob exits George. ‘And I was grateful. God absolve me, but I was glad it was them and not me. I didn’t want to be a name on the wall for the kids to etch onto a piece of paper. I didn’t want you building a monument in my name. I just…’ He trailed off as tears overcame him. He sniffled and wiped his eyes with the ends of his sleeves, but they kept coming, blurring the names on the gravestone and turning the candle glow into an orange blob. After a few minutes he leaned forward and rested his head on the cold stone memorial surface. He took quite a few extended, profound breaths, and at last brought the tears under control. He leaned back onto his knees again. ‘Little did I know my price would come later. Our kids grew up secure and clean, all college-bound and enthusiastic to start lives and families of their own. We had a house and each other, and we were creeping up on a elongated retirement together. It took me ten years to comprehend it, but the price I paid for my own insignificant skin was that day you were taken from me. I refused the world my blood, so it took yours.’ George’s hands go directly to his face as his tears grow more and more. ‘I’m sorry, babe, but I just can’t do this any more. I can’t exist without you, and I have to set things right. If I’m fortunate, I’ll see you soon. If not… well, I guess I’ll cross that bridge when I come to it.’ George rubbed his eyes clear and affectionately stared at the monument. ‘I love you.’ George leaned forward and braced one hand against the top of the monument as he placed the Sharpie over his name. ‘Not so charmed after all,’ he wrote. Once completed, he slid the Sharpie into his shirt pocket. If the hammer weighed three pounds, the .45 caliber pistol felt as if it weighed a hundred as he lifted it out of the tackle box. He deliberately avoided looking at it as he pressed the barrel to his temple and carefully pointed it toward the center of his head. He squeezed his eyes shut, licked his lips, and squeezed. Nothing happened. He forgot the Colt was single-action on the first shot and he pulled the hammer back with his thumb. The ratcheting click of the device reverberated through his skull and tickled his ear. Wrapping his thumb around the grip released the handle safety, and he knew the main safety was not set. Go time. All he had to do was squeeze. The edge of the trigger pressed against the inside of his knuckle. The side of his head ached under the pressure of the barrel, and he shifted its angle ever so slightly, then broke down in tears. He hammered the butt against the ground as he sobbed, then screamed as he jumped up and threw the pistol as hard as he could. It arced through the air toward the woodland at the base of the cemetery mound, where it clattered and crashed through the leaves and branches and landed on the ground with a thud. ‘I’m sorry!’ he wailed. ‘God, I’m so fucking sorry.’ He let the candle sit as he snapped the tackle box closed and carried it back to the car. He threw it on the floor on the passenger side, then climbed in himself and started the car. He could scarcely see the gravel drive through his tears as he circled around the main portion of the cemetery and back to the main entrance drive. Something white fluttered across the windshield, and he craned his neck to pursue it. Logic labeled it a sheet of paper or crap of cloth, but his heart hoped otherwise. ‘Annie?’ he whispered. Whether apparition or scrap, the wind whisked it beyond the trees and out of sight. The car sputtered as he made the turn onto the downslope. He goosed the accelerator, but the car shut itself off with a snide set of soft bings from the dashboard. The dash panel lit up like a Christmas tree, and the car picked up pace as it coasted down the hill. George gasped and hit the brakes, but the pedal went directly to the floor with no outcome. The drive’s natural grade steered him to the right, and he just scarcely managed to wrestle the wheel straight to avoid the nearest memorials. He hit the gate dead on; the wrought iron doors flew open with a mighty clang and he barreled through them and bounced across the shallow rise to the road. He barely noticed the unexpected appearance of approaching headlights, but the squealing tires were instantly recognizable. He opened the door to jump out and save himself, but he was already in the road by the time his aged reflexes could catch up with the deliberation. The speeding pickup struck his car dead center. George felt pain. Pain and a split second of weightlessness until he struck the asphalt and tumbled. It felt as if the Earth shook him to pieces and set fire to his flesh, until in the end he rolled to a stop and everything went fortunately numb. Glass rained down around him. Somebody groaned and swore. There was a scrape of metal, then muted conversation. A wad of blood blew out of George’s throat and he took a bottomless breath. It rattled excruciatingly around the center of his chest. He couldn’t see out his left eye, but managed to turn his head to some extent. His own car was nowhere in view, but one of the truck’s headlamps still cast light across the asphalt in the direction of him. The Sharpie lay close by, just out of reach. His laughter started slowly, but regardless of the hurt it caused him he could not stop. It rolled across the cemetery and echoed amongst the trees until, at last, it too faded and died. [Wesley Williams, the man once identified as Fnord, walks towards George Wollock’s gravestone. His feet rest on the earth still loose over where George now lay. Wesley places his hand on the gravestone and then looks out among the other gravestones.] Wesley: It’s all about fate. It was George’s time to unite with his family. It was his time to at last end his hurting. [Wesley bends down and traces the rest of the inscription that reads ‘Died 9/22/2004’ and sighs.] Wesley: Fate is a humorous thing. There are three people in the world. There are those who believe in fate, like George. There are those who doubt fate, like Serpenti. And there are those who create fate… I am one of the latter. I make my own fate. I observe my own potential and I know what will happen. GTT4 is something I’ve seen in my future. And it doesn’t finish with Serpenti. It doesn’t finish with that man. [Wesley turns around and walks back towards the entrance, retracing the tire marks of George’s car a few nights prior to today.] Wesley: Serpenti, it is fate that I was brought back into GTT4 and given a second opportunity. It is also fate that I will defeat you so that I may go on in this tournament. [Wesley gets downhill to the gate and glares at the road where the misfortune occurred just a few nights ago.] Wesley: I will not fear the serpent. The lone thing to fear is God and God alone. That’s what you need to fear. God is on my side. God is watching my back. It’s time that I unshackle you from your sins. It’s time that I make fate happen for you. [A snake slithers out on the pavement of the road towards Wesley. Wesley watches on as a truck barrels down the road and runs right over the snake, squashing it to bits.] Wesley: It looks like ‘the serpent’ is not in fate’s hands. I’m sorry if you had high hopes El Serpenti. I ask for forgiveness for any inconvenience you have been brought to. It might just be healthier if you don’t show up at all. [And with that, Wesley walks across the road and disappears into the woods across the road from the cemetery.]
