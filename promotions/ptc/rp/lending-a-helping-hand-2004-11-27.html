Lending a Helping Hand

27 November 2004
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2004-11-27 A friend in need is a friend indeed. It’s an odd maxim, but there’s some truth to it. When a friend needs your help, they’ll be the best friend they can be. They need something from you, and the easiest way to get that something is to be overly friendly. Friends for life, remember? Help me out. Is this a good indicator of a true friend? Probably not, for a true friend would not wait until he or she is in trouble to bring up the subject of friendship. Perhaps a better indicator of a true friend is how the person who is asked to help responds to that request. A true friend would help out a friend no matter what, regardless of the circumstances. But a true friend would also know when a friend was in trouble, even before a request for help was brought up. The Latch Key Kid considered himself a true friend of all his friends, and always made it a point to help out people in need. And that’s why he had made up his mind to help out Frank in his current round of troubles. Only problem was, Frank hadn’t asked for Latch’s help. --------------------- “You’re going to do what?” Katherine asked with surprise, her eyes growing wider as she her right hand subconsciously fiddled with the fork in front of her, her breakfast having grown cold. “I’m going to help Frank, of course. What would you expect me to do in a crisis like this?” Latch responded. “Well, I don’t know,” Katherine said, her voice showing hints of the anxiety that had festered in her mind regarding Latch’s participation in the GTT4, the lemonade stand venture, and his interference in Frank’s private, business life. “Perhaps you should ask Frank how you can help.” “I tried that two days ago,” Latch said, his annoyance becoming obvious, “but Frank didn’t tell me what I can do to help. In fact, he tried to talk me out of helping.” Katherine dropped the fork onto her plate, and picked up her glass of water and took a lengthy sip, then set it back down on the table. “So let me get this straight. Not only did Frank not tell you how you can help him, but he specifically said he doesn’t want you to help?” “Well, not specifically, Latch replied, “but he didn’t seem too thrilled with the idea. I don’t know what’s gotten into him lately. He’s just been acting so strange.” “Hell, Brad,” she said, “I can’t say I really blame him. He’s apparently being harassed by these goons who are demanding money from him. Who knows if they work for the mob or not. And now you show up and try to butt in? He’s obviously handled it well enough on his own thus far, so I think he can handle it without your help. And you say he’s been acting strange? You’re the one who built a lemonade stand on the corner in front of his coffeehouse. What does that say about you?” Latch flinched, closed his eyes and rubbed his temples. “That hurts, Katherine. All I want to do is help Frank. He’s been so kind to me and Doughboy over the past few years; the least I can do is try to get these clowns to stop bothering him. I saw them walk in there again yesterday, before the place had even opened, but Frank locked the door when he saw me walking across the parking lot.” “Well, there you have it,” Katherine said, picking at the omelet on her plate again, “he doesn’t want to get you involved in the situation. For whatever reason, he thinks it would be better for you to stay out of it. Maybe he’s just looking out for your own safety, Brad. If you ask me, that show how much he cares about you.” “But that’s just it,” Latch protested, “this should show him how much I care about him. But he doesn’t seem to want to let me help.” “He’s already told you that you can help by closing your lemonade stand. Take yourself out of this situation, and you’ll be safe. You’ll be helping yourself, and at the same time, you’ll save Frank from having to worry about you. That’s help enough to him, I’m sure.” Latch vehemently shook his head. “I can’t close the lemonade stand, Katherine. I just can’t. But I do have an idea.” “Why can’t you close it? You can build a lemonade stand anywhere. Why be so stubborn about this? Why not just move it and put it somewhere else?” “Because,” Latch said, now picking at his own omelet, “this where I want to keep it. You saw what Dr. Curiosity did to little Timmy’s lemonade stand, didn’t you?” “Of course,” Katherine said, shaking her head in disgust, “and if that doesn’t give you motivation enough to beat him, then I’d be surprised.” “Oh, it gives me plenty of motivation,” Latch replied. “Good. But what does Dr. Curiosity have to do with keeping your stand where it is?” Katherine asked. “Well…” --------------------- It was just a normal street corner, one nearest to his house, which was why he picked it. His mother agreed that he could set up a lemonade stand, as long as he did it close enough to their house so that she could keep an eye on him if need be. He protested, complaining that he was old enough to take care of himself, and finally she caved in. She agreed that she would not keep watch over him, and he was satisfied with this answer. After he had set everything up on the corner, she quietly walked out of the house and sat on a chair on the front porch. While she kept a watchful eye on him, he never glanced back towards the house to see if she was keeping her promise. An hour or so later, she saw a group of kids walking down the sidewalk on the other side of the street, headed toward the corner. They glanced over at the house and saw her sitting there, then whispered amongst themselves. They reached the corner and cross the street, said something to the kid sitting behind the table. One of the kids grabbed a small paper cup, and the child sitting down shook his head, stood up and pointed to a jar which now held an assortment of coins, and grabbed the cup out of the other child’s hand. Another of the group whispered something to the child, who glanced back at the house, saw the woman sitting there, and nodded his head. The group of kids walked back across the street, and then walked down the sidewalk in the direction they had come from earlier. As they passed by the house, they each turned slowly and glared at the woman sitting on the porch. She held their gaze, her face expressionless, her eyes showing the seriousness with which she viewed the situation. She watched them continue on down the street. When they had passed out of her view, they hid in some bushes in front of a house down the street. They waited. About fifteen minutes later, she heard the phone ring. She set her glass of iced tea on the porch next to her chair, and stood up and opened the screen door and walked inside the house. The kids in the bushes smiled as they saw her leave the porch. She never heard her child scream, because he didn’t cry out. As such, she did not run out of the house to help. The pieces of wood that lay scattered on the sidewalk had once formed a solid table. Now, that table was simply a smattering of oddly-shaped planks of wood, splinters, nails and tape. Pieces of white poster board lay randomly throughout the pile of wood, some showing writing apparently done with black marker. The voices came from all around, laughing and taunting. “Awww, poor little baby lost his lemonade stand!” The child on the receiving end of the taunting was weeping. “What’s the little baby gonna do about it?” More weeping. “Better call your mommy, dummy.” His sobs had died down, replaced with sniffling. “No wonder your daddy left, little baby!” That last one was the final straw. The boy kneeling on the ground next to the broken lemonade stand suddenly sprang to life. He grabbed a board that fifteen minutes ago had been nothing but a table leg, but now he brandished it like a club. He hopped to his feet and swung the table leg wildly about him, actually striking two of the other boys. They cringed, retreated a few steps, holding their hands out in front of them as if they were morose penitents foolishly begging for mercy which they did not truthfully expect. “It’s cool, dude. We were just having a little fun. Just put that board down.” “We didn’t mean to hurt you. Come on, man, quit fooling around with that thing before someone gets hurt.” He sniffled again, dried his eyes on his shirt sleeve, slowly shook his head. “Somebody already got hurt. Now, somebody else is about to get hurt.” And then he charged at them, swinging the board like a spastic baseball slugger intent on hitting the next pitcher over the center field wall. They ran, and he followed, but soon stopped and walked back to the pile of wood on the ground. He yelled after them as he picked up a piece of glass that had been part of the money jar, and hurled it down the street after them. He sat back down on the ground, put his head in his hands, and began to sob once again. His mother finally walked out of the house, glanced down the street to see how he was doing, and then called out in horror. She ran down the front steps, ran down the sidewalk, and knelt next to him. She pulled his head up and he looked up at her, still crying, and pulled free of her grip and looked down at the ground. She helped him to his feet, and together they walked back to the house. He finally stopped crying as they walked up the stairs onto the porch. She picked up her glass and looked down at him. “See? I told you the lemonade stand wasn’t such a good idea.” Brad threw open the door and ran into the house, his tears flowing stronger than before. --------------------- “My goodness, Brad, why haven’t you told me about that before?” Katherine exclaimed. Latch shook his head. “There was no reason to, really. A silly story about a lemonade stand I had when I was a kid?” He shook his head again. “You’d be surprised. That’s nearly the least of what I had to put up with when I was a kid. And besides, you didn’t ask.” “Fair enough, I guess.” She reached across the table and took his hands in hers. Her eyes darkened slightly, showing the sorrow she felt for her boyfriend at the moment, the realization hitting her that maybe he really had led a tortured childhood. It was true, she had never asked about this, had never asked about a lot of things from his childhood; but then again, perhaps that was because she had a disconcerting notion that she knew what his answers would be. “So you want to keep your lemonade stand where it is now, because your-” He interrupted her, and she sighed inwardly at having been saved from saying what they both knew was coming next. “Because my mother made such a big deal out of that incident. No no, you know what? It’s not even the fact that she made such a big deal out of it. It’s the fact that, even after those bullies destroyed my stand, smashed the money jar and took the little change I had made, even after I chased them off, all she could say to me was that she had been right when she said it was a bad idea. There was no sympathy, no comforting, just a stern ‘I-told-you-so.’ That’s not the kind of thing an eight year old wants, or needs, to hear at the time. All she had to do…the one thing that would’ve made everything better…was just to say-” “I’m sorry.” This time it was Katherine who interrupted Latch, again squeezing his hands and staring into his eyes. “I’m sorry, Brad. I’m sorry you went through that, I’m sorry I didn’t think to ask you about it earlier, I’m sorry you’ve lived with this bottled up inside of you for so long.” He squeezed her hands back, returned her gaze. “Katherine, it’s okay. You didn’t know, it’s not your fault. I don’t blame you.” Blame. A harsh word. She believed him when he said that, but for her, it wasn’t enough. “Well, maybe you should. I feel terrible right now, Brad. It’s not your fault, it’s mine. I should have cared enough to talk to you about this before getting so worked up over you opening that lemonade stand. At the least, I should have bothered to find out why it meant so much to you before I told you it was such a bad idea.” “Katherine, don’t beat yourself up over this. It’s okay. Really, it is.” He meant it, too. He cared too much about her to hold something like this against her. It wasn’t her fault; none of it was. If anybody was to blame, he was, for keeping this inside and not sharing it with her earlier. This whole situation could have been avoided, he told himself, if only I had been thinking earlier. No, he didn’t blame her, and she knew that from his voice. “I could have avoided this entire conversation if I had thought well about it when this whole thing started.” “It’s okay. I guess we’ll just have to agree that we’re the ones to blame.” Her voice comforted him; he saw the longing for a return to comfort in her eyes. “So what are you going to do now?” “Well, like I said, I’m going to help Frank. And the best way I can think of is to keep the lemonade stand right where it is.” “Okay, you’ll have to explain this one to me. If he’s being hassles by these goons, who apparently have control of the area’s businesses, how does your lemonade stand being there help Frank?” He shrugged. “Okay, so maybe the stand being there doesn’t help Frank directly. But it’s what I plan to do with the stand.” “Okay, explain,” she said, comforting him with her eyes. “Well, I’m going to take whatever money I make from the stand and give it to Frank to help him keep those goons away. I figure, if I’ve caused him this much trouble, the least I can do is try to take care of the other problems he has right now.” He gulped, realizing what she would say next. Frank doesn’t need your money; he has his own business that seems to do just fine. Besides, he’s been dealing with these guys for a while now, and he seems to have a fairly good handle on things. I’m sure if he needed your help, he would be more than willing to let you offer it, but there’s no need to offer him your money and just further cloud the situation. Also, you should think about your own safety. There’s no reason to compromise your own well-being by sticking your nose where you shouldn’t be sticking it. Those goons warned you, Frank warned you, it would be best to just walk away now. Keep the lemonade stand there if you want to, but don’t do anything else to put yourself in jeopardy. Handle your own situation, and let Frank handle his own situation. Everything will be easier that way. Allow yourself to focus on Doctor Curiosity and your semi-final match. But she didn’t say any of that as she squeezed his hand again. Her words took him by surprise. “That’s very admirable of you, Brad. Frank is lucky to have a friend like you. Everybody should be that lucky.” He smiled and she nodded knowingly at him. “Come on,” she said, “let’s go open that lemonade stand for the day.” They stood up, Latch pulled out his wallet and dropped some money on the table for the bill and the tip, and they left the café. A single thought continued to nag at him, despite his desire to get rid of it. Frank didn’t ask for your help. In fact, he tried to dissuade you from helping. Sometimes, it’s better to not go where you’re not wanted. But he shrugged off the thought and walked on out of the café.
