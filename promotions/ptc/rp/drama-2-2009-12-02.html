Drama 2

2 December 2009
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2009-12-02 Frozen. Literally, it was freezing outside. Fresh off the news that he possibly impregnated his best friend's girlfriend—or former girlfriend, whatever it may be—Buck Fledger was back in the land of igloos and lumberjacks. Brandon, Manitoba – the Wheat City, but not in the Kansas sense, at least as depicted by Truman Capote. The wind was not dry and warm but brisk and cold, stinging Buck's face like the news that a girl he had slept with was pregnant. He naturally did the responsible thing and ran off to Canada, saying he had to clear his head and talk it out with his mother. He thought it was a natural impulse: a mature, wise decision, which it may or may not have been, but to most people it was just a sign that he was scared. And, being human, Buck cannot be blamed for feeling a little scared that he might be a Dad. Maybe it was because of his own strained relationships with his father, who he barely knew and didn't particularly care to. The Dad was an English professor at the University of Manitoba in Winnipeg, which was about a two hour drive from Brandon, where The Mom had taken up permanent residence. Buck was back in town—to clear his head—and when he came home from a night of serious drinking he revealed, “Mom, I don't know how to be one but I'm going to be a father, and I'm going to be there for him, just like Dad wasn't for him—or—er, for me.” Thick with slurs, Buck was trying to figure out the construction of that sentence when The Mom expectedly began chastising him. This was unsurprising. Buck already knew what she was going to say: You can barely take care of yourself, so how do you expect to take care of a kid? A knock at the door. This is where things got strange. Buck stumbled over to open the door, trying to tell his mother that he didn't care what she thought, he was going to take on the ruuuuhhsponsibility. He opened the door to the last face in the world he expected to see: The Dad, in full bodily form. Not in the form of gifts or e-mails or empty promises, but his tangible self. He looked like the older version of Buck and acted like it too: just a little more selfish and little less self-conscious. Buck would later write in his journal: God, he is stereotypical. The salt-and-pepper beard and hair. Sophistication and intelligence in demeanour and in fashion. Buck could see why The Mom was attracted to such a sophist: he looked like a success and acted like one too. So, maybe not so much like Buck as he initially seemed. Perhaps this was the root of The Mom's criticism of Buck: she was expecting him to fill the shoes of the absent male, and it was an image that Buck could never quite fulfil. Buck momentarily thought about feminist theory, distracting himself from the disbelief he felt at the sight of The Dad himself. “You're my Dad.” “Yes, I am. You and I have a lot of talking to do.” In his slightly intoxicated state, Buck tried to figure out if this was an example of hypercorrection. Even though he was far past the typical age of the impressive son, he still wanted to outsmart The Dad – make him feel inferior, as if he made a mistake in abandoning Buck. Buck said, “We don't. Not now and not ever. I'm going to be the father that you never have or ever will be.” The Dad scoffed. “You're going to be a father?!” Buck: “It really doesn't make a difference to you anyway, so why ask?” He had a point. -- The next day, Buck was back on a place to LA. His main achievement: accumulating more frequent flyer miles, and reinforcing his reputation as that cute boy who flew all the time and charmed the flight attendants. He dreamt that last part up, and upon awaking reprimanded himself for thinking such thoughts about other women. He was taken now. Or was he? The talk that Jane and him had no real conclusion: it was circular in nature. Jane figured that Buck should have a say in whether or not to raise the child. Buck asked, what if it isn't mine? A fair question, he thought, albeit a bit suggestive. She said, it's yours. He said, okay, well, it doesn't matter what I want, it's ultimately up to you. She said, maybe, but that's not the point. He said, then what is the point? She said, I don't know, do you? He said: I asked first. They got that far, then Buck left. Jane was at her parent's house for the weekend, perhaps delivering the news of her predicament. She was from an evangelical family who would probably bury her with the baby if she had an abortion, so Buck was unsure if she would even tell them. If she did, it probably meant that she was going to keep it. The child, that is. If not, then it was up in the air. When Buck landed, five new messages. Four from Jane, and one from The Assistant. Where the fuck are you. What are you doing? Itoldmyparentswe'rehavingthisbaby, whythefuckdidyoujumptownwhen you'resupposedtobemakingaTVshow. What's more: Meet The Parents. Come out this week. I told them we're getting married. I had to, Buck, I had to. Just no fucking choice. Fuck, why did we have to do that. Oh well, no looking back now. Think positive, Jane, think positive. Okay, bye. Buck's head was spinning. He didn't particularly give a damn about The Assistant but felt like he should call him anyway and own up. But he didn't. He cared a lot about Jane, but he figured that calling her and accepting her invitation would be an affirmation of a life plan that he wasn't sure he wanted. What about the portrait of an artist as a young man? It doesn't involve a child and a wife and grandparents and a carriage and a 9-5 job, salaried, kissing ass so that when it comes bonus time he can afford Christmas presents for his father-in-law. The mere thought of it was making him ill. Does this mean they have to move to a suburb? Does this mean they have to start going to neighbourhood barbecues? Does this mean he has to take up alcoholism just to deal with aforementioned 9-5 job? Buck would no longer be The Writer, or The Wrestler. He would be The Swimmer. Life was hard before it even began. -- Jane's parents lived in Aspen, Colorado. They had a beautiful house, a three car garage, three cars, a spoiled fifteen year old son who took up expensive hobbies then abandoned them the next day. It was about the last place on earth that Buck thought could be capable of nurturing someone like Jane, who seemed nothing like her parents and did not subscribe to their lifestyle. Buck was close to the truth but not completely on it. Jane had relied on her parents financial support throughout much of university, though they were reluctant to provide money for the Master of Fine Arts that Jane had. Buck didn't know about this, yet. Jane was raised as an evangelical Christian. Buck found this surprising, though Jane assured him that she no longer held such beliefs. He detected a bit of resentment when she told him this, and on this level Buck related to her: his own struggles with his Mother seemed not so far removed from the internal conflict of Jane's family. And internal conflicts there were. Buck didn't help much. After an awkward meeting and an equally awkward evening, Buck was in bed when he overheard Jane and her parents talking: “So, sweetie, he seems like a nice boy. What does he do for a living?” Buck rolled over and grunted. This question bothered him in Brandon and it bothered him here too. The voice even reminded him of The Mom's tone: What are you going be? Where are you going with your life? Imagine Buck's response when Jane replied, “He's a wrestler and aspiring writer.” Here was a family who could hardly get behind their own daughter's commitment to writing. But this 20-something boy, who their daughter was romantically involved with, the father of their future grandkid, was a fake wrestler and an “aspiring” writer. The operative word here, aspiring, makes all the difference in the world. Jane's parents liked being able to tell their friends that Jane was a successful Hollywood reporter, but up until that point, it was just: she's being educated for a Master's degree. Why? Arts degrees are often met with some kind of resistance. Buck's self-pity, though sometimes laughably hyperbolic, stemmed from the fact that people looked at him as a failure and he had no real way of responding to his critics. His Mother, his Father. And now, his future mother-in-law. There was only Jane, and if there is only Jane then he guessed that meant he would have to tolerate the tone of her father, who said: “Well, that's an interesting choice of career. What university did he go to?” Ha! Buck's eyes lit up. Buck did, in fact, go to university, and a decent one at that. The funny part is that he used his education for bragging rights, and the reason why it's funny is because Buck wants it both ways: he wants the respect that comes along with being educated at a university, but doesn't want the criticism that goes along with his lack applicable knowledge. “I don't know,” Jane responded. “You don't know?” “I don't know.” “What do you mean you don't know?” “I don't know.” “He's the father of your child for crying out loud!” Jane was calm. “I'm aware of that.” “I can't believe this! Are you listening to this Marion?” Marion, weeping: “Why, oh why, dear daughter?” Buck felt horrible but he couldn't help but laugh. Jane felt the same way. It would be a little different if it was real human suffering, but here was a Mother overcompensating her role and making an unnecessary scene. The same thing would happen the next night, when they were all out for supper and Jane's smartass younger brother asked questions that asked for some kind of altercation: So, do you touch a lot of men as a fake wrestler? Do you like it? Jesus christ, thought Buck. Whatever happened to imagination in youth? Whatever happened to believing in the spectacle of physical violence? Mankind being thrown off of the cell. Off of a stage. Tables, ladders, and chairs. The Rock v. Triple H, ladder match. The sublime! Wrestling just wasn't cool anymore; not like Call of Duty Modern Warfare 2. Or maybe the kid just had elite taste in art. Mom and Dad were ashamed of The Boy. Not for being rude, but for revealing such a shameful fact in a public place. Buck and Jane exchanged telling looks. They found this funny, but also disturbing. Buck would later think about his own hesitance in telling people that he was a wrestler, and he figured that he was just as bad, if not worst, than Jane's parents. The conflict was mostly an undercurrent of the meeting, though. Buck didn't fight the Dad nor the Boy, and he certainly didn't sleep with Jane's Mom. Jane and Buck left Aspen on mutually respective terms with their parents. He figured that they dodged a bullet, but might not be so lucky with the next thing on their laundry list of loose ends: The Assistant.
