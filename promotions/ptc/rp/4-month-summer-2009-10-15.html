4 Month Summer

15 October 2009
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2009-10-15 1. LA; June There was a pretty woman. Twenty-something, wavy brown hair, and the most noticeable thing about her was her stoic acceptance of her current predicament. That her weight was being supported by an older man while he hung onto a rope ladder that dangled from a helicopter with only one working engine. There was a train underneath, doing what trains do, with bad guys and their handguns shooting at our two heroes. Missing, naturally. There was a precious object somewhere that both parties were after: it was long lost destroyed misplaced, though: a necessary sacrifice in an otherwise flawlessly executed action scene. The guy directing all of this ... people called him John. Just John, somebody who didn't like to stay all night drinking but got up early in the morning, swam laps, had his orange juice and toast while he caught up on scripts, then was off to the studio to work on his next classic. No, that's wrong: he was off to the battlefield to win a war. With a train and a helicopter and some stunt men and some big names and a hundred-million dollar budget which would put his spy-action epic into thousands of theatres and produce all sorts of fame and more money to spend on more spy-action thriller-suspense adrenaline epics. It was all very tiring. He didn't smoke, but he drank a lot of vitamin water on set and when he could get away from all that he had his own little office with his own little mini-fridge. Which, of course, was stocked full of the stuff (vitamin water), and even the ingredients for his own protein shake. His assistant – whom The Director had saved from crippling student debt and alcoholism -- had already prepared the protein shake when The Director got back to his desk. It was the only thing he looked forward to. The memos and the scripts could all wait. This was the reason that The Director had The Assistant completely clear his desk of the previous day's work before he got into his office. And, usually, The Assistant had done just that. Usually. Once upon a time, as The Director sat down with his protein shake, he noticed a recognizable collection of stapled papers placed in the middle of his otherwise-empty desk. The desk that should have been empty was corrupt by ... a script. As The Director would tell The Assistant later, it was about the last thing that he wanted to see at the time. He didn't have a wife or kids or any real friends but the last thing he wanted to look at was another fucking script that some no-name thought he could pass on and just get a paved way to fame. No -- this was antithetical to The Director's valuation of the struggle. Something he would not compromise. The Individual Struggle to Success. Buck Fledger, A Life. That title was on the front page of the script. Not that it was original, really. The Director had read, skimmed over, and trashed many biopic scripts over the past few years. It was the new "in" genre and he had no issues with looking down on the entire thing and the people involved in it. The Director called The Assistant into his office. The Assistant had a folder. To look busy, of course. He said: "Tell me you remembered your one o'clock with your brother." To which The Director responded: "That can wait. I have more important things to do, like read scripts. Scripts that I don't have time for." The Assistant, getting it, made his case: "It's not that long. I'm asking you to read it as a favour. I went to school with this guy. He's having a hard time and needs something ... something to get his confidence back." 2. Brandon, MB; July Money sucks, thought Buck. Not having money sucks even more. The more you think you don't need it, the more you need it. Buck Fledger, B.A., having his third existential crisis of the week, sat outside of his mom's modest home in the Wheat City. This was a city that Buck didn't know very well at the time: a small city that, culturally, would be better described as a town experiencing growing pains. Big box stores, supermarkets, and a shopping mall to boot. Buck would know in short time that it took about ten minutes to get anywhere in the city; that the two bars in town were country-themed but attracted all different sorts of boring and exciting people. Some people who loved to live, and others who thought they were above that kind of living but did it anyway. Just to feel something. Buck hadn't found his place, yet, other than the front step and guest room of his mom's house. This was slowly turning into a permanent arrangement, but Buck and The Mom had communication problems so it was difficult for them to have a constructive conversation. It usually went something like: "Buck, you're twenty-three years old,". . ."Last time I checked, yeah,". . ."Why don't you get a job?". . ."The only TV station in town just closed down!". . ."Or go back to school?". . ."I already tried that!". . ."Get a degree in Education! Just yesterday I met this nice young man, 21 years old, who is graduating with the same kind of diploma as you -- whatever you call it -- and is going onto Law school! Law school!" This city was much, much different from where Buck had lived before: New York, Toronto, LA. It seemed like most people got along, even though Buck sensed a discontent in some of the residents: the flame of desire flickers even here, thought Buck. The ones who thought school was their "thing" saw the path of the academic as the way toward bigger, better things: being an engineer, lawyer, medical doctor, professor, or recognized politician was like hitting the lottery in this city. The Mom called him naive and idealistic. Her and others didn't take writing seriously as a profession: they saw Buck's broke self as a failure. Undisciplined, misguided, failure. They said: contribute to society. Nobody ever contributed to society by painting a picture or writing a short story was the logic, he figured. He was wrong there, though. It wasn't that they were stupid, but that they had never known anything else. At post-grad 21 22 23, Buck was unfortunately vulnerable to making bad judgements of others based on his values and worldview. He projected his own vision of things onto others and assessed them accordingly. In school, they tried to teach him how to think but he never really Got It. He traded in the narrow-mindedness of the angsty teenager for the narrow-mindedness of the self-righteous university grad. Buck Fledger, B.A. But it was a struggle and Buck was not a victim. He was a bit misguided but not in the way that the collective consciousness of his new community thought he was. His misguidedness was in his romantic portrait of himself as the Artist as a Young Man. He thought he had good art and the only reason for his lack of success was the homogenous art-as-business model that Hollywood represented. But, as usual, there was only a kernel of truth in his argument, and it would only be a matter of time until The Director would prove him wrong. 3. LA; late September The Director had changed his tune after finally getting around to the script. He was in disbelief but he told The Assistant and a studio-head named The Suit: "This story has heart, okay? Heart. It has humour and romance and action and depth. It's tragic and comic. Tragic and comic! It's quirky and exactly the kind of film that movie-goers want to see these days. You know Juno and Slumdog Millionaire, how those were such hits? This is one of those. Well, it has potential. It might need some re-working. But it's good enough to buy. I am dead serious. I will buy this script if you guys won't. How much is he asking? What? You don't know? Is this guy under the union? Well if he's just a guy who wrote a script that wanted somebody important to read it then I guess he got what he wanted. But that's probably not enough because everybody wants money for their art these days! Just the thing to do! I don't blame him. Can we get him on the phone today?" The Assistant was trying to field all of The Director's questions, but finally was able to say: "Wow ... I -- I've never seen you this enthused." The Assistant was happy too. He liked it when a script that he "found" got attention. He was moving up in the world too: The Individual Struggle to Success. The Suit looked calm in comparison to the others. "We can probably buy the script. The chances of it being made are slim, but at least we'll control the source material." The Director responded: "Why can't we make it?" This question was rather ignorant coming from such an experienced filmmaker. The Suit scoffed. "Well ... you know, the usual. It's a complex process." He didn't even bother to explain. Money. Money and power money and power. The Individual Struggle to Success. 4. Brandon; October Buck was still poor and out of a job. No power money success. Him and his mom were on better terms, though, because Buck told her about an opportunity he had to make a quick dollar. He didn't tell her that it was wrestling because ... well, it was kind of embarrassing. He had a snobbish attitude about wrestling but his natural athleticism and desire to compete made him suitable for the ring. And, he needed money. The more you think you don't need it, the more you do. Buck hated cliches, too, especially those little nuggets of wisdom about life. He was wrong about how they failed to get at the real truth of things, because even after exhausting the different tenets of a subject it often comes back around to a clever one-liner. Hook, line, and sinker. Buck couldn't afford a cell phone, so his calls came directly to their landline. One new message. Beep. "Buck." It was The Assistant. "The Director loves your script. I don't know if he's fucking crazy or what. Seems to me like he wants to make something personal and emotional or some crap like that. Instead of the usual action films, y'know. Who wants another Die Hard?! Y'know. Anyway, he wants you on a plane right away. Clear your schedule. As of next month you're now known as ... THE HERO. HOLLYWOOD, BABY." Beep. End of message. Overwhelmed with emotion, Buck ... well, he sighed. It was a relief, in some ways. But there were other pressing concerns. One minute you have nothing to do – the next you have too much. The Individual Struggle to Success.
