The College Chronicles #21: 'Friar Lawrence'

13 March 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2005-03-13 7th March 2005 Sir Richard Blakey, 54, was a distinguished and decorated academic in the field of mathematics. His papers had won prizes; his writings had sold books. His impossibly clever brain had inspired many a legion of young intellectuals. He was English – only those of British nationality can receive the honour of knighthood, of course – more specifically hailing from Sedgemoor in Somerset. Nowadays, however, he resided in a comfortable house close to Albany in New York, on the Eastern band of the United States. He spent his days alternating between proving new theories, disproving old theories, spending quality time with his wife and three children (only the youngest still lived at home, however), and of course lecturing at the prestigious Harbrook College in Albany itself. On seeing my Harbrook timetable for the first time, I had noted with an element of surprise the name of a famous English professor alongside my Calculus/Trigonometry listing. It wasn’t the ‘famous’ aspect I’d been interested in, unlike others; in fact it was the simple observation that he was an Englishman in America and so was I that had fascinated me. By a glorious stroke of luck, he’d also turned out to be a very nice man. He treated us as individuals, not simply looking out from the lectern at rows of blank faces but dozens of fascinated students, each with his or her own merits and shortcomings. And – although I hate to be immodest – he’d quickly spotted that my brain was one of the few able to keep up easily with his trains of thought. We were on the same wavelength, Professor Blakey and I – or just ‘Richard’, as he encouraged those students with whom he had a strong rapport to call him. I was one of those students. Many an enthralling hour I’d spent with Richard in our free time, going over new mathematical models (some of which he had devised himself; even one we’d developed together on one engrossing Friday afternoon) and discussing age-old theories. Pythagoras was a common interest for us both, and my discovery on the Internet of a radical outsider’s subjective and systematic attack on the Greek genius had provoked a defiant and outraged response from Richard, every word of which I had devoured eagerly. Put simply, Sir Richard Blakey was an inspiration to me, and that was why I practically had to stop myself from jumping for joy at the end of today’s lecture when he invited me to stay behind for a chat. I felt just a touch of guilt as I ventured over towards him whilst the last few stragglers were hoisting their bags onto their backs and Richard was packing away the overhead projector. I hadn’t just let myself down by not revising for my mid-terms – I’d let Richard down too. I hoped fervently that I hadn’t performed too badly, and frantically began to formulate an excuse for not having studied harder. Not once, however, did it cross my mind that my Calc/Trig mid-term was the very reason I had been asked to stay behind in the first place. I was in for something of a rude awakening. “Tim Shipley,” Richard began as he peered down at me with a frown, pushing his glasses higher up the bridge of his nose with the tip of his index finger. It really would pay for him to get some glasses that fit, I found myself thinking. “Richard,” I acknowledged him somewhat warily. This hadn’t been the most friendly of greetings, after all – and why was he using my full name? “Sir Richard to you!” he snapped, drawing himself up to his full height of 6’3”. He would have seemed immense to most, but my tag team partner Tony Aliso had a full two inches on him, so Richard couldn’t make me feel dwarfed, at least: I was used to that sort of height in company. But that wasn’t quite what was on my mind just then – what on earth could have prompted this out-of-character outburst? “Sir Richard – sorry –” I corrected myself with a frown. He never made anyone use his title – even if they hadn’t been specifically invited to use just his first name, he tolerated the impertinence of being spoken to as an equal, because he was a nice man and that was that. What was going on...? He looked down at me with a disapproving glare – lips pursed, eyebrows raised. Sir Richard Blakey was formidable in this pose, and he knew it. While many, out of his earshot, ridiculed the tufts of white hair that poked out from seemingly random origins on his head, not one person in the world would even dare to find them remotely amusing when put in this situation. Age has no bearing on presence, nor on power. Power is something that is not acquired without a fight, and once you have it, it grows exponentially. As for presence – you’re born with it and you die with it. Presence cannot be learned or taught. Presence has that je ne sais quoi about it. And Sir Richard Blakey... he had presence in abundance. No matter that his trousers were shabby and his white shirt (no longer white) untucked. “Tim Shipley,” Richard repeated, looking me over from top to bottom as if formulating a critical judgement. I chanced a hasty glance down at myself, checking that I was well-dressed. I was – in fact I was attired even more smartly than I usually dressed myself for a lecture. I wondered whether that might have been a subconscious reaction to my poor preparation for my mid-terms. Perhaps I thought that looking smart would make everything better. Well, it can never hurt to make a good impression. He strolled over to his bag in the corner of the room, motioning for me to stay where I was. My palms becoming moist with sweaty apprehension, I watched his progress anxiously. He pulled out an almost-new ream of lined A4 paper, still in its plastic packet but having been ripped open at the top to allow access to the top leaf, which, I observed as he came back towards me, was covered in his scrawlings. These appeared to be set out in some kind of table... “Please explain yourself,” Richard said in a gravelly, quiet voice, startling me as the silence was broken. I frowned, but I now knew exactly what he was talking about, having identified the table on the paper in his hand as the marks from the mid-terms, and I threw a hand to my burning forehead. ”I –” “Perhaps it’s better we sit down,” he interjected wearily, and I followed him over to the front row of seats in this purpose-built lecture room. Avoiding his penetrating stare, I looked up along the incline, right to the very top row, shrouded in darkness and almost merging with the rafters. This truly was a massive room – I’d never really noticed it before. There was rarely any need to fill the entire theatre, other than when a famous outside researcher or scientist came to give a talk. I didn’t understand why this was the room used for our Calc/Trig classes, when our group of thirty-eight students could easily fit in the first three of more than thirty rows. Perhaps Richard adored the grandeur associated with giving a big lecture. “Look at me,” Richard ordered softly. I forced my reluctant eyes to stare into his. Grey, bloodshot, lifeless. Old. Much older. Why had I not noticed this before? Sir Richard Blakey was clearly not in the richest vein of health. He stared back, not needing to blink, and his gaze conveyed everything I needed to know. My suspicions were confirmed. A C – perhaps even a D. He was disappointed; hurt, even. He’d expected so much more from me. Clearly, I wasn’t the student he’d thought I was. “I’m sorry,” I told him sincerely, and just then, I wouldn’t have had it any other way. He said nothing, and I knew he wanted me to go on. He wanted a full explanation. But how much could I really tell him? What an idiotic question. Sir Richard Blakey had me at his mercy. Right then, I would have answered any question in the world if only he’d asked me. Anything – and it might have helped to get things off my chest. Maybe Richard should go into counselling. He had such a power over me – his presence at this close range was intoxicating, especially with his entire focus being directed at me. Never before had I experienced such a sensation of... submission. Like life was no longer mine. I was simply a clone, a dummy in a thin casing, bade to do whatever my frail and ageing master told me. Now I understood how the archetypal henchman would feel when confronted by an angry Darth Vader. It’s not just the fear. It’s the feeling of helplessness. Similar, in a way, to those who believe their lives are ruled by fate – but in this case, fate was not an intangible imaginary somewhere up there in the sky. Richard ruled fate. Richard was fate. And now I had to face fate. “I – I didn’t revise. I couldn’t,” I added hopelessly. “The first part is obvious,” he sighed, breaking the hold and jarring me back into life as he looked away sadly. “The second part – debatable.” “Please – really, I – I didn’t have time...” My voice failed as he looked back at me, that gloomy expression on his face almost causing a lump to form in my throat. This man really did know how to make someone feel bad. “Tim, you have a brain capable of anything,” Richard began quietly. “Your memory is outstanding; your application untouchable. There’s no question that once you learn something, Tim – you, individually, I refer to here – you never forget it. It’s implanted in your brain, tucked away for future use as and when is necessary. That is why I find it impossible to believe that a lack of revision was the problem.” Silence. I had nothing to say; even if I had, his impossible grip on my voice-box was stronger than that of a fully-tightened vice. “What I propose,” he continued, “is that you never learnt the topics in the first place. I could say that rather than not revising – you simply didn’t vise.” I let a gloomy smile creep onto my face and nodded. “You haven’t been attending classes,” he informed me, deadpan. “I –” My attempt to interject was swiftly cut short as my ears, eager to listen to the wise words of Sir Richard Blakey, overruled the wishes of my mouth and clamped it tightly shut. “There’s something in your life that’s... changed, Tim. I know it. I can see it in your eyes.” He watched me, unmoving. I watched him, unmoving. The electric current of understanding, passing both ways, reached fever pitch. I felt goosebumps traverse the top of my head, and something in the back of my mind told me that had never happened before. “A girl?” he went on, then shook his head. “No. Perhaps that is partly it, but I can sense there is something more. You are hiding a big secret, Tim. Yet you are not happy that you are totally in control of this secret.” He paused to rub his eyes. I was horrified to note that Richard looked worse than unkempt. He looked... haggard. “People know, I’m guessing,” he went on. I nodded in compliance. “People know, yet you are unwilling to tell others –” “How do you know this?” I blurted out with more volume than was intended. Richard straightened up and looked around as my voice echoed around the hall, and the ensuing silence caused my ears to ring. Finally, letting his mark sheet fall to the floor, he placed his hands back on his knees and focused on me again. “Tim, I don’t know any of this. Yet your face gives away more than you might think.” “How?” He frowned. I knew I’d sounded overly sceptical and rude. “I’m sorry, I didn’t mean to be –” “I know,” he cut me off, holding up a hand. “I could see it in your eyes... you see?” I nodded slowly. “Could you teach me?” I said hesitantly. “I beg your pardon?” “Could you – er – teach me how to – read people?” It sounded ridiculous, saying it for the second time. Richard pursed his lips to blow out a quick breath, and scratched the top of his head. “I don’t know,” he breathed finally. “I’m not sure it’s something that can be taught. The ability to read what others are thinking – it’s not an exact science. It is much clearer with some than others. With us... I don’t know. We just seem to be on the same wavelength, if you will. Do you see that?” I nodded, and sat my head in my hands, intrigued greatly by all of this. “So – have you always been able to do this?” I asked. He exhaled again. “Yes and no,” said Richard in reply. “Well –” He chuckled and held up a hand. “I know, I know. You hate that answer. You always have. But it’s justified! In this case, at least. You see – I would usually say the ability is something I was born with. As a child, I always knew when to stop teasing just before it got nasty. When to stop talking in a lesson just before a teacher exploded with rage. When to stop nagging my mother to take me somewhere or play with me and just give her some peace and quiet.” Richard’s eyes went misty as he gazed off into the distance. Sensing that he had recalled a fond memory, I stared at the floor and self-consciously flexed my fingers, allowing him some time to digest the nostalgia. Suddenly, he surprised me by blurting out a quick “You see?” I looked up at him and frowned. “See what?” His face creased up into a knowing smile; that which I’d grown to love; that which he’d given me the day we’d thought up our model; that which elicited a feeling of happiness down to my very roots; that of him seeing shades of himself in a protégé; that of passing over a mantle. And I knew that I liked, respected, adored Richard Blakey as much as anyone in the world. He was like an uncle and a brother and a father to me all at the same time. His influence on my life was unclassifiable, but I knew that in ten years’ time, when I’d publish my first mathematical theory and receive plaudits from all over the world, the first person I’d thank would be Sir Richard Blakey. Still, however, I didn’t know what he thought I could ‘see’. And then I realised I did. I knew exactly what he meant. And I saw. Saw for the first time that I could see. Sensing that he had recalled a fond memory, I stared at the floor and self-consciously flexed my fingers, allowing him some time to digest the nostalgia. I’d read Richard like an open book. I looked up into his eyes and creased my face up into that knowing smile. A glimmer of tears appeared in his right eye; a glimmer of tears appeared in my left eye. I knew instinctively that he’d seen something of himself in me. It was almost as if both of us were looking at reflections. Our connection was so electric, it was almost scary. “So, yes and no,” he continued finally. “While the ability is something I was born with – I grant that – it is also something that has been improved and refined over time. It is necessary for me to retreat within myself for hours at a time – meditation, some call it. It... it helps.” I nodded, understanding nothing yet understanding perfectly. Unfortunately, there was one issue we still hadn’t fully covered. “About the mid-terms...” “Another day, Tim. Save it for another day.” His eyes twinkled. In unison, we stood up. Richard offered me his hand. I took it, but before we could even begin to shake hands we both realised with a sudden clarity that this went nowhere near far enough in realising our synchronised spiritual awakenings. Sir Richard Blakey spread his arms and I hugged him and held him tight. Like an uncle. Like a brother. Like a father.
