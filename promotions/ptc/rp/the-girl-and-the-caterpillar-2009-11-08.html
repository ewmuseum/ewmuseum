The Girl: And the Caterpillar

8 November 2009
Content noticeA period document, served as recovered; withheld details are marked. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2009-11-08 "We are what we pretend to be, so we better be careful what we pretend" – Kurt Vonnegut Jr. * * * Clinton, IA – May 24th, 1987 The sun peaked out between clouds for a moment, before being swallowed up again by an overcast sky. According to the television there was a thirty percent chance of rain, but to look up at the sky you might have assumed it was a certainty. This was not an ideal day to go fishing, but sometimes there were factors that superseded logic. One of these factors: wives. As in, Jerry Stevens wanted to be far away from his. He walked side by side with his seven year old son, down a trail that his father had shown him decades earlier. They both wore Iowa Hawkeye baseball caps, but underneath the hats their minds dwelled on far different subject matter. Jerry was an insurance agent, and not a particularly good one. He had a reasonable amount of success because of his contacts, and his work ethic. He didn’t understand people, but that wasn’t his tragedy. His tragedy was that this didn’t bother him. Things he understood: football, fishing, and woodworking. Jerry knew the stats of every Hawkeyes running back in the last thirty years, he had over 200 different kinds of fishing lures, and he could make everything from grandfather clocks to bird houses. But he couldn’t hold a conversation with his wife to save his life, and this had begun to wear on the marriage. She had often accused him of having a quiet meanness about him, and he wondered if that were true. It occasionally scared him how little he knew even himself. Having a kid was just something Jerry did because…well, that’s what people do, right? He enjoyed taking his son, Lane, on fishing trips like this because he was too young to demand complex social interaction. Lane asked simple questions that deserved simple answers. Jerry feared the birds and the bees talk, he feared his son growing up period. Then he would become one of these "people". Walking down the dirt trail, carrying two fishing poles and a tackle box, dressed in jeans and a Heartland Insurance t-shirt, Jerry weighed all of these things. Life was so unbearably heavy, he thought. Lane, on the other hand, had a far more innocent thought. "When it becomes a butterfly, will it still know who I am?" the boy asked. "Of course," Jerry offered, and patted his son on the head. "Good, cause we started a club and we’re always going to know each other." Lane did not carry any fishing gear, but instead a caterpillar. It went back and forth on his finger, and when necessary he let the small creature wander onto his other hand for a change of scenery. "You can have a dog when you grow up," Jerry started, and studied Lane as he walked. His son seemed perfectly content with this caterpillar. "It’s just…" "You’re allergic, I know," Lane replied, not taking his eyes off of his first pet. He had named it Fuzzy. At home he kept it in a bucket with leaves and sticks. Jerry smiled mildly to himself at the simplicity of this conversation. There were no guilt trips, there were just the facts. Even at a young age, Lane was very logical, and rarely threw temper tantrums unless he was tired. Between two trees up ahead, the lake was coming into view. And the low buzzing that often plagued Jerry’s head quieted. "I had a dream last night that I grew up to be a huge monster! Cause of stuff I ate," Lane remembered suddenly, giggling. "Uh huh," Jerry was barely paying attention. "And I chewed on everyone I saw!" "That’s not nice." "Well monsters aren’t nice!" the boy giggled again, making exaggerated biting motions. * * * Cleveland, Ohio – Now. "Once again this is Jeff Coltz for Pro Wrestling Illustrated, interview session with Lane Stevens, part two." He spoke into a tape recorder. "And Jeff’s penis is out," Lane added. "That is not factual." They were two of only twelve people on a trolley tour of Cleveland’s more famous sights. Most of the others were crammed towards the front of the trolley, perhaps to better hear the tour guide, and perhaps because of the vulgar things being uttered on the back of the vehicle. Coltz and Stevens sat alone in the last row. The former was wearing the latter’s infamous "Get Fucked" t-shirt under an unbuttoned black coat, one of the stipulations on the interview being finished. The latter wore a black and gold Iowa Hawkeyes hoodie, because hey, as overstated as it is…some things never change. The trolley began to move, causing some of the crowd to clap. Stevens joined in, enthusiastically. "Why are we here?" Jeff asked, his gaze fixed on the skyline of the city. "People are paying money to get a tour of…Cleveland. Can’t you find the humor in this?" "Not especially." The River Rat shook his head, sizing up the tourists before him. "It just smells like PBR and piss everywhere here." "If you say so." "I’m rather fond of it," The Warlord mused. Coltz glanced at this interview subject out of the corner of his eye and found a subtle look of satisfaction on his face. Mischief danced in Lane’s eyes, as it often did. "But when I asked why we were here, I wasn’t talking about this trolley car…but Cleveland in general." Stevens didn’t answer right away. He had discovered a group of small children playing jump-rope, unsupervised. He imagined the endless string of tragedies that would make up their lives, and then he wondered if there was a good sushi place in the neighborhood. "This is about the skank I have sexual intercourse with," Lane said nonchalantly. "Oh," Jeff scribbled furiously "how sweet." * * * She had wanted to do this alone. Just one last chance, before things got too broken. Regan Guest handed the driver a twenty dollar bill, and told him to keep the change. She stepped out of the cab and was immediately hit with a blast of cold air. She was a confrontational person, a fierce person when the situation called for it, but there was a part of her that felt five years old. There was a part of her that wanted to chase after the cab and get out of here any way she could. She took a deep breath in, and pushed that desire deep inside of her. It was as if she swallowed it. Her thin frame was concealed by a black coat with large buttons. She swept her black hair behind her pale features, and took another deep breath. Regan began walking down Summer St. She tried not to stare at the city block, for it was an open wound. There was an abandoned grocery store, and too few letters remained on the sign to discern its previous title. Desperate faces peaked at her out the windows of run down apartment complexes. Cleveland was no Paris, but not all of it was like this. [detail withheld] sat in the distance, unassuming and treacherous at the same time. No one could remember what exactly it had been used for previously. It was not a hardware store, it was not a bar. It had just always been there, but for what? The building was as gray and lifeless as the sky behind it. It was two stories of uselessness, and purpose. As her destination got closer, she could see a woman in a brown coat huddled in front of it. There was a shopping cart full of blankets and bags next to her. As Regan got closer, the street person became more rigid, and eventually stood up. The woman’s hood concealed most of her stringy red hair. "Don’t go in there!" the woman pleaded, barely within ear shot. "Don’t go in the bad dream house!" Regan kept her head down, avoiding eye contact. But the homeless woman’s voice simply grew in volume. "I am not joking! I don’t know any jokes!" As Regan got closer still she could see that there were tears in the woman’s eyes. She stared at Regan with a certain lack of understanding, a desperate bewilderment. "Excuse me," Regan offered as she passed. The homeless woman shuffled to the side, sat down on the front step of 1411 Summer, and buried her head in her hands. The woman rocked back and forth slowly. "Midway upon the road of your life you found yourself within a dark wood, for the right way had been missed," were the final sorrowful words out of the homeless woman’s mouth, muffled through her hands. The quote sounded vaguely familiar to Regan, perhaps from school. But she only dwelled on it for a second before she ascended up a few stairs to the front door. Her hand lingered on the doorknob for a few moments, and in those moments she wished that Lane was here. The deal she had made with him, trading sexual favors for him bailing her out of trouble, had disgusted her on more than one occasion. But now, all she could think of is how there was no reason to try this alone one last time. There was no reason to brave, no reason in the whole world to ever be brave again. She swallowed this feeling too, and turned the doorknob. As the door creaked open the homeless woman openly wept, seemingly stuck to the stairs, unable to move on. The door closed behind Regan. "Ugh," she said aloud. The first thing she noticed was the stench, the image that leaped into her mind was that of rotting deer, stacked like firewood. The smell invaded her nostrils and refused to leave. The second thing she noticed was the dusty checkered staircase directly before her, leading to the 2nd floor. It dominated the entire small room. On either side of it a lamp sat on a circular table, and only one of them worked. This gave the lighting in the room a certain uneven quality. The wooden railing seemed like a splinter waiting to happen. And these stains on the ground, what were these stains? "Hello?" Regan called out, and immediately felt silly. She had been here before. She knew where he was. Methodically she began to venture up. The top of the staircase intersected with a hallway. Straight ahead the wallpaper was peeling and someone had written "How can you pray?" on the wall with an arrow pointing about three feet lower, where a different person had scrawled "When you have no God". She had never been to the left. It was dark that way, impossibly dark. In previous trips, she could have sworn she heard a faint combination of laughter and crying coming from that direction, but never cared to investigate it. To the right there was a door, and in front of it sat a welcome mat. It said, "Come on in, stay a while" with a yellow smiley face, seemingly disfigured by another stain. She knocked on the door. * * * "Off the record?" The River Rat started, "Problems with a loan shark. Some creepy asshole, pretty sure I’m going to have to handle this my way. Bitch thinks she can just go in there and play let’s make a deal." "I see," Coltz replied, and as his interview subject had requested, wrote nothing. To their left was a river port where the Cuyahoga River met Lake Erie. A few pictures were snapped. The tour guide was rattling off statistics that didn’t mean anything to Stevens. He squinted at it, as if trying to discern some greater meaning out of the sight. He saw no dancing girls or flashing lights of any kind. "At some point on this ride, they should have some kind of alien ship attack the trolley, and we have to defend it with turret guns or something. They got a suggestion box on this thing?" "This isn’t a ride, or a video game for that matter." "Meh," Desade’s WMD offered, as if it were an actual rebuttal. Jeff Coltz needed to get this back on track. He cleared his throat, and hit a button on the small recording device. "So, Lane. You advanced in the first round…now you have what some are calling the marquee match-up of round two. You face a man in Clinton Sage whom you served under in The Affliction, a group designed to take FUSE to its knees. The wrestling ring aside, what are your feelings about this man personally?" Stevens shrugged. "He’s a sick fuck, ya know. Always will be, no matter what kind of window dressing he puts on it." "And about that, it does appear the man has made some changes in his life. He has settled down and started a family…have you been in touch with him since he welcomed his child into the world?" The River Rat chuckled. "We never played golf Jeffrey, we didn’t send each other Christmas cards. I was in my position to make his life easier, to help him keep his spot at the top. He was in his position because…well, he was really fucking good. He was a better wrestler than me, better than anyone, except maybe Rhine at the end." "And do you still think he’s better?" Stevens shifted in his seat, facing the interviewer for the first time in at least a half hour. At first he didn’t say anything; he just stared a hole into Coltz and bit his lower lip. "I said he was a better wrestler than me, I didn’t say he was better at surviving, at winning." "The win/loss records in FUSE would certainly beg to differ with that statement." Lane giggled, and took out a cigar. "Then I’m afraid we don’t have the same definition of winning, buddy." "Would you care to elaborate on that statement?" "If you have to ask, you’ll never know," Lane said, and a smile flashed across his face for a moment. The tour guide was saying something about the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame. It was to the right, on Cleveland’s North Coast Harbor. There were many more pictures snapped for this attraction, than the last. Lane stole a peak at Coltz, and what he saw was frustration. He started to speak again when the tour guide stopped. "Clinton Sage knows in his heart of hearts that just because some bitch popped a kid out, it doesn’t change the things he’s done. He is running on borrowed time. The word redemption is…well, it’s just a word. It’s just a concept we’ve made up to make it easier to forgive people that we were going to forgive anyway, usually ourselves. It’s childish, and I don’t have time for it. The buzzards will pick the bones off his brat if they have to, they will rob the very concept of hope from her little fucking head. Hell, I’d do it for free…just to give him his nuts back. Everyone’s gone all faggy, and I’m sick of it." "What about all the things you’ve done? Don’t you worry how it ends for you?" The Warlord took a long puff of his cigar. He knew it was a matter of moments before someone attempted to make him put it out. "Only thing I’m afraid of is being bored," Lane replied, and laughed his damn head off. The wind took cigar smoke into Jeff’s eyes, and when he closed them all he could see was his own daughter on the day she was born. * * * She did not care for his office. She did not care for it one bit. Or was it his bedroom? She did not know. The walls were covered with the same mysterious stains that dotted the rest of the house. On one wall someone had written, "As I was walking up the stairs, I met a man who wasn’t there." An arrow was drawn halfway across the room, and pointed to another block of text: "He wasn’t there again today. I wish to god he’d go away!" In the center of the room there was an oak desk. On said desk was a lamp, and a comic book that was labeled "Equinox". She was not a comic book fan, but even so, it seemed out of place here. Behind the desk sat the man in the cowboy hat. There was no computer, no documents, nothing to amuse himself with but the sights and sounds of the plain room. It seemed as thought he had been sitting there, staring at the door with nothing to do for an eternity. "Mr. Darkhat," Regan started, and nodded. She was standing in the doorway. "Bernie Blackcap is the name, how can I be of service to ya?" the Cowboy offered in a chipper voice, his southern drawl never more apparent. She knew for a fact that he had called himself Dennis Darkhat the previous times she had met this man, but she was not going to make an issue out of it now. "Mr. Blackcap, my name is Regan Guest." "Born on the fourth of July," the Cowboy remarked. "Yes, yessir" she said, and he was indeed correct. "I’m here to discuss my loan." "Hmm," he stroked his chin thoughtfully, although to Regan it didn’t look like thought. It looked like a show. "I know my payment history has not been exemplary but…if you can just give me some more time, and give me a little more capital, I can re-open the restaurant and pay you back all of it. I have ideas, I can make it unique and…" she trailed off for a second, "I can’t give up on this." Regan felt as if he could sense the desperation in her voice, that he could feed on it. Yet his face did not even twitch. He was not smiling, but it was as if his previous grins were so hideous they left a stain on his face. "Please," she whispered. "Well little lady, I appreciate you coming here. And I want to help you, I do. But if I made an exception for you…then I’d have to make one for everyone. And by golly, that just ain’t good for business." "But you’ve made exceptions for people before! I know you have!" Regan screamed suddenly, and felt self conscious. The Cowboy frowned, and looked disappointed the way a parent might in the face of a bad report card. "I’m just going to ignore that, cause I know you didn’t come all the way down here to tell me how to do my job," he said, and for a moment bared his teeth. There was one discolored one on the bottom. She was surprised at herself as she got down on her knees. "I need the restaurant! The life I’ve started to live I…" Regan felt tears coming, and battled them as hard as she could. Her voice came back more determined. "I need the restaurant. If you give me one more chance…" "If If’s and buts were candy and nuts, it’d be Christmas everyday," Bernie Blackcap stated, his face twisting into his best impression of sympathy. "Now run along sugar pie. You’ll figure this out. You’ll find the money. You’re a smart girl." Bernie pointed behind him, where a clock she did not notice previously was attached to the wall. "But as they say, it’s ticking." She slowly rose from her kneeling position, her fear of this house all but gone. The few tears that had escaped from her eyes were now dried on her face. "You will regret this," Regan seethed. "Oh, I doubt that," The Cowboy replied, and smiled politely. * * * Lane Stevens and Regan Guest sat next to each other in the back seat of a limo. The River Rat had his arm awkwardly draped over his…well, what do you call her anyway? Despite the mild weather, she shivered. She had recounted her entire day to the River Rat, and had left nothing out in regards to the meeting with the Cowboy. Regan felt unsure of how Lane would handle it, because part of her desperation clearly was a desire to get out of her new life with Stevens. But he said nothing about that. His mind processed everything, and after five minutes of silence… "I have an idea," Lane offered. * * * Clinton, IA – June 1st, 1987 Down the road he would be called an asshole, a heathen, a sadist, and many other less than flattering terms. But on this day he was just a boy sitting in the front yard of his parent’s house wearing a Snoopy shirt. "It’s a bridge," Lane Stevens carefully explained to his caterpillar. His pet lived in an old ice cream bucket. His mother had poked holes in the lid, and he had decked it out to assemble what he thought a caterpillar’s ideal habitat would be. The bridge he was referring to was a stick that connected two rocks. Stevens set the creature on the stick, and smiled as it started to navigate to the other side. From the window Lane’s mother Audrey touched Jerry’s shoulder, as if to stop him from interjecting when their son pulled fistfuls of grass from the front yard for the bucket. A moment later they vacated the window and resumed preparing dinner. "This is where you play, this is where you sleep. See?" Lane’s guided tour was interrupted by a voice he knew all too well. "Hey dick licker." Stevens looked up to see Brian Hanzer, an ill tempered eleven year old from down the street. He was a stocky blonde haired kid that took great pleasure in bothering people. He was leaning over the fence that contained the front yard, a satisfied smirk present on his face. "Hi," Lane muttered, looking back down into the bucket. He nervously shifted the grass around. "How’s your only friend doing?" Brian asked, and snickered. Lane said nothing. "Next time you leave for school, I’m going to go into your garage and smash that little furry thing. What do you think about that fuckface?" Lane still said nothing. "Later, loser." * * * Now. "What does this story have to do with me?" Regan asked. Her man’s eyes were out the window, watching Cleveland fly by. "You’ll see," Lane said. "Where are we going?" she asked. "You’ll see." * * * Then. Jerry Stevens was heading outside to tell his son that it was dinner time. He was surprised to find that instead of playing with his caterpillar, Lane was standing in the driveway. "Hey buddy, pork chops are ready." As he approached, he quickly realized something was wrong. His son was crying. "What’s wrong?" Jerry asked. Lane’s sniffing quickly turned to full blown wailing as he slowly lifted up his shoe to reveal a crushed caterpillar beneath it. "What…" was the only word Jerry could get out. "I couldn’t let him!" Lane cried out. "I couldn’t let him kill Fuzzy!" "Jesus Christ," his father whispered, not fully understanding what had just happened, and embraced Lane. Jerry Stevens quietly thanked God that he was still human, because he almost cried for the first time he could remember. * * * Now. Night had fallen on Cleveland. Regan Guest and Lane Stevens stood side by side outside of what was formerly known as "The Cottage Café". The signs had been taken down, but the setup inside was still the same. There were tables, booths, a counter, etc. The building was nestled between a few apartment complexes, and across the street there was a bakery that had been closed for some eight hours or so. The street was deathly quiet. "Katsidy wanted me to be just like her," Regan said. "You don’t got the tits for it," Lane shot back. The stood in silence for a few moments. The River Rat fiddled with his duffel bag. The look on Regan’s face was that of realization. The world that she wanted to make for herself had slid away, and the problem was she could still see it there, struggling. She didn’t like to show emotion, or act afraid. But this had been a tough day. The words that came out of her mouth next were hard. "I’m scared, Lane." The darkness concealed his thin smile. "Well, you’re with me now. There ain’t no reason to be scared." And a small part of her believed him. A small part of her felt just a little warmer on this cool Cleveland night. She turned her head to see Lane Stevens pulling a small gas can out of the duffel bag. "Sometimes you got to show people that they can’t hurt you," The River Rat said, "that you have nothing left to take." Later, while the Cottage Café burned to the ground, she wouldn’t even turn to look at it.
