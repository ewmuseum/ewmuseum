Act VIII: Swan Song For A Martyr; The Dark Wherein Chaos Reigns

7 February 2010
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2010-02-07 There is never a good day for a funeral. Such a morbid ritual only serves to darken even the sunniest of days, for death is nothing if not a herald of the morose and the macabre. The loss they assembled to mourn was a tragic one to man. The even greater tragedy was that so few knew of it, or ever would, and even fewer knew how great a loss it was. Marcus Stryker had perished alone, his widow being war and the closest he had to children being those he led into the armed conflict he had dedicated his life to. Intimately, he knew only Blackhelm. And he’d given his life to serve it, and in turn, the world that would never know to thank him for saving it from the many atrocities he’d prevented. Alpha Strike team stood at attention on one side of the coffin as it came to rest at the feet of its military bearers. Vanessa Wilder shed her tears freely; she was a warrior, gritty and callous to the horrors of warfare, but to her Marcus Stryker had been as a brother and even a father. She’d had little parental upbringing, and Marcus had served to guide her along her path in life. It was all she could muster inside to retain any scrap of military bearing as the priest readied himself to utter the eulogy. The despair was no more hidden across her comrades; the bonds forged in the hell of war stretched beyond anything most could ever understand. Stryker’s death had brought them out in force. The organization’s top commanders had arrived to pay their respects, men who knew the man only by deed and reputation. Alongside the now leaderless Alpha Strike team, Maury Dawson fought to contain his own emotions, overcome with memories of a man to which glory was as empty, as hollow as the ceremonial ribbons and medals they gave to the politicians of warfare. Even Gabriel, the lone representative of the shadowy top of the hierarchy, appeared at his side. Stone-faced but attempting solidarity with those of his order, it was a courtesy he felt the need to extend to the grieving loyal. The priest stepped up. The somber crowd, heavy hearts aching in their chests, shuffled woefully in that cold February cemetery. It was a bad day for a funeral. There was never a good one. “It’s been said that while every man dies, not every man truly lives. If the measure of a man is in not only whether he did truly live, but how he spent that time, then Major Marcus Stryker should be considered a giant among men. A titan who touched the lives of all who knew him and served to also protect those whom he would never know in his lifetime. He did so with the spirit of a philanthropist, of a brother, of a man determined to see the world blossom with the kind of virtue we should all strive for. He set an example that all who met him should spread with his unfortunate parting.” A breeze began to blow, thrusting the grey skies into movement. Clouds shifted overhead as the wind whistled in the barren trees, a strange kind of high-pitched symphony, the goddess Gaia singing her own hymn of mourning for a fallen champion of a world nearing its demise. “He cherished his friends, those under his command, and any who likewise wanted to see mankind prosper, break free of the chains of sin and slaughter and oppression. And for any that opposed those beliefs, the villains and scoundrels of the world, he was forever at the frontline with a willingness to give his life in defense of those ideals.” The speech went on, giving the crowd insights to the major’s early life, his service, the commendations he never cared an iota for. It did little to touch on his illustrious sacrifices, the plots he’d stopped before they could have killed thousands. Perhaps the most heart-wrenching detail of the ceremony was just that: what it omitted. Maury Dawson was sullen as he watched the flag draped over the casket, the last farewell to a friend and ally. There was a 21-gun salute, the other rituals of military burial, and all of the individual goodbyes. It was a long and sullen process. “Rest in peace, Marcus. I vow to you that they’ll pay. Bruce, Luther, and every bastard who follows them. This won’t end without justice.” The coffin was gently lowered into the grave as the military attendees came to attention, saluting their fallen brother. Even if Major Stryker’s death hadn’t come with meritorious significance, it came with valor and with honor. At least in small part, it was the funeral he deserved. “A cross, heavy with the ashen robes of the wicked,” spoke Dawson in a hush, the first snow flakes beginning to fall. Gabriel lowered his head at an angle, listening. “Now I see the truth behind Kerry’s vision. His final, terrible vision.” There had been more to it. But they could only pray that his final revelation was his first inaccurate one. * * * * He marched fearlessly through the tombstones and knelt at the newly-filled grave. In one hand he held a single rose, its delicate beauty illuminated under a swelled and gleaming moon, a bright color contrast set against the blanket of white covering the ground and the darkness of night enveloping the land. In his other was an antique pistol, a Colt Single Action Army, its grip ebony heartwood. “I knew you’d come.” The voice was Russell Slade, the looming sergeant. He was still in his formal attire, the suit he’d worn at the burial. But a belt was strapped around his waist and from it dangled a holster, and in that was his sidearm. A .45 caliber pistol. His last resort. Maury Dawson emerged at his side. They knew little of one another, an agent operating in the background and a field soldier, Slade and the other being far more intimate in their mutual encounters with the ravenous force called death. Exhaling a cloud of breath into the winter, Luther Ridley lowered the hood of his parka. “I always honor the dead, sergeant. Most especially those devout in the ways of war.” He placed the rose on the headstone marking his enemy’s place of rest, the handgun beside it. Stenciled across the marker was a quotation: Heart, Courage, Righteousness. Forever In Our Hearts, Forever Vigilant. “He was a fine soldier. Among the best I‘ve seen. He simply…wasn’t the one I’ve been searching for. His martial prowess was commendable, but not the apex. Not perfection. It wasn’t enough to release me from my duty.” Slade and Dawson were astounded by the attitude of Luther Ridley. The man commanded a legion of killers and was posted as their sworn enemy as long as the Sect funded his activities. In Ridley the hulking NCO especially saw a man born into an age of war that ill suited him; he was the type that valued tactics, toasted the cunning of his adversaries, and saluted the fallen. An anachronism that should have been directing cavalry and muskets as opposed to lugging high-powered weapons and directing assassinations for the ruthless monsters of the world. “He belongs in the most hallowed of graves in Arlington,” he continued. “But you know as well as I do that that can’t be. Such is the price for what we do, warring for the soul of a world that doesn‘t know that we even exist. Or, for that matter, that it has a soul to fight for in the first place.” “We took the oath when we joined Blackhelm. We knew the risks and that there would be little reward in the end. There’d be no medals or commendations. We’re just men, doing what we can.” “Men impeding an entire planet’s march to its own grave. I salute you for that, Sergeant Slade. But there‘s something you must know.” He lowered himself onto another tombstone, twiddling with something metallic in his pocket. From it he drew a lighter, a strangely engraved silver Zippo, and a cigarette. Lighting it, he inhaled deeply before expelling a thick cloud into the night, the smoke mingling with the lightly falling snow and the mist unfurling from his exposed skin. “You don’t strike me as a smoker,” Dawson said. Something decidedly inhuman flickered in Ridley’s eyes, a trick of the light he decided. A parodied attempt at a smile. Like Gabriel, Maury got the same feeling that little of the emotion he knew so well was likewise a driving force in the captain. The business of war had a war of hardening men to caricatures of what they once were. “Forgive me, Agent Dawson, but I exist in a world where my life can be ended at any moment by a bullet, aimed or stray. I think these little sticks are the least of my worries. I don’t have much fear of disease, of cancer. I have far larger problems.” He stopped, tapping ashes into a smoking spot at his feet. He was strangely casual about facing down two of his most dire foes, himself unarmed and so soon after murdering one of their great champions. It was as if he felt shielded from their wrath, or knew something that contributed to his self-professed safety. Or perhaps it was simple vanity, knowing well his combat prowess. “Rourke has no strategic value,” he said. “Not to you, not to my employer. He’s a cretin that babbles in illusionary tongues and blurts out riddles with no answer.” He chuckled. It was a strange sound coming from such a dark and diehard crusader. “Bruce indoctrinated him, but may have gone overboard and crippled his brain. It’s hard to say. Capturing that whackjob hasn’t gained anything of value. He was bait. The only thing strategic about Rourke was his usefulness as a piece of meat to lure in the prey. Only what we placed in him.” Slade silently cursed Luther Ridley for his honesty. It hurt more than any wound he‘d taken in the field. Dawson could see the pain in the interim leader’s grief-stricken eyes; he knew Marcus was deserving of a more purposeful death, and him being denied that honor was an injustice without pardon. Ridley recognized the hurt consuming them. But he didn‘t care. “It’s painful. What I’m saying is that Major Stryker died for nothing. He fell on the battlefield, a warrior‘s death, but in pursuit of a cause that his enemy manufactured for him to chase. He walked directly into the cage we made ready for him. From there, all we had to do was slit his throat. That was my job, and I carried it out just as my benefactor directed me to. As he paid me to do.” “Were you truthful with Marcus before you killed him?” Slade boomed. “I overheard everything you said. At Sterling’s estate, the cabin when we first saw Rourke.” Ridley cocked his head, the falling snow coming to rest on his hat like cherry blossoms accentuated under the bright moon. “About ‘never knowing when HQ will issue your final orders’. Asking him to ‘set you free.’” “I’m a man encumbered by a duty I cannot refuse, and burdened by the myths spoken of me. That is all you can know of me, sergeant.” Pausing, he sniffed the air, eyes pulling left to flal on a tree swaying on the far aside of the cemetery. “Even now my men are near. I go nowhere without them. Such is my burden.” “Cut the bullshit, you fucking grease ball!” roared the mammoth soldier. It was a minor miracle he hadn’t already drawn his sidearm and emptied the clip into the strangely aloof Seraph leader. “Are the stories true or not?” “There are so many…” “The stories say you lead a legion of the damned,” recalled Dawson, giving Slade time to breathe, gather his wits. They didn’t need another fight so soon. “Wraiths with no past and no name, fiends from the dark side, soulless things built to kill and corrupt. They say you sold your soul for power, out of some craving for mastery in the ways of war. And this is your penance. Leading them from the dark abyss with your knowledge and skills forever as some asset of the unholy.” Beaming with pride, Luther seemed charmed by his own legendary profile. The guise of humility fell apart under a smug grin. “There’s more than just that. Indulge me further with the tales of this black-hearted seeker of strife, Agent Dawson.” “You’re ancient. You’ve commanded in every American conflict dating back to the Revolutionary War, and always as a mercenary. You killed on both sides, on every side. No rhyme, no reasons, sewing the seeds of chaos and death wherever you go.” “I’m only a soldier. What else do you want from me?” “‘He that humbleth himself wishes to be exalted.’,” Dawson replied, countering the other’s mock submission. “Nietzsche. Tell me, Luther…is that why you sold your soul? Was that why you wanted mastery in war? For glory and reverence? Was it so empty of an ambition as that, or was there more to it?” Ridley’s face was like stone. He offered not a clue as to whether Maury’s accusations were true, and if the latter had to bet, he’d gamble that Ridley preferred to go to his grave before openly stating about the accuracy of the remarks as pertaining to “Luther Ridley, The Damned‘. Finishing his cigarette and extinguishing the remains in the snow, the Seraph pulled his parka close again. “His Last Supper is set to commence. He‘ll be yours for the taking.” Captain Ridley swiveled, a swift and flawless military maneuver, and marched away. Can you deal with him them, a demon king resurrected?” Clouds passed in front of the moon, carpeting the graveyard in a fleeting darkness. When everything was illuminated again, Luther had disappeared. “We need to rebuild.” Slade’s temper had steadied. Already he pondered revenge, the image of jamming Ridley’s head onto a pike. Maury hoped he’d have the chance. He hoped the world would stay intact for him to exact it. Tully sent another Yakuza plummeting to the ground, his head smashed like a rotten melon off of the towering Buddhist fountain in the garden. Even then, from so many stories up, there was no chance of survival. His personal guards popped around the corner, blasting another of the Yakuza in the chest. The Japanese gunman fell into a garden pond, clouding the water scarlet as he floated face-down through the bright lilies. Another joined him moments later. He stalked up the steps, decimating anyone in his way with the strength of his fists, the resonating authority of his gun. Gangsters lay in bloody heaps, increasingly close together as they neared the objective. It was in a simple glass case, though as a symbol of victory or an instrument for the Yakuza’s gain was anyone’s guess. Death surrounded him on all sides, but as he smashed out the glass, Horace Tully felt frozen in time, tranquil. Perhaps he was; the stone seemed to pulsate in his hand and few knew of all of its capabilities. Shanahan did, the conniving and stubborn bastard that he was. And while he and Brother Knight still searched laboriously to unlock its secrets, the important thing was that their Boston-born ex-master didn’t have it. Surely it would only be a matter of time before he’d sent Ridley, or any one of his other hand-picked enforcer units. But now it belonged elsewhere. Conjecture was that he couldn’t execute all of his plans without it. They were banking on that. Otherwise their betrayal would prove disastrous. They may end up groveling at the feet of the man their designs were intended to overthrow. In the background, a bullet ruptured the corner of a television sprawled across the back wall. Tully, still engrossed in reading the fine intricacies of the stone, paid no attention to the urgent news broadcast; his limited Japanese barred him from understanding the frantic female news anchor anyhow. “Let’s go,” he ordered. They marched from the room, stepping over the splayed corpses of the Mizakawa Yakuza, just as the news feed changed from the horrified studio to a scene outside the capital of Tokyo. More riots, more destruction, and even the Japanese were being swept up in the madness. Those left rational warned to stay in their homes, do not panic, lock their doors. Static, and another feed change. A prominent art museum, one of its prized statues gone missing during the course of the night. Guards cut to ribbons, no fingerprints or leads. Only trail is a slick blood trail leading from the guards and out the door. Footprints outlined in blood, oddly enough, are only those of the reptilian statue… Static. The feed changes. Suicides increase sharply in past two days. People nationwide awaken from night terrors, beseeching their loved ones to save them from the slavering god that lumbers like a mountain, before throwing themselves from windows in gory acts of “self-soul preservation”… Static.
