Title: Have A Little Faith Wrestler: Syko Arsonist The first thing that greets patrons to the Red Rooster Bar on 44th street in Times Square is a mannequin of an Irish drunk wearing suspenders and holding two mugs of ale, sporting a flame-colored Mohawk . He’s grinning far wider than any man could possibly do – it is a mannequin – and it’s hard to judge whether it’s an invitation or a warning. The inside of the bar is splattered in turn of the century European architecture and décor. The tables are covered in European newspapers held firmly down by thick glass. Everything on the menu seems to only have one size: large. Ceiling tube lights give the inside its red glow. Last call, the barkeep yells. A few patrons still linger at the bar with their lukewarm drinks. Come on buddy, the barkeep says tapping a patron asleep on the counter with his own drool as a pillow. The guy wakes up, wiping the side of his face on his sleeve. What do ya want, he slurs. Last call, time to go. Just gimme another one. The barkeep shakes his head. The man pulls a crumbled up ten from his pocket and puts it on the table. Whatever ya got, man. Your money’s no good here son, the barkeep says pushing the ten back towards the man. The man lets out a small chuckle that sounds half like a sob. I just want a drink, man. The barkeep shakes his head solemnly at the man. In his lifetime, he’s seen every face there is to see in those who don’t know when to quit. And he is positive the face this man wears is one of regret and remorse. He had done something, or something was done to him that he couldn’t forget or forgive. Whatever it is that’s got you in here every night, it’s going to change. The barkeep picks up some spare glasses and wipes them with a white linen cloth. The man laughs. Right, because everything always works out in the end, right? Because good things come to those who wait? You know why people say those things, the barkeep asks. Because they’re true. It may never happen the way you want to, or when you want it to, but things always get better in the end. All you need is a little faith. Faith, the man asks, raising his eyebrow. I had Faith. Once. I think. She was a good girl. Too demanding though. The barkeep isn’t amused. You need to believe in it. Believe in God. I tried that once, the man says shaking his head and waving his hands in front of him for emphasis. That ain’t for me. Even if I suddenly decided to talk to God, I wouldn’t even know what to say. Maybe you’ve been talking to God your entire life, and you haven’t realized it. God must be the worst listener in the universe, the man replies. The man stands up gingerly off the bar stool and puts his ten dollars back into his pocket. He stumbles a bit, but manages not to topple over or trip himself. You going to be all right, the barkeep asks, noticing the man’s legs ripple like they’re made of jell-o. The man nods. Yeah, I’ll be fine. I meant to ask you if we’ve ever met before, the barkeep says. You definitely got that familiar face. I get that a lot. What’s your name? Just call me Syko. Psycho, the barkeep asks. That’s quiet an unusual name. You should hear my last name. Syko Arsonist walks out of the Red Rooster Bar before the barkeep has a chance. Syko takes out a Marlboro cigarette, lights it, and begins to smoke. A few paces down towards Fifth Avenue and rain begins to fall down on the city. Syko looks up at the clear night sky, mostly illuminated by the giant neon signs of Times Square, and lets the rain fall on his face. He sighs. He gets into his car, a 1968 Camaro painted red, and drives home. # A black Infinity speeds down Lexington Ave. It has a new paint job, the coating glistening in the moonlight. A blond woman with bangs drives the car, wearing a white spaghetti top. She is thirty-two years old. The woman’s daughter is riding in the backseat, trying to fight off sleep. She is twelve. I can’t believe you actually convinced me to watch that late night movie. But it was good, wasn’t it, the girl asks, rubbing her eyes. Mandy, that’s not the point, the woman says looking into the rear view mirror. You know you have Sunday school tomorrow morning. This morning, rather. I’m not the one who bought the tickets, mom. I just forgot what time it was, mom says. It’s kind of hard to judge time when everything’s so bright here. But you liked the movie, right? I thought Coraline was a good movie, yes, she says taking a left, towards Fifth Avenue. Mmmhmm, Mandy says and starts to lay down on the backseat. The black Infinity comes to a red light at Fifth Avenue. The mother looks towards both sides and doesn’t see any cars approaching. As the light begins to flash back towards green, she slowly keeps ahead to get a head start on the light change. She is practically halfway across the intersection before the light finally turns green for her. What was your favorite part? Mom turns back and before she can reply, Mandy yells and the Infinity is struck on the driver’s side. The sickening clash of steel against steel resonates in the intersection and onlookers rush towards the scene. # Oh, Jesus Christ. Jesus didn’t do this. How old is she? Someone get the jaws of life! Omigod, are they even alive? What about the other car? Is he dead? Syko slowly begins to regain conscious. A terrible headache makes him regret waking up. He struggles to focus his sight, but everything seems blurry. Red and blue lights flash all around him. Syko looks ahead, and realizes his entire front window is smashed. Sir, are you all right, someone yells. Syko can barely hear it with the ringing in his ears. He shakes his head. He puts his hand to his head. It comes back blanketed in blood. He has a huge gaping cut across his forehead. Sir, just hold still, someone yells. Syko can barely reply or even move. He turns to try his door, but stops when he hears a high screeching sound of steel being cut open. It is then that Syko realizes his car is crumbled up like an accordion and his door is latched tight, impossible to be opened from the inside. Syko watches as a firefighter uses the jaws of life and pries apart the hinges of the door, making an opening for him. Get him out of there! Two firefighters grab Syko by the chest and arms and drag him out of the vehicle. The second firefighter grabs his legs and they carry Syko over to the sidewalk and put him on a stretcher. Syko immediately tries to get up before they strap him down. Sir, you need to lay down! Syko slurs that he’s fine. He can walk. He sits up to prove his point and starts to get back to his feet. The firefighters urge him to lay back down, but Syko persists. A police officer walks up to Syko and studies his eyes. Syko tries to look away, but his actions are clumsy. Syko throws up. I need you to take this in your mouth and blow on the tube, the officer says, dangling a breathalyzer in front of Syko. Syko gingerly grabs the tube between his fingers and blows on it. It’s barely an exhale, but it’s enough to raise the meter on the analyzer. That’s what I was afraid of, the police officer mumbles. You’re fine, right? Syko nods. I feel a bit dizzy, but yeah I’m fine. That’s good, the officer says putting a hand on Syko’s shoulder. It’s good that you have all that alcohol in your blood, crashed into another car, and only have a cut on your head. It’s good that with all that, all you feel is a bit dizzy. I guess I’m lucky. You’re a piece of shit. Excuse me? Thank God you’re fine, because look at what you could have been. The police officer directs Syko’s gaze to the black Infinity cut in half, dangling by bits of steel and upholstery. Syko’s heart rate quickens. He looks around frantically for signs of the people inside the car. Finally he spots two stretchers. Two females. One young, one old. The young one has heavy bandages around her face, and her eyes are covered in cotton swabs, drenched with blood. Syko begins to hyper ventilate. This isn’t how this was supposed to go. Couldn’t he ever get a break? Starting to feel sorry, the police officer asks. That’s a good start. I-I-, Syko stammers. Why does the girl have all that on her face? The police officer looks down. He looks pitiful. He says, she was laying in the backseat when you hit them. The impact shattered all the windows and the glass flew inside the car. She was staring straight at you when the glass pierced her eyes. The poor little girl was shrieking so loudly when we got here, I’ve never been so scared in my life. We gave her a sedative to calm her down, so we could get the shards out of her eyes. Syko throws up some more. The police officer puts Syko’s hands behind him and cuffs him. You’re going to jail, son. And considering what could have happened, you’re getting off free. Are they, Syko begins to ask. No, the officer replies. Luckily, both of them will live. # A year had passed since that crash. Syko was charged in reckless driving, driving under the influence, assault, and endangering the lives of others. It was a miracle that none of the victims – Mandy or her mother Cindy – died. Syko’s sentence was reduced on good behavior and the fact that he agreed to pay for all of the victims medical bills. Syko served 11 months in jail. Cindy suffered a broken collar bone, broken hip, and a broken left arm. Her recovery was slow and painful. Mandy suffered extreme lacerations to her eyes, two broken knees, and mostly cuts and abrasions to her face from the window shards ricocheting at her face during the crash. She recovered faster than her mother, but now had eyesight problems. Syko walks down 73rd street on East Elmhurst. He found out where Mandy and her mother lived and after a month of debating had finally decided to make a personal visit. They had talked a few times, but all of it was during trial and under the light of a judge and jury. He wanted to talk to the family as a regular human being, so they could see that he was truly sorry and not just a defendant trying to get a reduced sentence. So here he was, headed towards 736 73rd Street. He wasn’t sure what would come out of it, or if they even wanted to see him. He knew he wanted to apologize, but didn’t know what else to say or do. He had already paid their medical bills, and offered to give them a generous donation as an act of forgiveness. But the Mercers declined the offer. Seven thirty two, Syko says aloud, recalling the address. Should be about two houses down. Syko stopped at 734. He stared straight ahead, stiff as a cock. Eyes wide, like saucers. The front lawn of the white-painted brick house was littered with police officers and forensics specialists. Some of the police officers were interviewing the neighbors. They tried to answer as best as they can between sobs. In the distance, Syko can barely make out the silhouettes of bodies being zipped up in black bags. His heart sinks. Leaning as far as he could on the cautionary tapes, Syko yells for someone to give him some information. A police officer guarding the perimeter approaches him and asks him to leave the scene. What happened here? An entire family was murdered. Everyone? Syko was afraid of the answer. In his gut, he knew the worst had happened. Everyone that was inside, the officer replies. Syko begins to get teary eyed. The officer looks at him. Did you know the family? Yes, I did. Syko lies. Well, not that much really. We have a history together. You a relative? No, Syko admits. But why do you ask? The officer sighs. I said everyone inside died, but I don’t think that everyone was inside. There’s a little girl missing. We think…we hope that she’s at school. Syko lets out a weak laugh. She didn’t die? The officer smiles a bit. She’s all right, we hope. But I sure would not want to be here when her school bus shows up. I have no idea how you tell a little girl her entire family is dead. I think you’re wish came a little late, Syko says, shaking his head. Tears flow down his cheeks. He points behind the officer. The officer turns to see the school bus arriving in front of the house. Oh fuck, the officer replies. What the fuck am I going to say to her? How did it happen? Attempted robbery. Some lowlife found out that these guys just got a huge settlement from a lawsuit and wanted the money. He shot the father, not knowing that the wife and the other kids were in the house. So he panicked and started to kill them all. Six bodies total. Father, mother, two young kids, and I believe their uncle and aunt. Syko looks up at the sky as if to say what the fuck do you want, God? He doesn’t get a chance to ask. Mandy’s cries resonates across the lawn. The 13 year old realizes what has happened. Mandy rushes towards the house, but she’s stopped by two police officers, who pick her up. Mandy flails her arms and legs, crying hysterically. She doesn’t deserve this, Syko mutters. No she doesn’t, the officer replies. We ran a background check on the family. What happened? I think this was it, the officer replies. This was all she had. And that was all Syko needed to hear to have his faith shattered. Whatever ounce of faith he may have had was obliterated with that remark. He felt regret when he killed the stripper, Lilly, two weeks ago. He felt bad for a lot of things he did. But this time, this was the first time when he felt true remorse. The truth was that he wanted to visit the Mercers, because he wanted a real chance at redemption. Giving them money was not enough, he wanted to be there for them, to prove to himself that he was ready to change his ways. But he was never given that chance. And now here he was leaving the scene of a crime, leaving a poor little girl all alone in the world. He knew how hard it was to live alone, completely alone. He knew the struggles he faced everyday knowing that no one was out there for you, no matter how hard you looked. He was lucky, he had money, he had an outlet. He had wrestling. This girl. What does she have? School? Foster care? She has nothing but lies and false hope ahead of her. She has a life of misery ahead of her, a life full of hate and disgrace. All this, because some idiot decided that he was better than everyone around him. Syko walks away from the front lawn, trying to drown out the sounds of crying and hysteria. She's going to be all alone, Syko thinks. She's going to end up dead in a ditch, with the scars and bruises of a life of servitude. Syko sighs. He looks back towards the lawn, and gazes at Mandy who is calming down, surrounded by a legion of officers. She's going to be alone, Syko thinks. Then he begins to walk back towards the lawn, towards a lost little girl with nothing left for her. Or maybe, she does have something left. Maybe all isn't lost for her. Or for Syko. Everything happens for a reason, you just need a little faith. No, she won't be alone, Syko says.
