The Low Road

28 November 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2005-11-28 GTT4. As the defending champion, he was obligated to enter, and while there were others predicted to win, mostly from their own mouths, none were as popularly favoured or as often mentioned with awe as Angelo Deville. He hadn’t experienced otherwise since the fourth round of GTT3, the last Golden Turnbuckle Tournament, the final time the modern moniker would be an acronym instead of the title. Virtually unknown to the Universe at large, having snuck into the tournament in the third round and frightened off the vampire-slaying Matt Blitz, The Deville was a heavy underdog to sentimental sweetheart Tessa Windsor. It was only once he decimated her in stunning fashion that the reverence began, and from that moment forward perpetually picked up steam, until there was never a time The Deville approached a ring and people thought he might lose. Ironically yet cynically predictably, resentment grew. People began citing his dominance as somehow unfair, as if he were the New York Yankees operating with a bankroll multiple times the size of the opposition. They hated the untouchable air that cocooned him, rued his supremacy, waited with crossed fingers and jilted brow for him to fall. It didn’t matter whom he faced, any competitor was automatically the cherished underdog; Angelo Deville the begrudged perennial favourite. Entering the fifth round of GTT4, nothing had changed. There was no reason it should have. Across the canvas from the unconquerable Angelo Deville was the semi-retarded Dave Hurst, a man matured only by virtue of his years, in truth an overgrown child with a polyurethane fetish. Deville was familiar with him long before it was determined they’d champion their bracket, and there was little of the man's childishly pedantic style that impressed him. In the ring, nothing had occurred to dissuade The Deville’s opinion. In fact, it had further diminished. Hurst had a rudimentary understanding of the fundamentals, but he was sloppy and uninspired, gassed from fluking well beyond where his merit should have taken him. Outclassed in every facet, Hurst couldn’t even summon anything resembling an offensive, opting instead to simply mimic what The Deville did. What Angelo accomplished with unparalleled finesse, Hurst copied with insipid bumbling, making them both appear less. The only benefit The Deville derived was foreknowledge of what was to come, the ability to plan for and mock it. Throughout the match there was never a period of worry for Deville, not one moment where he felt his victory threatened. Hurst was second-rate, and Deville was manhandling him easily, dancing circles around him like a professional boxer with the town drunk. When Angelo finally tired of putting on a show, of making it seem like a contest, he spun behind Hurst and dumped him with a German suplex, bridging for the pin. The three was academic; even if Hurst had the strength to kick out, Deville doubted he could summon the will to flex his inept muscles. The bell rang and Deville released his victim, rising to his feet with a cutting sneer and a shake of his head, almost angry at the indignity of having to face someone so incompetent at what was supposed to be the higher level of competition. When Ernie and Bert’s Rubber Ducky sounded over the PA system instead of The Deville’s Sympathy for the Devil, there wasn’t enough bile in the sewers of hell to rival that rising in his stomach. He demanded answers from the referee, who fidgeted and mumbled that his shoulders were down, that he’d inadvertently pinned himself, before hightailing it with his head tucked low. From the crowd a few unintelligible cheers erupted, but otherwise there was paramount booing, near to rioting, a spectacle previously unheard of, forcing security to double-time. Characteristically stupefied, Hurst rose to his feet and raised his arms in triumph, immediately overcoming and no-selling what he knew as well as those gathered to watch. He’d done nothing to win, he was running on empty, bankrupt of ideas, but if they wanted to declare him the victor, he’d offer a sloppy smile and embrace it. The Deville could only shake his head in bewilderment, rolling from the ring unjustly dejected, wondering how he could be so blatantly cheated, doubting he could bring himself to compete the following year.
