The Slow Life of Moe Desjardins: Times Like These.

15 September 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
# Horrible Desjardins was sitting at the kitchen table, drinking whiskey. It was nine in the morning and snow cancelled Sunday Mass. Beans cooked slowly on the stove. He was sick of beans, but it was the only meal he could cook. His wife the bitch divorced him years ago for a poor banker in Louisiana (but a great lover in bed), and Moe was as inept with food as his father. So he swiped his shot glass off of the table and it shattered into hundreds of little pieces on the green linoleum floor someone years ago thought was smart. He picked up the whiskey bottle by the neck and drank from it straight. He put the bottle down. For a long time he stared at the wall and thought. His head was tight and he had developed more than a buzz. His mind was like a bruised sky full of pregnant clouds. The clouds moved slowly and carefully at first, but then they sped up and ripped open. It was better in Louisiana, he thought. At least there he had a house. Now he had a cramped apartment Moe paid rent for. At least in Louisiana he had a wife. He could forgive her cheating and lying. Or maybe not. He just wanted to dip his wick again. In Louisiana he was happy. The city rips that out of people. Horrible stood up, unsure of himself. He grabbed the whiskey bottle by the neck again and stepped forward onto the broken glass. He swore and hopped back, knocking the chair over. The sharp sound dulled itself in his condition, but it still pierced through his head. For regular people, everything seems louder when drunk, Horrible thought. For me, everything seems even louder than that. He hated it and he loved it. He forwent picking up the chair, thinking to himself that he needed to rest on the couch and have another drink, and how that drink would heal the cut he probably got. He limped out of the kitchen and into the living room he hated. He sat and took another long take from the bottle. Almost in slow motion, he passed out and the rest of his body fell backwards. The bottle turned and the rest of the whiskey poured out onto the couch and of what did not soak into the material soaked into the carpet. # Horrible woke up what seemed like years later when someone pounded on the door. He raised himself on the couch and stayed still. Then he heard the soft jingling of keys and knew Moe was home for a quick stop, probably to grab some cash he could be using to help them both move out of this apartment. Moe opened the door and walked in. He wiped his feet on the piece of carpet he had excised from the extra he found at a construction site, dropped his gym bag, and kicked off his sneakers. “Going on?” he asked. And when his dad didn’t answer: “Fucking noon, dad. Nobody gets shitfaced this early. Even Ireland just now waking up from last night’s hangover.” “Shut the fuck up.” “Swear to God,” Moe said. “You don’t take AA or some shit? I’ll find twelve steps and push your ass down em.” He walked around the front of the couch and rolled the empty bottle back and forth with his foot. “This shit, dad? This shit ain’t cool. Wasting my money letting you get three sheets in the wind every day and night, and then you ain’t got the courtesy to make sure your blood don’t get in the rug.” “Shut the fuck up,” Horrible said. “It ain’t blood. It’s whiskey.” Moe bent down to check if the rug was wet. He rubbed his fingers around the rug. It was dry. He stood up, disappointed, and looked to his father again. “Amount you drink, whiskey is your blood.” “Shut the—” “I gotta go get some cash. And dad? Throw this bottle away.” He rolled it with his foot again. Before Horrible could reply, Moe was in his room down the hall. Horrible looked down at the bottle. He picked it up and looked inside. At least a full sip remained. He took it, then dropped the bottle back on the rug. He fell into the couch again. The first thing Moe did when he walked into his room was close the blinds over his window. Leaving the blinds open was depressing. If one were to look out of the window, one would only see the neighboring building and the graffiti amateur criminals tattooed to its side. He hated living here. He walked back across the room to the dresser and pulled open the top drawer. He took the clip of money out, pulled out a fifty, threw the clip back onto his socks, and closed the drawer. He went to his bed and kneeled, pulling out from under it a switchblade. He had never used it, but city life turned him paranoid. He pocketed it and left the room. “I’m going to lunch and a movie,” he said, walking into the living room. “Wanna go?” Horrible stayed still and never looked at Moe. “Your ****** friend going?” Moe sighed. “He’s driving, yeah.” “I got beans on the stove.” Moe shook his head. He put on his shoes and left. And walking down the stairs to the parking lot to the car in wait, he never thought to say “I love you.” # The funeral was close and simple. Not many people showed. Four days earlier, Moe mailed his mother an invitation to the funeral and was surprised when she arrived, dressed appropriately in black. She brought a man with her. Moe remembered his face. He was the banker his mother cheated on his father with. After the burial, Moe’s mother came up to him with a graceful glass of red wine in her hand. “We got your invitation.” “Don’t call it that,” Moe said. “We got your letter.” Moe stayed quiet for a moment and looked over at his father’s grave from the hill. Then the banker walked up and stood beside Moe’s mother. Moe turned around, first looking sternly at the banker, then looking at his mother. “Why’d you bring him?” Moe asked. His mother took a sip of wine. “Dave?” “Yes.” “He’s your new daddy, Moe.” She took another sip. Dave looked uncomfortable and half scared, almost waiting for Moe to jump on him and dig a grave into his chest to stuff his mother into. Instead, Moe nodded. “You good to her?” he asked Dave. “I think so.” “He is,” Moe’s mother said. “That’s good,” Moe said. “Dad was a good man, too.” “No he wasn’t,” his mother said. Moe looked at her again. Dave took a step back. “No,” he agreed, “he wasn’t.” All three of them stood on the hill and looked down at the gravestones. Moe’s mother sometimes sipped at her wine and Dave nervously played with his hands, sometimes clearing his throat to break the heavy silence. People often avoid conversation in cemeteries, Moe thought. Not because they are afraid of waking spirits, but because a cemetery lacks the environment, the atmosphere, for conversation. After a while, Moe’s mother left for more wine. Dave stayed where he was. “Damn shame,” Moe said. Dave looked at him. “What is?” “People don’t talk much here.” “It’s a cemetery.” Moe nodded. “Sure is.” Half a minute past before Dave asked, “Last words you said to him?” Moe turned to Dave now and patted his pocket. He shoved his hand in it. “Got a switchblade here, Dave. Pretty good with it, I think. City makes me wanna carry it all the time and everywhere, you know? Why don’t you hop in your car?” Dave left. Moe smiled and looked out across the myriad gravestones again. All of them looked the same. They were all gray and plain and could in no way convey to visitors what the person underneath them was like when he or she had been alive. That was a good thing for some cases, Moe was sure. But it was a shame for others. Moe yawned. When he looked out again, he lost his father’s grave. # The heat outside was something solid, and deeper than what most rivers aspire to be. Moe sat in the air-conditioned living room of his new apartment with his new wife Emma beside him on the sofa. On the love seat across the coffee table was Officer Ernest Stein. “I get you anything, Ern?” Moe asked. Ernest shook his head. “Nah, I’m good. So, how was the honeymoon?” “Best time of my life.” “It was amazing,” Emma said. “France was just wow. Ever been to the Eiffel Tower, Ernie? So romantic up there. But my favorites were the, uh—what was that chateau called?” “Château de Chenonceau.” “That’s it. Looked like it was floating on water.” She leaned forward to the table and poured herself a glass of wine. “Oh, and Côte d’Azur was a blast. The beaches were incredible.” “How’d you take it?” Ernest asked. “Took a trip to Normandy,” Moe said. “Interested in D-Day and that whole war, you know? Now I wanna plant a fucking garden, you believe that? You think of Normandy and D-Day, you don’t think that the place where all them boys died could have all those flowers.” “Oh, and the food!” Emma said. Ernest smiled. “Spain was even better,” Moe said, grinning. “Ever been to a bullfight?” “No, can’t say I have.” “Shame. You’d like it. It’s artsy.” Ernest grinned. “It’s Hemingway. How’d you afford it?” “Dave broke more than even.” “Dave?” “The dickhead my ma’s with?” “Oh, yeah. Dave, right.” “Be nice,” Emma said. “So he paid for it?” “The trip and the new apartment,” Moe said, and took a sip from Emma’s glass. “He’s still a dickhead, though.” Ernest nodded, shifting in his seat. They were all quiet for a while. Moe took Emma’s hand in his. Ernest asked, “When’s the baby due?” “Three months,” she said, looking with pride at her swelling belly. “Know the sex?” “Nope.” “Yes,” Moe said. “We just won’t tell it.” “I respect that.” “So you here to check up on us or what?” Moe asked. “Little of both.” He shifted in his seat again. “What’s second?” “The honeymoon,” Ernest said. “Just thought I’d bring it up first to be friendly.” “Still on the case?” Emma asked. “Yeah. I feel obligated to it.” “You mean you feel chained to it,” Moe said. “Let it go, Ern.” “No,” Ernest said. “I feel obligated to it.” “Same shit. Just let it go, though.” Moe sipped from Emma’s glass again. Emma refilled it. “I mean, yeah, it was murder, but it’s over. Happened, what? Three years ago? Nothing bringing back Horrible now.” “I know,” Ernest said. “It’s just interesting, though. Your knife kills your pop, but you got a rock-solid alibi. Everyone else you know checks out clean, too, and we got no real suspects ‘cept the ones we can’t find.” “It was a good knife,” Moe said. “Don’t care ‘bout the knife now.” “I did.” “Don’t understand why you can’t find the suspects,” Emma said. She sipped her wine and folded her arms over her belly. “Well,” Ernest said, and paused to think. “All the friends or enemies Horrible had had nicknames like him. Most people who coulda helped us don’t know the last names, though. Their descriptions don’t do shit for us, either, ‘cause they were all alkies or druggies or both and they’d have trouble doing one of those my summer vacation essays, you know? We tried their descriptions and had our guys do renderings, but that didn’t work. We’d only be able to maybe find them with last names, but with just a nickname it’s almost impossible. And now all those nicknames left the state.” “Oh.” Emma thought for a moment. “How about first names? Maybe they’d click it with a last—” “Nah,” Ernest said. “First names are common, you know? Anyone can be a Jim or a Bill. Believe me, we tried everything we could think of to do.” “Phone numbers?” He shrugged. “Phone numbers, yes, but no one knew those either. Probably they wouldn’t even be listed in the phone book, you know? Addresses were pretty much the same. Went down to where they’d probably lived and asked around, but just got the same bullshit there, too. Then we went to places where they probably didn’t live and got even less.” “Sounds aggravating,” Emma said. “It is.” He massaged around his temples. Then, to Moe: “Maybe I will take a drink.” “Wine?” Moe asked. “Got anything harder?” “I got everything.” “Stiff pounder?” “Jägermeister and a beer?” “You got it,” Ernest said. “Drink the neck for me, though. Hate to seem like a drunk.” # “You never told me everything,” Emma said hours later when she and Moe were in bed. “Never told you what?” Moe put a bookmark in the middle of a paperback and closed it. He placed it on the nightstand to his right, then turned on the alarm clock. “Just thinking,” she said. “About what?” Moe asked, thinking, Don’t bring it up. “What Ernie said.” She cleared her throat. Moe hoped she would yawn. She didn’t. “Oh, right. It’s late.” “C’mon.” “I’ve told you before.” “Not everything,” she said. “He got stabbed, right? With your knife.” “Bled all over our couch out there.” Incredulous, she asked, “What? You kept the couch he died on?” “It’s a good couch. Comfy.” Then, when he got no reply: “I cleaned the cushions.” “Never mind that,” she said. “Tell me about it.” “Not much to tell.” “Then tell as much as you know.” “Well,” he said, “went to town for lunch with a friend. Caught a flick after that. Cast Away, I think it was. Tom Hanks and the volleyball? Anyway, I always brought my switchblade to town. City got me paranoid, you know? And after the movie we had some cash left, so Steer and me—” “Steer?” “Steer, yeah. Not sure you know him. Probably not.” “Nope.” “Well, real name was Sterling,” he said. “Forgot his last name, but he was a friend. We trained together down at French’s Shop when I still did that fight shit. Horrible didn’t like him ‘cause he’s black, though.” “Oh, right. Okay.” “So Steer and me, we went down to Barrie’s for a drink.” He sat up a little. “Had a few, talked to a couple of guys we knew, couple of guys we didn’t. Few hours pass, he decides to drop me off at my place. He was off his tits more than me, so told him to come up, right? Well, reached for my keys and felt my knife wasn’t there. Opened the door and there it was on the couch next to Horrible.” Emma sighed. “Don’t think he moved at all after I walked out the door. He drank a lot.” “So then what?” Emma asked. “Then what? I called the cops.” “That did a lot.” “Then we did the funeral. Not a whole helluva lot of people showed up. Didn’t feel too bad.” “Why not?” she asked. “Hold on,” he said, then sneezed. “Oh, ha. I told Dave the dickhead I had the knife in my pocket. That was fun. He left pretty fast, anyway.” “Why didn’t you feel bad?” “What? Oh.” He thought of an answer Emma would like, but the truth was rarely likeable. “Because he was Horrible.” “So someone from the bar stole your knife and killed your dad. And you don’t care.” “Right.” She frowned. “Oh.” “Yeah,” he said. “It’s late.” “Okay.” “No more questions?” “No more questions.” “Goodnight, then, you two.” He patted her belly. “Night.” Moe turned off the lamp above their bed and they went to sleep easily. # Moe’s mother held Nora in her arms, rocking the baby gently. She still had her winter coat on. Dave sat next to her, drinking sociably from a glass of champagne and looking at Nora with the same expression as his wife. Emma and Moe held hands on the opposing sofa, watching Nora sleep. “She’s two,” Moe’s mother said. “Hard to believe.” Emma smiled. “You sure I can’t get you anything?” “Oh no,” Moe’s mother said. “I’m fine.” “Precious little girl you got there,” Dave said. He finished his glass. “You’re keeping the place up pretty nice, too. How’s it treating you?” “More than we could ask for,” Emma said. “We can’t thank you enough, Dave.” “Don’t worry about it,” Dave said. Then, to Moe: “Fill me up again?” “Yep,” Moe said. He leaned forward to the table and grabbed the bottle by its neck. “Mercier?” “Mercier Demi-sec,” Dave said. He gave Moe his glass. Moe filled it. “Thanks.” Moe gestured to the bottle. “Expensive in France?” “Not bad as here,” Dave said. “It’s a blessing living there now, isn’t it?” “Definitely,” Moe’s mother said. She pulled Nora closer into her chest. “I think we lost her,” Emma said and laughed. Moe and Dave followed Emma’s lead. Moe’s mother said nothing and did not laugh, too engrossed by Nora to acknowledge other company. She combed back with her hand wild strands of Nora’s hair. Nora breathed lightly. “How’s the job-hunt going?” Dave asked. “Freelance doesn’t pay shit,” Moe said. He poured himself a glass, his second. “I know that,” Dave said. “How’s the hunt?” “Over for everyone but me,” Moe said. “Everyone else? They found their foxes. Thinking about throwing in the towel and jumping back in the fight scene. I lose some smarts, but it pays well.” “Should work for me,” Dave said. He sipped his champagne. “Nah.” “I have to use the lady’s room,” Emma said. She got up and left the room. “Couldn’t do that,” Moe said after he heard Emma shut the bathroom door. “You already paid our honeymoon and this apartment. Can’t take no more from you.” “It’s not charity,” Dave said, “if that’s what you think.” “It’s not,” Moe said and drank from his glass. “I’m just not a businessman.” “Shame,” Dave said. “You’d like the travel. We just got back from Madrid. Been there?” “Stopped there for a bullfight,” Moe said, drinking again. “Catch one?” “Yeah. It’s a blast.” “Know the difference between matadors, picadors, and banderilleros?” “I’m not dense,” Dave said, and drank from his own glass. “Best thing I came away with, though? The Paco joke. Ever heard it?” “No,” Moe said. “Enlighten me.” “Well,” Dave said, “supposedly Madrid’s full of boys named Paco, or used to be. So a father comes into town and advertises something in one of the papers, saying something like ‘Paco, meet me at the hotel Tuesday at noon, all is forgiven,’ and signs it ‘Papa.’” “Yeah?” “Yeah,” Dave said. “Well, Tuesday at noon comes and a squad of Guardia Civil has to be called in to get rid of the eight hundred boys who saw the ad. The end.” Moe smiled, then laughed louder than he liked to. Dave smiled back and drank. He glanced over to his wife, whose attention still had not left Nora. “Delivery lacked,” Moe said, “but it was good. Refill me?” “That your third glass?” Moe’s mother asked. Dave filled the glass anyway. “Don’t start,” Moe said. He sipped the champagne. Dave corked the bottle and sat back and eyeballed his wife askance. His eyes seemed like they were pleading with his wife. “You’re gonna turn into your father,” Moe’s mother said. “Don’t start,” Moe said. “Please. Just stop now.” “Please, honey,” Dave said. “Not now.” “Put that glass down now,” Moe’s mother said. Moe didn’t. “Moe, put that glass down now.” “It’s champagne,” Moe said. He drank from the glass. “It doesn’t do much to me.” “Put it down!” Moe heard the toilet flush. He thought, Shit. “Did you hear me or not?” Moe’s mother asked. Dave touched her arm. “Honey—” “Shut up, Dave.” She swatted away his arm. “Moe, put the glass down on the table now. Now.” Emma walked into the room. “What’s going on?” “Nothing,” Moe said and drank the rest of his champagne. “Nothing,” Dave said and did the same. “Your husband’s turning into his father,” Moe’s mother told Emma. “That’s his third fucking glass. He’s gonna drink and drink and drink and then he’s gonna wind up dead. Did you know that? Did he tell you? I bet he didn’t. Ed didn’t tell me. That’s why I kicked his ass to the curb. Piece of shit he was, right? Right?” Nora started to wake up. Emma looked at Moe. She said, “It’s just champagne.” “For now,” Moe’s mother said. “Then it’s bourbon and tequila and vodka and whiskey, fucking whiskey!, and then he’s dead all over the couch and then you’re stuck like me.” Dave said nothing. “You don’t wanna be like me,” she told Emma. Moe’s mother turned to her son. “Put that glass down now, Moe. Right now. Don’t be a shit. Don’t be a bastard. Don’t be Horrible. Put it down and go think about what—” “Stop it,” Moe said. “The baby’s waking—” “Don’t you dare interrupt me, you little shit,” Moe’s mother said. Nora started crying. Emma took her from Moe’s mother and patted the back of Nora’s head, shushing her, trying to get her to fall back asleep. “We’re gonna go,” Dave said quickly. He stood up. “You can keep the champ—” “Dave, shut your fucking mouth,” Moe’s mother said and stood up from the couch. “I’m looking out for my son! I’m looking out for my son, Dave. Stay out of this. It’s not your business—” “Yes it is,” Dave said, and grabbed Moe’s mother by her hand. He walked to the door, pulling her behind. Moe’s mother looked at her son with smoldering eyes, and Moe guessed that her eyeballs were just a few degrees away from melting out of her skull. “Bye, Dave,” Emma said, still shushing Nora. Dave nodded to her. Moe’s mother pushed Dave out of the way and walked out the door, making certain to slam it behind her. Dave winced. “Sorry it didn’t work out,” Dave said to Emma and Moe. “Guess tens years should be the minimum,” Moe said and tried to smile. “Probably best for you to go. Ernest might come over later, but you never know with him. He’s unreliable.” Dave smiled sadly. “It was going well, at least.” He opened the door. “At least there’s that,” Moe said. “Keep in touch, Dave.” “Later days,” Dave said. “And remember, job offer’s always on the table.” “Appreciated.” Dave left. Emma looked at Moe. Moe looked back and thought, Things never change. # Dave slid the key into his rental car’s door and unlocked it. Moe’s mother opened it from the other side and slid into the passenger seat. She slammed the car door, too. Dave thought the sound the slam made was louder than it should have been, but he had other things on his mind. He ascribed the volume to the empty parking lot, sighed, and opened his door, but did not get in. Instead, he looked around the parking lot. The view was uninspired and miserable. Dave saw only cars and snow and the black of the asphalt where snow melted or didn’t fall at all. This view was something he liked about France. France didn’t offer this. “Dave,” Moe’s mother called. “Dave.” He ignored her. He wished she would shut up every now and then. Sometimes he wondered late at night if he made the right decision in marrying her. Some days he was sure of that decision, but some days—days like these—he doubted his decision. Rather than seeing the ringing of bells and the spread of love and happiness that marriage was supposed to be, he saw himself stuffing his wife into a small hole where she could not escape and beating her in the head with the shovel he used to dig the hole. Blood and dirt mixed well, he was sure. “Dave,” she said again. “What?” “Come in the car,” she said. “Now.” He didn’t. He looked up at the room he just left, already wishing he could be back on the couch and sipping Mercier with Moe and Emma, and watching Nora crawl around the floor and try to walk and fall. “Dave.” He slid into the car and put the key in the ignition. He started the car. “What?” he asked. “I don’t want you giving that little shit any money.” He looked at her. “If you do,” she said, “I’ll divorce you.” He put the car in reverse and gassed the pedal. He stopped, looking up at the apartment again. Moe’s mother whined about something. Dave ignored her, but put the car in drive and left the parking lot. Moe’s mother kept bitching. Dave hoped he wasn’t late for his meeting. He needed a drink. # Moe sat on the couch. Emma walked around with Nora, who had fallen back asleep. The bottle of Mercier stood tall on the coffee table and Moe wanted to finish it. But not in front of Emma. Not after that scene. “You and Dave got along,” Emma said. “He’s ain’t dickhead,” Moe said. “No,” Emma said, rocking Nora, “he isn’t.” “Don’t think he’ll send us money anymore, though.” Emma stopped rocking her daughter. “Why not? Did he have a problem with it?” “No,” Moe said. “He ain’t a dickhead. Ma’s got him by the balls, though. And after that? We’re on our own now.” “I’ll get a job,” Emma said. “No,” Moe said. “You need to stay with Emma. Don’t want her raised in a daycare. I’ll get a job.” “Freelance?” “No,” he said. “A real job. Something that pays.” “Wasn’t Ernie coming over?” “Called him when you were walking around,” Moe said, thankful that she dropped that job talk for now. “Told him not to bother today. He wanted to talk about Horrible.” “He should give up,” Emma said. “It’s his drug. Can’t take away a cokehead’s fix. Can’t take away Ern’s unsolved murder case.” “I’m gonna put Nora in her crib,” Emma said. She blew a kiss to Moe and walked away. When he couldn’t hear her footfalls anymore, Moe reached for his glass and the Mercier again. He needed this. He poured himself a glass and kept the bottle by his side. He savored the taste. Need to do something, he thought. Anything that works.
