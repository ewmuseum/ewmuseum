To Dine with the Devil (IG Round 25)

7 November 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
My hands were shaking. It was like I couldn’t grip anything tight enough, fold them neatly enough or stuff them in my pockets deep enough to keep them from jumping around like a pair of yipping dogs. Even though they didn’t quite bark, the nerves in my fingers sent waves of pain howling through my brain. They didn’t just tingle; they burned. My first thought was that I had suddenly developed a bad case of the gout, but sadly I’m not that naive. I knew my hands were the least of my worries, convenient medical explanations or no. “Jin, are you feeling okay?” Donna Pringle smiled just a little, enjoying the fact that I was obviously not comfortable. If she only knew. “I hear this dive makes a mean Cuba Libre cocktail. I’m sure they’d substitute a good bit of arsenic for the rum, if you asked them nice.” If only she knew. I wrapped my aching joints around the mug already sitting in front of me, drawing deep of its outrageously bitter contents. I coughed, nearly threw up, but regained control. “I think I’ll pass,” I replied. God, my eyes watered so bad I thought I’d drown in my own tears. She was right, though; the restaurant was an absolute hell hole. I chose the dingy locale specifically for its darkened booths and dimmer hallways, a place where one could be completely ignored in plain sight, where nobody asked questions of nobody else and you couldn’t even see the mold on the Grade D meat they tried to pass as pork. Gerladino’s the broken neon sign read, a haven for the poor traveler who preferred a good bit of roach in his diet. I chose it because nobody knew me here. That was going to change. A haggard employee approached our little table in the corner, recognized as a waitress only by the pen behind her ear and the nametag on her blouse: ELLEN. She retrieved the pen and opened her palm, jotting a mystery word or two on it before acknowledging Donna’s impatience. “You ready to order?” she asked, making it a stone cold fact that she could care less. Donna rolled her eyes. “You do have Italian dressing?” The waitress said, “No.” “Blue cheese?” “Nope.” Donna sighed. “Would a vinaigrette be too much to h-” The waitress cut her off. “We got ranch, honey mustard and… ranch. You want a salad?” It was clear that she wanted to be somewhere else, but Donna smiled sweetly anyway. “Yes, that will be fine, thank you.” A little too sweetly. “With honey mustard.” It may come as a shock to some of you (and if not, I promise you it will), but I’m not an angry person. I don’t dislike many people, never have. If someone has done wrong by me, I’m always the first person to shrug it off and shake their hand in response. My parents used to tell me I had no backbone; I told them they had no self-respect. It was a conversation that went nowhere, and believe me, we had it out loud and often. That being said, I hate Donna Pringle. If ever there were a woman who could turn a sour lip before turning a sympathetic ear, it was Donna. Twenty years of working the same dead end clerical job, making ten thousand copies on Monday only to push every last one through the shredder by Thursday, well… it wasn’t hard to see how Donna had become so cynical. Regardless, a bitter bitch is a bitch worth putting down. I took another drink, pushing it down my esophagus with a twist of the mouth. It tasted horrible, but since I was already starting to get lightheaded, the numbness creeping down my throat didn’t seem as bad as it could have been. The waitress was looking at me expectantly. Donna was tapping her finger on an empty plate. “Oh,” I muttered, shuffling through the grease-stained menu. “I’ll take a dozen hot wings, the one off the appetizer menu. Is that okay?” I returned the waitress’ expectant look only to find quiet amusement grinning right back. The waitress shook her head. “All right buddy, enough with the fancy talk. What do you want to eat?” “I told you,” I said, suddenly confused. Was she telling a joke without a punch line? “I want the hot wings appetizer as a meal, one dozen. Sweet barbeque sauce. Is that too much to ask?” Or worse yet, was I the punch line? “Jin, stop being difficult.” Donna kicked me under the table, shooting me a reproachful scowl. “Tell the woman what you want. Plain English.” I repeated myself for the second time, but once again the waitress just wasn’t getting the message. Her quiet amusement became annoyance far too quickly. Always the domineering busy-body, Donna finally jumped in and ordered me a turkey club with no tomatoes. I stared at her incredulously as the waitress walked off in a huff. “Donna, what just happened?” I asked. “I told you both three times, I wanted a dozen hot wings! Was that so difficult?” Donna blinked. “Honey,” she said sarcastically, “You could have been ordering a blow job from the boss for all she knew. Whatever it was, you were ordering it in Latin.” Shit. I was doing it again. I didn’t have much time. My head swam as I tried to chug another mouthful, but this time it didn’t go so smooth. I threw my face into a napkin to wipe away the saliva that flew out in a fit of painful chest heaves. Donna pretended not to notice, her attention caught by something interesting on the far wall. A small splotch of blood winked back at me from the napkin; folding it quickly, I dropped it under the table. This was only the second time Donna and I had taken our lunch break together. Fortunately for the both of us, it would be our last. I tried to make small talk for a few minutes, but the throbbing in my head and the tremble at my knuckles made it hard to concentrate. We talked about the weather (dismal), Donna’s attitude towards the hanging of Saddam Hussein (less than dismal), and my frustrations with the little law firm we both called home. She was a secretary, shredding finance reports and bank statements as if embezzlement was going out of style, angry at the partners in the firm for keeping her in the dark about a million different underhanded deals she helped keep them clean on without making a dime from any of it. My fellow lawyers felt she couldn’t be trusted. All the more reason for today’s exhibition; if Donna ended up in a white room with no windows, nobody would really miss her. My mug was half empty. I sipped at it, trying to clench my nose against the pungent aftertaste, trying even harder not to vomit. At least I couldn’t even taste the slimy club sandwich when the waitress brought it out. I still had to pick off the tomato. There were a lot of things I didn’t tell Donna about that afternoon. The voices. The dreams, or memories, or whatever they were. I’d been awake two days shy of a solid week, too afraid to close my eyes and witness another atrocious act of violence against the innocent. I knew better, though; I knew these weren’t dreams. They were the memories of men I had never known. Men I was slowly becoming. I didn’t show her the scars on my forearms. Deep pink gashes crisscrossed my skin in miniature canyons of agitated flesh, hidden easily underneath the long sleeves of my suit jacket. Some of those wounds had cut so deep they scratched bone, but not one of them had bled. Not one drop. I didn’t tell her how many times I’d tried to kill myself in the last month. Or why. Lord no, I could never tell her why. I had to show her. “So,” I said, completely changing the subject, “I’m handing in my resignation this afternoon. And I’m changing my name.” A leaf of iceburg lettuce stopped halfway to Donna’s mouth. “Come again?” “Actually, I told Don Lindburg he could go fuck himself this morning.” It was a joy to watch that smug and smart exterior melt from her face. “You told the firm’s founder what?!” Taking a deep breath, I inhaled one final gulp of kerosene, biting back an overwhelming wave of nausea as I clamped my nose against the acrid fumes that licked my face. Absorbed in her own little world, Donna Pringle had never even questioned the smell. I’m sure she figured it was just part of Gerladino’s allure. I couldn’t really taste the stuff anyway. I couldn’t taste much of anything anymore. “Allow me to introduce myself,” I said. “My name is Iblis. Excuse me while I freshen up.” I splashed the remaining kerosene on my face. Pulled out a cigarette. Lit a match. She couldn’t even scream. The sight of a man’s face going up in flames was too much for poor Donna to take. I’m sure there’s a vacant white room with no windows out there somewhere. She’s going to need it. I’ve tried killing myself a hundred different ways. One hundred different failures. Maybe the footlights of Primetime Central will be enough to do the job. I’m sure there’s some monstrosity of a man out there who won’t think twice about breaking my neck. God, I hope so.
