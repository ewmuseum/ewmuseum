Shot Down: The Effects of an Extreme Crime

29 January 2004
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Hello. Just another ordinary, case-filled day with an expectancy level raised high for the odd jack-in-the-box surprise. I hate surprises, especially the unexpected, unwelcome kind. The kind of surprise you just subtly laugh at, and let pass by. Part of my occupational territory is to expect a surprise from even the most unlikeliest of destinations. But this is what I live for and this what I work for. I have a responsibility to serve justice and, to serve justice until it prevails. It is with great concern that I admit that lately crimes have been left unsolved. The nousse that was once the tight rope and tact of my career and the nousse that put crime to suffocation has disappeared. I can only, and it hurts to do so, blame the events that have been beyond my control. Two bullets, decorated to kill and manufactured to hit their target had shot me down. Some call November the month to remember, and as a child growing up I never paid much attention to the muttering of the quote. It was mainly my mother, walking down the stairs with a pile of clothes stacked past her double-chin, bellowing it out across the rooms. It was time to wake up, though, and having been shot down in November, I know longer had the intuition to sense danger. Instead, a part of me that day had left. It left without a chance to plead for a return. It was like losing a jigsaw piece and consequently not having the correct pieces to complete the picture. I should feel lucky, granted that I did survive and perhaps it was a jigsaw piece worth losing, as the picture that was building up was rather grotesque moreso than picturesque. Left hospitalized, bathing in my own blood and lost without the one piece of silverware that was formerly etched across my shoulder. I wasn't a happy man. Then again, who would be in my position? I don't plead any case to have any advantage over others that have suffered to similiar circumstances but, the proverbial finger of the law was absent. Is it any wonder people take the law in their grasp and throw it out of the window? Not really, it's conveniently called for. I have a right, in a pitiable state of agitation, to vent my frustrations on the justice system. Still to this day the assailant has not been found. It's a lengthy process that has made my restless, frightened eyes like those of some hunted animal. My eyes are no longer positioned forwards, instead they are placed at the side of my head, like an animal of prey designed to watch for it's predator. This is not me, and this is not my role. This just isn't the way things should be. And I made it perfectly clear on November 11th, two days after the shooting incident, as I gazed around the hospital ward, physically sick and mentally scarred, and I knew nothing about what had occured before my very own hazel, flushed eyes. Shot down before a capacity crowd, title reign ended, and this was just the beginning. Little did I realize that death would call by and pass; and what little time I had to whimper, as medical attention saved my life. An ordinary, somewhat fortuitous man would call it a day, but I refused to let the pain consume me. November 9th, 2003, could be marked down as a red letter day, enveloping the anguish I felt. Never before in my dull life had I experienced a tragedy such as this, and witnessing it, well, participating in the incident made matters worse. I questioned myself after the incident. The questioning not my usual sly, inquisitive manner, the sort you expect when I take a case by the scruff of the neck and delve deep to solve an unsolved mystery, using a the most reliable of sources. It was the emotional sort, discovering the real me. I had to question my career, question my life. And deep down, from the bottom of my heart, I wanted to continue; continue to serve justice. I always hated being sidelined. Earning, if you can call it that, money lying in a hospital bed, comforted by those who care and nursed by those skilled enough. It wasn't my cup of tea, and I made it crystal clear as I twiddled my thumbs, concealing the anxiety. I always hated the death-like stench cursing the hallways of hospitals. The stench is worse than cigar smoke, at least with that you don't feel intimidated. I felt caged by my own fears; I just wanted to escape. The smell of death frightened me, and weakened, I tried to push the duvet that wrapped me in cotten-wool away from my body. It was like being a child all over again, wrapped up tightly and closing my eyes as my mother would gently whisper: "Sleep tight, don't let the bed bugs bite.". But as I tried to push, even a small task such as this required maximum exertion. I managed to free myself, and placed my bare feet on the cold floor. My blood circulation suddenly ran cold, feeling the lack of warmth I shivered. It was a warning sign welcomed. After all, I didn't want to freeze. My teeth jittering with my jaw, as I attempted to stand on my own two feet. It was becoming a rather difficult task, especially with the cold, damp floor. I didn't even have the energy to reach for the sock draw. But then, a nurse watching on, quickly came to my aid. She was a caring nurse, and did everything not for her job but for her own feeling of warmth, as a result of helping those in need. Vast in the knowledge that I was in need of attention and in great haste, she scampered across the ward. Approaching me, she hoisted my arm into the air and supported me on her own shoulder. She was a tough woman. I needed a shoulder to rest on, not too mention a shoulder to cry on. My frail, broken body could no longer support itself. "You aren't well enough to leave, Tony, so I'm going to put you back in bed." The nurse informed. I felt patronized. I had never felt this low, ever. The nurse confirmed my own worst fears when she put me back into bed. I knew then something was seriously wrong and the malaise began to come over me, like grey clouds displacing a sea blue sky. "What happened," I mumbled, touching my dry, chapped lips. "I don't understand how I got here." The nurse stood at the bottom of the bed reading my medical records. She seemed just as oblivious as I did, as she crunched her facial muscles with confusion. I waited patiently, as she read the papers. I didn't like waiting. The typical detective that I am, impatient as ever but with a sly, keen manner to nail a criminal. This was different, though, it was worth the wait, perhaps, as the nurse confirmed my own worst fears. At least I was put out of my misery. "You were wounded by two bullet shots. One hit you in the chest and the other hit you in the right leg, in the upper thigh area. You're the result of a vicious crime, Tony, but you are recovering well." "Vicious crime?" I raised my voice, angered by what had seemingly happened. "Yes, a vicious crime." The nurse confirmed. "Well, I never thought this would ever happen! This is just... out of control." An outburst, patterened by a cry. I sat back, resting my head against the pillow. I couldn't come to terms with what had occured. I didn't want too. I wanted things to be simple, and this was always the way I liked things. My life simple, my work challenging. I couldn't have asked for anything else, really, could I? Contemplating my injuries, I unbuttoned my pyjama top, revealing a wound covered with dressing. I quickly faced away. Call me squeamish, but I didn't dare look at it twice. It was an unsightly scratch, but a scratch too far. A scratch moulded by a bullet, but instigated by a villain. I had always been notorious for catching villains, and when the time came, I would rightfully put the villain who did this in his or her place. But the questions always were waiting to be asked... Who, What, Why, Where and When?
