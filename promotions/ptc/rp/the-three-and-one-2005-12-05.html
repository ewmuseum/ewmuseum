The Three And One

5 December 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2005-12-05 "Uber Judge! Uber Judge, Uber Uber Uber Judge!" chanted the crowd of would-be revolutionaries. They were packed in a crowded club, chanting as he took the stage. These people were curious. They were interested. They were a part of something that fascinated them, that felt like they were ahead of the rest of the crowd. Folks from New Jersey needed every edge they could get after all. They’d been tipped off that he’d be appearing through emails, through pod casts, through hastily photocopied pamplets from the kinkos strewn around coffee shops and comedy clubs, hotel rooms and whore houses, The Uber Judge vaulted onto the stage from the crowd, not bothering with the steps at either side. The Uber Judge spoke and the crowd ate out of his hand. Timely remarks about executions, justice, ridiculous absurdities on the way we lived delivered with a caustic yet so simple venom. Look at this. Does this seem right to you? This is the way that it ought to be, bringing forth baser impulses of revenge and heartlessness. Cackles came from the crowd. An appeal for a limited kind of tenderness, of care, yet something that because it was fiercely limited was more precious because of it. The definition of the enemy was revealed: people who had control and shouldn’t. They hadn’t earned it, they hadn’t bled for it, and they hadn’t even fought for it. And he didn’t mean that pussy "fighting" that doesn’t mean fighting. Not merely the kind of fighting where you struggle in the system by arguing loudly, or shouting loudly, or being Erin fucking Brockavich, or some shit like that. He meant fighting like taking up that beer bottle, breaking it over your own head, and really sticking it to the man the way it must’ve actually meant when people first used the phrase. And it tangented from there, entertaining people with his wit, his point of view, talking about words that are being misused in ways that they weren’t intended, about how we don’t know what we’re saying when we’re saying it. Anticipate does not equal expect. And it doesn’t. They ate out of his hand, his words, his ideas, his means of expression. His laugh was contagious, and when he found it funny they knew it was actually funny, and when he laughed they wanted to laugh too. When he shouted, when he bitched, they were also bitching. Something oozed off of him and it caught among the crowd, something that meant that somehow this guy was really living the way they all wanted to live, and acting the way that they all wanted to act. He’d show them the way, he said. But not because he knew the way, but because he knew what he wanted, he knew what they needed, and they were going to try everything until something worked. He was the Uber Judge. But he wasn’t just the Uber Judge. He was Henry Rollins. == "U-ber-judge! U-ber-judge!" It’s a testament to his skills that he hadn’t done a thing yet, but they already read it in him. They couldn’t see his face under the mask but somehow, it got through, that this was not only something important but something great before them. But that was just the beginning. He opened his mouth and his words came forth and they were in a new state of awe because he had the greatest voice of his age. He told them he didn’t deserve to be there. He didn’t deserve the adoration. He merely saw this as a course of action that he could not help but know that he had to take. He referenced what he knew, which he insisted was quite finite, but he also referenced what he felt about things. He referenced the idea of pacifying others with a gun that merely caused someone to lose control of their bowels. And how perfect this idea was, that this idea is not only perfect in pragmatism, but perfect in essence, perfect in representing what ought to be done to people who had sticks up their asses. Unlike the other Uber Judge, this one savored every foul word that came from his mouth, delivering it like an orgasm of rebellion to the crowd, aweing them with the idea of their own freedom to speak. He merely said, "Ass" and it was as if he’d opened a portal to another world. He talked about the fat cats, not like a revolutionary but someone who’s sat near them at one too many dinner parties and noticed that they really are as bad as people think they are if not worse. He talked about weakness, helplessness, and having no course of action to change anything. And he talked about flushing, "that load of bullshit down the bloody toilet." He talked about weakness and helplessness were the greatest tools of the enemy. He talked about complexities, and craftly lies, and he referenced the fact that anytime something needed a complicated explanation that it was probably a load of shit, because nothing needed to be that complex, because everything that mattered "EVER" was fundamentally simple. That bullshit is bullshit, and when you can’t tell when it begins and ends that "EVERYTHING EVER" had to be bullshit and you started "THE FUCK" over and you figured it out on your own. The crescendo of profanity spoke of an honest outrage, an outrage that seemed so different from those that sought outrage out. It was the outrage when someone found it unexpected and reacted as they properly should. He didn’t know he could do this, but it’s what he wanted to do, and he’d be doing everything in his power to make certain he did it well. They loved it. Because there is greatness in a man who does not believe he is great, he was loved. Because there is greatness in a man who does not believe he is great, and yet aspires to greatness, he was loved. The people in the theatre were convinced. He was the Uber Judge, but not just the Uber Judge. They couldn’t mistake his voice. He was also Patrick Stewart. == They didn’t say a word. They fell silent as he took his place at the podium, and they waited patiently for him to begin. The smugness itself outraged so many. The casual way he moved and spoke the simplest of concepts, he could turn reality upside down. The man had a way about him, but he’d turned it down. He felt too big, a giant among midgets, and had decided to live quietly. Yet in him burned a sort of playfulness, a desire to perhaps grab hold of a pair of nipples and twist them. He felt whatever he wanted to feel, and it spread from him before he’d finish a sentence, the emotional cues planted, causing his listeners to be able to feel their way through his ideas, conclude his conclusions before he even delivered them. The simultaneous merging of his thoughts and their feelings created a ferver. And he danced with the entire crowd, calling them back and fourth, letting them out a bit, then reeling them back in. He knew he was nearly a god in this respect, but adopted the humility necessary to speak to men. He had discovered long ago that he could do whatever he wanted whenever he wanted to do it, and that amused him. But that power remained, and for what purpose? To what end? To hold the reigns of the world for mere moments, when the world needed him far more than he needed it. The ideology of the call didn’t matter in the least to him, he’d take it where he wanted, and only he could change what he set down as truth. But he liked the idea. Liked it too much to say no. Liked it too much to let his pride talk him out of it. So he did it. And it gave him such joy to really turn things on their heads. He didn’t even remember what he was talking about while he was talking about it, the glibness wouldn’t let that matter. He merely covered his points until convinced that his listeners were all with him, and then he finished. And then he started the real work, what he was best at. He mingled and chatted and laid the real steps of making it happen, telling people what they should do as they eagerly ate of his suggestions. What worked for some would not work for others, but this was no obstacle. From there, his own network of servants would form, and his own means of establishing domination were at hand. He was treacherous in this regard. But he was too talented to be refused. He delighted in the mask most of all. It allowed him to have the plausible deniability of his identity being compromised by his will to play the game. They all knew who he was, but he wouldn’t confirm it for them, a little taste of mystery and possibility that made everyone feel as if something great was really happening and they wanted to be a part of it. He was the greatest liar of his age, and like most liars, thought himself quite a romanic. He was the Uber Judge. But he was also Bill Clinton. == This one was all alone. He had studied. He knew the way of the world by looking at it and displacing all things that were tilted to their proper axis. It was the core of judging, that sense of the truth between the lies. A lie is not true, and by eliminating untruths, the place of hiding where the truth was set was discovered. Dis-covered, uncovered, revealed. The implausible did happen every day, but all had to be prepared for it to accept it. And it came down to motive. Any act ever with motive comprehended spelled out to perfect sense, even in nonsense, because nonsense is motivated by sense. Non-sense. Comedy itself delivered a message. Comedic violence turned our view on violence upside down, delighting in the horrible. Comedic pain brought forth laughs, the agony slipping underneath it to escape. Comedic anger justified the horrors of rage. The evidence was overwhelming. Belief itself delivered a message. The leaders of a new way of thinking were always weak. It was the true-believers that truly ruled, truly drove the movement, and a leader could never be a true-believer. In this the leader was made into something that he wasn’t by the true-believers. Sanctified, deified, and denied in truth. This was the risk, to run with the ball, and when your image got too big, stop doing anything, and let the work sort itself out. It was a case of momentum, once you got the ball rolling you could not control it, and that’s the nature of power. You initial thrust had to be spot on, because before long, things will get out of hand. Ask Christ. Many of his servants today would make him weep. It helped that he knew he was slightly insane. He could manage, and he could cope, bearing the approximation of normalcy, just enough to get away with being human, but he knew in his heart that he was off the radar in many senses. How hard is it to balance humility with ambition? How hard is it to balance fury with patience? He was putting himself to the test. Because everything was going to get much better, or much much worse. And hesitating felt too much like giving up. Among them all, he was really doing the least. Physical conflict under a set of rules. Who could ask for an easier rise to power? But his role had to hold, his place at the center needed to remain fixed. Henry Rollins wanted to strangle Clinton after being in the room with him for more than ten minutes. Patrick Stewart didn’t believe he really belonged here, thinking himself a mere mouthpiece when he was so much more. Clinton’s arrogance threatened to derail the whole enterprise. But … For now, he accepted that he could not control them, and in the worst case, they were accepting responsibility for his actions. All this one had to do was defeat some men in the ring. He was the Uber Judge. And in this, he was only the Uber Judge, because his inflated identity had started to dwarf his authentic identity. On the bright side, his motivation was clear. He would fight as if the whole world was at stake. And as far as he was concerned, it was. (Patrick Stewart, Henry Rollins, and Bill Clinton, are clearly and obviously real persons appropriated for this work of fiction, and in this fictionalization of them, it’s intended to be clear that this does not represent authentic representations of their views, politics, and willingness to take over the world, but a fancy of possibility based on speculation of the unknowns of their personalities, and outright misrepresentations of said personalities where it serves this story’s purpose.)
