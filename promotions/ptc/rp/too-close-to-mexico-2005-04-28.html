Too Close to Mexico

28 April 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2005-04-28 I’d like to tell you that the gunshots ripped through my chest as I dove to protect a woman caught in the crossfire. I’d like to tell you that she held me close to her body, my head perched between her perfectly round bosoms, as I breathed my last breath, and died, as the car pulled away in fear. I’d like to tell you that I was revived, and that the woman and I, and her perfect bosoms lived happily ever after, doing nothing more than making love, and thanking God that we were brought together by tragedy. POW! I’d like you to even believe me. It’d be great if you looked at me with envy as I presented her all over town as Mrs. El Rayo Azul II, and my kids, Rayito, Azulita, and Ellio. BANG! But, alas, I could tell you that until I was blue in the face (get it?), but, it would be a lie. KABANG! I did what any other warm blooded American male would do when he heard gunshots out of no where; I ran like a screaming bitch. And what a scream it was. It filled the quiet air, and hung there, seemingly for hours, ringing through the streets for days. I didn’t stop running until I knew I was safe behind the stairs of the church. I turned around, with morbid curiousity to see what I was running from, while the shots filled the air. POW! BANG! BOOM! And there it was. A 1978 Chevy El Dorado, smoking up the street, backfiring as it moved slowly, filled with ten people. Instantly, I felt like an ass, as four people on the streets turned and laughed at me. My face turned red, and I ducked inside the church to get away from the embarassment. Sorry for making you think I was in any danger. It’s a horribly overused literary device, and I’m truly sorry for that. I figured I needed an edge, you know? Something to make it look as if there was going to be something exciting for the second part of this story, like I was a new age Indiana Jones or something. Thankfully, I’m not, and this isn’t going to be one of those stupid “Bubba The Vampire Slaying Wrestler” stories that some Whedon wannabe tells, thinking that by pretending vampires exist, he’s a hardass. Once inside, I looked around the decrepit catholic church, and sighed. I was born a Catholic, but, quickly learned that any religion that requires practice wasn’t for me. I still remained somewhat spiritual, but, like anyone else, I was spiritual only when it was convenient for me. I paid reverence to the large crucifix near the altar by signing the cross and kneeling before I ducked into the back row of pews. I sat with the intention of simply waiting for the people outside to pass by, before starting my journey again, but, the nice, cool air of the dusty church, coupled with the somewhat strangely relaxing sensation of being taken back to my childhood made me lean my headback, and nod off for a few moments. I was awoken when I felt eyes on me, and a figure standing close. I opened my eyes to see an older priest, who’s description would definitely match the church. He was older, probably in his mid-seventies, with little hair on his head, but a large beard, almost like Santa Claus. Only, he was small and frail. He smiled at me as I looked at him, and I returned the smile. “Need something, my son?” “No, I was just…passing through.” “Oh. It’s a shame. I don’t get nearly enough company these days. Mind humoring an old man for an hour or so?” I didn’t know what to think. Part of me wanted to simply leave, not much caring for this man’s troubles, but, the sensible part of me knew that since it was midday in Texas, I wasn’t going to get much farther without a place to sit for a while. “Got anything to eat?” I asked, unabashedly. I was hungry, and since I had no money, I wasn’t about to eat any time soon. The priest laughed, and motioned for me to follow him. “Yes, yes. Plenty. But first, we talk.” I followed him down a dark hallway and into the rectory. Now, I know what you’re thinking. Young, verile man walking into a rectory with a priest? Get over it. Seriously. Not all priests are child molesting bastards. I followed him down a small, dark hallway into the brightly lit rectory. The church had fallen into disrepair, but strangely, the rectory had not. It was vibrantly lit, and looked absolutely out of place in contrast to the desolate church. He motioned for me to sit down in a small breakfast nook, and I did so. “Do you like soup?” he asked. “Of course. It’s food, isn’t it?” He chuckled like an old man does, and smiled. “Indeed.” He made himself busy for a few minutes, putting a pot onto the stove, and pouring in a few different ingredients. In minutes the air filled with the thick smell of roasted vegetables, chicken and beans, a smell that made my stomach gurgle, and thirst for the tasty concoction. He cut a few slices of bread, and put them onto the table in a small basket that you’d find at a restaurant. It smelled wonderful, sourdough, I think. I was tempted to reach for a slice, but, waited until the meal was on the table. The two of us made a bit of small talk, and I learned a bit of history about the church. It was built in 1823, and served as sort of a mission for the catholic church. Being that we weren’t that far from the Alamo, it wasn’t much of a shock that Davey Crockett and Jim Bowie had stayed here en route to the Alamo. “What about you? What’s your story?” he asked me. I smiled, and looked down at the table. “You don’t want to know all that. I’m just a guy that found a great deal of dissatisfaction in his former job, and now, I’m looking for something else.” “Ooooh. That sounds like a great story. Why wouldn’t I want to hear it?” “It’s a bit vile. Not something that I should be telling a man of God.” “Were you one of those adult actors?” He seemed a bit too eager with that question, almost hoping with a gleam in his eye. I shot him down, quickly. “No, I used to be a wrestler. You know, the Hulk Hogan kind of wrestler? That’s what I did for a long time.” And then I told him a few stories about why I didn’t want to do it anymore, and what ultimately lead to me leaving the ring for good. He hung on every word, and was genuinely interested. I grew more comfortable, and even acted out a few of the spots. The entire time, he watched me grow excited talking about the sport, and in turn, he got even more exited. “A real wrestler. Wow. We don’t get many of those here.” “I can imagine. Too close to Mexico.” “Good point. Tell me, my son, would you ever do it again?” I thought about it for a few seconds, and nodded. “Yeah, I would. But, I couldn’t do what I used to do. I’d have to do something that was meaningful, something that wouldn’t result in me bleeding out in the middle of the ring.” “I understand. I’ll be right back.” The old man stood up, and scurried off into another room. I sat and stared at the bread in the center of the table, and my mouth started to water. I saw a few crumbs sitting beside the basket that had just fallen off a piece of bread, and I licked my fingertips. I couldn’t see the harm in tasting just a few crumbs before the meal. I reached out slowly, and put my fingers into the small pile of crumbs, pulling my fingers to my nose, and inhaling deeply. Perhaps a bit too deeply. The crumbs came off my fingers, and went up my nose, and into my lungs. Instantly, I coughed, desperately trying to get them out of my airways. Thankfully, they just kind of tickled. I stood up, and hocked a loogie into the sink, and washed it down the drain. It might be a better idea to just wait for dinner. The priest returned a few minutes later, with a smile on his face. “My son, I have a friend I want you to meet. Come, come. I’ll drive.” ”What about the soup?” “Soup? The soup can wait my boy!” I took a deep breath, and looked longingly at the pot on the stove. “But, I’m hungry.” The priest stopped in his tracks and turned around. He looked at me, and frowned. Immediately, I recanted. “I’m sorry. You’re right. I should do something for you before you do something for me. Let’s go.” He smiled. “Bless you, my son.” We left, but, not before I took one last breath of the wonderfully scented air, and turned the soup down to a simmer.
