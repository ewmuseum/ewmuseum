Date: 2009-10-19 ”The opposite of love is not hate. It is indifference.” ~Mal Pancoast * * * Henderson, Nevada – now. The Elephant Bar is a safari themed restaurant that features food from Asia, the tropics, as well as regional favorites. It is the kind of place that you might take your parents when they come to town to visit, cause really they’d just eat this up. Just look around: giraffe statues, plant life, colorful walls, overly cheerful staff, etc. During the week the Elephant Bar did not get busy until closer to 5 pm, but on the weekends the rush started at noon. Seated at a booth in the middle of the restaurant was Pro Wrestling Illustrated writer Jeff Coltz. He has been covering wrestling in one way or another for the last 13 years. But looking at the man, you’d never guess he was associated with the absurd and colorful world of pro wrestling. Jeff was a small and fragile looking man, who seemed about ten years older than his age of forty two. His suit was gray, and his hair was getting there. Across the table was a younger man who was paging through a notebook. Coltz couldn’t see his eyes because of the strands of dark hair that hung in his face. He hated that. “You sure you don’t want me to stick around in case, you know…” the young man offered. Jeff thought his name was Rich, but he couldn’t say for sure. He was just the son of someone who used to be relevant at the magazine. Coltz had already come to the conclusion that Rich didn’t want to stay for Jeff’s protection, but because he wanted to meet the sociopath. And to Jeff’s credit, he was right. Rich actually wanted an autograph. How backwards had this all gotten, Coltz wondered, that someone like Lane Stevens was thought of as cool by a certain percentage of wrestling fans? It seemed that this grave question had eroded Jeff’s face, the way water could a rock. “I’ll be fine,” Jeff said, looking over his own notes. “Fair enough, I’ll be at the bar.” Coltz nodded, not watching the intern leave the booth. He took out a small recorder and set it on the table in front of him. A few moments later Jeff felt a presence lingering over him. “Seriously, I will be alright…” “Alright, maybe. But if you want to upgrade that to super duper…might want to try some of the Wok-fried Mongolian beef” Jeff looked up to find his interview subject, a man of average build (for wrestling standards) and short red hair. He was dressed in a retro Clinton Sage Affliction t-shirt, and tan shorts. Lane Stevens grinned fiercely. “Jeffrey Coltz, I presume.” “Indeed. Hello Mr. Stevens.” The River Rat, a nickname assigned for multiple reasons, extended a hand that Coltz shook tentatively. Lane took a seat. “Please, call me Lane.” “Alright, Lane.” “And please pay for stuff.” “…uh, okay.” * * * There are a lot of ways to illustrate Alexandra Pierce’s (or Desade’s, if you prefer) power in Sin City Championship wrestling. You could bring up statistics of the win/loss record of her Dead Man’s Hand, of their achievements, of their championships. You could show video clips of her dominance. You could listen to the tales of the retired men and women, crushed at her whim. But oddly enough, one of the most convincing displays of this power is Miles Cavanaugh. Desade gave Lane Stevens the funds to acquire his own team, to enforce her will in a particularly violent way. They broke the President of the company’s hand, injured many, and intimidated many more. The thing is, even her lackeys have lackeys. And Miles Cavanaugh is one of these. Standing at a hair over 6’5” and weighing in the ballpark of 250 pounds, Miles had surprisingly gentle features. His blonde hair was shoulder length, and when his smile presented itself, it could be a dazzling thing. Lane often teased him, calling him “the model” or “the pretty cunt”. But the days of him smiling have gotten fewer and fewer over the years. A lot of people in Desade’s employ had fancy cars, and it could be said that Lane’s 1999 Tope Pontiac Grand Prix was an exception to this. On this day Miles it drove through Henderson’s downtown area, a haven for shoppers and drinkers alike. It was going to be a while before Lane’s interview was done, and he was given simple instructions by Stevens: Take her wherever she wanted to go. In the backseat of a car that was apparently being confused for a cab, or a limo, she looked out the window with disinterest. “So…you a hooker or something?” Miles finally asked, breaking the silence. “I don’t know yet,” she responded, shifting her gaze to meet his in the rearview mirror, “how about you?” “For all intensive purposes, yep,” Miles mused. The fact that she was attractive did not surprise Miles, but the way in which she was attractive took him off guard. He imagined Lane picking up blonde bimbos with impossibly huge racks, Stevens liked the overkill of things like that, the sheer obviousness of it. Her hair was dyed black, and she even had a tattoo. There was a small owl on her right upper arm, the strap on her light blue sun dress cutting it in half momentarily, before she readjusted it. “Hope you weren’t offended by my question. It’s just that my boss doesn’t have a lot of women around uh…voluntarily.” She rolled her eyes, seemingly recounting something. “I picked up on that.” “So besides prostitution, what could bring a girl like you into all of this?” “First of all, never said I wasn’t a prostitute.” She smiled briefly, and it made Cavanaugh feel human momentarily. “True." “Second of all Marilyn,” she started, and paused. That was another one of Lane’s nicknames for Miles, this one she had overheard, “I got some real problems.” Miles laughed aloud, and took a right into the strip mall. “You think that this is the kind of group that solves problems?” “Nah, but if things get worse enough I might get lost in the shuffle,” she said, scanning the stores in front of her. “Do you want to chain smoke for a while?” “Fuck it,” Miles offered. They exited the car, and made their way towards the Tobacco Outlet. * * * Back at the Elephant Bar, the first round of drinks was being delivered. Background noise: cheesy safari music, small talk, and children crying. Jeff Coltz took a sip of his water. Lane Stevens, for his part, took a sip of an Alabama Slammer. This drink was made with Southern Comfort, Amaretto, orange juice, with a dash of Sloe Gin on the top for color. It had a way of looking harmless, sneaking up on you, and pummeling you into a DUI or some kind of absurd fist fight with a guy in the bathroom. Desade’s WMD nodded in appreciation of the drink, his mind trying to categorize and rank this Alabama Slammer against others he had in his lifetime. He had settled on it being top thirty, at least, before his train of thought was interrupted. “A decade ago your actions might have gotten you kicked out of this sport, or perhaps worse. Do you consider yourself fortunate to exist in the current climate of professional wrestling?” Jeff pushed the tape recorder a few inches closer to Lane, prompting his response. “I’m not sure if lucky is the word, I am a product of my environment, a reflection if you will." “I’m not sure if many of our fans will agree. Your actions don’t exactly mirror others.” Lane smiled broadly at this. His smile had a way of sharply contrasting his bruised and scarred face. “Look, we’ve all done our part. I mean it’s no secret that over the years, competitors who don’t have the same god given wrestling ability as others have resorted to cheating. Stevens leaned in, suddenly looking more excited about the exchange. “At first, all the bad guys wanted was to win. Remember the 80’s? All the cheesy gimmicks. And all these heroic turds, they couldn’t stand it. So they upped the ante, they wanted to fight fire with fire, and so on…and so on. It became an arms race. And now look what’s happened to these poor assholes. Their families are getting fucked with, and boo hoo. The goody goodies brought this on themselves. So fuck ‘em, and anyone that looks like ‘em.” Jeff Coltz leaned back, slightly fazed by the last rant. “But just the same, if Desade wanted me to deliver toys for tots…I’d be doing that. Just the nature of the beast,” Lane remarked and shrugged. “This drink is exceptional by the way, you should really order one.” “So you’re saying you don’t really enjoy your work, it’s just a job?" “Well,” Lane started, and pursed his lips in thought, “I wouldn’t exactly say that.” * * * Two days ago. The walls were cement, and there was no furniture except for two chairs in the center of the room that faced each other. One of them was a steel folding chair, and a lanky young man with a buzz cut was seated on it. The duct tape and rope that had previously been used to bind him was strewn about on the floor. The boy didn’t look a day over twenty. There was blood on his wife beater, but it was unclear as to where it came from. The second chair was a beige recliner. Lane Stevens sat on it as if it were a throne, as if he was the king of this filth. He looked over the man across from him and laughed so hard he almost threw up. It was apparent he was not operating with all of his faculties, but it was also apparent that it didn’t much matter, given his current company. “A name…I’m going to need a name,” Lane said, all his humor evaporated. The young man’s eyes grew in horror as he noticed his own fingernail sticking to Lane’s shirt, held there by blood and sweat. As the young man sobbed, The River Rat popped one of the pills that had fallen on the cement floor. There came a small knock at the door, which seemed to startle Stevens as much as it did his victim. "Excuse me, would ya'?" Lane asked, shambling to the door to yank it open. "Well, would you look at that. If I'd known I was going to have guests..." Her dress was red, a deep, rich color that she wore well; it was a sheath dress with spaghetti straps and just the hint of a slit up the left side. Prologue: Everywhere, Man It was a cold night in Cleveland. Maybe in two months it wouldn’t be considered cold, so much. But right now, to a weary group from the poor side of town, this had come much too soon. There had been no fall. The unemployed and underpaid that lived on or around Sherman St subconsciously regarded this as another sign that things had simply moved on without them. That if they could just break out of here, it would be sunny and 75. Darren Hayward was not an exception to this. He was supposed to be the next Michael Jordan. Okay, so that was his mother’s line, but at least Grant Hill. He was six foot five, a lightning quick first step, good range, tenacious on defense. Ohio State, Indiana, Michigan State, Georgia Tech, and Clemson were all very much interested. But as you can already tell by the tone, this story did not have a happy ending. When he blew out his knee, it wasn’t chasing after a rebound, it was chasing after an upset girlfriend. Just one wrong step, that's all it takes. Just ask him. He does not cash checks from the Cleveland Cavaliers, or the LA Lakers. The checks he cashes are from Brennan’s, a grocery store three blocks away from his home. But on nights like these, when the misery of his life seemed like too much, the debt and the heartache, when it threatened to build up in his throat until he choked, he still shot a few baskets. To have that ball in his hands was a bit like therapy, a bit like masochism. It just depended on the day, on the mood. On this evening, it was a bit of both. Darren was dressed in a green hoodie and green track pants. He stood at the free throw line of the Yahara Elementary school basketball court. Hayward dribbled three times, exhaled, and put the ball in the air. He could barely see it, but the sound of the ball passing through the chain net was music to his ears. But a few moments later, there was different music in the air. It was singing, faint at first, growing in volume every second. “I’ve been everywhere, man. Crossed the desert bare, man. I breathe the mountain air, man.” It was a Johnny Cash tune, but Darren was not aware of that. It felt to him, for a moment, that the song was coming from all around him. That he was being surrounded by it. He looked around frantically for a moment, and found nothing. “Of Travel, I’ve had my share, man.” The last line was spoken. “I’ve been everywhere.” Darren continued to spin around, and it occurred to him suddenly that the voice was vaguely familiar. When he pivoted to the left again, he saw a figure leaning against a wall of the school. It was a tall and broad man. Despite the cold, he wore a short sleeve white t-shirt. But the feature that made the man unique, that made Darren remember him, was his black cowboy hat. “You scared the shit outta me, man,” Darren cried out, he was breathing rapidly. “My apologies partner,” the cowboy said in a southern drawl that Darren did not buy. “What’s shakin’?” Panic. “How the fuck did you find me? There’s better ways to handle this, man. I don’t got the money, alright. I just don’t got it.” The cowboy (Darren thought he introduced himself as Dennis Darkhat at one point, but he didn’t buy that name either) took a few steps towards the former basketball star. “Fine by me,” Darkhat said. He had something in his hand. As he got closer, his middle aged face was slightly more visible. “It is?” Hayward asked, still not feeling anymore comfortable. He involuntarily took a step back as the other man approached. “Yep, in fact…I’m not too interested in your debt. Old news if you ask me.” Darkhat looked him in the eyes, taking a second to enjoy the simultaneous fear and jubilation that he saw there. But there was greater satisfaction that this night offered him, so the moment was not savored for long. “Just going to need a little bitty teeny weeny favor. How does that sound?” “What…what do you want?” In response, the cowboy produced a small photograph. Hayward held it up to his face. Regan Guest frowned back at him.
