Historical Perspective and Retrospective.

13 April 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2005-04-13 When it comes to sequels, few have been as successful as their predecessor. For every Caddyshack, lies a Caddyshack 2, argueably the worst sequel of all time. But every so often, the second generation outshines the first. This is not one of those cases. Allow me to explain it to you, how it was explained to me. Or, more importantly, how it was sort of explained in a general, round about kind of way. Quite obviously, I am not the first El Rayo Azul. Otherwise, the name El Rayo Azul II would be a horrible misnomer, but, that name in and of itself, is a huge contradiction. I’m actually the thirty-eighth person to wear the El Rayo Azul mask, but, let’s face it. El Rayo Azul XXXVIII just doesn’t have a nice ring to it, does it? Regardless, the legend of the Rayo Azul mask goes like this: Fifty or so years ago, a mexican rudo named Jose Hernandez Junior was presented with an opportunity by a shady promoter to become one of the biggest draws in all of the Ciudad Juarez wrestling circuit. Granted, that’s like becoming the biggest name in Ankle Lick, Kentucky, but, that’s not the charm of the story. Senor Hernandez took up the promoter’s idea and gimmick, and became El Milagro Del Jesus. The Miracle of Jesus. Hernandez was a pretty decent wrestler. As just a simple, maskless wrestler, he had a somewhat impressive record, and always had the respect of his opponents, a very strange thing for a rudo. He lived by a strict moral code that’s since been passed on to the El Rayo Azul gimmick, but, that’s getting way ahead of the story. Going from a rudo to a tecnico wasn’t an easy transition for Hernandez, but, he tried. Unfortunately, no one really gave a damn about the gimmick or the character behind it, and as a result, it flopped. Horribly. We’re talking a Survival-esque flop here, it was that bad. The Promoter had paid Hernandez a bunch of money, and when the gimmick failed, he wanted it back. Like any other poor, struggling wrestler, at his first taste of fabulous wealth (in his case, about five hundred dollars), he spend his money as if it was water, and didn’t have it to give back to the Promoter. (This is the only name he is currently known by. It’s been lost through history.) Angered, the promoter stabbed Hernandez while he was on the way to the ring, and seemingly killed him. A few months later, the Promoter was haunted by a man in a blue mask, that often came to the ring, and interrupted his shows. His champion, another gimmick he thought up, a tecnico named Mascras Del Milagro, or “Masks of Miracle” started going through some changes, and his mask changed from a pristine white, to a few streaks of blue dabbled in. Legend has it that one day in the ring, Mascras Del Milagro beat a large American wrestler by the name of Lou Thesz in a border town near Texas. It was a huge upset, and the Promoter was livid. The match was booked to go in Thesz’s favor so that the NWA would cast an eye toward his fledgling promotion, and bring a bit of money to it. Backstage, The Promoter confronted Milagro, who at the time was thought to be a man named Cristobal Guererro, a distant cousin the the Guererro wrestling family. The Promoter demanded that he turn over his mask, and leave the business forever. It’s said that Cristobal was so heartbroken, he went into a forest near his home, and never returned, dying in the middle of a stand of trees with a broken heart. Months wore on, and the small promotion was nearly bankrupt, thanks to the Promoter’s business practices. He wasn’t savvy with his money, and paid appearance fees to wrestlers no one had heard of, or bring in bears and big dogs to fight with local talent. His wrestling promotion turned into a circus sideshow. Down to his last dime, the Promoter decided to go all out, and promise some exorbitant appearance fees to some of the biggest pieces of talent in Mexico at the time. His main event, a large man named Super Gigante was set to face off against Maniac, a small rudo with a history of taking advantage of his opponents. The Promoter’s plan was simple: He was going to begin the show, excuse himself to the office to count the gate, and slip out a hidden door in the arena. He was on his way to the car about when Maniac was set to pin Super Gigante, as a huge roar came from the crowd when it shouldn’t have. The Promoter was enraged, and walked to a small spot he would often watch the show from, to see if a wrestler was worth being paid. As he looked toward the ring, he saw a man in a blue mask, out with the other two, running circles around them as he unleashed a fury of moves. The small man literally ran circles around the two bigger men, and showed the crowd moves they’d never seen before. For a minute, the man in blue ran circles around them, and the crowd went ballistic. Backstage, the Promoter scrapped the plan to leave, and waited for the man in the blue mask, and grabbed him from behind, cursing him for ruining his show. He ripped the mask off, and revealed it to be the previously thought to be dead Hernandez. The Promoter was livid with Hernandez, and was ready to finish the job until he overheard a few of the fans talking about El Rayo Azul, or “The Blue Streak”, named by the fans by the flurry of moves he hit out of no where, before apparently disappearing. The Promoter reconsidered killing him, and saw his savior. He struck up a conversation with Hernandez, forgiving him for his debts, so long as he kept coming to the shows each week. Hernandez agreed, and the two shook hands on the deal. All Hernandez ever wanted was to wrestle, and all the Promoter ever wanted to do was make money. The Promoter left Hernandez with a stern warning. "If this backfires, amigo, I am going to kill you. I will not hesitate to ensure that if I don't have a future as a rich man, you will not have a future as a man." Hernandez thought about this for a moment, and smiled. "If I fail again, you will not have time to kill me. I'll have beaten you to it." So, as the story goes, the Promoter mortgaged his house to the gills to pay off the debts to Super Gigante, and the rest of the crew. Hernandez took his last thirty dollars and had a spectacular mask and costume made for him, in a shimmering blue fabric. They took everything they had, and arranged for another show to take place in two weeks, in Ciudad Juarez. It started slow, and many times, the Promoter was ready to head down to the ring, knife in hand to fulfill his promise to kill Hernandez. But, the more patient he was, the better the crowds were. The fans loved to see El Rayo Azul come back from tremendous adversity, and triumph over all that was in his way. Wrestling a match against four large men wasn’t uncommon. Standing at just five feet and six inches tall and weighing only one hundred and fifty five pounds, it gave great hope to the citizens of Cuidad Juarez to see the epic struggle of the little man taking on and defeating everything in his path. Of course, back then, the rules of kayfabe were still shrouded in mystery. In Mexico, it was as if real life superheroes were doing battle in the ring. The rudo masks represented demons, sent from the depths of hell to rule the world, and the tecnico masks symbolized the greater good, to fight the demons of hell. And it was huge buisness. Hernandez and the Promoter decided that having just one El Rayo Azul in their small town wasn’t enough. Eventually, they replaced the gimmick with someone similarly sized to stay in Juarez, and toured themselves around Mexico, to huge audiences, and even more money. In each city, they left an El Rayo Azul, and moved on to another, repeating the process over and over. It was absolutely genious. Eventually, Hernandez had enough money, and decided that he didn’t want to be El Rayo Azul anymore. He was ready to give up his life as a wrestler, and become a man of God. So, that’s what he did, and the Promoter found another man by the name of Francisco Gonzales-Cordova to take the character over. Cordova was a career tecnico, and wrestled well. Cordova was a mute who suffered from polio as a child, and became strong through his faith in Jesus Christ. Or, so the legend goes. The years went by, and as the Promoter aged, he knew he couldn’t hold on to the gimmick forever. He sold the entire operation to Hernandez, and died three days later. Hernandez cried for the man when he passed away, despite being stabbed by him, and abused for years before that. Hernandez was quoted before his death in 1998 as saying, “No matter who the man, no matter what the heart, people can, and do change.” Prophetic words, to be sure. Through the years, the gimmick has been passed on quite a few times, and the whole story didn’t go under any really massive changes. It’s stupid to try and reinvent the wheel. The entire premise of the gimmick was to simply give people faith in themselves. And for over sixty years, it has. The gimmick has become symbolic to people in the most poor areas of Mexico, giving them hope that one day, they will be able to rise above. So, how then was I, the first American born, non-hispanic El Rayo Azul able to become one of the men who’ve worn one of the most storied masks in Mexican Wrestling history? I’ve got to tell you that you’re going to think it’s awfully cliched, but, it’s the God’s honest truth. Being from the South, I was a huge wrestling fan. I watched as much as I could, and when I stopped growing at the age of 14, I cursed my five foot, eight inch frame. It was around that time that I discovered Lucha Libre while watching a WCW show and fell in love. There were guys half my size doing moves so acrobatic, they should have been in the circus. I knew if these people could do it, so could I. So, I went to wrestling school and I worked on my body. I got in fantastic shape, and at one-hundred and seventy-three pounds, I was about as big as I could possibly get and still be athletic. At the time, however, Lucha Libre was taking a backseat to the American Strong Style favored by the WWF (it’ll always be the WWF to me. Screw the Pandas.) and I knew my options were going to be limited. When I broke into wrestling about six years ago, I wasn’t the most talented guy in the ring. Sure, I knew how to effectively use my body, but, I got into a position where I was used as spot fodder in larger matches. If some promoter had a crazy idea for some crazy spot, I was usually the guy they called to take the fall. I got paid fairly decently for my services, and back then, that’s really all that mattered to me. I’d do anything for two hundred dollars and proved it, spilling blood in seventeen states, four countries, and over two continents. Still, I wasn’t feeling very secure. Obviously, there’s a huge difference between a guy that can excite the fans just being in the building, and a guy that excites people because of the kinds of punishment you can take. I remember a match in Japan in 2002 that nearly cost me my life. I was going to take a big back bodydrop off a scaffolding, and down onto concrete from 20 feet. I’d done it a few times before, and I knew how to protect myself. I was ready, and I was confident. It was really nothing more than simple rotation vs. velocity. I knew I had to flip slowly, or else I was going to be in a world of trouble. The greenhorn that I was wrestling didn’t know his ass from his elbows, and as I flipped off the scaffolding, I knew something was wrong with the way I was falling. I was going far too fast, and quite honestly, I don’t remember hitting the ground. Watching the footage of the match. I hit face first, stood up instantly, walked a few steps, and collapsed. It was absolutely scary to watch, and I knew I needed to change the way my life was going. I came home after being in the hospital for a few days with a pretty horrible concussion, and a wired shut jaw to hold six teeth in place, and immediately stopped taking the “at the mercy bookings”, as I called them. I figured since I was at the mercy of the banks, my landlord, the gas company, and my credit cards, I had to take them, simply to keep myself living as most people were accustomed to. And that’s when the calls pretty much stopped coming in. Thanks to my reputation, I couldn’t get a regular job to save my life, and ended up in one of those hardcore garbage companies, that got fans simply by doing the most unsafe things they could possibly do with their bodies. I won’t even give that company any press by revealing it’s name, but, any company that’ll allow two females to headline an event promising what amounted to a full on sex show in the ring, doesn’t need any more press. I left after a few months, and stood at a crossroads. The business that I loved, the one that I’d paid my dues with blood, sweat and tears didn’t want me anymore. I could try and hold on to my feeble attempts at getting regular employment or I could simply leave it all behind. I was living in Corpus Christi, Texas at the time, and I was miserable. My car had been repossessed, I’d been evicted from my apartment, and the girl I had been dating decided she couldn’t stay with an out of work loser, so she left me for a drunk. At least he had a job, right? I took what I could carry, and I walked out with just a few shirts, a few pairs of pants, my boots, and an extra pair of shoes. I had thirty four dollars in my pocket, and I walked. I had no destination in mind. I simply wanted to walk until I couldn’t walk anymore. I needed to be somewhere; anywhere other than here. I told my feet not to fail me, and I set out on foot, in seek of somewhere better. My travels took me exactly four miles. I was tired from walking in the dry heat of the day, and stopped at a church in a somewhat rundown neighborhood. The children played in the streets, as the adults made themselves busy. I stopped and set my bag down to rest on a bench in front of the church. People walked up and down the street, and I watched them. I didn’t exactly fit into the neighborhood, but, I didn’t care much. I just wanted to get away from what I knew. “Got any change, esse?” I looked to my left, and saw a haggard older man, tugging at my sleeve. I looked at him for a moment, and reached into my pocket. “Here.” I handed him a folded bill from my pocket without looking, and smiled. “Sorry it can’t be more.” “Thanks, senor!” he said with a gleam in his eye. He held the money out at me, and smiled a somewhat toothless grin, his breath smelling of wine and peppermints. I’d handed him a twenty, not a dollar like I’d thought. I was going to reach out and take it away, but, it was my own fault for not looking first. “You sure you wanna give me this much?” For a second, I thought I should say no and wanted to give him a dollar instead, but, I knew that my conscience wouldn’t let me do that. “Yeah. I’m sure. It’s my fault for not looking, and just wanting you to go away. Let me rephrase that…” “No, it’s alright. Thanks, esse.” “You’re welcome.” He started to walk away as I cursed myself. He turned around again, and handed the twenty back to me. “Thanks. I don’t need this.” “No, take it. Seriously. I don’t need it. Might as well throw everything else away too.” He looked at me, sagely, and nodded. “No. I think you need this more than I do.” He was right. I did need it. But, I was going to stand my ground. “Look. I don’t want to be reminded of where it came from. In fact--” I said, as I reached into my pocket, and pulled the remaining fourteen dollars out of my pocket “—take the rest. I mean it. I just want to be done.” “Man, you’re heavy,” he said to me. “You know, sometimes, when everything seems to fall apart, there’s only one place to turn.” He reached into his pocket, and pulled out a large blue crucifix, with large, gaudy jewels on it, and “no matter what, esse, He’s listening.” “Yeah. Trust me, there’s people with bigger problems than me. He should help them. I’m just a guy that falls under the radar.” “He’ll listen. You just need to listen first.” I handed him the fourteen dollars and stood up, putting a hand on his shoulder. “Thank you. Please, take it. Consider it me paying you thirty four dollars for this crucifix. That way, when he’s ready to talk, I’ll listen.” The old man smiled at me, and put his hands on my face. “Bless you.” He walked away, with a smile on his face. I stood with the cross in my hand, and looked at it’s reflective surface. I spent a few minutes looking deep into the mirrored finish, looking into the eyes of Christ. People walked behind me looking at me, and I honestly can't tell you just how long I stood there. After a few moments, I saw a car pulling up on the curb. I looked up and turned around just in time to hear the gun fire.
