Letters: Juda's Perspective

30 November 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2006-11-30 What follows are the remains of a journal (to call these charred scraps of scribbled notes, found buried beneath the toppled carcass of a ruined hovel an actual book, would be a slight exaggeration), a journal written by the hand of a little-known man in the lands of the East. Some argue he was never an Easterner to begin with; that he and his Order are largely unknown to the lands of Man and Beast should come as no surprise to the unwary reader. You know not the horrors of their past, and should be thankful. He is the sole surviving initiate of Jugis Ordo (translated from Latin as the Order of Constantine) champion of a secret sect long dead, and savior to those who no longer draw the breath to speak their gratitude. His name was Juda Jarib. This is his story. < . > < . > < . > "Give a man a torch and he’ll feel his way through the terrors of the night. Show a man the Light and he will feel no fear, and will himself become the torch." They were my father’s words. He was fond of ending his sermons with them, or opening with them, or spouting them to anyone who stood still long enough to catch his ever-roaming eye. People of the village appreciated his knack for verbose soliloquy, a trait that landed my father a tidy little living as the only holy man on our lonely little island, a place found on few maps outside the Roman Empire. It has since been the burden of the Order to keep these records, but the appearance of the One Evil has instigated more historical bonfires than could fill the fields of my homeland, even as they roasted along with the smoldering remains of my countrymen. I still smell the piles of the diseased, sightless wrecks of men and children stacked up like so many dominoes and set ablaze in a futile attempt to preserve our way of life. But I get ahead of myself. First came the Stranger and how my father’s own goodly nature proved to be the undoing of our quiet little universe. When the Stranger washed ashore on the splintered remains of what we could only assume had once been a lifeboat, nobody quite knew what to do with him. The man was thin as a reed and reeked of whisky, half-mad and half-starved, screaming incoherent gibberish at people who didn’t exist whenever one of us tried to touch him. My father eventually placed his palm over the man’s fevered forehead, using salts to put him to sleep long enough for us to transport him back to the village. My ever-advancing age may have rendered my memory less than dependable in recent years, but my friends, I tell you I will never forget the look of raw terror that held this Stranger as surely as a hawk does its wriggling prey. No man looks as he did who greets the rising sun with sanity. "He is extremely sick," my father declared after having spent an hour trying to communicate with the poor bastard. Most of the village of gathered outside my father’s temple-turned-infirmary, trying to catch a glimpse of the Stranger since word of his terminal condition had spread like popicorn seeds on the back of a gailstorm. "His speech is riddled with fever, but by God I know a cry for help when I see it. This man is from the Dark Lands." Knowing what I know now, even after having touched the soil of every region of what the Order had always called the Dark Lands (the Unknown Kingdom, the war-addled nations in the East, beyond the Mediterranean Sea) I would still agree that what we did was right. The Stranger died before dawn the next morning, but my father’s attempts to ease his pain were nothing short of heroic. In his fading moments the man spoke one simple phrase, his eyes suddenly clear and his voice as conversational as if he had just woken up from an afternoon nap. This Stranger spoke out in our own native tongue, a language he had no rightful way of knowing. "The Army of the Damned cometh, and with it rides the pale face of a new King." Frightening final words. My father and I were the only people to bare witness to his passing. We quickly agreed that our quiet little village did not need to concern themselves over the Stranger’s dying prophetic rhetoric; we would tell them he died in his sleep, peacefully and without pain. When the Stranger rose from the dead two days later and descended upon two of the farms on the outskirts of our village like a bloody walking nightmare, everyone knew an evil hand had begun to hover over our island. We prayed for the Light to show us the path. We turned to my father. He was our torch. < . > < . > < . > The following entry was found on a shelf of the Vatican library, stuffed between two copies of "Ten Great Ways to Tell Black Demons from Green: A Compendium for the Colorblind." It hasn’t been pawed over since the day it was catalogued; a lonely piece of tattered parchment considered by most historians to be nothing more than a scrap of fleeting fiction, a foreign spook story used to keep children in their beds after the sun goes down. Most historians would therefore be wrong. < . > < . > < . > Ezraél Jarib was without equal the most righteous, selfless man to set foot along the shores of the Isles. The entire expanse of our pocket Empire (a concentric ring of nine tiny islands and atolls; a week’s journey south off the peninsula of Macedonia could put you there with strong enough winds and favorable waves) was but his unofficial backyard, a wounded land he saw as his duty to make well again. He was playfully dubbed the Sultan of Salvation around the village, which I suppose would have made me the crown Prince of Piety. After all, I was just the son of a preacher-man, as the old bard’s hymn goes. Even so, not even my father could do anything to cure the dark affliction that possessed the Stranger. It were as if the rules that govern life and death had been defiled at such a primal level that not even the Light could illuminate its darker recesses. That lone vagabond sailor brought a world of undeath into a community of peace, spreading a disease so vile we had no defense. "Have heart, my brothers," became my father’s mantra. "There is no evil afoot here, only misguided souls of the dead looking for a Shepard to lead them back to providence." I loved my father dearly, but even then I knew that his optimism was dangerous. When we finally managed to rouse a group of villagers to find and destroy the undead Stranger, I think that he honestly believed he could bring that reanimated corpse back from the clutches of death. We knew he was stubborn, a trait that I proudly inherit to this day; we never thought he had become completely mad. We found the Stranger in a cottage along the coast of the sea, tucked neatly beneath a circle of willow oaks that had provided the Belize family a beautiful plot to raise their children for seven generations. No one had seen Hamád or his wife for a good many days, which was unusual considering Hamád was one of the more active members of the village council. As our resolute band of holy farmers entered his darkened homestead, we discovered a scene so wholly obscene, I dare not lie and say I was able to hold control over my bowels. The Stranger was there, all right. Our torches spilled an uneven light into the corner of Hamád’s cottage, catching the damned monstrosity in the act red handed. Literally, considering his soulless white eyes turned away from the meal he had made of Hamád’s youngest daughter to regard our horrified party with wordless contempt. It hissed; I lost my bowels for a second time. "Child of the Light!" my father bellowed, raising his staff in an act of illumination that forced many of our eyes away. "Leave this feeble demon! Turn away from the corruption which has taken you and drive it back into the ocean whence it came!" My lips moved in a silent prayer that served to shield my ears from the guttural insanity this unholy Stranger then began to babble. Steeling myself against the putrid odor of spoiled meat that permeated every inch of the macabre massacre that used to be Hamád’s cottage, I spread my palms to the heavens and began to pray in earnest. I began to pray for understanding. I began to pray for guidance. Mostly I prayed for forgiveness, because it was then that I turned the awesome powers of the priesthood on this pitiful creature, no matter if it had once had a human heart or not. "No Juda!" said my father. "It still has a soul, you cannot banish it without first cleansing the man beneath!" In the face of this outcry, the living corpse began to laugh. He once again spoke in the language of our island, turning our own native tongue and corrupting it with hate: "You cannot banish that which does not die, mortal. I’ve roasted the limbs of your ancestors over the spitfires of Hell, and I’m here to tell you, they tasted sweeter than life." "Blasphemer!" my father roared. I had studied the arts of faith under the patient gaze of my father for decades, and never once, not once, did I ever see him lose his temper. Until the moment he backhanded the damned Stranger with his bare fist, sending the creature tumbling into the darker corners of the cottage, I don’t believe he had ever laid an angry hand on another living being. He must have finally realized they were not dealing with anything that possessed the capacity for life; just a morbid obsession and eternal kinship with the more sinister side of death. Half a dozen villagers charged into the heart of darkness, flailing family heirlooms and pointed farm equipment, using weapons that were as unfamiliar to their hands as a fish is to sand. The Stranger snarled in the shadows, fighting against the crazed piteousness that burned in each of our eyes, unable to overcome that which it ultimately did not understand. The creature had threatened that it could not die; this was only a half-lie, as since it was already dead, a quick and brutal dismemberment seemed to produce the same result. Nobody saw Hamád’s dead baby girl until it was too late. By the time my father noticed the remains of her toddler-corpse crawling across the floorboards, her teeth were already embedded deep within his leg. "Not Haley! Oh Light above, not the child!" My father, the great Ezraél Jarib, was more offended by the existence of the bastardized baby girl before him than of the fact that it had sunk its suddenly fang-like teeth into his calves. He wilted like a flower; clattering to the floor, his staff fell beside him. I remember very little of what happened next. Lost in a haze of fear and adrenaline I can only remember that it was at that moment I realized I would never be as strong as my father, and as such, had absolutely no qualms about dropkicking an undead toddler back into the grave. We cleared the cottage and piled the remains of Hamád’s family (the parts we could find, anyway) along with the Stranger and set the entire homestead ablaze. We watched, holding hands and praying to a more merciful Creator, as the tiny little house on the beach disappeared against the setting sun. My father never walked again. We thought we had vanquished some greater evil, but… [The rest of the parchment dissolves into smeared water-spots, some of the words intelligible but mostly just runny ink.] < . > < . > < . > The final piece to Juda Jarib’s thousand year-old puzzle was found between the pages of an old Bible, an original Gutenberg in the Österreichische Library of Vienna, Austria. Originally thought to be a poorly transcribed verse in the Book of Psalms, it was quickly identified as a foreign document that was never meant to be found. The chilling first-hand account of what occurred on that little Macedonian island stands as one of the earliest recorded cases of complete racial genocide in written history. Aside from the incident with Noah and his floating zoo, of course. < . > < . > < . > A man should only have to bury his father once. I was forced to put him under the earth twice. Do I pay penance for the sins of my race, and the evil we failed to defeat? Answers fail me now, as they did then. As they will when the One Evil decides the time has come. I knew the sickness was going to take my father, but what I didn’t anticipate was how quickly it began to manifest itself. In the aftermath of the tragedy that befell Hamád and his kin, a candlelight vigil was held in my father’s temple to honor those who had passed on. He led us all, as he always did, bestowing his strength in turn to each of our silent faces. In his own way, my father felt relieved to have destroyed the demon we discovered that day in the cottage, even at the expense of his own failing health. He knew it was coming, as did I, but we never spoke on it. Instead he prayed, hiding his weakness with a muffled cough into his hand between readings, never blinking, always fading away. He used his staff as a cane once his leg had turned black around the edges of the child’s bite. So much weakness; so little warning. I was awakened by the village midwife, still yet an hour from first sunlight. Hysterical and sobbing, the old woman roused me without saying a word; she simply wiped her eyes across her apron as she shook me from slumber. I knew my father had passed away. I thank God to this day I was never forced to do the things to his body I was later called upon to perform on countless others, mindless as they were. We buried him behind the temple; I mourned his passing deeply, but I knew the tides had changed by the look on every sour face in our village. They all looked to me, Juda, to lead them. I was no longer the pious prince; I was now their sultan, the new face of their salvation. The day I delivered my first sermon at my father’s podium, I don’t believe I had ever seen so many heads packed shoulder to shoulder in the space of his holy temple. Everyone on the island had come; in their greatest time of need, in the fearful throes of the night since the Stranger had set his foul foot on our sand, not once did the congregation swell to such impossible numbers. My heart was swollen, filled with the love of my father and the power he had held for so long. It was the power of the people, and in those reassured faces I found solace from my grief. But the faces… Something was horribly wrong; I felt uneasy for no reason. I kept looking through the crowded hall, trying to place my finger on what it was that seemed out of place, all the while trying to honor my father in my first sermon under his roof. The people prayed, they cheered and cried, we mourned his loss together, and at once they rose to their feet. Not the living ones. The dead ones. It was uncanny. As soon as the scattered few became every fourth villager, then every third, the faces that stood from the crowd began to change somehow. I saw relatives no longer alive holding hands by the light of a candle, their sullen eyes sunk deep within skulls dressed in the skin of stretched leather. These were not the savage creations of the Stranger; the living dead, these ghosts from the grave, were of another making all together. Their complexions were far more fair than they had any right to be, unchanged by years under the earth, but here they stood, basking in the glow of my fiery eulogy for the man who taught me everything. A woman screamed somewhere in the crowd, only now noticing that her brother, three years deceased, had appeared at her side. The midwife fainted as her dead husband placed a firm hand on her shoulder. Children cried. Women shrieked. The specters sighed. They all died. It were as if a great wave of movement had seized my father’s temple, churning the gathered mass like butter as it descended into utter chaos. The bodies of the deceased acted as one, grabbing their nearest relative and tearing out a chunk of throat or arm or belly, tearing into lovers and feasting on their slippery entrails, goring children and tossing them gaily to the ground like broken dolls. Death hung in the air like a thick blanket, surrounding the congregation and drowning them in its stench. The center aisle had become a running trough of blood, filled with writhing bodies and grinning ghouls, too many dead to count and too few living to care. I saw these things, the horror close enough to touch and thick enough to taste, herding those brave and quick enough to run to my podium through the back. It was a silly gesture, really; was I naive enough to think we would escape? That we would be allowed such a mercy? I was terrified enough to believe so, anyway, and angry enough to act. Our action proved futile. A horde of animated undeath met us beyond the back gate, and at their head stood my father. His jaw sat awkwardly in his skull, perhaps a result of his fever? He bore holes into our alarmingly tiny group with sockets devoid of eyes, ripped clean from his corpse by a force I’d rather not describe in this text. Dressed in the simple black robes he had worn to a thousand sermons, my father, Ezraél Jarib, raised his arms in welcome. A trail of dirt fell from his sleeves, raining back upon the ground. "We are part of a greater plan, my son." His words were hollow. They were not the words of my father. "We are living in the time of the One, the days of the Worm, of the coming King and his infallible and majestic hold over this simple mortal coil. Yea he tempteth me, and lo; through the eyes of my child shall the world be undone!" The walking memories of our dead surrounded us, pushing us back against the temple. I screamed at the creature my father had become, raising my hands to the heavens and damning His very name, the name of the One Evil that sought to destroy our way of life. I spoke the name of Iblis, and in that moment, the temple began to burn. The few surviving villagers were taken and tossed into the inferno of my father’s life work. I tried to close my eyes against their pleading expressions; my ears bled as they cried, begging for mercy from a God that refused to listen or lend a sympathetic hand against these unholy abominations. The bonfire of my kinsmen built, smothering the land in the smell of sugary meat, but there was no smoke to mark its movement. Smokeless fire, a symbol of the One, had engulfed my very world. Windows cracked and shattered, leaving nothing but a bare husk of stone to stand alone against the rising of the flames. My father smiled, stretching the skin around eyes that did not exist, holding my chin in one hand as I tried not to lose my mind. His familiar smile, twisted with evil, parted one final time to release his silver tongue. "Accept me, Juda. Accept me now and willingly, and all of this will end." I spat in the demon’s face. Releasing my chin, the creature masquerading as my father let the spittle run down his cheek. "Your father is dead," the sinister minister declared. "I sucked the marrow from his spine and gouged his eyes with my bare fingers. In time, you will wish I had shown your race the same brand of sympathy." Spreading his arms, the demon fell backward into the flames of my father’s temple, laughing in a most shrill and unpleasant manner. The inferno seemed to swell, then shot through the roof in a column of white lightening. I tried to move out of its path, but the musty creatures at my sides refused to let me leave. I saw the sky rip open with denial, swirling dangerously, soaking up the white hot light that filled my father’s sanctuary. As if sucked through a funnel, the smokeless fire jumped out of the temple and into the air, shooting through the clouds like an arrow of evil. It happened so quickly; before I could adjust my eyes to the sudden darkness, the fire was gone. All that remained was the smoldering wreckage of my birthright. Then, there was silence. A tingle in the air. And then it had me. I felt the peal of thunder more than heard it, rumbling in my chest like a herd of raging deer. It shot back from the clouds, striking me center and incinerating the dead all around me. My hair burned away and my skin began to crack, pulsing with a white hot energy that surged from the sky with terrifying power. It was too scalding to touch, but I was drowning in it, losing myself in it, feeling the tainted presence of the One fill every pore, part and appendage of my body. My soul was simply stripped away. All that remained was Him. He speaks to me in my dreams. He let me live so that I might carry on his seed, spreading his presence far and wide through the history of time, planting his future kingdom so that a new garden might grow. I grow so tired. ~<>~<>~<>~ These three letters, written more than a thousand years ago by the great-great ancestor of Jin Jarib, were stolen over the course of six months and compiled in secret. Translated by hand, Jin stuffed them into an envelope and mailed the story, in its entirety, to the one man he thought might find it useful: Jason Snow. Attached to the translated manuscripts, written on a yellow Post-It note, was this: Grime and greasepaint torn asunder, The secret is not mine to tell. Lie awake do I, and wonder, Are you my Heaven, or I your Hell?
