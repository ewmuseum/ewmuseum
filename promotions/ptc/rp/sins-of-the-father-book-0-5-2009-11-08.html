Sins of the Father: Book 0.5

8 November 2009
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2009-11-08 Home was a split level in the Bronx for most of my life: once I went to college, home became a room in a house with seven other people and the chaos that you’d expect. Now, my life is mainly on the road, but home is split between my Washington Heights apartment and Momma Knox’s brownstone in Williamsburg. “How are you not nervous?” asked Rosie, as we sat on the L train, moving far too slowly, “Your mom is dating some guy for the first time in forever and we’re meeting him for the first time.” Dunno, I said, I’m just not. The truth is, I really wasn’t. Mom hadn’t dated, as far as I knew, since she and my dad divorced, but why shouldn’t she be happy? This dude seemed like he made her happy, so who cares? Besides, I’m never home anymore. Heck. I even put on a tie for this one. Rosie looked gorgeous in her skirt and tank top, and she even wore heels. If she could make it up the stairs to the street without tripping, we’d all be surprised. ************ “You must be Randy,” said the man with the graying temples and tweed sport coat, “Nice to meet you, I’m Robert.” Randall, I replied, I prefer Randall. Nice to meet you. He seemed like a decent enough guy, he had a firm handshake and laugh lines on his face, and he could look you in the eye. “And you are,” he asked. “Rose,” said Rosie, “I made this for you,” as she handed over a pie. Not just any pie; this was an apple cinnamon pie that took her more than a day to make. Don’t ask. “Great,” said Robert, “Bridge, the kids are here!” Mom walked quickly through the kitchen door, and immediately hugged both of us. “I’ve missed you, Randall!” she said, “You’ll have to tell me all about your recent trip. Rosie, you are more beautiful every time I see you. Can I get you anything?” Let me help, I said. “No,” replied Mom, “You are a guest in this home, therefore I’d like you to just sit and enjoy the night. Now what can I get for everyone?” “Scotch and water,” said Robert. Beer, I asked. “If you’ve got wine,” said Rosie, “I’ll give you a hand.” “That, I’ll take,” said Mom, “Would love your help.” The ladies disappeared into the kitchen, while Robert and I sat down in the living room. I took my dad’s old chair, while he sat on the couch. So, I said, where did you two meet? “My business partner had a heart attack,” said Robert, “and she was the nurse on call when I was able to go visit him.” What’s your business, I asked. “Lawyer,” he replied, “I have a private practice in the village; we do personal and corporate finance. What do you do for a living?” Here it comes. Everyone hears this and has the same reaction. I’m a professional wrestler, I said, and I held my breath. “Interesting,” said Robert, “And that affords you travel opportunity?” Quite a bit, I said. AWKward… Fortunately, the ladies reemerged, with both hands full. I stood up, ostensibly to help Mom and Rosie lay out some snacks, but mainly I was glad someone else was in the room. Maybe I wasn’t quite as calm as I thought. ************ What bothered me the most, I think, wasn’t that he was condescending, or prone to anger, or any more arrogant than any other lawyer I’ve ever met. “So when Robert’s firm finished the due diligence, it turned out there was plenty of money in the budget to finish remodeling the ICU,” said Mom, “We’re going to have the fundraiser, regardless, since everyone put so much work into it, but it’s nice to have the cushion.” “Great,” said Rosie, “I was thinking, you could probably still hold it at the bar—“ “Come now, Rosalyn,” said Robert, “Don’t you think a seedy bar in the Bronx is hardly the place for an important fundraiser that could really help people?” It was the way he was constantly putting down Rosie. Miss Ivy has put on a ton of events there, I said, and I think it’ll really draw. Besides, Rosie is a great manager and will pull people in off the street if need be. “I think it’s a lovely idea,” said Mom, “and I think we could really do well there.” Does the world really need another black tie affair that normal human beings could never afford, I asked, or is it more important for more people to come to these things and support the actual cause? “I have no doubt,” said Robert, “and I’m sure Rosalyn can throw a nice little party, but something as important as this shouldn’t be left to amateurs – no offense, sweetheart.” “Oh, none taken,” replied Rose. But there was. ************ “Then he asked me if I ever ran a business before,” said Rosie, as she downed a shot of a mystery concoction that Loriann had created earlier in the evening, “Who does that?” Dinner had been pleasant enough, for the most part, and Mom did her best to defend me and Rosie from Robert when things got heated, but she really shouldn’t have been stuck in the middle like she was. “God, what a bastard,” replied Loriann, working her first solo Sunday shift at TC’s in quite a while, “What did you say?” “What could I say?” asked Rosie, “I’m not going to get into an argument with the guy that Momma Knox is dating.” “Knox?” asked Loriann. I shrugged. He’s one of those guys, that unless you’re contributing something to society in the traditional sense, you’re really not doing anything of value, I said, and while he’s wrong and I know he’s wrong, and Mom knows he’s wrong, that wasn’t the time to bring it up. “You’re calling her tomorrow, right?” asked Loriann. No doubt, I said. Loriann poured another trio of shots, and we downed them in a hurry. “You should invite him over here when Ivy is working, and goad him into saying something negative about wrestling. That’ll learn ‘em.” Rosie bristled. Don’t go there, I said to Loriann. “Why not?” asked the bartender. “Because apparently RK is exempt from the judgmental eye of Douchey McDoucherson, esquire, because he wants Momma Knox’ little boy to like him,” replied Rosie. He told us point blank, he sees the value in what I do in that I’m an entertainer, and I can use the experience in all the travel that I do to learn about the world in general and be in a better place to contribute, I explained, But I’m sure there’s a great heaping hunk of wannabe stepdad mixed in, too. Loriann excused herself to wait on another set of customers, and I leaned into Rosie. Don’t let him get to you, I said, he doesn’t know you, he doesn’t know how hard you work, and he didn’t seem to want to hear it. But you can bet if he wants to hang around, Mom is going to make him understand. “It’s not that,” replied Rosie, “Well, it’s partially that. But it’s mainly the fact that I’m just a bartender. I’ve never felt that way before, ever. Miss Ivy trusted me to keep watch on this place, knowing how much it means to her, and that’s the job I thought I took.” Isn’t it, I asked. “It is, but it isn’t. What would my father think about the fact that his daughter tends bar in a dive in practically the same neighborhood she grew up in? I wonder about that, and I think he’d be disappointed that I never got outta here.” You can’t second – guess yourself like that, I replied, as I stroked her hair, I think your dad would see how much you mean to this place and he’d be proud of that. Do you think my own father would actually be proud of me? He point blank told me that wanting to be a professional wrestler was a waste of time, so all I’ve been doing for the past six years has been meaningless. “There’s a difference,” replied Rosie, “You had a dream and you pursued it and got to live it. Pop died when I was barely a teenager, I didn’t know what I wanted to do, and sometimes I feel like I’ve just been drifting ever since. Besides that, your father is still alive. You can still have the talk with him.” My father, I started, but had to take a second to continue. That’s always been a sore subject. Let’s try that again. My father hasn’t contacted me outside of a graduation card six months after high school finished since he left. He’s as much a part of my life as yours is, but you’ve got the good memories on your side. “Another round?” asked Loriann. Yeah, I said, but just give us a minute? I like this one – she’s efficient and tactful. Loriann is going to be the next great TC’s bartender. My point, I said to Rosie, is that you can’t live your life based on imagined expectations that someone who isn’t around anymore may or may not’ve had for you. You need to live the life you have to live, and trust that your father would’ve been proud of the fact that your main goal is your own happiness. If I wrestled solely to try and get my dad’s acceptance, I’d probably have broken my neck two years ago. For the first time in hours, Rosie smiled. “I love you,” she said. I know, I replied, with a smirk that made her laugh, and I hugged her. As I did, my gaze drifted to the telephone behind the bar. I had the old man’s number and address; I sent him the clipping from the Torch after I won Sears Tower, because I did need him to know I was living the dream, with or without his support. But I still have to fight the urge, after every match, to call him up and tell him that I’m even more of a success now than I was before the evening started. I think Rosie would understand that perfectly, if I ever told her. But I can’t. At the very least, in this moment I have to be her strength.
