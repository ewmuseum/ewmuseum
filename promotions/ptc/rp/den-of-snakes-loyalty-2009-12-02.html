Den of Snakes: Loyalty

2 December 2009

Date: 2009-12-02 September 13, 2009 Jethro bent backward, his back cracking as he stretched himself out. Mead Street Fitness was empty, allowing him to blare his Metallica CDs while he spat curses during his lifts. Sweat drenched his red tank top, bleeding down to the crotch of his black biker shorts. His fake tan had streaked over, making him look like a foolish bulky tiger as he sauntered around the gym, filled to the brim with false bravado. It was back to the deadlift. He had to get himself ready; the Mr. Southwestern Mississippi Regionals were in a few weeks, and it would be his first body building event. He wanted to make an impact, and do so as quickly as possible. He had to get himself in better shape by then though. Also had to learn how to hold a pose better; nerves were his problem, causing him to spasmodically twitch every time he looked in the mirror. Judges were going to look at that as a weakness, and he wanted to show that he had full control of his body. Centering his ring fingers over the metal strip grooves of the bar, he exploded through the lift, bringing two hundred pounds to his chin before lowering it back down. Come on pussy his mind roared, second place won’t mean shit. He powered through, burning himself out at twenty reps before dropping the weight, wheezing as he stumbled around on rubbery legs. Pounding his chest like a gorilla, he roared over James Hetfield, wiping sweat from his forehead with slaps to the face. He walked past the front desk, grabbing his keys and cigarettes before going outside to take a break. The breeze cut through him, causing him to shiver as he locked the door, planting his back into the metal frame after he was done. His trembling fingers fished a cigarette out of his paper pack, cupping the end as he flipped open his zippo. He took a drag, closing his eyes and resting his head against the cold steel. “You must be Tolliver.” “Last name.” His eyes darted to the right. “But yeah.” “We’ve never met I hazard to say.” It was an older man, not heavy set but also not rail thin. Wrinkles and pockmarks covered his face in odd spots, his graying mane done up in a ponytail. He was in a wrinkled dress shirt and black slacks, his fingers pressing creases out of his amber colored tie. “I’m Clarence Watkins.” Oh shit he thought. It was the man brokering the deal from the Mexican cartel. This couldn’t be good. “Why don’t we go for a ride?” “I don’t think so.” “So you know who I am?” “Terrence and Royce have talked about you,” he started, suddenly losing the taste for tobacco. “I don’t have no say in any of that though. I’m just a soldier.” Watkins gave him a pat on the cheek. He wasn’t the one who made the decisions in the crew, his job mostly dealing with cooking and cutting methamphetamine at the Stonewall Motel. But that wasn’t going to stop Watkins; if anything, that might have made him an even bigger target, due to the fact that he was such a big time factor in Kingsley’s meager supply side. If he went down, the crew was going to be set back, almost forcing their hand to go through with the deal. They’d brought it to the Mexicans, Royce working some of his old contacts from his days in California. The idea had been to stretch out their operation along the Mississippi border, maybe moving into Louisiana given that the whole area was an untapped market. But ever since Terrence had come back, there’d been no talk of making the move. There was only so much they could cook thanks to the Patriot Act making it so damn hard to get the simple precursors. Watkins tried to relax him, knowing full well that if Valley had been in his ear that he’d have to get the boy to trust him. “I did time with Royce Valley. I know what you are. And I know what he’d try to do to me if I tried to do what you figure I’m set to do. But I’m not set to do anything. We’re just going for a ride.” Jethro swallowed hard. He knew he was getting in Watkins truck, no matter what he said. “Where to?” “Jackson,” Watkins patted him on the shoulder. “Trust me. It’ll be worth your while.” The lights to Autumn’s trailer were still on, but Terrence knew she was already asleep. She was never one to stay up this late. While they might have been distant since the incident with the Kingslians, he couldn’t help but feel an obligation to make sure she was safe. After all, with his crazy father still out there manipulating his cult to do as he saw fit, another attack could come at any moment. Royce was in the driver’s seat of the Thunderbird, Annabelle on his lap and at the ready just in case. Terrence had walked around the trailer while she’d been at work at Lilybelle, noticing that all the windows had been replaced. Her door was another issue; the thing was held shut by an elastic cord hooked around the metal lip of the door frame, and every time he went to push it open, the hook would come loose from the groove and fall onto the porch. Royce couldn’t be there at all times; he had meth to cook. But more and more, Benny’s constant pawning off of his little brother on him like he was some sort of babysitter was making it difficult for him to get any sleep. Thankfully, Oscar was willing to watch of the kid most evenings. The old man ran the Stonewall Motel, and rather enjoyed the company, even if Bobby Crowley Junior was a load to handle under the best of circumstances. Not that Terrence could blame Benny for his constant disappearing act; he’d practically ran out on the crew to chase his foolish wrestling dream, spending the entire time on the travel back thinking of how to get out while he still had the chance. His cell phone came alive, startling the both of them as he dug into his pocket to fish it out. He didn’t recognize the number on the caller ID, so he let it ring a few times before finally pressing the button to take the call. “Is this,” she had the rasp of an older woman, “Terrence Kingsley?” He wasn’t used to not knowing who exactly was calling him up, thinking for a second on whether to respond. “Yeah. Who is this?” “I know this is so late into the night, but we’ve been absolutely swamped all day today. My name is Cheryl Watts. I’m calling on the behalf of the Vice President of Talent Relations of PRIME, Lisa Tyler.” “Uh,” he started as Royce peered over towards him, ready to ask who was on the other end. Terrence raised his finger up, motioning for him to keep quiet. “Yeah. No trouble, just hanging around my girlfriend’s place.” “That’s great,” her response was static, almost automated in its nature. “As you may know, PRIME runs an annual tournament called the Jewel in the Crown. What we’ve been doing the past few years is selecting free agent talent to fill spots, the thought process being that PRIME is making an investment in said talent to see how they perform on the big stage.” He tried to ask her a question, but she kept on reading off her practiced speech. “We just received a tape of one of your matches with Just Wrestling and we’d like to offer you a chance to work with PRIME in the Jewel in the Crown.” “Su...” he stuttered, his heart beginning to race. “Sure.” “Keep in mind this is not a contract offer. If you perform at a satisfactory level, we will be willing to discuss a formal offer, but that is predicated on your level of success as well as your professionalism.” His head was swimming, the call he’d been waiting for his entire life finally having come. Now, he wouldn’t have to work the American Legion Buildings and sweatboxes in the South just hoping to get a chance. “I’m for it,” he tried to sound collected, but he couldn’t stem to the tide of his elation, practically beaming through the receiver as a wellspring of emotions threatened to rob him of dry eyes. “That’s good Terrence Kingry. We’re in contact with the Just offices. We’ll be sending you a plane ticket with an itinerary for next Tuesday. PRIME looks forward to seeing you then.” “Than...thank you,” he responded, but the other end had already hung up. He closed the phone, his trembling fingers brushing through his beard as he tried to regulate his breathing. This was his ticket out of Jefferson County, out of the drug dealing and loan sharking, out of specter of his crazy old man and his cult. Now, he could leave and never have to worry about coming back. It was their ticket out. Royce had been watching the entire scene play out, watching Terrence as he shifted about in his seat. “Who was that?” “Something big,” he started, looking over into his eyes. “Something real big.” “Somebody hurt?” “No,” he chuckled. “You know how I’ve been trying to do the wrestling thing?” The second he said anything about it, Royce was already rolling his eyes. “Next week, I’m going to be taking another vacation.” Royce had abided by Terrence’s desire to wrestle because all he’d been doing were spot shows that were within hours of the county. It kept the boy sharp and in shape. And it probably made him feel better to get a paycheck that at least seemed honest for something he’d been doing for free most of his life. But when he’d sprung up on his crew that he was going to the west coast to wrestle, it rubbed him the wrong way, especially because he never said when he was coming back. He never took their calls while he was out there, leaving Royce to run the operation as best as he could while the leader was out trying to make a name for himself doing something else. Then, there was the deal with buying supply from the cartel. They’d been waiting on Terrence to make the call; they had the money ready to buy in, and they had the contacts looking to help them. Royce knew that they’d been jerking the cartel around for far too long, that eventually, they’d want an answer, regardless of what it might be. They would probably want blood just on the principle of having to wait. “You ain’t been the same since you came back,” Royce started as he rubbed his temples with his thumbs. “You acting like you’re half in and half out.” Terrence looked away. “Maybe I am.” He wasn’t going to let him act like a damn tourist. “That might be okay for Jethro Tolliver, but you’re the brains behind this. It’s on you to keep this going. This is realer than any of that wrestling shit—” “Why’s that?” “It just is. Don’t be thick. Before you left to go out west we were talking about expansion. And we all wanted it. But we waited for you to get back to make a decision. Now we got seventy-five grand sitting in a crummy ass motel, waiting to be used to buy into the cartel. That’s why it’s more real.” “Whatever, man.” “No!” he shouted, grabbing Terrence by the shoulder and making him look him in the eye. “I’m sick of this shit. Your heads in the fucking clouds and when it ain’t you’re using that bullshit with your old man as a crutch. And we can’t afford that shit right now.” Kingsley grabbed him by the wrist, violently yanking his arm away. He was surprised by the strength the boy had. “You gave this to me. It’s my call. Don’t you forget it.” It was far too cold in the cabin of Watkins truck. Jethro wrapped his arms around his chest, rubbing his hands up and down his biceps to try and keep warm. For whatever reason, the air conditioner was cranked to the max, Clarence’s puffs of smoke filling the cabin with a sickeningly sweet smell that made him gag. While he’d been promised that he wasn’t going to be harmed, he couldn’t help but worry, his stomach churning his sinking heart as he fought to keep a stoic veneer. But everything inside him was screaming; Watkins had been in the area trying to force the Kingsley crew into making a decision for the Gulf Coast Cartel to supply their methamphetamine. Royce might have known Watkins from when they were cellmates in San Quentin, but that didn’t mean he trusted in the man; he told them he’d slit all their throats if he had the chance. They’d ridden to Jackson in silence, keeping to themselves. Now, they were just in park, waiting at a bus stop for lord knows what. “So how they treat you?” He didn’t know how to take Watkins question, his teeth chattering as he spoke. “What?” “I told you not to worry. I ain’t going to do anything to you. How they treat you?” “Fine. Fine...I guess.” “If you weren’t shaking, I might be liable to believe you.” He reached for the vents, trying to move the gusts away from his body. “It’s the AC.” “Oh...” Watkins chuckled. “I’m sorry. Let me fix that.” He reached over to the console, turning up the heat. He smoked his cigarillo, easing back into his seat. “I know Royce from way back, and I have an understanding of where you got your stripes. You weren’t working with those grease monkeys in the Cerberus MC. You were with a bunch of Neo-Nazis, weren’t you?” “It was a complicated time.” “Nothing complicated about it,” Watkins told him as he snaked his hand onto his shoulder. “You’re a front runner. You just got that look about you. You also look like you’re over compensating for something. And you also look like you’ve been having to eat shit from a bunch of bitter fuckers. And why? Because you were an errand boy for the guy who was running them fucking bikers out of existence?” He didn’t like to think about it, coming up with a justification for why he’d pledged his loyalty to the Brotherhood in the first place. “It was either Spisak or nothing.” “Believe me; I understand. I’m more like you than you realize.” As he continued to talk, a bus ambled by, slowing to a stop before letting the last passengers off for the night. Watkins watched as the people began filing away. “I need you to get something out of the back of the truck for me.” He didn’t move. “Just do it.” Jethro got out and popped the tailgate, lifting the tonneau cover see what the hell he was looking for. There was nothing back there. What the fuck man he thought, slamming the tailgate and spitting into the street. Glaring through the back window of the truck, he gritted his teeth as Watkins watched the bus take off down the road. He couldn’t help himself; he looked over there as well. An unassuming figure parted from the fray on the other side of the road, sauntering towards the truck. Jethro didn’t believe what he saw at first, his eyes rapidly blinking as he tried to focus on the person drawing ever closer. As the image became clearer, he furrowed his brow, thinking he was watching a ghost coming towards him. He froze where he stood, his face slackening as he went pale. The man’s scalp was clean shaven, the leathery skin laden with the rough threads of a spider web tattoo. His face was tightly wound save the pockmarks on his upper left cheek, deep blue eyes glowering in the direction of anyone who dared look at him. What showed of his arms were an amalgam of Confederate remembrances and Iron Crosses weaving together in a perfect unity. Meine Ehre heißt Treue was written in bold white across the chest of his black t-shirt. Once he was close, Jethro watched the smirk snake across the man’s face. All he could do was tremble in its wake. Ernie Spisak was out of prison. September 14, 2009 Justin had waited been waiting at bus stop in Fayette for hours, the remnants of crank mere embers as he watched the sun begin to filter over Main Street. The night had been cold, causing him to shiver like mad in his lonesome on the bench. All his possessions were in a small backpack held off to the side. How could I have been so foolish he thought, the overwhelming desire for just a taste having so consumed him that he had lost everything. He couldn’t contend with Ana’s glare, and even though one burden had been alleviated, the cost of it all was too great. There wasn’t a future for him and Ana. Not here. Not anywhere. He wished he felt something at that moment, but that would’ve been a lie. Maybe the entire relationship had been built on lies, a fling for two junkies who needed a pillar to stabilize their destructive behavior, to make it okay to keep going down the dead end path because at least someone else was there to fall with them. But she’d conquered her demons, growing past the high and yearning for something more. He’d yearned for something more too, finding escape from his responsibilities by making empty promises to himself. He thought that through her, he could change, and that he could become a better man because of it. The empty bus to Jackson made its first swing of the day, but for whatever reason, he was compelled to stay now that he had to face his departure. Suddenly, all the emotion flooded back, and he felt as though he was running out on the one thing that could save him. Ana Marie didn’t want to be that saving grace, though. She’d had enough of it all. He didn’t know that the phone call he made to Benny had saved Ana’s life, but then again, fate had a nasty habit of springing surprises upon people when they least expected it. Maybe he could change. He had to stay here to change. He left the vestibule with his backpack slung over his shoulder, walking forward on shaky footing as the bus door folded open with a rusty groan. There were rehab centers in Jackson, support systems that would take the place of familiar trappings which made it easy to use his bad habits as a backslider. It wouldn’t be pretty, and he had no illusions that it would be. He couldn’t conquer his addictions on his own. Shambling to the back of the bus, he had every intention of coming back when he was free of his own demons. Whenever that would be. Jethro swallowed hard, a long tail of ash forming on his cigarette as he shifted in his seat. He’d been in Spisak’s hideaway for what seemed like forever, chain smoking and tasting his heart as he waited for someone to speak. Both Spisak and Watkins had been as vague as possible. It had been years since anyone had heard from Spisak. He was glad for that; he knew how crazy Spisak was firsthand, witnessing his rabid bloodlust as he watched men and women beaten within an inch of their lives in the quiet corners of the county, all because they wouldn’t pay off their debts. He’d watched them as they were broken into twisted idiots through the pain, their eyes and mouths wild with jabbering pleas for them to stop. They always went unanswered until they were finished. He was just their gopher, the midget pledge that did the shit the grunts wouldn’t. Back then, he was the only one digging graves in the swamps by News Lake, getting rid of those that died while being whipped with extension cords. And some who were still suffering through shock. But things had changed, and after Spisak was put away, Jethro was brought into Kingsley’s crew even though it was against Royce’s wishes. In the years since, he’d become a good soldier, cooking meth and being a jack of all trades, adapting to situations as they occurred. “Those Salmons niggers are still farming soybeans I see,” Spisak croaked as he looked out the window of the old cabin, his eyes scanning the swamp a mere hundred feet away. “Nothing’s changed.” The secluded hideaway had been set up near the far west corner of the county, a perfect place with nothing around it save marshland and the Mississippi River. It hadn’t been used in years, dust caking over much of the interior trappings. Snakes shimmied underneath the floorboards, visible through the slants between the planks. There were dark blotches on different areas of the floor, the crimson having dampened over the years. Each of them had their own unique story of torture, now lost in the mildew and rot of time. “I hate this place too,” Watkins chimed in, his eyes peeled to the floor as rats scurried by his feet. “Did I say anything about hating it?” Spisak deadpanned as he turned around, his glare stopping him from saying anything more. “Just said nothing’s changed.” “That’s the way it’s always been.” “Not always, Jethro,” Spisak smirked as he looked back out the window, strumming his fingers across its spider web of fractures. “I heard you landed on your feet with them pieces of shit. Congratulations.” “I didn’t have—” “Shut up.” Spisak growled as he craned his head over his shoulder. “You don’t think I got eyes and ears knowing what’s going on here? Don’t think I’m that naïve. Valley must be a real spineless bastard to be handing my county over to that dumbass Kingsley kid. But that don’t matter none. Probably for the best, since you and him like each other so much.” “It ain’t like--” “Shut up.” Watkins sat down at the rickety old table with him, trying his best to shake off his own pangs of nervousness. “Your friends aren’t helping my business partners out. So it’s time there was someone out here that was.” He licked his lips, staring back at Watkins as he narrowed his gaze. “I don’t make those decisions.” “Who said anything about you making decisions?” Spisak’s footfalls rumbled like thunder through the small space of the shack as he stalked over the two of them, his shoulders coiling as if he was ready to strike at any moment. “You were mine first. You have loyalty to me. Right?” Jethro was flying on instinct; he knew what would happen if he didn’t agree. “Of course.” “Good. Because Watkins over here tells me that my little lot of land might be useful for more than just a bunch of trailer park trash snorting crank.” “Yeah,” Watkins voice quivered, “this can be over quick and easy.” “You’re my horse in this, Jethro. My Trojan horse.” “What do you mean?” Spisak’s fingers slithered around his neck, his touch cold and unyielding. It made his body break out in gooseflesh. “You’re my eyes and ears inside the Kingsley crew. You’re going to tell me exactly what they’re thinking. Where they’re going. What they’re doing.” Stabbing his way back to the window, he spoke once again, his words dripping with venom and filled with foreboding. “They took what was mine, and I don’t much care for a bunch of inferior pieces of shit taking what don’t belong to them. They lost the turf war here. I’m just putting it back in order. Making things right.” Jethro peered toward the back wall of the crypt, the glow of the sun started to filter through the window. Spisak looked away. The harsh light burned his eyes. “She’s very lucky you were there for her,” Deputy Conyers reassured Benny Crowley from across his desk, his eyes scanning the last bit of paperwork from the incident the night before. Given his line of work, the police station made him very uncomfortable. “I did what I had to do.” “Well, she’s lucky someone was there for her,” Conyers stated as he continued writing little notes into the margin of the report. “She’d been calling about this guy for the past couple days, but my hands were tied. Internal politics, you know?” He didn’t respond, rocking against the back of his chair nervously as he looked at the collection of Coca Cola memorabilia dotting the walls. “Do I have to worry about anything coming down from all this?” “Wouldn’t say so,” Conyers started as he put the report in a manila folder, brushing it off to the edge of his desk. “Your firearm is registered and you have two witnesses corroborating the story. Only thing wrong is the fact that the incident wasn’t caught on tape. But that has more to do with the people running the QuikStop than it does you.” He nodded his head. “That’s good.” “I know you ain’t a saint, Benjamin, but you probably saved her life.” If only he knew the circumstances as to why he was over there in the first place. “You’re free to go. If anything else comes up, I’ll give you a call.” Rising from his chair, he shook the hand of the Chief Deputy, nodding his head. “Thanks.” He couldn’t get out of there fast enough. Walking out of the station house, he rounded the corner, pressing through a few officers to try and get to his Neon. That’s when he spotted Ana Marie lounging against the brick building, smoking cigarettes as her hair flitted about in the breeze. He felt compelled to move towards her. “Hey.” She looked towards him, her face a mess of swollen bruises; she smiled anyway. “Hey.” Their stare lingered for a moment, their eyes locking as the rest of the world stopped moving. “I don’t think I got a chance to--” “You don’t need to,” he brushed away her need to thank him. “Was just in the right place at the right time.” “Yeah.” They both shuffled their feet, putting their hands behind their backs as the tripped over what exactly to say. Finally, Benny opened his mouth, though the words he spoke didn’t even seem like they were his; if they had been, they would have been laced with stutters. “You doing okay?” “Would you believe me if I said I was?” “Probably not.” They both chuckled at the comment. “I’m going to have to go to the dentist. Get my teeth fixed. And now I have to worry about where the money is going to come for all that.” “You still look beautiful.” “Do I have to believe you?” “No,” he said as his heart fluttered about in his chest. “But I mean it.” She blushed, unable to stop herself from smiling. “You’re one of Jethro’s friends, right?” “Wouldn’t call him a friend,” he smirked, “but I know him.” “So he’s as much a dick to you guys as he is to me?” “I reckon he’s just compensating. He’s sort of one of us in name only. Terrence likes him well enough though.” As much as they both could have gone on about all the things Jethro did to piss them off, it didn’t feel right for her. “Can you thank both of them? They sort of helped me out. With that guy. Not like you, but they tried to get him to stop.” “Sure can.” The two shook hands, delaying any movement away from one another. He made the first step, meekly waving as he turned to walk away. “Look...” She stopped him dead in his tracks. “I don’t feel safe. This whole thing sort of has me messed up in the head and I couldn’t get to sleep last night.” Her fragrance wafted through his nostrils, tickling his brain as she drew close. “Would you mind, I mean you’ve already done so much--” “I got nothing else planned. Even if I did, it probably ain’t all that important.” “I have a place in Claremont. Can make you dinner or something.” “That’d be nice.” “Yeah,” she stammered. Both of them were glowing despite their physical imperfections. She thought that her feelings might have just been because of what he’d done for her, and after everything she’d experienced in the last few hours, she wasn’t sure she had a handle on anything. But this felt right Getting on her tip toes, she kissed him on the cheek. “Thank you.” To him, it felt like it was his first kiss. Royce held his breath through his respirator as he closed the lid on the glass jug, gently swirling the iodine and sudafed with the acid. He’d done this so many times over the years, but that didn’t stop him from being careful each and every time. One false move, one spark, and the entire lab would be blown apart from the dynamite and grenades he’d stowed away inside the kitchen cabinets. Thankfully, he didn’t smell garlic, meaning it was safe to move on to the next step of production. Jethro was in the living room, sweeping the cooked methamphetamine into bags with the straight edge of a plastic triangle. Unlike Royce, he didn’t have to be all that careful with what he was doing. Which was a blessing given his current mental state. Terrence opened the motel room door, holding his hand over his mouth and nose as he peeked his head through the opening. He didn’t need to say anything; Jethro just nodded his head, getting off his chair and walking into the kitchen. “Terrence is here.” Royce sat the glass jug off to the side, turning off the burner and following Jethro out of the motel room. Once they got outside, the two took off their respirators, squinting into the sunlight as Terrence rested his shoulder against one of the Stonewall Motel’s metal support beams. “I’ve been thinking about a few things.” Royce had an inkling as to where this was going from the night before, but he let him continue on. “You said things ain’t been the same since I came back. And you know what, you’re right. But that don’t mean I’m all that shook up over it.” He tightened his fists, folding his arms across his chest. Terrence just continued talking. “I ain’t saying you have to stop. But I’m getting out while I still have the chance.” “What’s that supposed to mean?” “You gave me the county about five years ago, Royce. I know why you did it. You didn’t want to be the face behind it all in case the law came down our throats. So I’m the guy who took the county.” “That ain’t how--” “You don’t need to lie to me,” Kingsley paused, lighting up a cigarette. “There ain’t no future in this.” “So you found something bigger and better, huh?” “Legit,” Kingsley corrected him. “Something I can do and make money without having to worry about the law busting down my door. Only reason I even came back to this was because nobody in their right mind would hire me after I spent time in prison. It’s something I don’t have to lie to Autumn about.” “I don’t like this,” Jethro interjected. “You don’t have to,” Kingsley told him, his eyes remaining locked on Royce. “It’s my decision. I’ll stick around until you all figure out who is going to lead.” Kingsley turned around, beginning to walk away. Royce was fuming. “This is how it ends?” “It ain’t the end,” Terrence told him as he looked over his shoulder. “There’s still the deal with the Mexicans. You get that set up and keep it quiet like we’ve been doing and you all will do fine. But I can’t stay here. Not when I got my girl to worry about and not when I have to worry about my old man wanting to kill me.” Before anyone could respond, he was backing out of the lot, speeding off into the distance. Jethro didn’t know how he felt as he drove back up to Spisak’s hideaway. He understood why Terrence was getting out; he too had a dream. He wanted to become a professional bodybuilder. And like Terrence had framed it, what they were doing wasn’t something that was ever going to last. No matter what, they were going to get caught. It might be something as random as being pulled over for speeding. Or the cops might arrest them out of the dead of night after years of building a case. If the law didn’t get them, then there were the work hazards; a spark could ignite phosphine gas and blow them to pieces. Or they might just have a bad filter on their respirators, ending up swallowing too much gas. Or they could run into a crazy son of a bitch who wanted to move in and make a name for himself. It was what they had done to get in their position in the first place; cutting the heads off of drug leaders who had planted their flag in the county, burying their remnants in Jefferson County’s swamps. They might not have been the smartest guys in the world, but both he and Terrence knew the score. They’d been doing alright for four years, evading the cops, keeping quiet while taking care of their business, and putting an end to any group that wanted to take the county as theirs. Their ticket was about to come due. It was just a question of who was going to punch theirs. Royce didn’t understand any of that. He was too old school, always going the extra mile to keep out of harms way, only doing so because he’d spent a decade of his life behind bars. It was never the business or the drugs or any of that put him there in his mind; it was all on the lawman, and he’d sooner scorch the Earth to burn his tracks than be told what he could do by a bunch of bureaucratic pigs. As Jethro pulled up by the ramshackle cabin, he couldn’t help but feel some sense of relief over the whole thing. Royce made himself scarce when things got hot and heavy back when the Brotherhood and the Cerberus MC were having their turf war, running back to California before he was swept up into their mess. If it got like that again, Royce would probably run away. Terrence would be gone, and so would his girl. Jethro figured that, at the worst, he might stick around for a while, making sure not to get too deeply entrenched with the Neo-Nazis. He was pretty much replaceable. He stopped feeling relief when he realized that after this was over, Spisak might not have any more use for him. Getting out of his car, he knocked on the door of the cabin, putting his hands in the pockets of his hoodie as he waited. When the door opened, he was greeted by an unfamiliar face. He was big and bulky, his ears and neck covered in Schutzstaffel mottos and swastikas while his blond hair was shaved into a Mohawk. He wore a black jacket littered with hate speech badges along with army fatigues and boots that pounded the must out of the floorboards. There was another guy dressed the same way with a shotgun pointed in his direction. Jethro raised his hands, gulping hard as he waited for the blast. It never came. Spisak looked over his shoulder from the table, catching Jethro from the corner of his eye before saying something in German to the man holding the shotgun. He holstered his weapon, taking a few steps forward and scanning Jethro up and down with his eyes. “He’s one of us,” Spisak hissed. “Let him in.” He was busy pounding shots of whiskey, belching as his tendril like fingers motioned for him to come in. He took a seat across from Spisak, watching him as he swirled the shot glass around wildly. “This is my first shot I’ve had in about five years and I’ll be damned if it ain’t the best thing I’ve ever tasted.” The crazy bastard took another shot, pounding the glass into the table. He was waving his fingers, point to the two men who were keeping guard. “Meet the muscle. You’d never know how many contacts you can make in a pen with a bunch of filthy fucking niggers and spicks. The Brotherhood is always strong.” Jethro nodded his head timidly, rapidly blinking his eyes as he tried to speak. “Shit’s changing with the Kingsley crew.” “Oh? How’s that?” “Terrence is about to do some other shit. It’s going to leave a leadership vacuum. You can take this county back without having to spill a drop of blood.” The prospect sounded so great the more he thought about it, but Spisak didn’t seem like he shared his sentiment. “What the hell’s he doing?” “He’s got another career thing going on.” “Well that’s good,” he said as he poured himself another shot. “Young pup has some balls. Be a shame to put a steel jacket in the back of his head because the son of a bitch don’t realize he’s been squatting on what’s mine.” He didn’t growl as he threw it back, taking the whiskey in as if it were water. “I got this muscle here for a reason though. A damn good reason. This is just the start. The Brotherhood takes care of its own, and I’m about to get me a whole bunch of guys who are just dying to cook up some meth.” Jethro was confused. “What about Watkins?” “Son,” Spisak started, “I’ve been doing this shit all my life and I can tell which people are kings and which ones are pawns. That piece of shit is a pawn, hanging off the dicks of a bunch of wetbacks who think they’re bad.” “Why’d he--” “He came to me,” Spisak snarled, pointing to his chest. “He’s up here to get some cash or the spicks are going to put his head on a pike. He’s chicken shit. Nothing to worry about. And when we’re done with Kingsley and his little crew? I’ll bury him too. They’ll think it had something to do with them boys. And then we will take care of what’s ours.” Jethro shook his head, stupefied by what he was hearing. He’d told them that they didn’t even have to fight for county. It didn’t make any sense. “Why you got to take care of Terrence? He’s leaving.” “Simple,” Spisak growled as he rose from the table. “Because this damn county has been too quiet for too long. All of us? We’re sending a message to them.” The man with the mohawk tossed an extension cord onto the table. Suddenly, Jethro couldn’t breathe. “And we’re sending it tonight.” The hardest part about replacing doors was dealing with the pins. Terrence had measured the space, getting the right fit for the frame, but had trouble keeping the thing balanced as he tried to steady the pins into the hinge brackets. After he hammered them into place, he tested the swing, making sure he didn’t screw up somewhere along the way. When it swayed perfectly, he felt a sense of accomplishment, but the job wasn’t done. Cutting the new doorknob package open with a knife, he put the pieces into place with his power drill, making sure everything was snug. He was lost in his work, not noticing the footfalls coming up the brush until they hit the stairs. When he turned around, he was greeted by the sight of his girlfriend, her purse over her shoulder and a quizzical look spread across her face. They hadn’t talked since the fight, and he felt bad about that. But his scars were starting to fade away. From the look of her eye, hers were as well. He got up off his knee, dusting himself off as he took a few timid steps forward. “You do know I’ve seen your Thunderbird hanging around the trailer, right?” Terrence nodded. “I figured you were at work.” “Got off early. Was going to look for doors. Guess I don’t have to now.” “Yeah,” he coughed. “I checked it a few days ago. Whatever made you think that cord was going to keep this thing shut?” “What made you think I wouldn’t notice your car?” “Well I guess I had to be hanging around, making sure nobody tried to come in and steal your stuff.” He was dancing around the question, but she wasn’t going to let him off that easily. “Is this the way you’re going to try and win back my heart? With a door?” “I figured that it opens the way for things.” “It also closes them.” The bad jokes caused them both to giggle. “You done?” “Pretty much,” he said as he patted the door. “Want to talk?” “Sure.” He opened the door for her, letting her go in first. “Insurance paid for the windows but for some reason, they wouldn’t pay for the door. I was trying to figure out where I was going to get the money to replace it.” Shutting it behind them, he watched her put her purse on the workbench before she moved to her couch. “I have some money stowed away, in any event.” He lingered around the door as she untied her shoes, resting them underneath the coffee table. “I’m sorry about how I acted.” She nodded her head. “I was pretty stubborn about things too. It was a rough night. It changed a lot of things.” “Yeah,” he started, still shuffling around because he wasn’t sure she was comfortable with him after the way he’d grabbed her. “I don’t ever want you to see that side of me.” “How am I ever going to be able to watch you wrestle then?” “I’m not joking. I grabbed you and I hurt you. I wasn’t myself.” “You were hung for a tree, Terrence. Beaten up pretty bad. I don’t totally blame you for reacting the way you did. That morning, I was more worried about what you could do than anything else.” He took that as an invitation, softly walking to the couch and sitting next to her. Gently massaging the supple skin of her wrists, he tried to make her trust his touch, to show that he wasn’t what she’d seen that horrible morning. “It’s all in the past. My father. All of it.” “It’s not all in the past.” “Yeah it is. You said there wasn’t a future in what I was doing. And I got to be honest; every single day since I came back off that tour I’ve been thinking the same thing. I didn’t want to come home because it meant going back to this to make ends meet.” He looked into her eyes, trying his best to reassure her that he was speaking the truth. “But that’s over. I told Royce and Jethro that I was done with it. I’m not going to keep lying to you about the stuff I’m doing. We got to move on from this...from this place. There’s nothing here.” “I want to leave too.” Her hands brushed his beard, her warm touch sending calming ripples throughout his body. “I got a call last night. From PRIME. They want to see me next week to get me set up. You know what this means? It means that I’ve made it. It means that we can leave this place. It’s what we’ve been waiting for. I work hard enough for them and I’m going to be able to provide for you. For us. We can go anywhere. We can start a family. We can leave the past behind us and move on and become regular people.” The news might have brought the world crashing down for the significant others of many wrestlers, but she didn’t see it that way. There was no way she could; this was the chance of a lifetime, their chance to get away from the haunts of Jefferson County and start a new life. “That’s good. That’s really good.” He felt his heart strings being plucked by her tender smile. “And you can travel with me. We can see other towns and figure out where we want to buy a house. We can be together, every single day. You don’t have to worry about your job no more. You can go to school like you always wanted to and become a teacher. We’re going to be able to have the chance to be who we want to be like we’d never of had before, and it’s all because of this.” “Next week you said?” “Next week,” he tried to keep his composure, but his emotion was cracking through to the surface. “Come with me. I want you to see how this is. See how real this is.” “I don’t know what to say.” Clasping her hands, he bowed his head, pleading with to come with him. “Say yes. Please. Let me make up for all the things we’ve had to go through living here by treating you the way you deserve.” Tears trembled down her cheeks. Suddenly, there was hope in her world. He didn’t have to treat her any which way; she’d follow him to the ends of the world if she had to. “I’ll say yes then.” Both of them were choking up, their foreheads resting against the other for support. Separate, they were lost. Together, they were whole. Dinner was gumbo, something with a New Orleans flare that Ana said she’d picked up when her family lived down there. That was before Katrina, and she didn’t really speak much about it beyond that, which Benny figured was just as well given what he’d heard about from others who’d been displaced from their homes. She looked like someone who’d seen loss and had gone through pain. Or maybe she was still in pain over what had happened the night before. As she stirred the pot, her forearms quaked, every sharp noise in her trailer startling her psyche. But each of those moments was fleeting at best. That’s how he knew there was something rugged underneath that beautiful exterior, as odd as it may have seemed. He’d seen it before with others, but most of all, he’d seen it in himself. Taking care of his brother when others said he should have been in a home because of his mental deficiencies was part of it, but the fact that his mother was an alcoholic disciplinarian who rose them by the end of clothes hangers and belts put more of an imprint on him than anything. She was taking out her frustration caused by her deadbeat husband, bipolar in her manner until she finally ran away without saying goodbye. Part of him still missed her, regardless of what she’d done to them. Ana didn’t eat anything, blaming her teeth. The two shared timid stares in silence. He didn’t comment on the broken pieces of furniture, figuring it was best to leave that sort of thing alone. The two had known each other for years through simple pleasantries at her job, but had never gotten close. But all that had changed. To him, she was radiant, a Spanish beauty with a well proportioned hour glass figure. But what he paid the most attention to were her eyes; lime green and penetrating, though they were also dulled by the bruises on her upper cheeks. He cleaned up, saving what was left in a Tupperware container and putting it in the fridge while she tried to squeeze in his way because she figured she was being inhospitable by making him do all the work. They were locked in a competition to see who could be the most courteous, causing them to bump into each other quite often, sharing nervous giggles each and every time. Neither of them really spoke because nothing was coming to mind. She had every reason in the world to be shell shocked, but instead seemed relieved. That made her strong in Benny’s eyes, and as such made her all the more attractive. Maybe they’d both dealt with so much personal pain in their lives that they were not longer fazed by it. When done cleaning up, they sat on the couch, watching comedy on the television. They both found themselves laughing at the same things. Time was moving too quickly. As their chemistry developed, so did their willingness to touch. They’d both had experience in the past, but Benny’s lanky and awkward fingers shook as he gave her reassuring pets. She laced her fingers with his eventually, rocking their wrists back and forth as the sun went down. As the phone rang, he remembered that he was supposed to go out with Donovan, acting as he designated driver for another torrid night. He didn’t answer the phone. This just felt more pertinent, more right. He let the phone ring before eventually turning it off. He spent more of his time watching her than he did the television. Eventually, she was doing the same. Her smile, thought it had been chipped and maimed, still shined wonderfully in the moonlight. She fell asleep on the couch, their hands still clasped together. He got up, turning the television off before grabbing a sheet off her bed. Wrapping it around her, he surprised himself as he lowered his head, gently kissing her on the forehead. He sat in the rocking chair, keenly keeping an eye on her falling asleep. Donovan closed his phone, taking another swig of his beer as he looked up at the television in Griswold’s way. It wasn’t like Benny to stand him up. Then again, it wasn’t like Benny to go against him when it came to Justin. Benny didn’t understand; the two had grown up in the same apartment complex in Fayette, and back then, it was Justin looking after him. Friends were supposed to look out for one another, and while he fashioned himself as not being just another slimy drug dealer, he was starting to realize that it wasn’t true. He went to call him again, but as he watched the patrons of the bar playing pool, he thought against it. He was going to stew for a bit, get his mind off the bullshit argument Benny fronted as being a justifiable reason for selling to Justin. He’s an addict he kept thinking, but he couldn’t help but feel that the guy deserved better. After all, they had been doing well enough selling to people they had absolutely no real connection with. As he finished his beer, he got up, cracking his knuckles as he figured it was time go to the Stonewall to get some sleep. Staggered footsteps carried him out of the bar, his head. Should call someone for a ride he thought as he jingled his keys, knowing that he’d probably take a header through his windshield if he tried to drive in his current state. The crickets were chirping too loudly, the moon blinding him as he looked to the sky. He didn’t see them coming. They knocked him to the concrete, blindsiding him with a sucker punch to the back of his head. Whoever hit him sounded like they were loaded down with metal. Rolling on his back, he looked up. There were two men standing over him. They looked like six. They kicked him like they were eight. Each one grabbed an ankle. He tried to kick them away. His lack of motor control made it so he couldn’t. He tried to yell for help as they yanked him around the corner. A stomp to the head silenced him. They dragged him behind the bar, off to the secluded forest. His head rammed against fallen brush and broken cinder blocks that had been thrown out back. They were taking him to a spot where nobody would be able to find him. Jethro swallowed his vomit as he waited. He knew that the messages the Brotherhood sent out were always loud and sickeningly clear. Spisak sat in the rotten hide of a nearby stump, smoking cigarettes and shotgunning whiskey as the maggots from his throne scuttled across his body. “Are you prepared?” Jethro was numb and on autopilot; he nodded his head. As he heard the others coming, his hands instinctively balled into fists. Moonlight filtered through the branches in only one spot, and as they drew near, he could hear Donovan’s beaten groans bubble up in haggard wheezes. They drug him over, tearing his shirt off. The one with the mohawk produced a rope from his pocket while the other stood the beaten body up, forcing him face forward against a tree. They tied his wrists around the base, waiting in silence as he began to try and fight the knot. “Wha...wha...” Donovan croaked, his head wildly twisting as he tried to catch a glimpse of who was standing near him. Spisak lifted himself out his stump, grabbing the wound up extension cord that had been resting on a bed of brush. “Step away,” he ordered to the others. They complied. He pushed the cord into Jethro’s chest, staring him in the eye. “It’s time to prove your loyalty to me, Jethro.” His entire body went numb. He was only supposed to be a gopher, the guy who cleaned up the messes made by the Brotherhood. But now, he was being forced deeper within their ranks. Tears began to form in his eyes, but he dared not cry. He wrapped the extension cord around his knuckles. He swallowed what hope he had left. The first strike across his back opened Donovan’s eyes. He squirmed. The second caused him to yelp. He wrenched his wrists against the tree. The third made him pull back harder. The knot refused to give. The forth brought about deep bruising. The fifth forced him to his knees. The sixth, seventh, and eighth all caused him to cry. The ninth made him bite his tongue. The tenth finally broke his flesh. There would be many more to come.
