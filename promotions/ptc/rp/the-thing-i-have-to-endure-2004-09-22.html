"The thing I have to endure..."

22 September 2004
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2004-09-22 **** Prologue “Eons ago, a tournament so prestigious existed that only the men who were willing to go that extra mile entered. When I speak of men going that extra mile, I mean those who have no fear, no anxiety and especially, no cowardice. These brave souls stepped into the ring you see before you now and with an iron will, they fought like the dogs of war. Battling for everything they had, you would seldom see one man dropping out. But there was -- ” End Prologue “What kind of second rate action flick are you trying to make here, Ricky?,” was all I could spit out after that horrendous voice-over prologue I was just forced to endure; luckily only a few seconds of it. My mentor and close friend, Ricky ‘The Bomb’ Metton stood there at what he liked to call his ‘business desk.’ He was holding a script in his hand and waving directions to the half-witted extras on stage, who he had called along today for the first day of shooting of his film. In truth, it wasn’t much of a film, though. At least, judging from the poor camera work and the God-awful voice-over. “Hello! Famous film director at work here, Tony! Can‘t you see I‘m moulding these brainless fools into stars?!,” he retorted with his classic ‘director’s voice’ which he had been using since early this morning when he paraded into my room not a minute after dawn. Metton was a middle-aged man, no more than about forty-five. His facial features left nothing to be desired; it would be a safe bet that he was quite the ladies’ man when he was in his prime, or so he would have me believe. Despite this, he had a rather expressive beer belly which stuck out over his slacks like an oversized bomb. Today - and I hoped never again after today - he was wearing his director’s t-shirt which had a cartoon illustration of a director shouting into his megaphone printed on it. Metton had told me earlier that he wanted his name across the back of the shirt. I said we’d think about it. Metton's schedule for today was literally mental. Getting the extras to learn how to act, somehow giving himself a crash course in directing within the space of a few hours along with my constant criticism of his lame film - what a day it was going to be for him. I watched on in amusement as Metton again blurted out some commands to the extras. The lemmings all ran over to the wrestling ring at the west end of my training facility, a facility which I had been using for several years. “Tony! Could you go get me a bottle of water or somethin'? Please... I'm dying here with these fools!,” Metton pleaded with me. I was half inclined to help the poor guy out and actually fetch the water in what would take all of two minutes but in a strange way, I wanted to see him suffer. I recalled all the times he had almost knocked my bedroom door down, morning after morning, just for training. Metton was a morning man and he liked people to be aware of that, even if it meant giving a new meaning to the phrase ‘the early bird gets the worm’. “Ah-hah! You see, even you - the almighty director - are admitting that these extras are a bunch of clowns,” I exclaimed with an extremely audible voice. Metton shrugged his shoulders and pulled a water bottle out of his business desk's second drawer. I almost fell off the chair I was sitting on with amazement and shock. The cheeky so-and-so had a bottle of water all the time; all he wanted was to see me suffer. Metton winked at me with a knowing smile and I knew there and then that maybe he was more than just a tubby man making a film. The hustle and bustle began then. The ultra-modern grandfather clock against the wall of books struck 9:00am, otherwise known as ‘way too early’. That was the moment it started. A generic techno beat began to play through the speaker system set up by the director himself, with the help of a few extras. That explained the abysmal set-up, I figured. But that techno song - it had to be Jamaican or something. The only way to describe would be this - picture a small baby crying; add a foghorn to that; then top it off with a bland Britney Spears lullaby song - that was it. “So...,” Metton began, “are you feeling this hop-hip beat?,” he shouted over at me, having to do so because of the volume of the terrible ‘music’, if you could call it that. “First of all, it's hip-hop, and second of all, you can't pull off a cool director. Don't even try it, big man,” I replied, still holding my ears shut. The extras had now organised themselves on the stage, which was in fact the ring, to begin their first scene. Each of them was dressed in all sorts of crazy costumes ranging from a white wig mixed with polka-dot trunks to an extra who was wearing only a robe... and nothing underneath. I liked to pretend there was something there, however, in case of any ‘mishaps’. Metton directed some of the less intelligent extras into their correct positions and returned to his director's chair. I thought if he sat down on the chair, he would surely break it. Somehow, he avoided it, sitting down quite comfortably. Metton picked up the megaphone and readied his booming voice. “Aaaaaaaaannnnnnd... ACTION!,” Metton screamed at the top of his voice. I sat there for five minutes and watched a bunch of confused individuals attempt to put on a movie about a wrestling match. Why there were over twenty men in the ring at the one time, I have no idea. This film was supposed to be about Golden Turnbuckle Tournament 4, after all. It being a singles tournament, this made no sense. It was either bad scripting or bad directing - or a mixture of both courtesy of Ricky Metton. I couldn't help but chuckle to myself as the man wearing nothing but a robe started executing suggestive pelvic thrusts on another unfortunate extra who looked about ten years old. All in all, this movie was a joke. The first scene of Ricky Metton's self-proclaimed masterpiece ended in a spectacular mixture of screaming, pulling, chomping on ropes and nursing wounds. Metton applauded like he had just witnessed the presentation of a Nobel prize to himself. I couldn't understand for the life of me why, but he was actually proud of that monstrosity. I stood up from my plush armchair and approached Metton, still mid-applause. “No need to congratulate me, Tony. I already --,” Metton boasted before I cut him off. “Ricky, I seriously think it's about time we stop making the movie and concentrate on other things. Keep in mind I'm gonna need the ring later on for work on my technique.” I made a few hand signals to show that I needed the ring. I didn't know how to communicate it to him verbally as right now, Ricky Metton was about as awake as a badger during hibernation. Metton nodded at me and picked up his things from the desk. I think it may have been the very first time I ever saw a hint of disappointment on Metton's face. He was genuinely bummed that he had to leave his prestigious director's role. What the hell, I thought. “Tell you what, Ricky. You keep on making the movie, alright? This time, though, put me in with those extras and let's see what we can do. I'm warning you not to put on that techno music and I am not acting if you play that,” I turned to him as I walked over to the ring where all the extras were scattered. I passed by a pair of extras on the ring apron who appeared to be - of all things - picking their noses and then rubbing their fingers on the apron. I said nothing for the moment; I'd deal with those five year olds later. Metton took his megaphone in his hand again and got ready to announce the beginning of a new scene. Nobody in the room had a script for this scene. I don't even think it was a scene. The only I knew was that it was about to happen. A killer rock anthem began on the speakers which I seconds later recognised as “Achilles Last Stand” by the legendary Led Zeppelin. The extras all looked at each other, totally dumbfounded. I took centre stage in the middle of the ring and rubbed my hands together in wait. An extra walked over to me - I think all he wanted was some information on what was going on - and I took him down with a picture-perfect hip toss. Yes, I thought, that is what I call a satisfying move. A few more extras ran at me and they all received the same set of moves - a hip toss, a kick to the sternum or sometimes, a kick to the nether regions. The poor lighting around the ring was adequate enough and as the scene came to a brilliant close, I took two of the redheaded extras and rammed their heads together, not thinking anything of what was inside their respective heads. Metton almost exploded out of his director's chair and alas, it broke. He didn't care, though. He had just witnessed Tony Aliso's masterpiece - ‘Dissection Of The Extras’. I exited the ring under the bottom rope and sauntered over to Metton, who was still whistling. I then realised that it was not my performance he was applauding but the extras. I didn't know what was wrong with this guy that he enjoyed the performance of a few loons probably just ripped out of an asylum over my exciting number. Still, that was Ricky Metton for you. I waved it off and headed towards the double doors leading to my living room. A man stepped in my way suddenly. Dressed in a black suit and pinstripe trousers, he did not look anything like one of the extras. It was obvious he was wearing a wig as it was halfway off his head, dangling to the side. Finally I looked down at the nametag attached to his jacket. It read: ‘Argyle - The Guy Who Runs The Tournament’. ‘Argyle’ winked at me and began waving at me. I had no idea why he was doing this; that was just his style. I eventually pushed the happy-go-lucky style ‘Argyle’ aside and made my way into the comfortable heart of my home, away from the racket that Ricky Metton's production brought to the table. I headed upstairs to my room. I knew it was going to be a long day. Several hours later... There was nothing like a good nap after the torment I had to endure earlier. I felt refreshed. Like a new flower, I had bloomed anew, awaiting the new sun to shine down on me. I heard some faint sounds of men shouting coming from downstairs. I decided to chance it and go down to see what was going on in my home. Cheers for Ricky Metton rung around the room which I now caught sight of. The extras and Metton had set up a makeshift cinema room in my living room. The sofas, just this morning washed, were now dirty with mud from the extras' shoes. Metton stood adjacent to the huge monitor which was erected just in front of the double doors leading into the training room, where filming had gone on earlier. He was laughing hysterically at what must have been a picture of himself bending over to get a quarter - that would be the only valid explanation for his eccentric behaviour. The extras seemed to laugh along with him only because they had to. Interesting... The door was slightly ajar when I opened it. An extra shouted at me, ordering me to get down and not block their view. I did as I was ordered - even by an extra - with no hesitation. I was more concerned with what was happening here. I tugged Metton away from his moment of glory. I needed to have a word with him. “Where'd you hide the money, Ricky?,” I joked. “Tony, I gave that money to the stripper last night, remember?,” he replied, flabbergasted, then realising that perhaps mentioning his post-midnight antics was not the best of ideas at that point in time. “Stripper, eh? Ricky, you obviously need to give it up, man. Step aside and let the young guns” - I dusted off some imaginary substance from my leather jacket - “do all the work, my friend,” I announced with a confidence that unlike I had ever felt before. The extras started hollering as they saw themselves on the big screen. The only problem was that they were being beaten from pillar to post by yours truly on the screen. I watched myself drag a pimple-faced extra to the turnbuckle and nailing him with a knife-edge chop. Nasty stuff... for him. “You know, I've been looking for you,” Metton revealed. “Yeah, what for? To invite me to the bridal shower?” I had to get in some sort of rib at my mentor. It was almost a mandatory thing for him and I. Every day, we would try to catch the other out at something - or sometimes someone, if you know what I mean. It was a friendly competition, nothing more. “Yeah,” Metton said through a wave of laughter, “you got me there. Seriously though, Tone, I think you might want to look at this film. It's the real deal. I'm talking big money stuff here. You've heard of the dollar bill, right?” Metton said it with such zest and such life that I had to bite. “Yes. Go on.” Metton knew this was his chance to convince me that maybe, just maybe, this idea of becoming an amateur director and creating a ‘masterpiece’ wasn't such a bad idea after all. “So, hypothetically, this film could -- ,” Metton began before I cut him off. “Hypothetically,” I interjected, “is not good enough, Monsieur Directeur!,” I exclaimed with an added bit of a French accent at the end for effect. Metton turned around just as the film came to a close. It was a film that lasted all of about five minutes about the life of a man in the tournament known as the Golden Turnbuckle Tournament. Sure, it had no strong storyline, if any at all. There was no main character, if you really thought about it. Throw in a bunch of extras who literally have no clue about anything and basically, you've got yourself the biggest flop of all time. This one was so bad, it would probably rank worst than that awful Spice Girls film. Man, that was pure torture to watch. For the record, I only watched it because my girlfriend at the time was one of those ‘girl power’ enthusiasts. Metton cleared out the extras quickly, thankfully not wasting any time on congratulating them for a job well done, if they had even done so. Instead, he simply stared into space as they cleared out. I ignored each and every one of them as they filed out, leaving behind a mound of dirty furniture and a foul-smelling room in their wake. I turned to my mentor. “Well Ricky, you're not going to be in the movies. I gotta say that I'm stunned...,” I said in a defeated tone. It felt like I hadn't finished my sentence but I really didn't know what to say at this moment. “You know what they say, kid - You can't win 'em all. Hey, if it makes you feel any better, I kinda knew I wouldn't succeed in making the film, anyway,” Metton admitted at last. I breathed a sigh of relief then. It was over. Time to go back to reality and concentrate on the obstacles life throws in front of you at every turn. As long as those obstacles didn't involve extras, images of Ricky Metton fooling around with strippers or any form of film, I figured I would be fine. Metton patted me on the back and we began strolling through the tiled floors of my hallway where the group of extras had left clumps of mud during their departure. “I think it's about time we got some training done, buddy. It's something you can actually do, right?,” I said to Metton in a monotone so as to hide any trace of humour. He knew what my game was, though. He always knew. Metton grabbed me by the head and locked me in that painful headlock again. You know the feeling when you're trying to fit your head into a square box? Yeah, I don't know it either, but that is as close as I can come to describing how that headlock felt. Together, we headed off into the training room where I was sure I would learn a new meaning for the phrase ‘hard work’. The tournament's second round was upon me now. I wasn't about to let up or show any sign of weakness. I would walk into the ring with a grin and take whatever would come at me. Blow after blow, I would continue on. Then, as the last bell rings, it would be my name announced over the P.A. system. Victory was my M.O. and that wasn't about to change anytime soon. Let the games begin... ****
