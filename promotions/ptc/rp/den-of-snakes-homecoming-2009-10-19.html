Den of Snakes: Homecoming

19 October 2009

Date: 2009-10-19 September 3, 2009 Fayette, Mississippi was a quiet town nestled between stretches of birch and crabapple, the sort of place politicians pointed toward as the embodiment of American values. Where Main Street contained bakeries, hardware stores, and pharmacies which had been passed down from generation to generation and the folks greeted each other with a smile. Terrence eased his fingers over the grooves of his steering wheel as he drove down the heart of the city. The streets were lined with poles holding America flags that sashayed in the lazy southern breeze, plots of magnolia around their bases. He’d driven over seventeen hundred miles to get here from Berkeley, and all he could think about was how much he didn’t want the trip to be over. With nothing to do and hundreds of miles of American highway as his only company, Terrence had been stuck reflecting on his life. He’d realized he wasn’t happy with the road he was traveling down. Not even twenty-five and the weight of split personalities made him feel like an ancient man. The entire process was tied to the silliest of objects; a Confederate Flag bandanna. He didn’t always wear it, especially when he was traveling down Fayette’s Main Street. But this was not because the folks would take offense to the symbol; without it, he was a nondescript everyman with long, dirty brown hair and a wild beard. To an outsider, Terrence Kingsley looked like nothing more than a haggard grease monkey. When the emblematic Stars and Bars were square knotted around the breadth of his skull, it changed who he was. He became a character. He became an outlaw. In wrestling, Terrence called himself ‘The White Trash Messiah’. Until a few weeks ago, the amount of people outside of Jefferson County who knew him was fleeting at best. He had been the champion of the midcard, a rough and tumble talent who made infrequent appearances in southern independents. It was rare that he earned enough money to pay for his travel expenses. But somewhere along the way, he’d caught lightning in a bottle, and rose from outright obscurity to fracture the foundation of Tim Shipley’s Just Wrestling. He went from Tucson to Tijuana to Berkeley, etching his name into the minds of those who had witnessed him for the first time. Days ago, he’d suffered his first loss. But the tape of his battle with two of professional wrestling’s elite had just begun to circulate. Soon, every major professional wrestling company in the United States would get their first look at this ‘hot’ prospect. Outside the ring, the bandanna went on when he had to take care of his affairs. Loan sharking had been where he started, but after a stretch of prison time, he’d moved on to more ambitious goals. He pushed into the drug world of rural Mississippi in 05, outlasting Jefferson County kingpin Ernie Spisak. The Neo-Nazi was emasculated, his operation falling into ruin even before he was sent up to Parchman Farm. Like Katrina, Kingsley swept on in a torrent, and had ruled ever since. He kept wrestling with just how much he wanted to go back to it all as he drove the long American stretch home. Even before he left for the Long Hot Summer tour, Kingsley was thinking about the future. Thinking of getting out. Thinking that his girlfriend would hopefully make an honest man out of him. Now he was back. Staring at the reflective glass doors of this ageless town, his heart sank at the thought of stabbing his way across Jefferson County’s unsavory but far too familiar haunts. Twirling a rose between his fingers, he hoped to avoid the county's seedy side for at least a little while longer. All Justin Kershaw could do was look away. There was nothing else he could do. Frozen into the seat of his chair, he couldn’t maintain eye contact with his boss. Justin had been working for Unity for close to three years, moving people’s possessions from one home to another. It was hard work but it was good pay for someone who hadn’t graduated from high school. His boss rapped the wooden top of his desk with his knuckles, wheezing as he sternly glared through Justin. “I want you to realize that you’re not here because of your race,” he began as he shuffled a stack of papers which had been spread across the desk. “Unity is an equal opportunity employer.” Justin’s hands were buried deep in his jean pockets. “Uh, yes sir.” “Good.” With a lick of his lips, his boss continued. “I also want to tell you that Unity has more than its own regulations to look after. We have to adhere to the regulations of the country’s interstate trade commission. There’s a long list of things we have to be in line with or else we lose our license.” Justin needed a drink of water; his throat was dry. He began to feel the onset of a headache. His boss continued talking. “Now I’m not out there to see that these regulations are followed through completely, but your shift leader is. I don’t want Unity to lose everything because your shift leader caught you with a controlled substance.” He turned the papers around and pushed them forward with his middle finger. Justin gazed at the typeface, trying to focus his eyes. When he did, he realized he was reading his own termination papers. He felt like he was going to throw up. His boss got up from his chair, catching a faint glance at Justin’s dilated pupils before he looked away. “Unity is within their rights to require you to get a blood screening.” Justin’s forearms went numb, and suddenly he broke into shakes. With a haggard breath, he ran his hand through his dark waves. The skin on his cheeks felt far too hot. “Do we- uh, have to do this,” he stuttered as he finally locked eyes with his boss. “I like you Justin. You always worked hard for us. But your shift leader said he caught you snorting-” “That shit is bogus, man!” Justin shouted, his lips pressing together as tears formed in his eyes. “There’s just no way. I never done anything like that in my life.” He was lying. He couldn’t afford to lose this job, not when he was so close to putting the ring on Ana Marie’s finger. “That’s what I want to help you with Justin.” His boss was overeager in his response, bending his knees to get to his employee’s eye level. “If you come up clean, then you’ll be fine.” That was the moment Justin broke eye contact. He couldn’t cheat it; the second they tested him, all the signal flares would fire off. And that would make it almost impossible to find another job. It was hard enough with the way the economy was moving. With a termination because of on the job drug abuse, it would be almost impossible. “I can’t do that.” “You don’t let us take your blood, that’s as good as you putting in a resignation.” His boss sighed, rubbing his eyes and standing up. “That’s your only other option.” Fear cut through Justin as he slowly realized how dire his situation was. There was no way to avoid this. “Well, I’m sorry. But I can’t do it.” “That’s alright. Just…” his boss hesitated, slowly ambling back to his seat. He pulled the papers back, setting them off to the side. He knocked his knuckles against his desk a few more times to the break the silence as he pondered his words carefully. “Get yourself cleaned up Justin. You do that, we might have a spot open for you in a few months.” He wasn’t sure if his boss was telling the truth or not. As he stood up and left Unity Movers, all he could think about was how he was going to explain this all to the love of his life. Autumn Marin crouched over, sorting a row of fresh muffins through the back of the glass display case of “Lilybelle Bakery & Confectionary”. It was a Fayette treasure, having stood in a corner shop on Main Street for generations. Young children still scampered inside after school, and it made Autumn happy to see their dimpled smiles. She’d always been fascinated by children, always liked to be in their presence because it provided balance. Once the little darlings were nice and neatly placed, she brushed flour from her apron as she made her way to the columns of jellybeans on the sales floor. She got on her tip toes, her fingers tickling the edge of a box just out of reach, the troublesome item refusing to budge. Biting her lip, she jumped up and tried to pull the box out just enough so she could reach the rest of it, but her short arms were just not strong enough. Blowing strands of amber hair away from her lip, she resigned, calling for help. “Toby, you still back there?” His unkempt blond mop flitted about as he peeked his head from behind the back wall. “Yeah sure, what’s up?” “The jellybeans need to be refilled but I can’t reach the refill box.” He paused, rolling his eyes. “You sure?” She stared at him and pursed her lips. Toby had been like this ever since she’d help get him this job, which was a chore given that word was spreading around town that he was a lazy worker who wasn’t worth the hassle. But she had to look after her congregation brother, if only because she felt an odd, tender obligation towards her ‘siblings’. Not that he hadn’t earned his reputation. She continued glowering, causing Toby to trot from the back to lend her a hand. It was hard for anyone to feel comfortable when little lamb Autumn got angry. “Sorry. Was just keeping myself busy.” “It’s okay,” she reassured him as she moved out of his way, letting him reach up and pull the box down before putting it on the floor. When he was done, she kneeled down, pulling a box cutter out from her pocket and cutting along the tape. “I can’t help that I’m so darn short.” Even when she shot glares, she was always quick to defuse the situation with a chuckle and a smile. It was just who she was. As Toby turned away to go to the back, the bells on the door began to jingle. When she looked up to see who it was, her heart skipped a beat. It’s been ages she swore, the desire to run toward him and clutch him in a welcoming embrace nearly overpowering her. But she stayed nailed to the ground, her eyes rapidly blinking as Terrence gave her a meek wave. She didn’t notice it instantly, but he held a hand behind his back. As he slowly walked forward, he showed her what he’d been hiding; a rose. A singular red rose. The sensation might have been juvenile, but she couldn’t help but squeak as he teased the petals across her cheek. Toby stared at Terrence as he walked into the shop, but Kingsley paid the glare no mind; his focus was only on Autumn. The pair was a stark contrast, but opposites were supposed to attract. He slinked his head downward as his flowery plume eased across the faint depression on her chin. “I’m home,” he whispered in her ear, taking her into his arms and kissing her forehead. “I thought you were going to shave that thing off,” Autumn chided him over his scraggly beard after his lips parted, causing Terrence to flash a smirk. “You know how it is,” Kingsley joked, handing the rose to her. “Can we go outside and talk for a bit?” Nothing was more important right now. “Sure.” They walked side by side out of the store, taking a seat on nearby bench. Terrence eased his arm over the back, cupping Autumn’s shoulder to bring her close. She rested her head against his leather clad breast, the world slipping out of focus. All that existed was the two of them. “It’s been a long time.” “Too long,” she stated, her hand rubbing against the cotton of his undershirt as they watched cars amble by. “I thought you said you were going to call.” He’d promised to keep in touch. When Terrence went on tour, he expected to fall into the habits other wrestlers had while on the road. He snorted cocaine for the first time, becoming agitated and belligerent in a restaurant just outside of Tucson. She didn’t need to know that. She didn’t need to know a lot of things. “I kept the prepaid off,” he said as he tossed tufts of her hair. “Didn’t want to get distracted.” She didn’t know how the take that, her hand leaving his abdomen and her head leaving his shoulder. “It’s not like that.” Terrence, despite his infinite stupidity, understood how the comment was being perceived. “I didn’t want Jethro or Donovan or Benny or Royce calling me with their bullshit. Had nothing to do with you, hon.” She tried to bury the nagging notion that it was a lie, finding herself believing him far too easily. “Your father’s been looking for you.” “He do a sermon on me or something?” “No-” she paused, “no more than usual anyways. But he’s talked to me privately, about us.” The comment filled him with apprehension. “You know he doesn’t like me.” “Oh, he’s made that clear,” she raised her voice, shaking her head as she eased back against Terrence. “Said it wasn’t right that a nice girl like me was getting mixed up with his dip…well…his kid.” She never cussed, always finding other ways to get the point across even if she was repeating what others had said. “According to him, you’re his kid too.” It was meant as a joke, his arm giving her a small squeeze. “Everyone in the church is his kid.” To Father Freddie Joe Kingsley, that meant one of his daughters was sleeping with her brother, even though the two were nowhere close to being related through blood. She laughed at the thought. But their spiritual connection was what drew them together, the old Presbyterian Church in Rodney being where they first noticed each other. The Church had taken her from the government child care system and gave her a home and something she could believe in. But the faith had grown too foreign, too militant and removed from the roots she remembered. But unlike Terrence, she kept her roots in place, too afraid to leave it behind. “How’s that all going?” Terrence might have been excommunicated, but he still wondered. “Those militia boys treating you well and all?” He wasn’t worried about the children of the Kingslian faith, at least not as much as he worried about the fascist snake charmers who had wormed their way into his father’s heart. Autumn nodded. “They’re busier taking care of your father than anything else.” Desperate to change the topic of conversation, Terrence kissed her forehead again. “Things went well on the tour. Very well actually.” “What’s that supposed to mean?” “They really liked me out there,” he said as he rubbed her dirty apron. “Not at first so much, but as it went on, I moved up. I actually wrestled for a championship.” “Wow, really?” Terrence wasn’t sure if she was being sarcastic. “Didn’t win or nothing, but when I got in the back all the people in charge huddled around me and told me I put on a helluva show. Said I was welcome to come back.” Knots tied up in her stomach at the thought of him leaving again for such a long time. “What you say?” “I said I had to talk it over with you at first,” he said, cupping her cheek and rustling her hair. “Said I would like to. It’s a good chance, Autumn. There were a lot of really big guys working there. Said I was going to catch on someplace.” While he rarely talked about his experience in wrestling being great, she knew he always wanted to make it big in the industry. “Oh?” And, in spite of her selfish desire to always have him close by, she realized wrestling was an escape from what he had been caught up in around here. But it never actually seemed realistic before. “Yeah. But the guy who runs the place, he said he was going to make some calls around, get me involved in something big. There’s this tournament they were talking about constantly, and they said if I did well there, that I’d basically get to call my own shots. I’m kind of excited about that, save one thing.” Autumn licked her lips. “What’s that?” “I’m looking for the approval of this beautiful thing that I drove all the way across the country to be around again.” She sheepishly stared at the sidewalk, kicking rocks into the street. “And if I say no?” His hand went lower, grabbing around her exposed side and wiggling his fingers against her ribs. She bolted away with a shriek, his other hand pinned to her side as he stood up and playfully tickled her. She helplessly slapped her hands against his forearms, her glasses threatening to fly off as she violently shook her head with laughter. Terrence stopped, pushing the glasses back in place and kissing her on the lips. Autumn gave into his charms completely, tenderly grasping his head. “You can come with me.” “No I can’t.” “Yeah you can.” “Who’s going to watch the shop?” “Toby can watch the damn shop,” he reasoned as he looked through the windows, seeing Toby glaring at the two as he talked on the phone. Terrence’s eyes shot back down towards Autumn's warm face, completely ensnared by her smile. “We can have some fun. Get out of Fayette for a few days and see some of the world. I don’t even know where the hell this tournament is but that don’t really matter. Let’s just go.” Autumn smirked pensively. “And if I say no?” His wiggling fingers told her the penalty for refusing. “Alright, Alright!” It was better than being embarrassingly turned to a pool of jelly in the street for everyone to see. Terrence stopped his threats, growing serious in his question. “You really want to go?” “I don’t trust Toby,” she started, “but I think it would be interesting to see how you do. Never seen you wrestle before.” He nodded his head, extending his hand towards her and gingerly pulling her off the bench. “Well, that asshole keeps staring at me. Looks like a retarded puppy dog trying to act all angry.” “Don’t say that,” she scolded, teasingly punching his leather jacket. “I’ll let you get back to work then,” he said as he combed her hair back, giving her lips a final parting kiss. Once they let go of their embrace, she waved to him, turning around and walking back into the shop. The second she stepped in, Toby’s head shot downward, his free hand wrapped around the receiver of the telephone. The bells chimed as the door opened again behind her, causing her to turn around and see Terrence poking his head in from outside. “I’ll be at your place this evening. We can talk about it more then. Alright?” Autumn nodded her head, waving goodbye to Terrence as he walked away from the shop one last time. Hanging up the phone, Toby’s head craned in Terrence’s direction, following him as he got into his Thunderbird and drove away. She caught the odd stare, but thought nothing of it. The Jefferson County Sheriff’s Office was relatively quiet, another lethargic day speeding by at a snail’s pace. Deputy Colt Conyers jostled papers around his desk, his eyes absentmindedly glaring at the Coca Cola keepsakes he had littered around the edge. He flicked the buttons of his shirt as he steadied himself in his seat. The office across from his belonged to his father, the same man who’d been the Sheriff of the county for over thirty years and today was the day it all ended. The day where he got his gold watch and a pat on the back. The day the Conyers legacy ended. After seventy-five years, a Conyers wasn’t going to be Sheriff of Jefferson County. Maybe it was only a hiccup, a momentary lapse before the family would once again command the police force. Colt had had the opportunity to keep it going, but decided against it because it would interfere with his other ambitions. The office door opened, his father squeezing into his evening jacket as he turned the light off. He’d said he didn’t want a big celebration, that he just wanted to go on his way like he had any other day. To him, he wasn’t worth fussing over. They looked so similar, father and son; both were burly men with big shoulders and shaved heads. His father didn’t look like he was ready to go off easy into the sunset, but had given up the badge willingly. I’m going to fucking Cancun for a month, his father told him over beers, a content smile resting on his face. Colt should have taken the offer to become sheriff. He felt selfish now as his father began walking away from his office for the final time. Colt had spent the better part of two years working to get funding for a special project for the department, stressing the location of Jefferson County in correspondence to Louisiana. He wanted to start a Strike Team, figuring he could use the resources in the department to deal with illegal activity both in the county and on the state border. Fayette might have been a quiet town, but meth and other criminal problems were beginning to seep in, threatening the town’s sanctity. But if the Conyers legacy meant anything, it was that they were the protectors of this small place. They’d dealt with the terrors of Ernie Spisak, dealt with biker gangs, and kicked the mob out when the bastards tried to set up shop in their backyard after Katrina. But what was going on now was harder to trace, harder to pinpoint. Screaming signal flares in Jefferson County was rare because the force was quick to take care of issues. Colt wanted to fix it so that even the cockroaches had something to fear. He figured the best way to keep the legacy going was to become as proactive as possible. The Strike Team was going to be his baby. “Sheriff,” he yelled as he got up from his chair and left his office, stopping his father in his tracks. The officers on the floor all paid attention to him as he galloped towards his old man, wrapping his arm over his shoulder and pulling him in. “Where you think you’re heading off to?” His father was demure, making sure to whisper in his son’s ear. “I told you-” Colt turned him around to face the station house, the officers standing up and looking towards the pair. “My old man would like to think that nobody much cares if he goes out, but I think that’s actually not true.” The Sheriff tried pushing away, but his son’s strength had outgrown his own. He was going to have to take this, whether he liked it or not. “Sheriff Wyatt Conyers is leaving today, moving on to that patrol car up in the sky.” They chuckled at the remark, Wyatt suddenly feeling way beyond his years. “Just let me say this. Today marks the end of an era. The Conyers family has held this position for-damn-ever. My old man inherited the job when his father passed on; he was just about my age when he did it. And he’s been here ever since, fighting to keep this community safe, and if you ask me he’s done a damn good job of that.” There were hoots and hollers from the back of the station house, causing Wyatt’s face to flush. His son continued on. “What I’m saying here is this; he’s loved this community, put his life on the line to defend it. And the fact is, as glad as I am to see him stepping down to take care of himself, I know this department is going to be hurting because we just lost one of the best lawmen in this entire country.” He paused, a wellspring of emotions firing off in his mind. “I love you, dad.” Wrapping his arms around him, he hugged his father, heavily patting him on the back. Colt never knew his old man to be emotional, but he could feel him quaking against his frame as he rocked him back and forth. “We all love you Wyatt,” a voice boomed from side. “And you did a fine job serving this community.” Colt let go of his father so Wyatt could engage with his successor, Kwame Kilpatrick. Kwame wasn’t just ending a legacy; he was going to be the first black Sheriff in Jefferson County’s history. The two shook hands, Colt easing onto his heels as he brushed away some tears. “You’re going to do better than I ever did,” Wyatt assured Kwame, patting him on the shoulder and handing him the star pins he’d worn on his uniform collar ever since he’d taken the job. Kwame nodded as he closed his fist around the stars. “Just so you all know; there’s cake and punch in the break room. Why don’t you go get yourself a slice?” Wyatt sheepishly nodded, turning his head up to face his former charges. “Sorry fellas, I ain’t got no Bailey’s to spike that punch.” They all laughed, the troopers grouping around him as he began walking towards the break room. Kwame stepped to Colt’s side, grabbing his shoulder. “I’m sure it means a lot to him.” “The old man’s going to redden my ass tomorrow because of this.” Kwame got a chuckle out of the comment. “We’ll talk about your future tomorrow, if that’s alright by you.” Colt nodded, his eyes following his father as his peers showed their appreciation for his service. “Sounds fine by me,” he answered, quickly heading in the direction of his father and the rest of the force. Terrence’s throat was parched and the sun was making its descent. Griswold’s Way sat just outside of Fayette’s city limit, and it was the only bar outside of Main Street worth talking about. Getting out of his Thunderbird, he kicked chipped stones and dust with dragged steps, his eyes following the blinking neon sign just above the door. He left his bandanna in a heap on the passenger seat of his car. The hope was that nobody inside would recognize him without it on. His first step inside the sweat stack was loud, everyone looking in his direction as he made his way in. Their stares lingered on him as he took another step forward. The place was mostly empty, making all the dark dingy scrap wood trappings stick out. Both pool tables were occupied. Two patrons sitting on barstools broke eye contact with him the first moment his gaze touched them. Their faces were vaguely familiar. Terrence took a breath, small hitches sounding because his body was apprehensive. It was like he was walking into Parchman Farm for the first time all over again. He sat down in the far corner, the only visible sounds he could hear coming from the folks busy with their pool balls. Resting the soles of his boots on the wooden lip underneath his stool, Terrence ran his hand through his hair. “Terrence, what can I get you?” The bartender’s name was Roy. He kept his hair long, traces of age showing up in white strands, and his gut stuck out from beneath his t-shirt. His body always reeked due to his profuse sweating. He’d been running the place for twenty odd years, catering to the more unsavory types around the area. He’d known Terrence by his first name from his first time walking in at the age of sixteen. Before Terrence could answer, a foaming pitcher of beer was dropped in front of him. This one was on the house. They were all on the house for Terrence Kingsley. It didn’t take long for him to grab the handle and take a hearty gulp, making sure to pour the beer over his tongue to taste the sweet hops. Satisfied, Terrence raised his mug. “Always a good decision, Roy.” Roy had moved on to his other customers. “Yeah,” he paused, turning his head in Terrence’s direction. “Always is.” In the past, Roy Griswold would have stopped everything he was doing to make sure Terrence was taken care of, even if it was just to exchange a little bit of small talk. Anything for my favorite customer, he’d always say. Terrence hunched over, cupping his mug and peering down at his faint reflection on the polyurethane. It’s only been a month he reasoned, choking another swig of his lager as his heart rate elevated. Not too much can change in a month. His thought was more a reassurance. Jefferson County only changed in waves. Perhaps one had swept through in his absence. Perhaps the current had pulled him out to sea, and he just didn’t know it yet. Roy wiped sweat off his forehead with the same balled up rag he rubbed the top of his bar with, his attention on his register. The two guys at the end of the bar had gotten up and were making their way towards him at a slow pace, the murky lighting covering their faces with broken shadows. They stood side by side, the one on the left wearing a shaggy mane of brown which framed his tapered cheek bones while the one on the right had his ginger locks shaved high and tight. They were a study in contrasts; one bony, the sinews of his elbows showing prominently whereas his red haired friend was rotund with broad shoulders. “We’re sorry.” It was a simple statement spoken softly by the fat one. But for some reason, the voice was enough to grab Terrence’s attention. He narrowed his eyes and lazily pointed to the skinny one. “Doug Casey and…” The name of the other escaped him for a minute, but it was right on the tip of his tongue. “Lane…no Lloyd Stevens. Lloyd Stevens.” They both stepped back. Stevens held his arms out to surrender. “Only reason we’re here is ‘cause we heard you’d left.” Kingsley rubbed his forehead. This was the reason he’d dreaded coming here, but for whatever dumbass reason, he came in anyway. Why did they have to be so fucking stupid his brain roared as he ground his jaw in a grimace. They both could have walked out of Griswold’s as silhouettes, perhaps the worst coming out of it being that he’d suddenly remember who they were three weeks after the fact. But there they were, flaunting themselves in his presence. “It’s okay.” “Well we heard you’d left,” Casey repeated, his fists clinching. Casey’s about to throw a damn temper tantrum. He was a scrapper, the kind of guy who kept his fingernails long and brushed his teeth every night just in case he had to use them in a fight. But Terrence wasn’t here to start fights. He wasn’t here to collect the five thousand, plus interest, that the two owed him. He just wanted to have a beer and get on with his evening. “Excuse me, but my ass is raw, and I ain’t in the mood for this shit.” “We didn’t mean nothing by it,” Stevens reassured him. Suddenly, the thirst for beer made him sick. “I ain’t worried about that. Just…” Terrence paused, the most odd of thoughts running around in his mind. “All debts is paid. You boys ain’t got nothing to worry about now.” Lloyd was puzzled by the gravel toned statement, furrowing his brow as he continued staring at him. “Excuse me.” “I said all debts are paid. Go have your beer and leave me alone.” He went to take a drink, choking on the suds. It tasted filthy, disgusting. He pushed the mug forward, rising from his seat and pressing between the two. Walking along the bar, he caught Roy’s momentary glance; his entire body anxiously quaked after Terrence caught him, his hands frantically polishing shot glasses with his sweat rag. Terrence left Griswold’s, his hand slithering across the front of his face as he exhaled. Had he just told those slobs that they didn’t owe him anything? His skin felt hot, the slight breeze the only thing preventing him from breaking into sheets of sweat. All he’d done was set out to get a drink before going to Autumn’s trailer. It was going to be an uneventful night. The door to Griswold’s shot open. The knob bashed into the outer wall of the building, two pairs of footsteps pounding in his direction. Terrence was caught by surprise as someone jumped on his back, wrapping their arms around his neck. He looked over just in time to see Lloyd bury his fist into his exposed side, dropping him to his knees. Doug followed with him, trying his damndest to yank him back with his rear naked choke. Kingsley’s eyes caught Lloyd’s foot just as it was about to connect across his face. He blocked the brunt of the kick with his forearm. Two on one, and his face was beginning to turn purple from Casey’s stranglehold. He threw his head back. He caught Doug on the bridge of the nose. He realized he’d done damage because the stranglehold was broken. Casey wailed loudly. Lloyd tried to kick again. Kingsley grabbed him by the shin, leveraging himself. He sent Lloyd to the ground, exploding through him with a double leg takedown. Doug Casey had scurried away from the melee, his hands cupping his screaming lips and his broken nose. Kingsley got up and belted Lloyd in the liver with his boot. The ginger flinched to grab his side. Terrence whipped around, slapping Casey’s hand from his mouth. He dropped him with a punch to the jaw. The girlish pleas had stopped. Doug’s sobs were the only sound he could hear. Stevens was trying to get to his feet, his hands draped over his girth. Kingsley kicked him in the face, dropping the bastard back to the gravel. Spit hung from his mouth. He jerked Lloyd up by the shirt collar. Autumn fought with all she could muster, but she wasn’t strong enough. Tossed back to the bed, she tried to re-enter the fray. “Settle the fuck down!” But the chain wielder had a better idea. He stormed over her boyfriend. She stood up. He hammered his knuckles across her face, dropping her to the floor. He huffed, staring at the other four men and shaking his head. Looking down, Terrence meekly grabbed at his ankle, trying to push himself up to continue the fight. A kick to the head stopped him. The leader grabbed at the bottom of his mask, tearing it upward and throwing it onto the bed. They all looked at Gentry as he pointed towards the broken form of Terrence Kingsley, jabbing his finger out of the trailer. He hung back, his gloved hand grabbing at tufts of Autumn’s hair as he yanked her head up. “You were warned,” he chastised before throwing her head down. The others carried Terrence Kingsley out of the trailer, his legs dragging beneath him. A sudden bout of strength came through his body, but it was a mere flicker. “No use in running. We're everywhere,” Gentry said as he joined the group outside. One of the militia men produced a rope with a noose tied at the end. “This is from your old man, you drug dealing piece of shit!” He tried forcing the noose around Terrence’s neck but was unable to because of his defensive bucking and wiggling. Those holding him slammed his body against the Thunderbird, one pressing his head against the hood. Autumn stumbled from the inside of the trailer, wobbling as she tried to run to Terrence. A pair removed themselves from the crowd and held her back. Gentry, wormed the noose over the top of Terrence’s head. The feeling of the rope fibers across his skin caused Terrence to spasmodically flinch and smack against the car as he squealed. The noose was dragged down to his neck, followed quickly by the heel of Gentry’s boot to his spine. “Get his hands.” As he pressed him into the car, three others fought with Kingsley’s arms, lassoing another bit of rope and tying his arms behind his back. “Let’s do this in the woods.” “No,” Gentry said, looking over his shoulder towards Autumn. “It’s a lesson for her just as much as it is him.” Dragging Terrence’s limp and frightened body towards the nearest high standing tree, Gentry tossed the rope over the branches to a strong nook, grabbing the end and yanking it down. “Going to need some help here boys.” Terrence couldn’t fight back. Autumn could only cry and whimper for them to stop. Ten joined Gentry. Kingsley’s eyes went wild with the first tug. All the air was forced out of his throat. He began choking. His head began to feel cold. They tugged hard on the rope. He stood on his tiptoes for as long as he could. He tried to steady himself. Each tug punctuated hopelessness throughout his panicking psyche. The pulls became easier when Terrence’s feet left the Earth. Kingsley’s futile fighting had turned to raw, dire panic. His arms were pinned. No matter how much he yanked, the knot wasn’t coming loose. All his muscles tightened. His legs kicked upward. He danced in broken bending rhythms as he struggled against the noose choking the life out of him. Wrapping the rope around the base of the tree, they tied it off and watched Terrence as he flopped and twisted about in the air. They chuckled beneath their gas masks at his guttural chokes. Gentry pulled out a cigarette, lighting it up and taking a seat to watch the show. Autumn stood mortified, the image of Terrence Kingsley’s death rattle burning into her brain and haunting her mind.
