Cruising Over the Causeway

23 November 2004
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2004-11-23 Going into business for oneself is always a tough thing to do. You find yourself hit with all sorts of expenses and surprises; overhead costs, taxes, disgruntled employees demanding raises, the competition, and the occasional hardass who shows up demanding money from you in exchange for being able to operate your business where you choose to do so. Fortunately for the Latch Key Kid, he didn’t have to worry about the first few of these. There aren’t many overhead costs associated with running a lemonade stand; after all, you’re standing outside, so there are no utility costs to pay. As a large percentage of transactions are conducted in cash, taxes become more of an honest policy than an enforceable policy (though the Latch Key Kid is upstanding enough that he would make sure to pay all necessary taxes). There were no employees other than himself, and possibly Katherine, therefore, he could give raises whenever he so wished. The competition, well, there was Frank’s, in as much as a coffeehouse can compete with a lemonade stand. All that seemed to be left were the hardasses who were bothering Frank. Someone had to put a stop to that, and the Latch Key Kid knew just the right person for the job. ------------------------ “You? Are you crazy? You can’t get involved in this,” Katherine said scoldingly. “You have no idea what’s going on in there. The last thing Frank needs is for you to barge into that office next time they come by and make a scene.” “Maybe so,” Latch replied, “but Frank is my friend. Even after he fired me, I still have to do what’s best for him.” He thought back to a time long ago when another friend needed him, a time when he failed to help, and suddenly his head jerked at the memory. It had been a typical weekday afternoon, when classes ended for the day at Alamo Elementary School. Brad and his friend, Steve, were walking to Steve’s house to spend the afternoon playing video games. Brad had received special permission from his mother the day before, and was excited to be able to go to a friend’s house rather than having to go home and sit by himself until his mother got home from work. There was a slight breeze blowing across the island, coming in from the Gulf of Mexico, as was often the case on fall afternoons in Galveston. Brad and Steve approached the corner where they would make a right turn towards Steve’s street, but Brad had to stop to tie his shoelace. Steve wandered on ahead, alone, still talking loudly enough for Brad to hear him. And then suddenly, Steve was silent. Then Brad heard the pleading, the taunting, the laughing, and he knew what was about to happen. Brad slowly peeked his head around the bushes on the corner, and saw his worst fears. A gang of bullies, fifth-graders probably, maybe even sixth-graders, had surrounded Steve and were tossing his book satchel back and forth, teasing him to catch it. Brad knew what he should do, he knew what the right thing was, but then again he usually did. He started to move, and then hesitated. He thought about all the times he himself had been picked on, always wishing someone else were around to help him, and he thought about the consequences of interfering in what was happening now. The hesitation was brief, but it was enough to let those thoughts seep into his head. The life of a second grader is a tough thing at times. And then, Brad stood up, turned around, and walked back in the direction he and Steve had just come from. He began to job, and then to run faster and faster, until he could no longer hear the bullies’ taunting and Steve’s crying. Brad could not bring himself to look at Steve the next day in school. But that didn’t matter much, because Steve never got close enough to talk to Brad. In fact, Steve never got that close to Brad ever again. “I’m sorry, Katherine, I know you think it would be best for me to stay out of this. But I can’t turn my back on another friend. I’ve done it before, and I vowed I’d never do it again. Frank’s being bullied, and he needs me.” “I can’t stop you,” Katherine sighed, “and I know you’re just stubborn enough to stick to your guns here. Do what you think is best, Brad, but please don’t do anything foolish.” She had the nerve to tell him what was foolish? Foolish was not helping out a friend in need, at least in the Latch Key Kid’s mind. Foolish was turning your back on that friend, deafening your ears to his cries for help, running in the opposite direction while praying the entire time that you wouldn’t be spotted and picked on as well. Brad Jameson had been foolish at one point in his life. This was not the time to repeat it. ------------------------ He would always remember the first time he went back to Galveston after he moved away. He thought about the first time he saw his hometown after having been away. Taking Interstate-45 south from Houston, driving past small towns that dotted the highway here and there as if in some bizarre connect-the-dots design, the smell of the big city gradually replaced with the distinct saltwater smell of the ocean, until just past the halfway point when the ocean smell took over entirely and the air took on a crisp feel. Galveston Island lies just off the shore of Texas, and is connected to the mainland by a causeway bridge high enough above the water to allow boats and barges of all sizes to pass beneath it. As Brad drove over the bridge and reached the crest of it, he gazed across the bay at Galveston and his eyes lit up. It was just as he remembered it. Another fast food restaurant or two, perhaps a superstore of some sort where the old mom-and-pop used to be, but other than that, it was home; it was his home. He remembered reading an old Thomas Wolfe book, You Can’t Go Home Again, and he shook his head and laughed to himself. Of course you can, Brad, he told himself, why, you’re already there. His mother’s house was exactly the way it was when he left five years ago, save for minor damage from the periodic storms and hurricanes that had hit Galveston. The wooden stilts that supported the house fifteen feet in the air had held up well, like most of the houses around theirs. The sand dunes still showed signs of use, seemingly imprinted forever with footprints of little children, outlines of pails and tracks left by toy trucks. The sunset over the horizon was still the most beautiful he had ever seen, and Brad smiled inwardly as the sun passed completely to the other side of the world and the last of its rays shimmering on the waves died out. He smiled as he sat on the deck and watched this unfold. He was home again, Thomas Wolfe be damned. The next day, he drove into town, parked in the old Strand area that was no overrun by shops selling trinkets, t-shirts and tacky souvenirs to tourists, and began to walk. He knew where he was walking, yet at the same time, he didn’t know. He found the old elementary school. It was still there, but the years had taken their toll on it. He kept walking, and eventually came to the corner where he had made his decision and left Steve. It took him a moment to realize that that was the corner where it happened, and when he finally did, it hit him with more force than he had anticipated. He had already passed spots where he had given in to the bullies and allowed them to take his lunch money; spots where he had held his own against the bullies but had still come up short; and the spot where he had finally fought back with enough strength to overwhelm the lot of them. But this corner, this was the site of what still lingered in his mind as his ultimate betrayal. Never again had he run away from a friend in trouble. The emotions overwhelmed him, and he stood on the corner for a good five minutes, remembering all that had happened. As he walked back toward his car, he took care to look around him, to take in the scenes unfolding on the streets, tourists and residents intermingling, surfers and businessmen alike enjoying a refreshing beverage in an open-air bar, and he thought about how his home had changed. He thought about how he had changed. It was only five years, it hadn’t been all that long, but it was long enough. No matter how slight the changes really were, things were different here. As he drove back to his mother’s house, he reflected upon this. And that night, as he sat on the deck, watching another stunning sunset, he finally realized what the title of that Thomas Wolfe book meant, what it referred to, the significance of it. And he realized that Wolfe was correct; he could return home, but he could never be home again. As he drove back over the causeway bridge the next day, on his way back to Houston to catch his flight, he looked in the rearview mirror and watched Galveston slowly disappear behind him. That had been five years ago. Each time he had gone back since then, it felt a little less like home. And now, as he stood next to his lemonade stand, he realized that wrestling was his life now. Whether he wanted it to be or not, it had consumed him. It had brought him success and fame that he carried everywhere as one-half of the XUW tag team champions for a total of roughly 13 months, it had given him one of the best friends he had ever had in Doughboy, and it had brought him Katherine, the greatest girl he had ever met. He realized the place that wrestling held in his heart, his mind, his world. And he realized the significance of this tournament and his next match against Dr. Curiosity. This tournament, he now understood, was his causeway bridge. How he responded when he reached the other side would be entirely up to him. How he responded when he would finally cross back over would also be entirely up to him. As he stood there, still selling no lemonade, he began to wonder if he would like what he found on the other side. ------------------------ “Hey, Frank, how’s it going?” Latch called out as Frank walked toward the storefront, his keys jingling in his hand. “Long time no see.” Frank walked over to the lemonade stand. “Yeah, it’s been what, about 16 hours? What are you still doing here, Brad? Why are you doing this? I told you about those goons that stopped by last week. That should’ve been enough for you, Brad. This stand, this business, it’s all fine and good, if this is what you want to do; but why, of all the street corners in the world, did you have to pick mine?” “That’s exactly why, Frank. Because it is yours. I just wanted to be here, next to you, running a business just like you.” Frank scratched his head. “I’m flattered, I guess. But look, Brad, this just isn’t…say, I’ll tell you what. Why don’t you come on inside, have a cup of coffee with me, and we can talk about this whole thing in a more comfortable setting.” “That sounds great,” Latch beamed, “but who will run the lemonade stand?” Frank pointed to Katherine. “She can do it, can’t she? You’re not going very far, and it won’t be for too long, so I’m sure she can handle-” Katherine cleared her throat. “She’s right here, you know. She can hear you,” she said, indignantly. “Go ahead, Brad. Go inside and talk to Frank. I’ll be fine here. Don’t worry about me…or your lemonade stand.” “Okay, sounds good to me,” Latch said, as he turned around and walked off with Frank. Katherine scowled at him from behind, and crossed her arms as she turned back to face the street. Frank unlocked the front door and held it open for Latch, who turned on the lights of the coffeehouse as he stepped inside. Frank followed, closing the door behind him, and after thinking for a second, locking the door. He ushered Latch over to the counter, and motioned for him to sit down. Latch sat on a barstool as Frank walked behind the counter and began to prepare the first pot of coffee for the day. “Now then,” Frank began, “how’s the business treating you, Brad?” “Well,” Latch hesitated, “it’s a bit on the slow-going side to be honest with you, Frank. I don’t know if I’m doing something wrong, but there just don’t seem to be that many customers. I don’t get it; it’s lemonade, after all. Everybody likes lemonade. So why aren’t people buying it?” “If I knew the answer to that, Brad, I’d tell you, I really would. Unfortunately, however, I don’t know. All I can suggest is that you look at your business strategy. Examine it carefully, especially your marketing plan; marketing is key, you know. Work on developing it, figure out how you can tweak it to start to work for you, and you’ll be good after that.” “I’m sorry,” Latch replied with a curious furrow of his brow, “did you say business strategy? Marketing plan? Developing?” “That’s right,” Frank grinned, “I said all of those. Don’t tell me you don’t have a business strategy in place, a plan that you’ve developed to bring you success.” “Well, I…” he paused, “…uhm…Katherine…well, no.” “Oh, boy,” Frank groaned as he poured the first cup of coffee and set it on the counter in front of Latch. “Maybe you should rethink this whole thing, Brad. Maybe this whole running a business thing isn’t for you. There’s no shame in trying and not making it, Brad. In fact, most small businesses do wind up failing. But don’t worry about it. You’ve got other stuff going on that’s more than enough for you right now.” Latch smiled and began to wiggle his index finger. “Okay, I see what’s going on here. If you think you’re going to change my mind that easily, you’re wrong. I can’t be fooled like that, Frank.” “I’m sorry?” “You’re just trying to discourage me from keeping that lemonade stand open, aren’t you? You don’t want me to run my business out there because you’re afraid I’ll take customers from you. That’s it,” Latch said, growing impatient. “Brad, that’s not it. You’re way off base. For one thing, people who buy coffee first thing in the morning aren’t going to buy lemonade instead of that to start their day. Your lemonade isn’t going to kick start the day for people like my coffee does.” Latch smiled. “You obviously haven’t tried my lemonade. The sugar in it is incredible.” Frank frowned. “Okay, Brad, I see you’re going to be your usual stubborn self about this. If you won’t listen to me when I try to help you with your business sense, perhaps you’ll listen to me when I tell you the truth about why you shouldn’t keep your stand there.” “And so it comes out,” Latch said as he snapped his fingers. “Spill it, big guy.” Frank paused, thought something over in his head, and then shrugged his shoulders. “You remember those three guys that stopped by here before, the ones that threatened you outside?” Latch nodded, leaned forward, resting his elbows on the counter and putting his chin in his hands. He looked like a young child eagerly awaiting hearing the next page of a Dr. Seuss book. “Well, I didn’t want to tell you this, Brad, but they’re…” he paused. Latch nodded. “It’s okay, Frank. You can tell me.” “They’ve been harassing me, Brad,” Frank said, “barging in on my business. They come by every so often to take a payoff, and that’s when you saw them come by before.” Latch interrupted. “So wait, why you? Why do they single out your coffeehouse?” “That’s just it, Brad,” Frank said, tossing up his hands, “they don’t single out my business. It’s everybody around here. They’ve taken over the area, and in order to operate our businesses, we’re forced to give them a cut of our profits. It’s not just me; it’s the newsstand next door, the bookstore next to that, all of us, down to the mini-mart on the other corner. They-” “So that’s what they were doing over here the other day, when they pushed you into the office?” Latch asked, his voice betraying a bit of anger. “You should have told me earlier, Frank.” “Wait, you saw that?” Frank sighed. “Brad…you’re really getting in too deep here. Please believe me when I say that this is too dangerous for you. You’re putting yourself into a situation you can’t control. You have nothing to do with this, so I can’t let you get drawn into it now.” Latch started to speak, nearly started to agree with Frank, came close to walking away. Then he remembered the incident on the street corner, so long ago. He walked away then, and he swore he’d never do it again. “I’m sorry, Frank,” Latch said, “but I can’t leave you on your own.” “It’s not worth it, Brad,” Frank protested. “It’s just a lemonade stand. You can move it and set it up somewhere else, somewhere safe. For me, Brad. Please?” “No, Frank,” Latch replied, “it’s not just a lemonade stand. It’s much more than that. I have a chance to make something right, after all those years. This time, I’m not going to pack up and run away from the corner. I’m going to turn the corner this time, Frank. I’m going to help you through this. I promise.” Latch stood up, drained the remainder of the coffee in his mug, and turned toward the door. Frank called out after him. “Brad, stop! I can’t-” Latch didn’t even stop, just held up his right hand. “End of story, Frank,” he said, as he looked back over his shoulder at Frank, “I’ve made up my mind. That’s it.” Latch opened the front door and walked out of the coffeehouse. Katherine looked up and saw him approaching the lemonade stand, walking with a purposeful gait. She saw the emotions betrayed in his face, and hurriedly gave a young child her change and cup of lemonade before rushing over to meet Latch halfway between the parking lot and the lemonade stand. She grabbed his hands with hers, and then drew him toward her with a hug. “What is it, Brad?” she asked, obviously concerned for him. “It’s Frank,” Latch replied, “he needs me. I have to help him.” “What do you mean?” Katherine asked. “Let’s get away from here for a bit. Let’s close up the stand and go get some breakfast.” She nodded, and together they began to secure the lemonade stand to ensure it would be safe while they were gone.
