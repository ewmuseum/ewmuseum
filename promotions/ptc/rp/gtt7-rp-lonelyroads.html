Maybe You Can Polish A Turd After All: The Garbage Bag Johnny Story Chapter 6: Lonelyroads A Garbage Bag Production JANUARY 1, 2010 December had been a trying month, and at the time, I wasn't even aware that the year had ended. I woke up nursing a hangover that had been building up inside me for weeks. It was only mere coincidence, or perhaps statistical probability, that I had adhered to tradition. For the most part, I had shut myself in, afraid to confront the questions I had about my own existence. At first, I was too confused to even consider the possibility that I was a fabrication of some sort, and I kept on with my routines and appointments. Somewhere along the line, I refused considering that possibility at all and wrote it off as unfeasible, but a sliver of uncertainty nestled itself into the back of my mind, and I had to get rid of it because I could feel its slight sting every time I thought about anything. Even after I advanced in a wrestling tournament I had entered, at first to replenish my leaking bank account, I wasn't convinced of my own reality. I had some time to kill until the next round, and as much as I hated to admit it, I was looking forward to it for more than just one more paycheck, as badly as I needed it. Dina hadn't cashed the check I had written her, and truthfully, I didn't have nearly enough to cover it anyway. I spent the money I made in the previous round quickly, stocking up on supplies that I hoped would last and kept inside to resist any unnecessary purchases. I figured I'd have a go at making some real progress on the autobiography, and it all started out well enough until a few days went by, and the sliver rooted itself deeper and stung sharper at me. I realized that days had gone by, and the outside world had left me alone. My first instinct was to check to see if it was still out there, but afraid of potential outcomes, I retracted myself further from human contact, opting instead to conduct an experiment. I figured that someone would eventually seek me out, and it would prove that I was real all along. I was used to being alone on Christmas. I didn't make it a point to be alone, but it didn't bother me any. It was kind of just how things went. I let a few more days pass, and as they did, my suspicions grew. I forgot about the autobiography, and too afraid to venture outside so that I could clutch denial to the end if there was no beginning, In a frenzy, I began to exhaust my resources, tearing through two cartons of cigarettes and completing five fifths of whiskey in less than a week. I was out of whiskey, and I started to panic. It didn't take long for my body to crave alcohol when I went without it after I'd started drinking again. My reflection was sitting on the toilet, passing an empty bottle of Jameson from one hand to the other, wondering what the fuck to do. I watched him there, that poor son of a bitch, and part of me felt sorry for him. Resigning to my own lack of fate, I tried to put a positive spin on being non-existent. I told myself it would be just like rehab without any of the obligations. Nobody would bother me, and if I had any alcohol left, I was free to drink it without consequence. I stood from the toilet and opened the medicine cabinet. The shallow shelves were mostly barren, and several of the vials in it were already empty, ignored reminders that I had been too lazy to throw them out. I almost missed the brown plastic bottle as it was only a few shades darker than the wood and didn't look like much in the first place. I turned it by its cap, and a grin stretched across my face. I had heard that isopropyl alcohol was serious business, but as desperate as I had been, I'd never been in a tight enough spot to resort to drinking rubbing alcohol. "Well," I grabbed the bottle, examining the dangerously long words on the label. They didn't mean much to me. "Maybe I got something that'll taste good with this." I lumbered out of the bathroom and waded through a layer of discarded trash that was growing on the floor. There was a certain microcosmic desolation to it all, and I couldn't decide how I felt about it. My feelings changed depending on the parameters I used to define size, whether they were relative or not and what they were relative to, using the same concepts that determine all of mathematics on some scale. In this particular model, the refrigerator was almost polar to the bathroom, and the time it took to get there across the perilous terrain measured about as long as I'd ever need it to measure. The rules of non-existence were few and far between. I don't know what I was expecting to see when I opened the refrigerator. I never really kept the thing stocked with much. The shelves held some old condiments, but I didn't have much of a clue whether jelly or mustard would mix more pleasantly with the rubbing alcohol. Mustard eventually won out by textural virtue, but considering either one of them a winner would be a heinous exaggeration. I opted instead to sip the rest of the bottle without trying to garnish its burning flavor. It tasted like the fuel that powered down the hopeless. It didn't take long before leaving the kitchen proved too difficult. I slid down the counter onto the floor and sat there for a while, looking over at my reflection in the oven's tinted window. "I guess it's just you and me now, buddy!" We raised our bottles together, almost toasting if there hadn't been too much distance between us. "Bottoms up." That was the gulp that took me. What was left of the world went out, and I knew then that I didn't exist, even if it seemed like I had before. There were things that did exist, even if they didn't seem to, and I wasn't one of them. "Donald!" I cried out, but he didn't respond. "Donald White!" I tried to think of what he'd say in my situation. He was far more experienced than I was, neither of us having actually experienced anything. I saw myself in the oven again. He'd burn it all down. That's what he'd do. I stretched my legs out to access my pocket and grab my lighter before recrossing my legs. In the other hand, I brought the isopropyl bottle as high as I could reach and poured all of the alcohol that remained over my head. There wasn't a significant amount left, but some of it got in my eye. "Fucking shit!" Rubbing my eye only exacerbated the pain, and I was having second thoughts about the lighter until I reminded myself that pain was only a mechanism of a reality where I didn't exist. My arm trembled as I brought the lighter towards myself, and my hands were shaking so badly that I had trouble operating the lighter. My thumb scraped hard against the wheel, but the spark died out, and I lost my grip. The lighter shot across the kitchen tile, and if that wasn't a sign, when I scooted to retrieve it, I heard the door. The knocking had frozen me there, halfway across the kitchen floor, wondering whether or not my head was playing tricks on me. A second knock quickly thawed me. I shot up quickly, too quickly even, and my body tipped over into the counter. I pushed myself around the perimeter and back through the debris-laden floor towards the door. The knocking persisted. The room got longer as I avoided walking in straight lines, and the necessary motor skills required to straighten my course were unlearning their functions as quickly as they could. I made it to the door, and after a bit of trouble, I was able to solve the puzzle that was opening it. The impact of the unexpected blow to my face sent me toppling down. MARCH 14, 2009 Somehow, I managed to stay on my feet after being kicked in the face twice, and it wasn't so much the third time Jason Snow's boot connected with my face as it was the energy I used up dodging the previous super kick that floored me. He looked down at me and our glassy eyes met an understanding. Both of us were worn from over an hour of chaos. I felt the mat shake with each step as he exhausted fumes towards the turnbuckle. I could feel the tremors in the mat soften, and I started quaking. I don't know why, but I was more scared than anything else. I guess I just wasn't used to having to do anything that important. It was a good kind of fear, though. Maybe it wasn't even fear, but I don't know a better word for it. It didn't matter that it wasn't graceful, but I sprung to my feet and stumbled towards Jason Snow, who was perching himself there just as gracelessly. Each step was a test. I had cramps in both my legs, and when I finally got up there, Snow and I just kind of leaned on each other, weak punches disguising efforts of balance. When it locked in, we both felt it. I wrapped an arm under his leg and plunged backwards as Snow rolled his body forward. It was so loud in there that I didn't even hear us hit the mat. I didn't even feel it. I thought we were still falling, even with my arm draped over him, until the referee stopped counting and it was all over. The Dual Halo was the second time I had been in the ring with Jason Snow, and if it wasn't for battle royal style events and random draw tournaments, I never would've been in the ring with him at all. Since doing the job to Snow during his run to victory in GTT6, it almost wasn't arguable that he was the best in the industry, and in three years, not much has changed. Losing to Jason Snow is a bigger accomplishment than pinning most other wrestlers, and pinning Jason Snow is like capturing a unicorn. It meant the world to me that Snow had no problem with the outcome, even if he didn't sell my character throughout the feud that followed. "You're good enough to sell yourself." He paused after I asked him and before he said it, and I didn't know if he was just saying it to avoid having to justify the issue any further. That night, I made myself an ice bath and sat in there for three hours watching the match in its entirety. Specifically, I was watching Jason Snow, noting how he moved and trying to figure out why he did what he did when he did. I came to the conclusion that I'd never be as good as he was, no matter how bad I wanted to be the best. I'd seen wrestlers try to model themselves after him. None of them came close. It didn't bother me that I'd never be the best. I never let things that seemed impossible bother me. I knew I just felt numb there in all that ice, like a floating head, and that was good enough for me. I could've cared less if anyone thought I was any good or not. It's funny how fast that changes when the party is over. OCTOBER 7, 2006 A production assistant slammed the clapboard to cue yet another take of the commercial we were filming. We had done too many takes to count and the suit was getting sweaty and itchy. Each take had gotten progressively worse as I continued to find more patience to lose in reservoirs I was surprised even existed. The camera was rolling. I went to work. "Hey there, all you hens and roosters, come on down to Wing King's, home of the Chicken Chowdown Championships, on Sunday, October 29, and take the championship challenge. If you can put down ten of Wing King's Mother-Cluckin' Hot wings in five minutes, you get a Wing King's t-shirt and your picture goes in the chicken coop! Take it from me, the King of Wings, they're COCK-A-DOO-DELICIOUS!" I breathed a sigh of relief inside the chicken suit, took off the crowned head, and lit a cigarette. "How was that?" "You nailed the lines, but we can't use it." The director did his best to appear polite throughout the whole shoot, but it was clear that he was growing frustrated with how long this was taking. "What? Why not?" After winning the AWC Championship, my celebrity stock rose a little bit, and I started receiving offers for commercial spots and endorsement deals. I had never heard of Wing King's, but they were willing to offer me a decent paycheck just to tell people I enjoyed their food. I thought it was going to be easy money. The wings weren't half bad either. "A bird shit in frame." I turned around, looking behind me at the storefront at the white streak melting down the window. Then I looked up, searching for the culprit with a great ire. "Can we get someone to wipe that shit up?" I pointed to myself, thinking he was referring to me, but he didn't notice my confusion, and another production assistant was quickly wiping the window with a cloth. I sighed and took a seat, working so furiously on the cigarette that I inhaled too hard and couldn't stop coughing. I felt like the window; like the bird shat on me and the only take where I didn't fuck up any of the lines. "Johnny, you going to be ready in five?" The director stood from his chair, stretching his legs. I tried to remember his name, but it was futile. I remember shaking his hand, but his introduction was so uninteresting that I didn't pay attention. "Can't you just have the shit removed digitally or something?" I figured it wouldn't be too hard to do that with computers. I had seen several doctored photographs on the internet that looked real enough to me. "We could, but we pay the video editors by the hour, and we pay you by appearance." He shrugged apologetically. "It would be a lot more cost effective if you nailed this in one take." "I just did, and then a bird shat all over it." I looked up at the skies again, but even dressed as a chicken, all the birds looked the same to me. "We'll just run through it a few more times," he directed, before sitting back down in his chair. I tossed the cigarette butt away and put my head back on, my face poking through the beak. "Take 51!" I waited for the clapboard to snap again. "Hey there, all you hens and roosters, come on down to Wing King's, home of the Chicken, uh, Championship. God dammit." I couldn't even read a cue card. The point of the upper beak kept getting in the way. I rolled my finger and tried to clear my mind. "Let's do it again." "Take 52!" "Hey there, all you hens and roosters, come on down to Wing King's, home of the Chicken Chowdown Championships, on Sunday, October 29, and take the championship challenge. If you can put down ten of Wing King's Mother-Cluckin' Hot wings in five minutes, you get a Wing King's t-shirt and your picture goes in the chicken . . . shit!" I was distracted by a flock of gulls that gathered at one end of the parking lot. In full dress, I sprinted over to where they were, flapping my feathery arms and kicking my foamy chicken feet. The birds scattered, and huffing and puffing, I turned back to the crew. They were all watching me. "Those mother fucking birds. I swear." "You know, why don't we call it a day?" The director rubbed his eyes. "You're right. We'll just use computers to get the shit off the screen." I pumped my feathered fist and found a hand to help me fight out of the costume. It made me realize a few things about being a champion, namely that there is a high cost to recognition. It turns you into a puppet, and everyone wants a chance to stick their hand up your ass. They have you running around in costumes, and none of them are there to help you up when they all watch you fall. There's nothing they love more than watching you fall. I found myself there on the ground after a rumor broke out that I had a strange bird fetish. Apparently, someone leaked video footage from a scrapped commercial that featured me dressed as a chicken running after a lot of birds. Taken out of context, I could see how someone could misconstrue my actions. I didn't understand why I kept getting up, thinking myself too savvy to fall again. I spent more time hocking products and signing autographs than I did in the ring. I realized, there at that shoot, that this was the job. This was what it meant to be the champion. If you don't stop at the top of a mountain, you just end up going down the other side. "I need a new fucking agent." The hand that helped me out of the suit thought I was talking to him. He stopped what he was doing and looked up at me. I lit another cigarette as I looked down at him through my nearest eye and realized I probably should say something. "Thanks for helping me with the suit." "Yeah, sure thing." He was the same guy that wiped the shit off the window. "Just doing my job." OCTOBER 7, 2009 Reverend Curt Harding had gotten me a spot at an event speaking to some misguided youths. Unfortunately, he hadn't told me that his definition of misguided youths consisted of homosexual teenagers from very religious families. Coming Back was the brainchild of Pastor Clancy Tate, a round, old man with a smooth moon face. His tiny mouth squished into a short line between fat cheeks, and it made him look emotionless, even if he was forcing a smile. He was an old friend of Reverend Harding's, and we were all in a back office outside of the conference room where my audience waited. "This isn't what I had in mind when you mentioned misguided youth." I gave Curt a knowing look as I took a cigarette out of the pack. "Please, Johnny, you can't smoke in here." The round man butted in, almost moving from his chair. "I didn't mean to give you the wrong impression, Johnny." I assumed he reached at me. "Look, these people are just like you and me. They're just confused. It's nothing to worry about." "That's exactly it. I've worked with plenty of wrestlers, male and female, who are gay, and they are just like you and me. It is nothing to worry about." I had never been a staunch advocate of rights for anybody in particular, I just felt that this was one of those issues that wasn't really my business. "When I said I wanted to be a motivational speaker, the only motive I really had was motivating people to reach their dreams." "Johnny," Tate chuckled, his chin-flap bouncing. "Motivational speaking ain't about dreams. It's about bringing morals back to society. It wasn't Adam and Steve, you know." "That is true," Curt cut in. "Nobody's saying they're bad people. They just need a little direction. Clancy puts a lot of work into this group, and almost everyone who has come back is much happier for it. Isn't that right, Clancy?" "Yep." He nodded, his skin bobbling off his skull. "Think of it like your drinking problem. If they were all just ruining their own lives, it'd be fine, but their setting bad examples for society. All these kids are here because their parents are worried about the lifestyles they're choosing." "That's ridiculous." I crossed my arms. "I don't think I can do this." "Sure you can," Reverend Curt put a hand on my shoulder, and I quickly brushed it off. "You don't have to go out there and berate them for making bad decisions, just tell them what it's like on the other side, being a strong, virile, heterosexual man." "Also, we're going to need that fifty bucks back right now if you refuse to speak." Even with his fat face holding its stone pose, I could tell from his eyes that Clancy was irritated. I needed money badly to get back on my feet, but I didn't know if I could live with myself if I spoke against my beliefs for fifty dollars. "And if you tell me which wrestlers are gay, I'll double it." "Uh, Hessian, probably Jon Rhine, um, and I'm pretty sure Nova is bisexual." I wasn't sure why he was so interested and didn't even bother with suspicion. Now, with profits in the triple digits thanks to the art of negotiation, I was almost ready to go. Even through my guile at the topical matters, I was still nervous to confront a group in a vulnerably personal way. Group was my least favorite part of recovery. I was never really comfortable sharing my problems with other people, as often as I just wanted to be myself once I figured out who I was. After the issues were settled, I prepared with a final smoke, leaving the center to head back to the car. I sat on the passenger seat with the door open. The wig was just where I had left it in the glove box. I don't know why I carried it around with me. I hadn't worn it for almost three years, but the thought of becoming Herb Sellers comforted me. As little as I thought I knew about who I was, I knew I wasn't someone else. I thought that life probably would've been a lot easier if I was always wearing the Herb Sellers wig. Ironically, becoming Herb Sellers permanently would mean that John Bagwell Bagwell-Gumphries would no longer exist. I put the cigarette out and headed back into the building where Reverend Curt met me outside the auditorium's backstage doors. "Are you ready? Clancy's about to . . ." Curt came to a sharp stop. "Why are you wearing a wig?" "What are you talking about?" I brushed past the reverend and walked through the door while Clancy Tate wrapped up my introduction. ". . . so without further ado, please give a warm welcome to the fiercely heterosexual Garbage Bag Johnny!" Clancy lifted a flabby arm towards the curtain, and I peeked through, watching the crowd of teens as they booed and shouted insulting things at me. I walked through, not blinking, and if Clancy wouldn't have said anything, I wouldn't have even seen him pass me. "Tough crowd. Good luck out there, kid. And what's the wig about?" A lot of wrestlers go out there and suffer the jeers of the crowd for business. Most people really want to be cheered, deep down, even the bad guys. Some guys learned to feed off of it and use it to get into character. It made me forget what the wig was about. These kids weren't booing a character. They were booing a person. "Um, hello?" I tapped on the microphone at the podium, but these viciously gay teens were shouting some profane things in my direction. "Please, if everybody would save their comments for later, I'm not here to judge anyone. I just want to motivate you to reach for your dreams." I looked to the side of the stage. Curt had joined Clancy, and Clancy gave him a look like he was happy to see me drowning out there. I took off the wig and put it down on the podium. "Alright. Hear me out. There is nothing wrong with any of you." This silenced the crowd, but Clancy Tate started towards me. "Don't let anyone tell you that there is. You can't spend your whole life hiding underneath a wig. My name is Johnny, and I'm a recovering addict. I don't know much about much, but-" "Excuse me." Pastor Clancy Tate squeezed his round body between me and the podium, stiff-arming me with an arm that wobbled like a bag of fluid. The room had turned, though, and the kids were cheering me. They wanted to hear what I had to say. "I'm not finished!" I pushed Clancy Tate to the side, and the clergyman stood there in disbelief. "I wanted to be a motivational speaker so I could help people take control of their own lives. I figured it was a way to take control of my own life, and I figured that helping other people would help keep me clean, but at the end of the day, you only have to answer to yourself. I thought I'd like being a motivational speaker, but at the end of the day, if someone's paying you to do something, you can never have control. I guess I could volunteer, but I'm pretty much out of money, and I really don't know what point I'm trying to get at. I guess, gay it up all you want or not or whatever." The confusing tirade seemed to baffle everyone in the room. Clancy was noticeably angry, and the crowd, though polite, clapped awkwardly and exchanged glances. Even Curt looked bothered in some third way, but I thought I had it all figured out. The old pangs were coming back. I missed the high. I realized that if I chased it again, all the problems would come back with it, but as long as I got that high, I didn't care. I needed the crowds and the fanfare. I needed my old fucking agent. JANUARY 1, 2009 The diner was fitted with drab colors that wouldn't offend the elderly, but the over-yellow lighting made the whole place feel somehow sad and droopy. I hadn't completely sobered up from drinking the rubbing alcohol, but I was getting closer, sipping a plain, black coffee, and it finally set in that she was really there, sitting across the table from me. I wouldn't have been drinking coffee if she wasn't there. I hated coffee. "I can't believe we just did that," Dina smiled, but it was hollow, and even after all those years, I could tell she could believe it, but she was starting to regret it. "I'm sorry I hit you." "I had it coming." I didn't know how I'd feel if I ever saw Dina again. Looking at her, none of that had changed. "I guess I owe you an apology. I shouldn't have left you there. I don't know why I even let you come along with me. I guess I just thought I wouldn't have gone otherwise." "I accept your apology." "Didn't know what?" "I was pregnant." She hadn't even waited for the waitress to leave. I looked at the waitress. The waitress looked away and walked off. "Why didn't you tell me?" My brain was sending off emotions so fast that all of them kind of leaked out at the same time. I didn't know if anything I was feeling was real, and if it was, I was sure it wouldn't be lasting. "I never would have left Arbuckle if I knew I'd gotten you pregnant. I would've tried." "That's kind of why I didn't tell you." She scrunched up her face like she was bracing for me to hit her. "You didn't get me pregnant." "Wait. What?" It wasn't the most difficult equation I had ever solved, but in my head, I'd never checked over my own math so many times. "What the hell do you mean?" "Johnny," she appealed, obviously taken aback that this news had hit me so gravely. "We both partied a lot. I mean, I slept with a lot of guys back then. I thought you were sleeping around, too." "Whose kid is it?" I tried to slow my breathing and ignore the pain that stabbed me right in the gut from learning Dina was unfaithful to me. I just focused on the burning guilt that welled in the back of my head since she was right about my infidelity. "Scott Finch." "Which one's Scott Finch?" "The kid with the bowl cut." I nodded, for the simple sake of nodding. I had no idea what she was talking about. We just kind of sat there after that, expecting the discomfort to subside, or at least bay itself. It grew like fire.I didn't feel like drinking any more coffee. "Are you okay?" "Yeah," I lied, so transparently that neither of us cared to question my response. "Your son. What's his name?" That one caught her off guard. She paused, like she had to think about the answer, but she was really thinking about something else. "I named him after you." She sniffed and rubbed her eyes and everything was starting to pour out of her, too. "I didn't love Scott, you know. We were all just so young and angry, and I didn't know what I was doing. He used to root for you when you were wrestling on TV. Maybe, when the time is right, it'd be nice for you to meet him." "Yeah," and this time it wasn't a lie. It wasn't anything more than a sound muttered through hands that clasped in front of my mouth. "Are you sure you're okay?" "I said I was fine." "I mean, are you okay in general." She tilted her head to the side and looked at me. I recognized what I saw in her eyes. It was distant sympathy, the kind you get from strangers. "When I came to your door today, you were a mess." "I've been messier." "Have you?" She frowned when I didn't answer, keeping her eyes on me staring at the table as she dug around in her purse. She slid an envelope across the table. I opened it up and found the check I left her. I laughed to myself, not at anything funny. I read over my address on the top left corner and realized that I wanted her to know where to find me, and hoped she never actually would look. "I think I should go." She pulled her coat over her shoulders and grabbed her purse. "Take care of yourself, Johnny." I stayed there for a while, not drinking coffee. JANUARY 3, 2009 It was still a ways to Miami, and I was getting tired there watching yellow lines, one after another, roll underneath me. I fought back tears to keep myself awake. My body was tired, and I'd been doing a fair share of punishing my insides. I had stayed away from alcohol since I had run out. I didn't need it, or I couldn't have it. After Dina left, I felt more real than I'd felt in a long time. I had been numb to everything, but that's what life does. I felt like there really wasn't any better way to deal with it. I never would have destroyed myself, physically or mentally, if I could feel what it was doing to me. I was probably in no condition to wrestle, but I needed the money, and more than the money, I needed the fix. I needed to step into the ring and forget that all my problems were surmounting outside the ropes. I needed the lights and the music and the pyrotechnics and the crowds cheering for me, even if they were cheering for a character that wasn't me. Life was easy when it let me pretend to be Garbage Bag Johnny. I envied him. His life was simple. I wished I could become Garbage Bag Johnny permanently, but once the cameras stopped rolling and the seats cleared, he didn't exist. I was all that was left, and I didn't know who I was. I knew who Garbage Bag Johnny was. He was everything I wanted to be, but unfortunately, I was much more. "Are we almost there?" The child's voice rose from sleep, and I looked at him curled up in the passenger seat. He looked just like her. "Not quite," I replied. "We've still got a lot of road in front of us." "It's so cool that my mom's letting me do this." "Sure, kid." "Now this is getting interesting." The hairs on the back of my neck stood up as Black Donald's voice hit them from the backseat.
