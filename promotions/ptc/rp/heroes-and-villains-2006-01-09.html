Heroes and Villains

9 January 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2006-01-09 Once upon a time, if you'd asked Doreen Pepperpot to describe a winner, her eyes would probably have glazed over as she unwrapped a Twinkie, lay back on her bed and she'd maybe have said; "A winner is someone graceful, powerful and beautiful. A winner looks like they belong at the top. They have this, like, air about them, you know? And they're always gracious too. You don't mind losing if it's to someone you know you can respect." Now, she thought about all the winners she'd idolised in her life. The brawny sports stars; wrestlers, football players, swimmers, the lot. She pictured a man, a montage image of her heroes, stood tall on a podium as waves of sound, applause, washed over him. He smiled and waved to the crowd, almost embarrassed by their adulation. He looked happy. He looked content. His long hours of training and sacrifice having finally paid off. Doreen held this image behind her eyes for a few moments. Was she all those things too? She could definitely now call herself a winner three rounds into GTT5 with two victories behind her. But was that enough? Sure, she had trained for years, but not with a goal in mind. It was mostly for fun and partly to please Coach. There were only two things he seemingly excelled at; one was cooking, the other teaching the skills involved in wrestling. Doreen had been happy to make the most of both. But she wondered if she were a cheat in some way. She had never before desired to win at wrestling. She'd known she was better than her entire high school team put together, but had never even considered trying out. All she'd ever wanted was a quiet life free from taunting and the disgusted looks of strangers, something quite impossible when you're the biggest and bulkiest person in two counties. Yet somehow she had come to be splashed across the front page of the local press, and not for her size, but her achievements. She felt something new inside her, something she as yet had no name for. So how had this come to be? How had a morbidly obese girl, a girl with no desire to win, no desire to be ever step into the public eye, no desire to change her sad existence, come to be one of such a prestigious group; one of the 32 remaining contestants in wrestling's golden event? How indeed. Doreen pulled on her overcoat and stepped outside. The garden was bare this time of year; held frozen in Winter's barren grip. Her breath hung about her in a fog as she stood there, almost content. It should have been a perfect time for her, stood as she was in that blissful space equidistant between matches. A space where the pain of the last has faded and the anxiety for the next has yet to build. But it was not in Doreen's nature to feel happy unless she could first ensure that those she loved were too. She sat down with a thump on a faded plastic deckchair, a forgotten relic from the summer months. Folding her arms tightly across her over-sized bosom to preserve the heat, she focused her mind on the question that seemed to forever nag at her these days; what was she to do? She had been so sure that the salvation of her small family depended somehow on GTT5, but again, self doubt filled her. How could a wrestling tournament possibly fix Coach, her only friend? How could it be positive for her 15 year old sister? How could she justify all the lies she told her mother? "Yes Mom, every thing's fine. Sharifa has a ballet recital on Thursday… Oh, I'll definitely video it for you… School's okay, I had a tough biology exam a few days ago, but I think I'll get an A, maybe a B if Mr. O' Riordan decides to be an ass with the marking… Mom! Saying 'ass' is not swearing!… So how's the book tour?… Really? An extra two countries?… Sure that's great publicity for you. I understand… I miss you too Mom." It was the last one that always got her the most. She didn't miss her mother, didn't love her. How hard it was for "Moby Doreen" knowing that her own mother, diet guru Gillian Gervais, shuddered with loathing every time she caught sight of a roll of fat sticking out over the top of her daughter's elasticated waistband. It had made for a difficult relationship. A shaky one that had finally dissolved with the announcement by team of specialists that Doreen would never be thin. The bombshell that it was not puppy fat, but a severe hormone imbalance that had led to Doreen's exponential expansion. The death knell had been discovering that if her mother had sought help when the problem first appeared instead of shirking conventional medicine and subjecting her eldest daughter to a rigorous exercise program and torturous diet plan, then Doreen might well have grown up normal. Doreen and her sister would have been effectively alone from then on if it hadn't been for Coach. A broken man, a once near famous wrestler, fresh from a long inpatient stay in a most unpleasant psychiatric unit, he had been cheap to hire with the aim of whipping Doreen into shape. When Gillian's public appearances and motivational seminars started taking her further and further from home, he took over in the sisters' life in all the important ways. It was him they turned to with their problems and petty grievances. He was the one who baked fairy cakes for the school fete. He was the one Doreen wanted to make proud. She almost screamed with the frustration of it all. Here she was, being the person Coach wanted to her to be and there he was… not noticing. Coach was digging through a pile of junk in the garage. His fingers were white and stiff with cold, but he didn't pay attention to their ache. He pulled the guts of a radio controlled model car from the tangle of twisted metal in front of him, appraised it and tossed it aside. He muttered some unintelligible thing to himself and continued his frenzied search. Doreen could see him from her seat. It was why she choose to sit there in the first place, to be outside in the freezing night air. She had to make sure he was… alright; as alright as a man almost drowning in a psychotic matrix can be. She eased her frame up and made her way towards him. He glanced at her once briefly and then resumed his work. "Coach? Hey, how are you?" There was no answer. Doreen could see him struggling to order his thoughts, to dampen the voices enough to respond to her question, but after a short anguished moment, he simply blinked rapidly at her and turned his face, and his focus, away from her. Doreen sighed. It had been getting worse this past week. Was it too late? Would he come through this and be himself ever again? What if she got him help now? It was useless. She'd run through the scenarios in her head often enough to know that there was no right answer. She knew how much hospital and psychotropic medications had damaged him the last time. She'd heard the steel in his voice when hidden in his jokes he'd as much as said he'd kill himself if he ever went back there. She was only seventeen for God's sake! How was she supposed to make a decision like that? Frustrated, she counted slowly from one hundred back. Calm yourself Doreen. Focus on something else. Focus on Sharifa. No, she wasn't talking to Doreen anymore; just shouting "Bitch!" every time she walked by. Don't focus on Sharifa. Too upsetting. Focus on the tournament. Focus on the good things. "Hey Coach. What do you make of me now? I'm a winner… I think. Like one of the good guys on TV or something. I defeated Butterfly Hamada, all by myself." Doreen giggled and put on her best ring announcer voice. "Doreen Pepperpot! Undefeated in every singles match she's bothered to show for. Defending the American Way from the onslaught of Butterfly Hamada, an… uhm, evil from Communist Japan!!" Her brow creased in confusion. Was Japan a communist country? Or was it just China? It irritated her that her school focused purely on American History and left her floundering in the dark when it came to knowing anything constructive about the rest of the world. Her school also hadn't taught her much about the proper etiquette when presented with one of the most notorious drug dealers in the western states. Doreen had been scared to the point of losing her very healthy appetite and stiffened at the very mention of his name prior to meeting Brian Arson in the ring. Strangely, it had been Sharifa who had pulled her through. She'd cajoled and encouraged her. She'd jumped up and down and screeched like a cheerleader on crack. What Doreen took to be a ringside show of faith in her had been the key to unlock her frozen muscles and allow her to move in on her opponent and fight the toughest match of her short career. "Doreen Pepperpot! The Beauty and the Beast in one! The teen who singlehandedly destroyed Brian Arson, the meanest son of a bitch this side of the room!" As Doreen warmed up to her game, she became more animated, more expansive. "Doreen Pepperpot! Wonderful! Fabulous! How will she fare when she meets the last of the gypsy rovers, the awesome Paddy O' Shea!!" Something clicks. Insanity is not a fixed thing. And neither is lucidity. Both are fluid and intertwine about each other. A sane man will often utter nonsensical rubbish and occasionally, the deranged will spew forth gems of wisdom. Coach, when well, will describe his madness as an infinite railway, laid in an ever increasing spiral. As his train of thought travels faster and further along this track, barely in control, so his delusions and psychotic visions intensify. Traveling at such a rate with forces pushing and pulling in all directions puts such stress on a thing that it may well twist further and break. It needs rest to survive. It needs 'down time'. The mind needs periods of lucidity if only to better recognize the insanity. Coach thinks of these fleeting moments of clarity as station stops along the ride. Catch the train in the station, and you might be surprised. With venom dripping from every word, he spat, "Paddy O' Shea's not a wrestler. He's a terrorist!" Shocked by the sudden outburst of a man she'd thought vacant, Doreen lost her balance and fell backward against the wall. Pinned there with surprise, eyes wide with shock, she listened to her mentor's rant. "He's a dirty terrorist! That's what I call any man who kills innocents in the name of a cancered cause! And killed he has. He admits having been a member of the IRA, he as much as admits that he's murdered. That he was young and stupid is no excuse for his actions. That he feigns hanging his head in shame on occasion is no cause to offer him forgiveness. Would you forgive the Al Queada as easily? When our president said he would hunt down and deliver justice to terrorists the world over, he didn't plan taking that justice to Ireland. It's a sad state of affairs that Ireland will forever be viewed through the rosiest of tinted glasses by this great nation. He admits he's a murderer, and yet he can walk free, wrestle freely on American soil." Spittle flew from Coach's lips as he paced the small garage, his eyes focused somewhere behind his eyelids. "Perhaps the 'Cause' was once the romantic adventure it's seen to be. Back before the nail bombs. Back before the tit for tat guerrilla warfare became an everyday occurance. Back before the IRA controlled all the drug dealing in cities north and south of the border with an iron fist. Back before the armed bank robberies, aided by guns bought and gifted by foolish Americans with a yearning for an Irish heritage. Back before the loan sharking, the intimidation, the crippling knee capping's. The romance was surely there back in the days of Collins and De Valera… perhaps the romance died with Collins? Because what's left now is anything but romantic…" "And I'm not talking just about the IRA here. I'm talking about all those who choose violence and lies and bloody death as the imagined path to freedom, Catholic or Protestant; IRA or UVF. And yes, those British soldiers who choose to meat out a twisted "justice" outside of the law. And yes, those government officials who turned a blind eye so often they could have qualified for free guide dogs. All of them have blood on their hands, dried into the nail bed, crusted between the ridges and whorls of their finger prints. There's enough blame to go round. But what I don't hear Paddy O' Shea saying is that none of them were right to kill and maim. None of them are heroes or soldiers. Just butchers." Doreen sobbed and almost hid behind her hands. She wasn't sure why she was crying, but as Coach's words burned themselves into her brain, she cried harder. She heard herself speak, "But he's a… a freedom fighter!" "One man's terrorist is another mans freedom fighter? Fuck off! A Terrorist's a terrorist no matter who's kid he's killing. Go to Basra and you'll see the Al Queda lauded and celebrated in the exact same way as the Irish Republican Army are in Belfast." "The IRA have in their day ripped the life from hundreds of people. The whole of England, and southern Ireland -yes, they killed fellow Catholics too- was truly terrified. Back then, when Paddy was just starting out, on his first handgun and box of bullets perhaps, you could walk into a pub on a Sunday, order a pint, have a nice roast pork dinner with crispy crackling. Maybe catch up with some mates. Maybe play on the fruit machines. Maybe be blown apart by a homemade pipe bomb. It happened, and happened far too often. The only difference between what Osama Bin Laden did to the Twin Towers and what the IRA have done these last few decades, is that the IRA took their fucking time with the killing" "Paddy O' Shea is a terrorist. And if he ever feels sorry for his past, then it is only that he is sorry for himself, for how he turned out. A washed up rage filled drunk! I doubt he ever feels sorry for those he hurt, those he murdered. If he did… if he did…" Coach's eyes blurred under a film of tears that promised to soon spill down his cragged face. "…then he'd feel like I do now."
