Born of Dreams

19 December 2005

Date: 2005-12-19 The night air was sweetly thick, promising rain, as Hezekiah and his wife, Freyda, made their weary return home. Pitch darkness cocooned the countryside, as it always did away from the sterile illumination of town, but their eyes had long since adjusted, allowing them to glimpse the silhouettes of farmhouses against the horizon. Beyond the clopping of their horses’ hooves and the grating of their wagon’s wheels along the gravel road, the rising wind and chirping crickets were the only sounds. Freyda nuzzled her head against her husband’s shoulder, enjoying the peace. She would have drifted to sleep, but the acrid smell of one of the cigarettes Hezekiah had purchased on a whim kept her awake. Normally they’d have been in bed hours earlier, but the harvest had just finished, ushering in the celebration of the Mennonite Relief Sale. It happened twice per year, in the spring and fall, a sort of carnival for their people to sell and barter what they’d made, and to pitch a final hurrah before the harsh winter set in. Good money was made from the townspeople, who loved the hand-woven quilts and carvings they displayed. Were it not for that, Freyda doubted she’d let Hezekiah drag her out for all three days of the affair, arriving each morning before dawn to get everything in order and leaving only once everyone else had sensibly withdrew to their beds. They didn’t need the money for anything in particular, but it was nice to have it tucked away in case of emergency. A sharp bang far up the road made her jerk, startled, but before she could wonder what it was, Hezekiah told her, whispering "A car," then offering calming hushing noises he pretended were for the horses. Another bang as the driver’s door slammed, followed by the muted roar of the engine. "I’m not blind," she snapped quietly, keeping her voice down out of respect for the night rather than fear of being overheard. "Who could it be? What would they be doing at this hour?" Hezekiah re-loosened his grip on the reins, leaning into the wooden back of his seat as he took another drag from his smoke. "I’m not psychic," he responded dryly, earning himself a glare he affected not to notice. The taillights blazed, like a pair of bloody fireflies, then zigzagged to the middle of the road and shrunk rapidly. "But they’re in a hurry." Fredya snorted. "They must have smelled you coming." "Or maybe—" Hezekiah began his robotic retort, but a wearied "Would you two please give it a rest?" erupted from within the wagon, cutting him off. Husband and wife exchanged a look, a smile. "Go to sleep, Mary," Freyda ordered softly, "we’ll wake you when we get home." They rode without speaking for a minute, again enjoying the tranquility, but it couldn’t last. Squinting into the darkness, cocking his head as if to find a better scrutinizing angle, Hezekiah wondered aloud, "What is that?" Freyda leaned over, trying to follow his gaze. "What?" "Where the car was, off to the side," Hezekiah said, nodding his head, "hanging from the . . . oh Jesus!" Before she could figure out what was going on, Freyda was thrown back against her seat. Hezekiah was on his feet, shouting at the horses to move, guiding them into the slight ditch. Mary squealed as the wagon almost toppled, but Freyda barely noticed. Whatever fear had gripped her husband had diffused into her. She shouted his name sharply, but he ignored her. As she grabbed ahold of the armrest to stop the jostling she saw why, and the sight turned her tongue to ash. There was a man hanging from the tree, swaying like a pendulum. They were almost atop him now. Freyda clutched at her husband’s coat just as he called "Woah! Woah!", yanking on the reins. The horses whinnied and tried to back away from the dead body, but Hezekiah kept the reins tight, bounding over the barricade and teetering on one of their backs. Freyda watched, frozen and trembling, as Hezekiah pulled a knife from his pocket and began sawing at the rope, hugging the man with his other arm and lifting him to release the pressure. The man wasn’t moving. His forehead gleamed dully with blood, and his mouth was open, his tongue protruding. "What’s going on?" Mary cried from the back. "Mom?" Freyda couldn’t think. "Quiet!" "Get over here!" Hezekiah shouted frantically, his arm moving in a blur. "Both of you!" "Mom?" Mary squeaked, her voice wavering uncertainly. Freyda was clambering over the barricade and onto the horse, trying to crawl across it to her husband. "On the gr—" Hezekiah began to shout in exasperation, but the rope snapped, and the weight of the body crumpling to the earth pitched him down atop it. Freyda tried to climb down and also fell, just as Mary came around the side of the wagon. Hezekiah was already on his feet, pitching his knife to the ground as he lunged for his daughter. Mary quailed, but he grabbed her forearm and yanked her forward, toward the body. "What are you doing?" both women demanded in unison, Mary frightened, Freyda appalled. "Save him!" Hezekiah bellowed. Freyda leapt forward and grabbed Mary’s other arm, swinging herself between them. "He’d dead, Hezekiah!" she shrieked. "He can’t be saved! Leave Mary alone!" Mary finally spotted the body and screamed, shrinking into her mother. Hezekiah turned his crazed glare on his Freyda. "She took the classes," he growled. "She knows what to do!" "No!" Mary sobbed. "I only saw it once, I—" "Save him!" "Hezekiah!" "Do you want him to die?" "He’s already dead!" "How do you know that?! It might not be too late! M—" "Stop!" Mary’s shrieked entreaty echoed in the following silence as her parents stared at her. "Just stop!" She sniffled, drawing herself up, then inched her way over to the body, shuddered as she knelt in the dirt beside it. The corpse wasn’t moving whatsoever. No chest movement, no twitching, nothing. Trembling, Mary bent over him and wiped the blood away from his mouth, then looked desperately to her father, who took his wife’s hand and nodded encouragement, his face pallid. Stifling her sobs, Mary tried to remember what she’d learned, but her memory was quicksand. Mechanically she forced his head back, placing her palm on his forehead and pinching his nose shut, resting her other hand on his chest. She tried to ignore the fact that there was no heartbeat as she bent in, pressing her mouth to his, visualizing life flowing into him with her breath. "Pump his ch—umph." An elbow into Hezekiah’s ribs cut his advice short. Mary crossed her hands over the dead man’s chest and forced all her weight down, once, twice, three times. She paused, then didn’t it again, panicked that she wasn’t doing it right, that she wouldn’t be able to save him. She offered him more breath, again tried to kickstart his heart, but he remained as they’d found him, dead. Mary turned to her parents, her face streaked with ever-renewing tears. "I can’t . . . He . . ." She screamed and scrambled towards them as a coughing fit surged behind her. The corpse was on its side, wheezing and gasping, clawing at the rope around its neck. "Dear Lord," Hezekiah offered in thanks, then slid to the dirt beside the revived, telling him it was okay, that it was a miracle. Freyda took her daughter into her arms and kissed the crown of her head, stammering with pride. "It’s okay, son," Hezekiah huffed, almost breathless himself. "Here, let me help you with that." The recently deceased acquiesced as Hezekiah untied the slipknot of the noose. The man flopped onto his back, still wheezing, as Hezekiah discarded the rope. "Are you alright?" he asked, crawling to kneel beside him. The man blinked at him, then squinted. Hezekiah laughed nervously. "Right. Sorry. Stupid question." Running a hand through his hair, Hezekiah laughed again, this time with relief. "It’s a miracle we came across you when we did." He half-turned to his daughter, smiling. "Mary saved your life." The man tried to smile at her, but it came out a grimace. "Thank you," he managed hoarsely. "Mary’s my daughter," Hezekiah went on excitedly. "And this is my wife Freyda. I’m Hezekiah." He extended his hand to the man, nodding in anticipation of the return. "And you are?" The man blinked again, then licked his lips. His brow constricted. "I . . . He was used to the daily tedium of the chores, but he hated every grating second of them. Memories he couldn’t pin down told him country air was supposed to be the freshest, but the place stank, especially inside the barns. Worse, the people living there were content to never leave, scrunching their nose at the very thought. Feeding the birds was the first thing on his schedule this morning, and it would also be the last, as the sun set. Tearing open the next bag, he couldn’t believe how much they ate. He hadn’t even eaten yet. He still had to milk the cattle before he could sit down to breakfast. He emptied the last bag and went to leave, then stopped, jaw tightening, at the sound of all the turkeys rushing him. He turned back to face them and they went still, then retreated slowly, eyeing him stupidly. Grumbling, he went to leave again, and once more he heard the rapid shuffling of their feet approaching. It was the same thing every time he came around. They were meek as could be whenever he had his eye on them, even frightened, but as soon as his back was turned, they charged, as if they might overpower him unsuspecting and – what? What did they think they could do? Upgrade their menu and live like kings for a day? He glared at them over his shoulder, and naturally, they stopped in their tracks, backed up. In the past it had amused Abraham, but he wasn’t in the mood for it this morning. The night before hadn’t gone at all as he’d planned. Instead of fun and relaxation he’d found nothing but more questions, and an army of gawkers that insisted on treating him like a celebrity. Supposedly he was. He hadn’t seen the man – himself – but John was certain, as were the rest of the bar patrons. Some wrestler. The pinnacle of the industry, the epitome of refined violence. Those were some of the phrases bandied about concerning him – who he apparently was, at least. Abraham wasn’t so sure. He didn’t feel like a world-class athlete. He didn’t feel like the entire wrestling universe was hunting him, measuring him with crosshairs to shoot their instant stardom. He felt like a farmer, overtired and hung-over. Walking over to the wall, hackles rising as the turkeys renewed their storming, he grabbed a steel-bladed shovel. Alright you fucking birds, he thought with an acidic smile, let’s see what you think you can do. "What are you doing?" Abraham jumped, turning to the door. He hadn’t heard Hezekiah enter. The patriarch was eyeing the shovel dubiously, clutched in Abraham’s hands like a battleaxe. Clearing his throat, Abraham returned it to the wall and shuffled toward the door, trying to bypass Hezekiah. "Going to milk the cows." "Hold on," Hezekiah said, stopping him with a hand on his chest. Abraham stopped, meeting his measuring eyes. Hezekiah cocked his head. "Angelo Deville, huh?" Abraham heaved a sigh, thinking of how he’d have to explain the finer nuances of secrecy to John before the day was out. "That’s what I’m told." The older man grunted. "Sounds made up to me." Shrugging noncommittally, Abraham tried to move past him again, but Hezekiah held firm. "So you’re a wrestler?" He frowned, puzzling it over. "I guess I should have known," he went on thoughtfully, poking Abraham in the ribs, suddenly smiling slightly, "what with your muscles being for show rather than work." "I don’t know if I am or not," Abraham snapped, ignoring the attempted jest. "John swears it was me, but . . . I don’t know. Given the circumstances, it doesn’t quite fit." Hezekiah nodded. "True. I doubt wrestlers getting abducted or hanged is a regular occurrence." Something tickled the back of Abraham’s mind. "Actually . . . you might be surprised." Scrutiny overcame Hezekiah’s face. "So you do remember something?" Abraham shook his head. "No. I don’t know. It’s still the same as yesterday, and the day before, and the day before. Nothing new." Nodding sympathetically, Hezekiah asked, "So what now?" "I’m going to milk the cows." Hezekiah laughed. "I meant about the rest of it. You know . . . your life. Aren’t you curious?" Silence stretched for a long moment as Abraham studied the ground. When he looked up, his face was schooled to stone. "My life, whatever it was, got me hanged and lost me my every memory, as if I’d never lived at all. It thrilled her having Abraham there, doubly so due to the need for concealment from her family, the requirement for silence. The mere principle of it was exciting, and the practice was beyond anything she’d ever imagined. For Abraham, not so much. He enjoyed it, revelled in it, but it didn’t make his world. It was through Mary’s grace that he was still alive, and that created an instant bond between them, but beyond that . . . to say Abraham thought her partially insane would be a gross understatement. He didn’t know what Hezekiah would do upon discovering he’d deflowered his daughter, but Abraham gleefully embraced the risk, the only tinge of excitement blessing the doldrums of his new life. She believed they were to be married – he’d had to insist upon it before she would submit to his advances – and originally he’d strongly considered it, but with each passing day the inevitable misery of such a union crashed down a little more. She was . . . "I’m pregnant," she cooed, nuzzling her ear against his shoulder. Abraham felt like he’d been punched in the stomach, then he laughed and hugged her close. "Don’t be silly," he scolded with good humour. "You remember the talk we had about condoms, right?" "They’re not foolproof," she pouted, gazing up at him through her lashes, her lower lip protruding. "No," he agreed, "they’re not. But the odds . . ." "I don’t care about the odds," she insisted, almost forgetting to whisper. "I dreamt it." Abraham stifled the urge to eye her askance. She took his silence as acceptance, cuddling closer to him. "I can feel it growing inside me, like it was planned all along. A woman knows these things." They lay in silence for a long time, Mary blissful, Abraham wanting to crawl out of his skin. He couldn’t remember whether or not they were all like this, but he sincerely hoped not. At least her pitiable delusion had helped him make up his mind. "I’m leaving first thing in the morning," he stated, trying to infect his voice with regret. Mary gasped. "But—" "I need to find out who I was – who I am – and I can’t do that here, with you." He brushed her cheek with a kiss when she sat up to stare at him, offering a look of false sympathy. "I hope you understand." Biting her lower lip, she nodded, then collapsed onto him, burying her head against his chest. "You’ll come back for me, won’t you?" The Deville smiled. "Of course."
