Experience Counts

8 October 2004
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2004-10-08 ‘Life is too short for this…’ Just a few days removed from defeating his Round Two opponent in the Golden Turnbuckle Tournament, Brian Calvin found himself, surprisingly, having a case of extreme doubts. Severe doubts. Doubts about the tournament, his wrestling career, and, as fate would have it, his whole life. It was almost funny; most people who won their Round Two matches were experiencing one of the biggest highs of their careers. A co-worker from the WWA, Mr. Darkness, had overcome all odds and defeated one of the most highly regarded wrestlers in the tournament, Inphino Blitz. Many of the other wrestlers were having similar results! Hell, Brian could almost see the confidence seeping out of the skin of the remaining competitors, inflating their egos even more then he thought was possible. And what was there to make someone unconfident? The odds had sure seemed a lot more intimidating when one hundred and ninety-two names were on the bracket instead of forty-eight. This made it all the more unusual that Brian would choose this particular time to question his own abilities. But Brian’s second round match-up with Gonzalo Rey, even though he had ended up winning, had forcefully opened his eyes to a situation that he had been consciously avoiding in his past few months back in action. ‘I’m just not as young as I used to be.’ And that was a bit of an understatement. Brian had turned forty years old about a month earlier, just before he made his return to professional wrestling. Before that he had been sidelined for over two and a half years due to a knee injury that many doctors told him he would never recover from. The first few weeks back in the ring were like he had never left; he had wrestled brilliantly. For five straight weeks he put on a show in the World Wrestling Alliance. His record spoke for itself: four wins to one loss. Notable wins included a surprising victory over the red hot Muppet Man, who was the current United States Champion, and a win in a free for all battle royal against over twenty other competitors. His lone loss came against the very capable JuceMystA, the Television Champion at the time. Brian had thought he was at the top of the game. When the opportunity came to sign up for the PTC singles tournament he didn’t even hesitate to throw his name in the hat. His first round win against Miles Jerni was another highlight for him, but it was the last time he had felt confident after a wrestling match. He remembered waking up the morning after he had defeated Gonzalo Rey. It wasn’t one of his better moments. ‘Daddy, daddy!’ It was a cold autumn day the morning after his wrestling match, and he hadn’t got much sleep. It had always been difficult for him to fall asleep after a tough contest; his adrenaline was always up for at least three or four hours afterwards, but on that particular night sleep had come exceedingly slow. Going a million miles an hour for the past month and a half had finally taken its toll on his rusty old body. And he REALLY wasn’t ready for interruptions when his bedroom door burst open that morning. But, for some odd reason, his two little boys had decided that seven o’ clock in the morning was a great time to play with their dad. The little tikes scampered into the room at full speed and leapt onto Brian’s bed, laughing and giggling the way one would expect kids their age to act. ‘Come on, daddy! Come out and play wrestling with us!’ ‘Mommy’s in the kitchen making breakfast, dad. It’s time to rise and shine!’ Brian turned over onto the bed, pulling the pillows over his face. Brian’s voice was very groggy as he replied, ‘Okay. Ryan… Cade… You boys go back out in the kitchen with mom and I’ll be up in a few minutes. Daddy just needs to have a few minutes to get dressed, all right?’ ‘Okay, dad!’ Both of his boys nodded excitedly and hopped off the bed, hustling back out into the living room as quickly as they had arrived. ‘Ah, shit…’ Brian groaned to himself after he was alone. He rolled back over onto his back and stared at the ceiling, contemplating just how he was going to get himself out of bed. Brian had had a few bad mornings in his career, and he could tell that this one was going to be a doozey. He scooted as close as he could to the edge of his bed and paused, wondering what his first step should be. Finally deciding, he reached out with his right hand and grabbed the nightstand for support. Pushing back on the bed with his left hand and straining equally hard with his right, Brian started to pull his torso up into a sitting position. The step sounded simple, but in practice? It was no easy task. The pain in his back… His shoulders… It was excruciating. ‘Oh lord, oh lord…’ Brian mumbled the phrase over and over as he slowly inched his upper body up, droplets of sweat beginning to form on his forehead. After he finally managed to get into a relaxed sitting position he hunched over, sighing in pure relief. It was then that he became aware enough to notice his increased breathing rate and the moisture glistening on his bare chest. The humiliation was worse then the actual task had been. But the struggle wasn’t over. Brian swung his legs slowly over to the side of the bed. Bracing himself, anticipating the pain that he knew was awaiting him, he gripped the edge of the bed and forced himself to his feet in one quick motion. ‘AAAHH, damn!’ He stumbled forward, both hands pressed to the small of his back. The pain had been short, but intense, and he couldn’t keep it bottled up inside. He shook his head stupidly; the last thing he wanted to do was to alert his wife that he was in pain. ‘Honey?’ He heard her approaching voice from the hallway and sighed to himself. When your wife doesn’t work and your paycheck depends on your ability to wrestle a two hundred and fifty pound man, you don’t really want people to know that you couldn’t manage to get out of bed in the morning. She poked her head into the bedroom. ‘Is everything all right, Brian?’ She gave him a once over and quickly noticed the pained expression on his face. ‘Brian?’ She repeated. He frowned, shaking his head at his wife. ‘Rachel, I need to see a doctor.’ Doctors weren’t some of Brian’s favorite people, but he knew the situation was pretty important. If he wanted to be up and running by the time he was scheduled to face his Round Three opponent, Craig Goat, he would have to store away his dislike and find out what the problem was and what he could do to fix it. Which he did. It turned out that Brian had suffered a pinched nerve in his match against Gonzalo Rey, and it was this that had been causing him such pain in his back and shoulders. Recovery time was going to be minimal; a few days of rest and a prescription of pain medication would take care of the problem well before his Round Three match. This prognosis was much more minor then Brian had initially thought his situation was going to be, but it didn’t raise his spirits at all. If anything, it had lowered them even more. The fact was, and Brian was finally beginning to come to terms with it, was that his body couldn’t do the same things that it used to be able to do. He wasn’t twenty-five anymore; he was forty. ‘Am I too old to do this anymore?’ He wondered to himself. Brian wasn’t sure, but he had gut feeling that it might have been true. Why else would he have been sitting in a park playing chess, of all things, against his Great-Uncle Louie? It wasn’t the kind of thing that professional wrestlers at the top of their game usually did, was it? Looking at the other chess tables nearby kind of confirmed it; he had to be the only person under sixty in sight. “Uncle Louie, am I old?” Brian’s Great-Uncle was a crafty retired farmer pushing ninety years of age. But, as wrinkled and as old as he looked, his mind was sharp as it had been fifty years ago. In fact, old Lou’s personality really hadn’t changed one bit for as long as Brian could remember. Lou had always had a witty, sarcastic kind of intelligence that had always surprised Brian a little bit. For a man that had spent most of his life working on a farm he had an aura around him that gave Brian the drift that he was extremely worldly and well-educated in most subjects. So his response to Brian’s vague question was fairly typical. “You’re forty years old, numbskull. I remember, I sent you a birthday card last month. What is the world coming to when a ninety year old man needs to remind relatives half his age of their own damn birthdays?” Uncle Louie moved a pawn on the chessboard forward a space. Brian loved his Great-Uncle to death, as Old Louie had been there for Brian in his most trying days. During the long period of time where Brian was avoiding his parents the one relative that symphasized with him was his old Uncle, and Brian had never forgotten that kindness. For that period of time, some twenty years, Lou had acted as a parental figure to Brian and had allowed him to speak to him whenever Brian found himself faced with a difficult problem or a fork in the road. And even though he had recently found himself reunited with his parents, he still came to Louie for guidance during his most difficult of problems, just like the one that had been ailing him for the past week. Louie had been to every one of Brian’s matches for the past two decades, and needless to say, Brian loved him and respected ALL of his opinions. “You know what I mean, Uncle Louie. It’s just… I don’t think I’m cut out to be a professional wrestler anymore. It’s getting too hard to keep up with these young hot shots in the ring. The difference in stamina, in strength… It’s getting hard to compete.” Brian stared at the chessboard, too ashamed to look his Uncle in the eyes. Louie smirked at Brian and stretched his fragile little arms back behind his head, grinning at his great-nephew. “Brian, you have to understand something. Age is nothing; it is just a number. Look at me, I’m about a thousand years old and I’m still going strong! If I thought that my age meant anything I would have died years ago.” Brian argued, “There’s a difference between being able to live and being able to wrestle professionally, Lou. I’m not giving up on life, I’m giving up on wrestling. I wake up every morning with pain in my back, my shoulders, my legs… I can’t do it anymore!” He slammed his fist on the table, knocking off a few of his Uncle’s pieces. Without saying a word Louie leaned down and picked up the pieces, calmly replacing them on the board and allowing his great-nephew to continue uninterrupted. “My opponent for Round Three of the tournament is a fellow named Craig Goat. The guy doesn’t even have any real talent; a lot of the guys I talked to said he’s a novice in the wrestling ring. How is a guy like me, a forty-year-old-washed-up-lightweight, going to counter someone who doesn’t even need wrestling TALENT to win?” Louie took a long look at his young relative, a smirk visible on his face. “It sounds to me like you’re making excuses for yourself on why you shouldn’t win, instead of focusing on why you should and WILL. Have you become so fascinated with the idea of age, of youth, and of strength that you’ve forgotten what got you to the place you are today?” Lou casually moved another piece on the chessboard forward another square. Brian didn’t answer. He began to rub his temples quietly, suffering from a headache. Lou continued, “Here’s a newsflash for you, buddy: you didn’t win all those matches the past twenty years because you were a million times stronger, faster, or more athletic then ANYONE you wrestled. I thought you would have realized that yourself a long time ago.” Brian stared forward at the board, pretending to look for a move to make, but he was hanging on every word his Great-Uncle spoke. “You won all those matches because you were smarter then everyone else, Brian. Not only smarter; you worked your ass off everyday to become the best possible wrestler you could be! Why would you want to give up now when you’ve come so far?” Louie ran a hand through the little gray hair that he had left, and he grinned at his young relative. Brian sulkily moved forward a chess piece on the board in front of him. “You said that there is a difference between being able to simply live, which is what you insinuated is the only thing that I am doing, and being able to work at the kind of intensity we both know you do. You’re right. But I know what hard work is, Brian. I worked as a farmhand until I was sixty-five years old, and even when I was sixty I still outworked three-fourths of the guys that were employed on that farm. The aches and pains are a given, Brian, but you can’t let a little pain stop you from doing what you love. Don’t you remember back when you were thirty years old? Or even twenty-five? You used to come home after a wrestling match gimping like an old man some nights because you were in such pain. Don’t let the fact that you are forty years old make you start to lie to yourself and tell you that you had never experienced pain before.” Brian nodded his head, remembering the aches and pains he had suffered through his career, at every age. Was he talking about retiring because of a little pinched nerve or because people thought that wrestlers just magically became old and useless when they hit the age of forty? Lou moved another chess piece forward, swiping one of Brian’s bishops off the table and onto the ground before continuing, “I understand the difference between people that give everything they have each day and those that do the bare minimum to simply survive. If you were a ‘bare minimum’ kind of guy you wouldn’t have lasted five years in the wrestling business, Brian. Look at you now; in your twentieth year of wrestling, you’re at your peak. Your longevity should speak for itself. Just because you are over the hill doesn’t mean that your body is done. How do you FEEL, Brian, that’s the real question. Not, ‘how old are you’, but, ‘how do you feel’?” Brian contemplated this for several seconds and glared down at the chessboard. ‘Of course,’ he thought. He said, “I feel… I feel, well, I feel as good as I was ten years ago.” Lou grinned at his great-nephew, and he added, “And you’re better then you were then, Brian. You’re more experienced, smarter, and just plain BETTER. And if you don’t win your match against Craig Goat, it will NOT be because you were too old.” Brian shook his head, grinning. “I don’t make it a habit to lose, Louie.” Lou stood up from the table and gestured down at the chessboard. “Then I wouldn’t advise looking down at the board, because you’ve been check-mated for about five minutes now.” And without a further word, Lou began to hobble back towards Brian’s car. Brian stared at the chessboard, looking it over, carefully. Yep, it was true. He grinned in spite of himself. He had just lost a game of chess to a ninety-year old man. ‘One of the smartest men he had ever met during his life.’ If the speech Louie had given to Brian didn’t have any effect, the chess game certainly did. Lou had proved to him that age didn’t make one damn bit of difference in competition, something Brian must have forgotten during the course of his life. If Louie could beat Brian in a game of chess as a ninety year old, then there was no reason why Brian couldn’t compete in the wrestling ring with guys half his age. As Louie had proven to Brian, experience counts, too. Brian jogged back towards his car, the sun beginning to set over the park. Experience counts.
