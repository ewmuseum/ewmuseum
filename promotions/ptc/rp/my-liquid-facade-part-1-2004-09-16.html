My Liquid Facade Part 1

16 September 2004
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2004-09-16 Every time I looked in the mirror, I could see the lines on my face getting clearer. It was much to my chagrin that the lyrics to one of my favorite songs, growing up, now applied to myself. I was far from being an old man, yet far from a youthful teenager. I'd let a dream of mine lye in the shadows as I achieved the "standard" of success set by Corporate America. My own personal fears and anxiety had left me from ever pursuing the dream on more than a minor league level. Anxiety can cripple even the strongest of men, and I suffered from two variations of the disorder. A fear of becoming famous, which was more of a fear of being looked down upon by those who viewed me as a prominent figure in my life. Combined with common everyday "stage fright," made it very difficult for me to compete in the sport that I loved so much. Those who do not understand wrestling view it only as a barbaric sport, that hosts the freaks of the world. They did not see the honor involved in the competition of grappling with the best fighters in the world on a daily basis. Professional boxers and shoot fighters fought on a monthly, sometimes yearly, basis. Wrestlers, on the other hand, competed sometimes seven days a week and twice on Sunday. With a busy everyday life like I possessed, I would never be able to keep up with a double life . . . with a facade. When I did manage to find time to compete, it was like running a marathon. Everything had to be in order. Plane tickets for New York City, and a call from a business man inviting me out for a meeting to view a "proposal" I had been working on for the past few months. In reality it was only a friend making the calls so that I would be able to partake in the one vice I had, wrestling. Even then, no one knew where I was truly going, friends more than likely thought I was cheating on my wife as most men do when they leave on "business trips.” If they only knew . . . The truth was I was banging, but only the heads of my opponents off the mat, not a beautiful woman on the side. I'm sure most would wonder how I would be able to wrestle if I was so afraid of being caught doing so, and afraid of crowds? The answer was simple, it wasn't me who would compete. It was the charismatic, hard-hitting, face-painted warrior, Facade. Once behind two layers of paint, two to ensure that it would not chip away easily, I could be my true inner self. The fighting spirit and witty banter, it was all me. Just the “me” I kept hidden except on these rare occasions. After a few years of wrestling 4-6 times a year, I began to find myself wrestling in front of more and more people on independently booked shows. My fan base began to grow, as I was an enigma who only showed up when he wanted, and never for a long period of time. This made me worry . . . and my worries were just. My own mistake of using my cell phone for bookings and work almost caught up with me when the wife was snooping around, and listening a few voice messages. She heard something she did not like. It was an independent promoter trying to get a hold of the mysterious Facade for a show coming up. After a few hours of interrogation, and a near heart attack out of fear of possibly being caught, I convinced her that it simply had to be a wrong number. I assured her that I could never wrestle due to my schedule with her and my job. As simple as that my worries, my fears, and my anxieties were laid to rest. The experience of having my secret revealed was enough to keep me out of the ring for almost two years straight. I always kept in shape by visiting a local gym, and training with the local aspiring shoot fighters or boxers. I was not some monstrous hoss who could hurl people across rooms, but I like to think that I'm stronger than your average competitor. Strength is not where my true skills lie, however. It was my speed and agility combined with my size that caught my opponents off-guard. At six foot three inches and around 250 pounds, I was far from a lightweight. I worked hard at work and at home trying to be a good husband. I did everything to keep my mind off my wrestling urges, as it was just too big a risk to be caught. My will was weak and I caved in eventually. I visited a pro-wrestling website to catch up on the wild world I'd missed for almost two years. Fights that were taken too far, powerful legends retiring, and strong promotions shutting their doors. It all seemed so surreal. The face of wrestling had changed drastically. No longer was there various national promotions such as Action! Wrestling, PCW, OSE, and various others that seemed to dominate the sport. You still had the juggernaut of wrestling, the fWo, but who was to say for how long. There were a few other things that remained consistent with wrestling. I began to read about multiple upstart promotions that rose from the ashes of the closings. Like a phoenix rising from the ashes, I could see wrestling was still in good condition. My heart felt at home as I stumbled over PTC. It was a wrestling commission of sorts, and housed the best promotions in wrestling under its banner. I’d never wrestled in a PTC-SPONSORED event, and to do so would be an honor. However, the chance would never come simply because I could not compete daily or even weekly for a long period of time for any league. A glimmer of hope filled my screen. A tournament that PTC had held in the past, one that I was always too afraid to enter, was being held once again. The GTT4 was an open invitational tournament, meaning if I had the courage . . . I could enter. Less than a week later, I found myself setting up the trip and phone call. I know some are wondering how I can just take off of work at sporadic occasions like I do . . . well, if I told you that, then you may know just a little too much. I arrived at the airport and awaited a cab at the doors. I would head to the hotel room, and wait for the first round to start. I was told of my opponent over the phone hours after arriving, and recognized the name slightly from an independent event years earlier. Joshua Taro Freedom was a decent competitor, and would be a good start. He wouldn’t be a problem . . . no not a problem at all. The only problem would be my own personal insecurities. Could even the paint mask my anxiety of performing in front of thousands of fans on national television? Could my excuses back home hide my true intentions? Would my liquid facade hold strong?
