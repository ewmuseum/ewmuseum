Contemplation

4 December 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2005-12-04 Contemplate suicide. Then, perhaps, have a cup of tea. Haven’t watched television in a long time. Might punch my neighbour for the loud music. Phone my girlfriend and tell her I’ll be late. Take a trip to the library. Not enough of the right tablets in the cabinet. Snuggle up in my duvet while it’s cold. Watch a movie and drink hot chocolate. Throw out the trash. Check my email after weeks. Take the dog for a walk in the park. Nowhere to hang the rope. Freshly cooked steak and kidney pie for dinner. The guitar hasn’t been plucked in a while. Phone home to see how they are. The dishes need to be washed. A good production at the theatre tonight. Forgot which vein to cut. Check all the doors are locked before going to sleep. A quick drink down at the bar. Yet another bill to pay. And then, contemplate suicide. And in the end, it’s not the years in your life that count. It’s the life in your years. (Abraham Lincoln) "Wake up." Huh? "For God’s sake, Armaan, wake up." Stop whispering in my ear, I wanted to tell him. "Please, Armaan, don’t die on me." I flickered open an eyelid, catching a glimpse of the referee kneeling over me. A sigh of relief spread over his wrinkled face, and I closed them again. It was all too much effort. "OK, get ready to kick out," he whispered again. A mammoth of a man piled himself on top of me, and I heard the referee drop to his knee. As he slapped the canvas with his hand, I felt my life pass before me twice. Third time’s a charm, and my arm shot up. My eyes burst open as the pain of twenty thousand roaring human beings screamed in my ears. Make it stop, came a faint plea from the farthest corner of my brain. He picked me up to my feet, and I stood frail and helpless. Something was whispered into my ear, but I refused to listen. As he grabbed my head, I tried to make another prayer to anyone listening. Make it stop, he asks again, but a brainbuster suplex onto a steel chair is all it takes to end a match, as Armaan will tell you. As he lies in the centre of the ring, barely breathing, he wonders if he will get up again. The big man falls on top of him, and the referee begins to make the count. This time, he stays down. The bell resounds around the arena, and one more period of suffering comes to an end. "Is he okay?" the referee asks, as a paramedic tends to a barely conscious Armaan. It is not his fault that the question is left unanswered; stopping the blood-flow from three different spots can be difficult. "OK, Armaan, nod if you can hear me." He is a muscular man, helped immensely by a handsome face. Black hair is combed back at just the right length, with wisps of grey hair creeping in uninvitingly. A tan line runs around the third finger of his left hand, where a wedding ring usually rests. "Can you hear me, dude?" Slowly, with as little effort possible, Armaan’s head slowly tilts up and down. His face is masked in crimson, dried stains having trickled down his eye to leave a bloody tear. He opens his eyes slowly a second time, only to be greeted by the glare of arena lights. "OK, he’s good to go." "Thank god," comes the quiet echo of a frightened referee from behind. Armaan has to hand it to the EMT’s; the stretchers are already in place for the quick getaway. But tonight is not about getaways. The Indian has little time to hear startled gasps as he jerks up to a seated position. Turning his head, he makes sense of where he is, before enduring yet another stinging headache to rise to his feet. He can imagine it now. The commentators going crazy…"even after enduring all this, Armaan is up on his feet"…"this just proves what a great athlete he is"…"no challenge is big enough for this Indian superstar"…and he can already hear the chants of god knows how many people, chanting his name. He can imagine all the guys in the back, standing up, applauding his heroics. A blood-stained hero up on his feet despite coming out the loser, truly still a winner in their eyes…"what else does Armaan have to do to prove himself?"…and then the walk back up the ramp, holding out his arms for strangers to praise him. So he raises up his arms. The noise. The chants. The applause. Every decibel of sound rushes through his ears. Every time his name is cried out, a new surge of energy pulses through his veins, and all the blood having been shed; all the pain having been endured; it all seems pointless in this moment of euphoria. For that brief moment, he is God. And then, he falls, only to wake up, my body wrapped in bandages. My eyes adjusted to the light, and I realised where I was. It was a hospital ward like any other. I tried to move, but the most I could manage to do was shift my eyes. A neutral coat of white paint surrounded me, and medical equipment rested in separate corners. Blinds covering the single window let in streaks of sunlight, producing a distorted shadow of anything and everything in the small room. I would have screamed out for someone by now, but my mouth refused to open. I tried to look down, and saw nothing but the white of the sheets covering the white of my bandages. I shifted my eyes towards the closed door. Why I thought it would open by staring at it, I did not know, but it was the last effort of a broken man. In what could only be described as an act of God, the handle twisted and the door pulled open. "Good afternoon, Mr. Singhania." He was an elderly man, a wrinkled face saved only by black-rimmed spectacles that hid his ageing eyes. He walked slowly towards me, carrying a black notepad in one hand, and a biro in the other. "You’ve been in hospital for two days now," he said quietly, a sympathetic smile on his face, "you’ve only just woken up." Two days? I tried to register my shock somehow, but nothing in my body or mind would allow me to. I had unwillingly forced myself to remain still. The thought of remaining unconscious for two days frightened me, but it was useless. I shifted my eyes, hoping the doctor would understand my distress, but he continued on in his levelled tone. Too bad. "I’m afraid you suffered quite a bad concussion while you were wrestling two days ago. From what I can see of the videotape I received, a chairshot to your head seems to have been the cause." My eyes shut momentarily, as I relived the moment my skull was cracked. I had dropped back, only to topple over the guard railings. As I felt the steel collide repeatedly against my head, a throbbing pain shot around my temple. "Unfortunately, the match did not stop there and, therefore, you suffered more blows to the head. And, as any other sufferer of a concussion will tell you, the last thing you want is more pain to the head." He paused, sighed. It was time for the worst news. "I could go on and on about how many injuries you’ve suffered, but that’s pointless. All I can say is that you need to think very carefully about what you want to do. This may only be your first concussion, but the chances of getting concussions in the future will now increase. At the least, I can tell you to take a lot of bed rest to let this heal up. And at the most…" No. "I would recommend you to stop wrestling? Not exactly what he would have wanted to hear. He sits on the couch, staring blankly at the television, his mind wandering to thoughts other than Sandy’s affair with Toby’s twin half-brother. His thoughts are a tad less melodramatic. His wounds are all but healed. A bruise above his right mound is still clearly visible, but that is a ‘battle scar’, as he so conveniently puts it. Having spent two weeks confined in his own apartment as per doctor’s orders, he passes time writing the story of his ordeal in his brain, in the hope that an excited well-wisher will position themselves in front of him and listen to his glorious tale. His epic is disturbed, unfortunately, as the mobile begins to ring beside him. "We need you in Kansas, Armaan." "Me? Why do you want me in Kansas?" "One of the guys dropped out, and we need you to fill in. It’ll be a decent crowd, thousand or so." Armaan could tell him that he was still not medically cleared. He could tell him that his head still feels light when he wakes up every morning. He could tell him that shooting pains attack his head randomly. "OK. Let me know where it is and I will be there." But another day at home is too much for him to take. It has only been four years, but everyday life is no longer television and freshly cooked meals. Life is long road trips to small arenas in distant states. Life is quick stops at diners in the middle of nowhere to buy soggy fries and overcooked cheeseburgers. Life is losing the feeling in his right foot but still managing to work the pedals on the car. Life is getting to arenas in the middle of the night, and having to sleep in the car. So, he gets up and walks over to the calendar stuck to his wall. One date is already marked clearly. His return date. "GTT5. Craig Miles." Whether the risk is worth it is a totally different matter (obviously), but he can risk a bump to the head to put on a pair of wrestling boots again. He can get out of the apartment and live life. Do what he wants to do. With a smile bordering somewhere between one of satisfaction and relief, he heads towards the kitchen. The milk carton is barely taken out of the fridge before it slips out of his fingers and falls to the ground. Armaan drops to the floor, clutching his head, screaming out in pain. Make it stop. He doesn’t know how long he will stay conscious. Doesn’t really matter, to be honest. What matters is that it is happening again. He likes to tell himself every time it happens that it is the last time. But it never is. Five seconds pass. Ten. Fifteen. Finally, his eyes begin to close. Make it stop, he had said and somebody would have shot me for doing it, but here I was in Kansas, lacing up my boots. I had made the long trip from home, lucky that the pain didn’t start up again. Having finished with my boots, I rested my head back against the bench and closed my eyes. Told myself what I was doing. You’re about to die, Armaan Singhan- "Time to go, boss." I didn’t open my eyes in time enough to see the kid run off to pass on another one of his dozen messages, so he stares around the tiny locker room, and back down at his hands. Shuffling through his bag, he pulls out a photograph of his family back in India. Bringing it up to his lips slowly, he kisses it softly. For that split-second, silence fills the world. The walk towards ringside was always the hardest part. As I paced along the corridors, a million thoughts flooded my mind. And, in the blink of an eye, they were replaced by a million more. Only one thought persisted to remain. This is no t worth it. The conscience isn’t always an easy thing to faze out of your thoughts, and Armaan isn’t having the best of times. He reaches the curtain and stops, awaiting the call of his name, before stepping out to a crowd of three hundred and seventy-two people. I stood at the top of the ramp, holding out my arms in the air. Emotion overwhelmed me and a tear trickled down my cheek. I waited for my head to rip itself apart again but all pain seems a distant cry away. He looks out, catching a glimpse of every man, woman and child staring back at him. He can see the excitement from where he is standing, and a realisation hits me. Wrestling was my cure, it was the answer to my pain. The pain felt when doing what everyone else was doing. Because, wrestling was not like everything else, wrestling is an addiction. Didn’t someone once say that life was an addiction in itself? Probably not, but Armaan realises at that exact moment that there is only one way for him to fuel his addiction; do it the way every other addict does. Live Life is nothing without a desire burning inside… it. And desire is nothing without the strength to live through the flames. (Armaan Singhania)
