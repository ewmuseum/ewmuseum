Date: 2009-12-31 Maybe You Can Polish A Turd After All: The Garbage Bag Johnny Story Chapter 5: Deadlines A Garbage Bag Production DECEMBER 4, 2009 I don't know where my focus had been up to that point, but when I regained my bearings, a puff of smoke wrapped itself around my face. There was a man opposite an oak desk from me. He was smoking a cigar, and it burned so strong I could feel its heat. A blurry veil lifted, and I took a look past the smoke and around the room. Most of the back wall was dedicated to shelf space for all sorts of books. One case held thick tomes etched with a variety of names I recognized from overhearing conversations about great books. Another more colorful shelf featured more obscure selections that appeared, by design, to be more current. The bindings were all in pristine condition as if the books had never been read. They hung on those shelves like punished works of art, uselessly turned against the wall. Sun shone brightly through the tall fixed windows. It was disorienting. The curtains had been pulled aside to disorient me, almost counter-tactically. "Good." I looked over at him for the first time as the smoke dissipated from between us, save a thin trail that rose from the ceramic ashtray where his cigar was holstered. "You're up." "What the fuck?" He looked about twice my age, worn unlike the books, and it was reassuring. His jagged face sucked the blood from the rest of his body, leaving a visible pallor on his hands and the few exposures of skin below his face. A good man, like a good book, looks tattered. A good man is tested and survives. "Where am I?" "Literal Press, Chicago office." The deep lines, even in the most distant regions of his face, flexed when he talked. His chair slowly spun clockwise as he stood from it and approached a counter beneath the shelves, reaching for a coffee pot. As he filled two mugs, I noticed his shriveled stature. He poured the mugs half full. "Regular or Irish?" "Huh?" I looked up, lifting my head like a weight, and his fingers were pinched around the neck of a bottle of Jameson. "Oh, um, better make it Irish." "Okay," he laughed, shaking his head while pouring the whiskey from one mug to the other. "To getting drunk while you sober up, right?" "I guess so." My head was heavy and hard to balance, and I was in no mood for polite laughter. "You wouldn't happen to know how I ended up here, would you?" "I'm hoping you didn't drive." He laughed again, balancing the mugs in hands that shook steadily. He placed the drinks down on the large oak desk and slid one to me. "You were asleep outside the building when I got here this morning, and I gotta say, the bosses were pretty pissed off about it, but I went to bat for you and really saved your ass. Now it's your turn to save mine." I was unenthusiastic about the prospect of returning some ambiguous favor I hadn't asked for in the first place, but there was just something about him, some uncertain familiarity perhaps, that commanded my attention. "They were going to drop your autobiography without even reading that draft you had in your hand, but I looked it over and thought it had some real potential." He reached into his desk drawer, pulled my notebook from it, and tossed it on his desk. "I should've kept my big mouth shut, though. Now they've got me editing the piece, and if it turns out to be a pile of shit, it's my fucking job on the line." "But you said it was good, right?" I asked over the rim of my drink. "I said it had potential." He corrected me, knocking the bottom of his mug against the desktop to punctuate his point. "It's not bad so far, but it could get better, and if the ending sucks, none of it matters anyway." "What's wrong with it?" I didn't know much about writing books and such, but I thought I had done a decent job with it so far. "Well, there's really not as much about your wrestling career as we thought there would be, but that's forgivable." He leaned back and crossed his legs. "After all, people are more interested in who you're fucking than what you do, nowadays, but aren't you back wrestling in some kind of tournament that's supposed to be a big deal?" "Yeah, but I don't want to write about it." I returned his pose, and the more I leaned, the more he leaned in response. It was like looking at someone else's reflection when there was nobody else around. "Besides, I'm up against the top seed next round. I probably won't win." "That's exactly it, though." He pressed as far back as his swivel chair would allow, and in my attempt to outdo him, I fell backwards. He unfolded his legs, stood from his chair, and looked over his desk at me. "You okay?" "I'll manage," I managed, in the process of dusting myself off. "Then as I was saying," he continued before I could properly become erected. "Nothing you're writing has any sense of closure to it. You spend a lot of time not forgiving your dead mother, and you do a lot of moping about an ex-girlfriend you can't confront. Hell, you can't even stay sober. Isn't that what your title implies the book is about?" "It's a working title." I was upright again and noticeably perturbed. "If you've got a better one, I'd like to hear it." "Hey, look. I'm not trying to rag on you or anything like that." He paused to take a drink, and it reminded me to take one. "I'd just feel a lot more secure about this story if I knew where it was going." "I wish I could tell you." I felt bad about chuckling because my editor started rubbing his temples, and there was little I could offer him in the way of consolation. "I'm open to suggestions." "Forget about that. The title's not important. What is important is that the chiefs want to see two more chapters in a month, so the way I see it, you can go down one of two roads." He folded his arms on the table and leaned forward over them towards me. "The first option you have is to wrap up some of these loose ends. You've written way too much about this Dina girl for things to end between you two with you being too afraid to even knock on her door. It's probably your only shot at a workable ending, so make something happen there. Whether you make amends or fail miserably, it doesn't matter as long as you get an ending to make the rest of your autobiography worth a damn." "What's the other option?" I was writing a story about living a life, not living a life about writing a story. At least, I liked to believe that, but it was hard to tell anymore. I just couldn't bring myself to face her and that kid. I was more afraid of what I'd do if it wasn't mine than if it was, and I was absolutely frightened about the prospect of the kid being mine. "Well, you can scrap everything you've got so far, start from the beginning, and write only about your wrestling career." He rolled back his sleeve and looked at an imaginary watch on his wrist before bringing it momentarily to his ear. "You're free to go that route, but I suggest that you get a move on if that's what you really want to do. The clock is ticking." "Dammit," I calculated, muttering. "I don't have time for that. What's the third option?" "There, uh, was no third option." He looked into my eyes like he was searching for something, and then his face changed, as if he had found whatever it was he was searching for. "Look, why don't you go home, clear your head, and try to stay away from the bottle. We should probably go pay Dina a visit tomorrow if we want your book to succeed." "We?" Somehow, that was the part that caught me. "Yeah, I figured I'd come along." There was a certain air to his voice. It was earnest in its insistence that his presence would in no way be imposing. Before I could think of a proper objection, he was out of the chair and buzzing over to his bookshelf. I wanted to see where he was going. "When I'm working on something, I like to take a real hands-on approach." The editor read over the titles methodically and pulled a few selections off the shelves after a rather fastidious inventory. Then, he collected them and set the stack of books on the desk in front of me. He held up the top one which featured a blurry photo of a man in drab, baggy clothes facing the opposite way with a bindle slung over his shoulder. "The Many Misadventures of Henry the Hobo?" I read the questionable title aloud and decided I didn't like it. "I spent a month riding the rails while I edited that one." He pointed at a modest trophy next to his coffee maker. "I won a Guillaume le Be Award for Excellence in Editing for my efforts." I don't think I fully appreciated the prestige of his accomplishment, but I pressed on through the stack, placing the first book aside to take a look at the next selection. "You've probably heard of that one," he assumed. "It's called Bad Goods: The Truth Behind Those Big Walls At Bulkamania. It exposes the shady bulk goods department store model and tears it down from their ugly hiring practices to their union dodging. So you know what I did?" "No, what?" I thought that this book had a better title and was much more inclined to hear its story. "I flew to Mexico, smuggled my way back into the States with a group of immigrants, and got a job off the books at Bulkamania." Perhaps if it was a job interview, I would have traded stories of similar experiences with him, but my mug was long dry and the pangs of my hangover resurfaced. "That next one there is a biography about that cult leader who drugged all those sick kids and burned them all to hell in an abandoned warehouse. It won Best Mob Boss, Serial Killer or Civil War General Biography at the Feinlein Non-Fiction awards, and maybe next year, you could be up for nomination in the same category." "Think I'd win?" I slid the book from the desk to my lap, looking up at the editor. "I don't know." He scratched his head. "There's supposed to be a hot piece on Ambrose Burnside due out, too." "Heh," I looked down uneasily at the book. It's title was simply Louis Guy. "I don't even want to imagine what you had to do to edit that one." "That one wasn't too special. I basically just smoked at my desk for days on end without sleep, occasionally lighting an accidental fire in my garbage can." Then, with the ease of an unwatched magician, he brought it back around. "The point I'm making here is that I know a thing or two about what makes a good story, and to do your autobiography justice, I'm going to have to slip into your skin, so to speak, and you're going to have to trust me to wear it." He stood there, arms outstretched and palms upturned. For some sudden reason, I trusted him easily and immediately. It was like he had already slipped in and worn my skin sometime when I wasn't aware of it, and I'd just put it all together by some divine force. "What can I do to make you trust me?" "If this thing's a success, how about you double my cut?" The tricky part about trust is that it becomes even easier with just the right amount of collateral, but too much or too little, and it ruins the whole damn thing. "Done." He fired it back quickly. "In writing." I was playing table tennis with a wall, and I could see my own shadow, almost defying my very movements with every consecutive return. "Sure. I'll have a contract drawn up for you by the time we leave for Dina's tomorrow." He paused, and I paused, and I could almost hear the ball rolling to a halt on the floor. "We got a deal?" "Sure." We shook hands, but as far as I was concerned, it was nothing more than a handshake. "As soon as I sign that contract." "Of course." His grip was firm and his eyes were warm. "Before we get ahead of ourselves, I do have a few little conditions." "Shoot." The grip loosened and his hand drew back. "First, at this stage in the game, try not to introduce any new characters unless they're really part of the story, and cut the metafictional bullshit out. Nobody wants to read a story about a guy writing a story." I realized then that he had truly earned his Guillaume le Be Award for Excellence in Editing, as visually lackluster as it was. He was passionate about what he did, and his confidence in his ability showed. This guy knew his stuff. "I can do that." I nodded and thought about asking for his name. For some reason, I felt it better not to know. "By the way," he reached into his inside vest pocket. He flipped out a business card that outshined his trophy. "If you need me in the meantime, ask for Donnie Wright. You can call me Black Donald, if you like. It's a little nickname my friends have for me, and Johnny, if we pull this shit off, I think this could be the start of a beautiful friendship." SEPTEMBER 12, 1992 My mother didn't bring too many black men around, some nonsense about how my grandfather would be livid if he caught her mixing colors, and that might have been why I took a particular interest to Donald White. He showed up at about the same time as Military Issue Dennis, but despite the fact that Donald White had confided in me that he didn't much care for Dennis, the two didn't seem to cross paths very often, and unlike the rest of my mother's suitors, Donald seemed to pay more attention to me than he did to Lisa. I found this vexing, but I wasn't about to turn away any scrap of attention I could get. We were sitting on the couch in the living room, and Donald White looked frustrated. The then strange sounds of copulation banged through the walls, shaking the painting of Jesus that hung above us. The sounds of the small television across the room drowned below the noise until it was just a flash of color projected back across onto a bare spot of wall. "What's that noise?" I asked along a long process of lost innocence. "Is Dennis hurting Mommy?" "Nah. They's just fuckin'." Donald White wasn't the smartest guy, but he was honest, and he didn't pull any punches. I was only seven years old at the time, but I had heard the word before, even if I didn't quite know what it meant. "What's fuckin'?" Donald's frustration cracked, and his teeth peeked through smiling lips, unable to resist the humor of my high pitched curse. "It's a bad word," he warned sincerely. "You shouldn't be usin' it." "But you said it." I was enviably confused. I wasn't a fast learner, but I had learned fast to learn slow. The more I learned, the harder it was to sleep. A loud thud shot through the wall. The painting of Jesus shook again. "Yeah." Donald took a drag from his cigarette and blew out smoke in a fascinating shape. "And I'm a bad motherfucker." I nodded in agreement, although I had no idea what qualified someone to be a motherfucker, and had I any idea, I would not have been able to determine what makes one motherfucker good and one motherfucker bad. The concept that being a bad motherfucker was better than being a good motherfucker was something far too advanced for me to grasp back then. These would be things I would learn in time, but even when Donald White said something I didn't fully comprehend, I couldn't imagine that he would lie to me. The painting fell off of the nail it hung from and landed in the space between the couch and the wall. "Fuckin' . . ." Donald stood, his thought left hanging in midair. "Come on, little man. Let's go for a walk." "Can we go get ice cream?" I looked up at Donald but didn't have to look far. He was proportioned like a full sized man, but he couldn't have been much taller than I was. It wasn't something that was very apparent when he was sitting down. I thought he would have looked funny at regular height, though. One of his legs was definitely shorter than the other. "Shit, I ain't got that kind of money." Despite his dire financial straights, the lopsidedly streetwise rock of a man felt pity on my situation, and empathetic to my affinity for frosted treats, he came up with a plan. "I think your mom left her purse on the kitchen table. Go see if there's any money in there." Sure enough, Donald had made the right call. I stashed Lisa's cigarette money in my sweatpants pocket, and we were off, meandering in constantly corrected courses towards Cone Zone, an establishment that did little to differentiate itself from other ice cream shops in the area. After ordering a soft serve swirl cone, Donald and I found a small table near the window. It had not been cleaned, but neither of us minded much. I think a lot of Donald's habits ended up seeping into me, one way or another. He wasn't eating any ice cream. "How come you didn't get one?" I had trouble seeing him over the peak of my cone. "Bad motherfuckers don't eat ice cream." He was looking out the window through his own vague reflection. "One day when you're a bad motherfucker, you won't eat ice cream no more either." "I want to be a bad motherfucker, just like you." I wasn't ready to quit ice cream then and there, and I was sure I'd be able to discover some loophole that would allow me to reap the benefits of both. "Oh yeah?" Donald cracked an amused smile. "You know what I'd do if I was a real bad motherfucker?" "What?" I was curious, and melted ice cream was running down my hands. "If I was a real bad motherfucker," his eyes grew wide so that his irises were islands. "I'd wait until it was just me and Dennis alone in the house, and then I'd take one of your mom's Virginia Slims, light that shit up, and toss it in a garbage can." The idea made me feel guilty because I didn't like Dennis, not after I saw him hit Lisa. I didn't like her even more for denying it. I was fighting a losing battle with the melting contents of the large swirl cone. "Yeah, I'd wait until Dennis was sleeping." His eyes grew so wide I thought his skull was going to pop through his sockets. "And I'd make sure there were no batteries in the smoke detectors." I really didn't know what to say to Donald. He had been right about the money in Lisa's purse. "Yeah, that's what I'd do." The other patrons paid him no attention. "That's what I'd do if I was a real bad motherfucker." MAY 17, 2000 The Americanator idled across a few spaces in a strip mall skeleton's bored parking lot, and Monster Truck Doug had no idea that the sunglasses he sported made him look hilarious. He handed me the manila envelope across the separator between the seats, taking care not to hold it too high. Reflexively, I pinched the metal flaps together, but Doug grabbed my wrist. "What're you doing?" I suspected he knew. "I told you not to open that." "What is it?" I was curious, but not really that vested. "It's our ticket to some of the finest purple microdots you'll ever have." He pulled his shades down the bridge of his nose to look at me eye to eye. "That's all you need to know. Can I trust you not to open this?" "Yeah, whatever." I reached for the door handle, but Doug stopped me again. "Hold on a sec." He released his grip and put his hands in the air to show no malcontent. "I just want to make sure you got it down." "Yeah, I got it down." We had been over it several times before, and though I never paid full attention to any single explanation, I had paid enough attention to each run through to piece the instructions together. "Guy's name is Donnie Dots, and he'll be in the park, but whatever you do, don't call him Donnie Dots." The park was a few blocks down the road, at a safe enough distance away where the monster truck wouldn't draw attention. "Ask him a simple question like what time it is or when the next bus is stopping by, and he'll give you the wrong answer. That's how you'll know it's him. He'll take you to his car. Don't mention my name, don't mention his name, and don't give him the envelope until he gives you the dots." "Take it easy, man. I got it." He seemed satisfied, probably reviewing the plan for his own sake to be sure he hadn't left out any important details. "We're going to trip some balls tonight." A relieved smile widened Doug's face, and we pounded fists before I exited the monster truck and began walking down Long Road, which was actually a medium length road named after one of Arbuckle's first mayors, Vernon Long, who was coincidentally a rather stout man. His bronze statue cast a lengthy shadow in the center of the park as I approached from the east. I walked along the curved trail of pavement, mostly because it was there, and passed a man adjusting his sneakers on a bench. He didn't notice me behind him, or maybe he was pretending he didn't to add to the absurd secrecy of the operation. Most illegal transactions I arranged had been straightforward deals without any of that cloak and dagger bullshit that bad fiction would have you believe. "Excuse me." He turned around faster than I could think of a question. "Uh, do you know what time it is?" "Ten to four." He smiled and then took a hand off his shoe to point upwards towards a the tower across the way that held the underwhelming title of the tallest point in Arbuckle according to a sign in front of it. "There's a big clock right behind you." "Oh, yeah. Thanks." He went back to his adjustments, and I continued down the path. Further along, there was a man underneath a tree, confining himself to within the shade's outline as he roamed nervously around. It was too warm for his jacket. I almost didn't have to ask, but the way Doug was playing it, I was wary of the obvious. "Hey, uh." He looked up at me like a startled animal. It made me back up a few steps. "You know where there's a Gas N' Shit around here?" "Sure," he eased, but then he thought. "The closest one is a couple miles down Third Street, though. Just keep walking west a couple blocks and take a right." "Thanks." I nodded, even though I had passed a Gas N' Shit a block back. I didn't really know what to do next, so I just stood there for a while, until after it got awkward. "You Johnny?" I looked around, and I would've taken offense that he had called me by name if I hadn't found the whole ordeal dubiously absurd to begin with. "Walk with me." He wiggled his finger like a hook and broke the invisible wall of his pen. "Don't make it look like you're following me." I tried my best to follow orders, and the more orders I followed, the more orders became easy to follow, even orders about following people without looking like you're following them. I can't remember if this was the thought that confused me into some mental paralysis then, but if it hadn't, some similar thought had. I had been too focused on focusing on Donnie without appearing to focus on him to notice the parked squad car as he walked past it, and as soon as I had passed it, I heard the door swing open behind me. "Easy!" Commanded a hard voice. I put my hands in the air and cursed the hell out of Doug under my breath. "Shit, kid, did I tell you to put your hands up? Keep your hands down and get in the back!" I did as I was told, unable to figure out what to make of the situation. The officer got in the passenger seat. Donnie Dots leaned against a car parked a few lengths in front of us, smoking a cigarette. There was another manila envelope on the seat next to me. "Kid, this shit will knock you into next fuckin' week." He didn't turn, but I met eyes with him through the rear view mirror. He was smiling. "You got your envelope?" I pulled out the envelope and saw the back of his head nodding, the skin folding on the back of his neck beneath buzzed red hair. "Good deal. Make the switch." I hesitated. It seemed too easy. "Go on." "I hope you don't mind me asking." I took the envelope next to me and replaced it on the seat with the one Doug gave me. "What's this all about." "It's just business, kid." He smirked a little bit. "Don't worry about it." I grasped the handle, and he heard the door unhinge. He turned and looked at me around the headrest. "Tell Doug we appreciate the information." The officer smiled and tipped his hat. I got out of the car, and the man I originally thought to be Donnie Dots waved to me as I walked away, unsure which one of them was supposed to be Donnie. Maybe neither one of them was Donnie, or maybe they both were. I suppose it didn't really matter. SEPTEMBER 15, 1992 It was a Tuesday, and I don't know why I remember that, but I guess I remember everything about the day pretty vividly. I was in the principal's office between my mother and Military Issue Dennis. Both had just arrived and were reaching around like blind people playing tag in the dark to try and act the way they guessed real parents were supposed to act. My mother recoiled in an almost mocking horror as Principal Sandy Linus, slowly deflating there in her knit shoal, slowly revealed the picture I had crayoned onto the sheet of standard letter sized construction paper. It seemed, for a while after the revelation, that no words were exchanged. Military Issue Dennis defaulted to anger, and looking back, I have to admit that my underdeveloped artistic skills on their own only made the picture more offensive, although it took everyone in the room a little while to figure out that the skewed uneven lines were supposed to make up the outline of a house on fire with a stick figure lying inside of it. "I don't get it." Dennis scratched his head. "What the hell is this supposed to be? It looks like a fire crotch." "I think it's upside-down," added Lisa. Principal Linus looked around at the picture, almost ashamed to see it again, but she realized that my mother was right and turned the image over. "It's a house on fire, and Johnny told me that the stick figure is supposed to be you, Dennis." It did kind of look like a redheaded woman's untended genitalia, though. "That's terrible!" My mother gasped, as if on cue. "Lisa," pointed Dennis. "Do you think that looks like me?" "We're very concerned about this picture." Sandy Linus interjected, maintaining control of the slippery conference. "Things like this can indicate that he's acting out about unhealthy influences in his environment. Has Johnny been acting unusual lately?" "Nothing I've noticed." In some backhanded way, Lisa was always honest. "Does Johnny have any friends or relatives named Donald?" The question prompted Lisa and Dennis to look at each other, but neither of them responded with anything more than a shrug. "He told me that someone named Donald White told him to paint it." "Wait." My mother held her hand out, trying to remember where she had heard that name before. "Donald White?" "Do you know him?" "Isn't that Johnny's imaginary friend?" She asked Dennis, who just looked puzzled. "I think it is. I always hear him talking to someone when he's alone. I think that's what Johnny calls him." I don't think the principal had anything to make of it, but that's where I stopped paying attention. The news that Donald was imaginary shattered the rickety foundations of my perception. The words became harder to differentiate from the silence. "Johnny just has a hard time making friends." I looked up at my mother as tears welled in my eyes. She wasn't looking at me. Nobody was looking at me. It was like I wasn't there, wondering who they were talking about. "I figured he'd outgrow it." I thought I lost it there and really made a scene, but the harder I try to remember it, the fuzzier that part gets, and the memory lives on without memorable consequence. SEPTEMBER 19, 2009 I was sitting there on a bench, smoking, and I was just starting to like the place. I got used to being in rehab and focused on its redeeming factors. Nobody bothered me here or expected anything from me, and it made sobriety that much more tolerable. Once and again a fading celebrity would pass through, and it'd remind me of some of the moments I'd seen on television shows they were in years back. "Hi, Johnny." The reverend stood before me smiling, and I never trusted churchmen, but the fact that he'd forgiven me for vomiting on him when I was going through withdrawals made reverend Curt Harding cause for an exception. "Reverend Frank." I nodded, and he chuckled politely at the intended misnomer, though I'm not sure if he remembered where the joke about his name started, being that it wasn't really funny enough to be keep mentioning. "How are you today?" He sat down, carefully unfolding his clerics so they wouldn't bunch up beneath him. "Not bad." I took a drag from my cigarette. "I think this place is finally starting to feel like home." "You're doing great, Johnny." He put his hand on my shoulder. I was still uncomfortable being touched. "You can't stay here forever, though. Haven't you thought about getting back to the real world? Getting back to your life and your career?" "I've been down that road." I dragged again, switching hands to pull away from his hand. "I got a lot more thinking to do, but I'm pretty sure that I'd want to do something else." "Well, turning over a new leaf can be helpful." He almost reached for my shoulder again, but he stopped himself. "Do you have anything in mind?" "I might try my hand at motivational speaking." At the time, I had no idea that the pay was garbage and the industry was hard to break into professionally. Most of the good gigs went to folks sentenced to community service. The price was right. "That's wonderful!" He rejoiced, unable to restrain his hand from patting me on the back. I think sometimes the hand controlled him. "I think you could really make a difference." As much as I didn't mind Reverend Curt, I knew what was coming next. It always started out with blustery small talk, but eventually, it'd come around to the whole Jesus thing. Every time he brought up religion, I'd remind him that I wasn't interested, but he was a dangerous cocktail of persistent and forgetful. "Actually," he started. "I know some good folks who run events for misguided youths. When you get out of here, I can help you set something up." "Yeah, sure." I waited, watching the pot and checking his words for segues in preparation for the switch. "That would be great." "Great!" He bent, like some flattering mirror. "I have to admit, though, that's not what I came here to talk about." The anxious tenses relaxed and my shoulders slacked in anticipation of rolling away. "Now that you're well past your withdrawals, and you got some color to your face," he paused there and watched the confused suspicion slide across my eyes. He picked up somewhere else. "The facility talent show is coming up, and I've got a group of guys working on a dance number to 'Jesus Is Just Alright With Me' by The Doobie Brothers. The problem is, the guy who plays Jesus twisted his ankle in practice earlier, and if we don't find a replacement, we won't have an act." "Huh?" The whole premise seemed ridiculous to me, but as I surveyed Reverend Harding, I could tell he didn't have it in him to piece together a scheme this absurd to somehow trick me into his biblical nonsense. Something about the act even paralleled some harmonious balance that allowed our worlds to coexist. "These guys have worked very hard, and they'd be really let down if this fell through." He clasped his hands together and shook them fervently. "Please, Johnny. I'm begging you." "I don't know." I looked around, scratching my head more to conceal my face than to tend an itch, embarrassed that he was making such a display of his appeal. "I'm really not much of a dancer." "That's okay!" He untied his tangle of fingers and put his hands out. "All you have to do is wear the costume and clap to the beat." "Christ." I couldn't catch my tongue, and Curt winced, biting his. "Sorry. That just slipped out. I, uh, sure. Why not?" "Thank you." He pounced, grabbing my hands. "I really owe you one for this. If you need anything, just ask." "That so?" I considered requesting that he never try to sell me on becoming religious, but something stopped me. Donnie White always used to tell me that an ace up your sleeve is worth two in your drawers. I never quite figured out what he meant by that. "Let me think about it." "Sure thing!" He stood up, still shaking my hands vigorously. "I'm going to go tell the guys the good news." "Wait a minute." As he tried to break away, I pulled him back. It all felt so incomplete. "You're not going to try to lecture me about letting Jesus into my heart?" "It sounds like you want to talk about it." He sat back down. "No, uh, I just meant," I grinned halfway, too reassured to finish the thought or know I had started it. "I don't know. It just wouldn't feel right if you didn't try." "I just figured that if you weren't open to finding Jesus, I'd let Him find you." "I didn't know he was looking for me." Reverend Curt just nodded, and I didn't know what to make of his facial expression. He simply patted me on the knee and stood back up, and it became clear to me why Curt was here, helping struggling addicts recover. Somewhere beneath the surface, he was just like us. He had his illusions, and we had ours. DECEMBER 5, 2009 I hadn't sobered up at all. In fact, I had gotten progressively more intoxicated as I waited for Donald Wright, the pasty, frail man who went by Black Donald, to arrive. The clock flashed 7:06 in a matchstick arrangement of thin, green hexagons as the buzzer made that awful noise it made, and Donald was there, pasty and frail as ever, sharply dressed and carrying a notebook clipped to a wooden board. His cheeks burned red from the cold as I met him at the door, still wearing a bathrobe over a t-shirt and boxer shorts. "Jesus, man." His arms shot out in such quick surprise that he almost pitched the clipboard through a window. "You're fucking smashed, aren't you?" "Me?" Had there been anyone else around, it wouldn't have strengthened my defense any. "I'm driving." He pointed a finger at me sternly, and afraid that being belligerent would only make matters worse, I threw my hands up in the air to show no malcontent. "Fine." I reached into the pocket of my bathrobe for my keys and threw the whole chain to Donald, who caught the jingling projectile with the precise coordination of a much more sober man. I put my finger up in the air and tried to remember what I was going to say before I diverted my thought processes towards the task of movement. "Just for the record, I think this is a bad idea." "Pull yourself together." I grabbed the half-finished bottle of whiskey from where I had placed it on the stand next to the front door. As I wobbled out, underdressed for the weather, I led the way to my space while Black Donald supported my balance like he was operating a divining rod. "You probably shouldn't drink anymore." "I can't do this sober," I admitted. We got to the car, and Donald rested me against the car, bent at a strange angle over the hood. "Can you get in the car without my help?" We were both breathing heavily from the mild exertion. "I'm not getting in any car until I sign the contract." The words bent out of me, and Donald was quick to slap the clipboard down in front of me. I readjusted myself cautiously, and after minor troubles, recovered the pen from within the clip and scribbled down something too blurry to read in free white space underneath an army of words too blurry to read. It made me notice how blurry everything else was, and the next thing I could remember, I was in the car, sprawled across the backseat while Black Donald navigated the car furiously through the night. Blotches of light passed overhead, and I curled up, fetal, feeling like I was under attack. "What the fuck?" Black Donald turned his head at me slightly in response, but not all the way. "Where am I?" "Good, you're awake." He flinched and decided to turn the whole way, to survey me over by whipping his focus back and forth from the road to the backseat like his head was a tennis match. "We're about ten minutes outside Arbuckle. I assume you know how to get there." "Oh, God." My soul felt like it was trying to evacuate my body. "I can't fucking do this." "Yes, you can. It's easy." He looked back at the road. "Is there going to be a sign for the exit, or am I going to need to turn somewhere?" "No, I can't fucking," I stopped abruptly, and luckily unbuckled, I pushed the door open and poked my head through the opening. The velocity of the car as it tore down highway sent the car door bumping against my head as I lost my guts in flapping streaks off the side of the car and all over the road. I wiped my mouth with the sleeve of my robe, and the wind shut the door behind me. "I can't fucking do this, man. We gotta go back." "We're almost fucking there!" He was exuberant with agitation, chasing his illusion into reality. "Shit, Johnny. That fucking stinks." "Sorry." Black Donald rolled down the window to air out the vehicle, and it screamed into the car. It was all blurry and then the sound became blurry, like the hellish cacophony of the world's largest detuned orchestra. "Turn's coming up." "What?" "Turn!" I garbled over the wind loud enough for Donald to hear, and with a jarring twist, he skidded the car over the shoulder's warning lines to make the sharp correction. The rattling car smoothed back to a speeding glide, and I took a second to catch my breath. "Donald, I really can't fucking do this." "Fuckin'." It had become its own sentence that meant anything and everything. "Don't make me have to drag your ass to her doorstep." "Listen to me, man. I cannot do this right now." All I could do was writhe there. "Turn left at the next light." I didn't know why I was giving him directions, but he had stopped paying attention to anything else. It was the only control I had over the situation, and I held onto it tightly, all the way to the next right and the right after that as we curled through the maze of one way streets to turn into the lot of her small complex. Then, I had no more control at all. I had gone and used it all up again. "We're here." Not long after the announcement, he was out the front door and at the back, trying to pull me out of the car by my legs. "Come on, you asshole. Get out." "No." "I didn't come all this way to go home empty handed." He strained and pulled, but I kicked my legs and held tight to the far side of the passenger seat. Even in a near comatose state of inebriation, I had the editor outmatched in feats of physical strength. "Then you go!" My legs were growing weary from all the flailing resistance. "She's in apartment 2R. Buzz Rodriguez and say you're Schwartz." "You're coming with me, dammit." He grabbed the clipboard. "You signed the contract." "Fuck the contract. I'm not going anywhere." "Fine." He tossed the clipboard up in the air. The clip snapped where it landed, and papers flapped. "You stay here. I'll bring her down to you." "Fine! Go!" From my wedge between the front seats and the back, I craned my neck and watched him walk off. "Wait! Don't mention my name! Whatever you do, don't mention my name!" The quiet steps of his dress shoes faded off, and he was gone. I willed my disoriented motor functions to squeeze my upper body out of the upholstered rut and wormed out of the car onto the paved lot. It overwhelmed me to try and stand, so I slithered on the ground, scraping across like an amoeba on a slide. There was no particular destination and no uniform direction to my movements. There came a point where I questioned whether or not I was moving at all, and then it became too tedious to move anywhere altogether. I flattened out there, weeping, and for some reason, I thought back to my childhood machinations. For once, it got quiet, deaf quiet, like I was just lying there outside some empty buildings. Somewhere along the way, I must've dozed off there. "Back in the car." Black Donald hoisted me up with all his strength and none of my resistance, and he led me back over to the car. "What happened?" I wouldn't remember his answer. He had to tell me again later. DECEMBER 6, 2009 "She had no idea who you were." Donald took a sip of his coffee and looked at me across the large oak desk like he was searching for something, but like he was searching for something that was behind me. "I don't know how I expect you to answer this, Johnny, but I have to ask. Are you sure you're real?" I nodded slowly, having never stopped to consider that I wasn't. "I think I'm going crazy," he said. "Which one of us is really writing this story?" I always thought I was pretty good at telling if things were real or not. It's a lot easier on drugs. Everything seems real when you're sober, and some of the time, it isn't. "Um, I'm writing it." I reckoned he was right about one thing, though. I leaned to my right, so he could see whatever it was that was behind me. I turned to look, too, and we both realized that we weren't looking at anything.
