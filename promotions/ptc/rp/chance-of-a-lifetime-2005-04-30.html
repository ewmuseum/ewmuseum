Chance of a Lifetime

30 April 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2005-04-30 I lost the priest behind a magnificent hedge, but found myself standing in the middle of a wonderfully tended garden. Like the rectory, it seemed completely out of place in the middle of the concrete and brick of the neighborhood. But, just looking at the flowers and the small fountain in the center was calming. Its intended effect was carried out to the t, and I was taken back to my youth in seconds, envisioning my grandmother tending to the flowers in her garden while I followed her with a watering can. “Not too much, child,” she would warn warmly as I tipped the can just enough to give the seedlings the right amount of water. “Is that okay, grammy?” “That’s great. You’re going to make a fine gardener,” she’d reply, and tousle my hair. I breathed deeply, appreciating the honeysuckle dancing through the heat of the air, filling it with a sickly sweet fragrance. The pink azalias shone through their dark green stems and were perfectly lined up along the back wall. BLAM! I woke from my day dream again, to more sounds of gunshots. KABOOM! BAM! KA-BLAM! I looked left while darting behind the hedge and saw the priest, sitting in the 79 El Dorado that scared me earlier. I grabbed my chest and took a deep breath before walking to the car and opening it’s door. BOOM! I jumped at the sound, making the priest laugh. “That’s the second time today, eh?” I grew instantly embarrassed at the revelation that he'd seen me earlier. “Yeah," I said off-handedly, trying to play it cool. "Second time today. Pop the hood, okay?” POP! I reached into the air intake and pressed the throttle straight up, causing the car to lurch forward, very nearly breaking the possibly ancient brake lines. I did it one more time and turned the carburetor a touch to the left. The car roared to life, the engine sounding more like a machine, less like a machine gun. “What was wrong?” “Carburetor wasn’t lined up right. Just needed a little tweak.” “That’s what was wrong?” “Has it backfired in the last fifteen seconds?” “I guess it hasn’t, no.” “Then it worked,” I said with a hint of a smile. The priest smiled back. “You really are a Godsend. Now, get in. I told him we’d be there in twenty minutes.” I entered the car and he carefully backed down the driveway, into the street. BAM! He stopped and looked at me, and I at him. “At least it took a minute this time.” He pressed the accelerator and we were on our way. I rolled down the window and pushed my head outside, just a bit, watching as the faceless members of humanity walked down the street, playing with their children, or just went about their daily lives. It was nice to see that even in a run-down neighborhood, these people still went about their lives. “What a neighborhood,” I said, thinking I was observant. “It’s a fine neighborhood. At least, it used to be. It was a vibrant community at one point.” “Really? Doesn’t look like it.” “Well, it’s what happens when people lose hope. It’s not their fault, you know.” The priest said, keeping his eyes locked on the road. “Huh?” “It’s not their fault that they live here. Many of these families are new to the US, like I was forty years ago. They see the promise of freedom and prosperity, and they can’t resist its call.” I nodded. I knew what he meant. “I was in their position, quite a few years back. I was a priest in a small Mexican village. Iglecia del Milagro. The Church of the Miracle. It was a wonderful life, but I saw many of the people from my village moving to America. I wanted them to have a familiar face to turn to in case things got rough, so, I moved here, and I haven’t looked back since.” “Wait. You gave up everything you had for something completely unknown?” “Yes. It was a risk, but I knew these people needed someone. I gave up my individual life when I became a man of God. Nothing I could do as a single human being would be as great if I didn’t have faith. I wanted my people to have hope. I wanted them to keep their dreams of happiness.” “What happened, then?” “Look around you. They’re still happy. They just don’t know it, thanks to television and movies. There’s never truly poor people on TV being happy. They’re shown as miserable, so these people feel miserable. They lost hope when they realized they’d never be rich and powerful. They lost faith when they lost hope.” I frowned at his words and looked outside, not wanting to realize he was right. Trying to convince myself he was wrong. “I think it’s what happened to you too,” he said, cutting through me. “You lost faith in yourself. You lost hope in being what you wanted to be. You lost hope, because you weren’t happy, and you’d lost faith.” “Yeah. Something like that.” The ride got silent for a while after that. It was a pensive, reflective silence. I looked out the window, watching as the buildings became more and more sparse and the cactii became more plentiful. After about a half hour we were in the country side, on a long, lonely road to nowhere. “I guess I should have asked this before, but…” “Where are we going?” the priest asked with a smile as he wiped his sweaty brow. “Yeah. Where are we going?” “To see a friend that I think could help you. And in turn, help me.” “Oh. Well, I guess I just have to have faith, huh?” The priest smiled and slapped my thigh. “You’re learning, my son. You’re learning.” Within about ten minutes, we pulled up to a ramshackle diner on the side of the road. Another car was in the parking lot, but judging by the dust, it hadn’t moved in years. The glass in the front was broken and the door slammed softly in the gentle breeze. “This is the place?” I asked. “This is the place,” the priest replied. “And this teaches me not to judge by an outer appearance, or…” He looked at me quizzically, then to the ground. “No. But, good try.” We walked around to the back of the building and opened the storm shelter doors, moving down into the cellar and through the old storage room, in through a stainless steel door. Like the rectory, behind the doors the place was pristine. The walls were an off white color and the smell of fresh paint hung in the air. It was almost like a hospital, but didn’t have a clinical feel. It was warm and inviting without being overly so. The priest turned around and smiled. “This is it. Look around, I’ll tell my friend you’re here. Down the hall, last door on the left.” I nodded and walked down the hallway and into a darkened room. I reached left and turned on a switch. Light flooded the room and what was once dark became blue. A vibrant, electric blue that reflected the white light through every surface of the room. I gasped as I looked around and saw masks, costumes, and all sorts of different wrestling gear, each in a different case, each one a similar size. Slowly, I made my way over to the first one on display. “El Rayo Azul?” I said out loud. “The Blue Streak?” I moved from case to case looking at the history, the names, and the stories behind each costume. “Never seen such a thing, eh?” a voice said. I turned to find the priest, flanked by a man that seemed almost a part of the room. He wore a mask that resembled the those I'd been studying. He smiled towards me as I stared. I was flustered, and at the same time, somewhat calm. “Never in my life. Hi, glad to be here.” “Glad to have you, stranger,” the man in blue said, extending a hand. “I am called El Rayo Azul.” I looked a bit apprehensively as I walked across the room and shook his hand. “Good to meet you. I’m…” “Shhhh. Names aren’t necessary.” he cut me off with, that hint of a smile never faltering. He motioned for me to follow him into another room, but I hesitated. “What am I doing here? I’m not a wrestler anymore, and I don’t think I want to be. If you’re looking to manage me, or get me to wrestle for you, I really don’t think I want to. I’m done with…” “Please. All I ask is just a few moments of your time. If what I have to say to you doesn’t strike a chord with you, then you’re more than welcome to walk away.” I was skeptical. Having an old man in a wrestling mask pleading for my ear wasn't what I was expecting the priest to lead me to. The man's request was reasonable enough, though, so I listened. He told me the tale that I relayed to you earlier, about the history of El Rayo Azul, its cultural significance to the Mexican people, and how the wearer of the El Rayo mask was more a man of the people than he was a man to himself. I soaked it all in and sat back as he made me the offer a wrestler only dreams of. “I’m getting old, senor. I cannot keep doing this. I need to become myself again, and sail off to the open seas. What I am offering you is a chance to become what I am now. The thirty-eighth incarnation of El Rayo Azul. You will have a place to stay, a roof over your head, and ample opportunity to wrestle,” he said to me. “But, if you choose not to, then you simply may go on your way.” “I don’t know…” “You’ll have fortune.” “I don’t care about money.” He was taken aback, but smiled wider. “You’ll have fame.” “I had that. It did nothing for me.” He looked at the priest as his smile widened even more. “You’ll have an entire country following you, looking to you for the hope they need.” I thought about it for a moment. “Can I try on the mask?”
