Veiled Intentions - Ariana Al-Doleh (FA)

27 March 2006
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Though my face is covered, I can still feel the eyes on me. Pair after pair of them, gazing at me, attempting to stare into my very soul. Their looks are disapproving, disgusted. I’m the other. Not like them. I am different because I wear this head-covering that my religion and nation call hijab. Never mind that I am a wrestler, just as they are. Never mind that I have trained for the past two years for this day, for the chance to compete in a contest the scale of the Infinite Gauntlet. The fact that I wear this veil opens me to scorn. Nonetheless, I move on. I carry my bag past my fellow competitors, headed for the female locker room. There, I will change out of the vestments that I wear under the black cloak that the women of Iran wear. For the first time in public, I will shed that cloak and clothe my body in spandex, the attire of a professional wrestler. But the veil must stay. It is my protection. Perhaps no one will understand. That is alright. I do not expect them to understand. They have not lived my life. They have not seen through my eyes. As I walk, I feel the scarf beginning to slip down a bit, exposing my nose. Frowning, I quickly adjust it, pulling the bottom fold of the black silk garment up, to just below my eyes. Those who can see me now see very little exposed skin, simply my hands and my eyes. I wonder if those staring daggers at me are looking into my eyes. If so, they could see the pain and anguish that reside there. The torment that I feel to this very day. Finally, the locker room door. Behind it, I can escape the stares, the points, and the scorn. I open it quickly, and step inside, scanning the space for my locker. Across the room, my eyes fall upon a small name placard, slide above one of the steel storage devices. I read my name, Ariana Al-Doleh, written in black, permanent marker. I drop my bag and nearly cry at the sight of it. So much has happened to get me to this point. So much has happened to allow me to be here, in America, about to compete before the entire world. Wordlessly, I remove my cloak, folding it neatly and placing it on a nearby bench. Looking at myself in the mirror, I suppose that I don’t look especially different from a Westerner. I wear blue jeans. A t-shirt, with my favorite band’s name on it. But that matters not to other people when I wear the veil. Unzipping my garment bag, I pull out my ring attire. It is simple, a pure white spandex singlet. In this country, it is perfectly normal. Some might even consider it prudish. In my homeland, to wear it in public would mean arrest, or worse. As I ponder the singlet, I can hear the sounds of knocking behind me. “Come in,” I say, expecting to see a fellow female competitor. Instead, standing in the doorway is a young man, dressed in a suit, and looking apologetic. “I’m sorry, Miss Al-Doleh,” he says, sounding genuinely contrite. “I didn’t mean to interrupt anything. I just wanted to tell you that you have about an hour before match time.” I nod slowly. “You haven’t interrupted anything,” I reply. “I’m not yet getting dressed.” Once again, I feel a pair of eyes on me. The young man’s. But his eyes are different from those of the others. His are curious, inquisitive. He looks at me for nearly a minute, as if trying to understand something, before finally speaking again. “Pardon me for being nosy,” he asked, gesturing at me with his right hand, “but why do you wear that veil? Beneath the silk, my mouth turns down in a frown. I despise that question. It is far too complicated to explain the way people want. They desire a simple answer, one that can be summed up in a sentence or two. That sort of answer is impossible for me to give. How can I summarize a lifetime in so few words? “I’m sorry for asking,” I hear him say, “but I was just curious. In my politics class, we learned that the veil is a symbol of oppression against women. You’re in the United States now. You don’t have to wear it if you don’t want to…” Sometimes, the educated are the most difficult to explain it to. They are taught half-truths, things that are correct in some situations, but not in others. Stereotypes. Before I can stop myself, I blurt out “Yes, I do!” Clearly, he is shocked by my candor, and takes a half-step back. Considering his shock, I suppose the two words that spilled from his lips were inevitable. “Why not?” I cannot answer him. And yet, I feel compelled to. After all, he does not mean to judge. He is merely a product of an educational system that doesn’t understand the whole story, nor can understand it. In that moment, that expectant moment, I can remember everything. Growing up in the Islamic Republic amidst the terrors of war with Iraq. Back then, the government made the veil a matter of patriotism. To wear it was to support the nation against Saddam, to be a good, virtuous woman. Is it any wonder that at the age of four I asked my mother to put it on? I, a child. I suppose I wanted to feel grown up. Mature. An honorable member of society. It was so simple when I was young. Before my mother and others realized what the veil was being used for. The Ayatollah Khomeini died, giving way to Ayatollah Khatami. The perception then was that things would loosen up. That the veil would no longer be a compulsion, but a choice. That a strict, radical interpretation of my faith would not be used to prevent me from venturing out alone. In retrospect, it seemed the Koran was cited as the reason for everything I could not do. And yet, when I read it myself, it seemed to say nothing that the extremists claimed it did. They had their interpretations. I had mine. But it was theirs that carried power in my homeland. How ridiculous, it became, after a time, that I could not walk out in public with my hair flowing in the breeze. How ridiculous that I could not attend a football match, to see my nation play. And how utterly farcical, I thought, when my mother was arrested for not veiling. Thrown in jail for not wearing a scarf. My father was enraged. I was inconsolable. And so, there was only one thing to do. My father came with me to get my passport. Alone, I would not have been allowed to do so. He came with me on my circuitutious flight to America. He would only stay here one night, before turning around to make the return journey. Before he left, he kissed me on the cheek and promised that he would be back one day, with Mother. That was three years ago. Three weeks ago, my father proved himself to be a man of his word. And they are here tonight, to cheer their daughter on, in a land where I could follow any dream I had. Even a dream as insane as this. A dream where I can be battered and beaten. A dream where I have fallen down, only to get back up hundreds of times in a day. But no amount of physical pain can compare to the emotional pain that was living in a land that had arrested my mother. No matter how sore I am, I will welcome that soreness. It is a sign that I am free. A sign that I am alive. “Miss Al-Doleh?” Suddenly, I snap back to attention, seeing the young man still standing there, looking slightly confused. Not that I can blame him. How long have I been here, lost in thought? I gaze at a wall clock, over the doorway, and gasp. Though it seems as if it has been hours, in reality, it has been little more than a moment. What can I tell this young man? The truth? Would he even understand it? …it matters not. My story must be told. Perhaps others can learn from it, can gain some knowledge that will help my sisters one day. “I am from Iran,” I say, the name of the country prompting conflicting emotions. “There, women such as myself are not allowed in public without the veil. Most, though, are not as fully veiled as I. I have a slightly different motivation.” My head nearly falls into my hands as I look at him. Explaining it is so difficult, so very difficult. I will come across as a coward. But with all I have at stake, a coward I must be. “I have family back in Iran, sir. Were anyone to know me and recognize me, there could be retribution taken against them by overzealous religious fanatics.” “Because you’re not properly dressed,” he asks, shaking his head at the thought of it. I suppose it IS a rather foreign ideal here. Though fathers often chastise their daughters for dressing improperly, few fathers would object to the attire I longed to wear for so long. T-shirts. Blue jeans. Without the obscuring black cloak. “And because of my profession,” I add. “In a world where women are inferior to men, a woman could never be allowed to compete on equal footing with a man. In Iran, that is true. But here…” Here I have idols. Karina Wolfenden. Amy Campbell. Dawn McFarlane. Women who have reached the top of this sport on their skills and merit. As I hope to. Tonight is the start. “Here,” the young man finishes, “you have the chance to aim as high as you wish. To shoot for the stars.” I nod. If nothing else, he understands the feelings of freedom that I have here. The freedom to be myself. The freedom to do what I have only dreamed of. If being free means I must wear a symbol of oppression, I will do so gladly. For, obscured by a thin piece of black silk, I will be more visible than I have ever been before.
