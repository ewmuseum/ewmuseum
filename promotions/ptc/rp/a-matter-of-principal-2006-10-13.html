Date: 2006-10-13 I think it’s time we had a little chat, PTC. Whaddaya say? I want to touch on a little piece of public perception for a minute, maybe alter your perspective on what really happened in GTT5, maybe not. I’m not your fucking mother, so I really don’t care either way. Point is, people say I set a new bar last year, that I molded some new kind of standard. They even go so far as to convince themselves that it could happen again. Newsflash, folks. I didn’t set any new bar. I took what was there and launched the damn thing into space. Wanna go find it for me? I won’t sit here and tell you how many titles I’ve won. I won’t list the names of men I‘ve beaten. I won’t try to get your attention by parading the past around like a high-dollar hooker in the hopes that you’ll come to loathe and fear me. I don’t need to; I know you already do. Besides, the whole "f3r my accolades" thing is Seymour’s shtick. ~<>~<>~<>~ "Do you remember the story of the Prodigal Son?" With a tender caress that only a father of seventy-three could possess, The Prophet placed his hand on the nape of Jeremy’s neck. His eyes softened as elderly reflection mellowed across his brow, blending their brown wisdom with an empyrean patience so profound that it stopped Jeremy’s heart cold. Shivering naked in the grip of the crisp October evening air, Jeremy nodded. "The gospel of Luke is a fascinating lesson in love," said The Prophet. In a rare moment of paternal humility, The Prophet sank to his knees on the uneven pavement, meeting his son nose-to-eyeball. "You see, God granted a man two sons, both raised and adored in equal favor. The youngest son took his father’s generosity for granted, spending his inheritance on foolish pleasures and pointless trinkets until he found himself poor, starving in the streets of a foreign land. He thought to himself, ‘Why should I starve when my own father’s servants have enough food to feed themselves, and shelter to keep them warm?’ You see, it was his pride that robbed him of nourishment, in both body and spirit. "When the youngest son returned to his father, he begged for forgiveness and asked to be made one of his lowly attendants in order to work off his debt. Do you know what his father did then, my son?" Deep confliction raged for a moment and was gone, disappearing in a swirl of conviction that slept just beneath the stronghold of The Prophet’s unequivocal faith. Some would call it more a stranglehold on sanity; those weak enough to believe such nonsense were dead. Jeremy was a handsome young man, father of seven children, husband of three wives. His tear-streaked face shook slowly, possessing now all the authority of one of his own offspring. The Prophet smiled. "He forgave him, of course. Without question, without hesitation or condition, the father loved his son as he always had and absolved him all his wrongdoings. The eldest son was, of course, jealous of this apparent favoritism his father bestowed upon his youngest. He had never disobeyed, never failed to honor his father’s love, and felt that his younger sibling was unworthy of that very same love. Do you think you are unworthy of my love, Jeremy?" The sobs came, quiet and ragged. Jeremy nodded his head as he whispered, "Yes I do." Lifting Jeremy’s wet chin with the tip of his index finger, The Prophet peered into his youngest child’s dilated pupils. Untold eons passed in the span of a heartbeat as they reflected upon one another. The Prophet finally nodded. Delivering an open-palmed slap to Jeremy’s cheek that knocked the young man’s head clear around his shoulders, The Prophet stood. "You have made a mockery of the Principal, Jeremy. You may mock my love, but you will not mock my God." Waving a hand to his firstborn son Markus, The Prophet accepted the coat that his third wife placed around his shoulders as he began to walk back to his limousine. Having been patiently standing at attention until now, Markus gave a nod to the drivers of both black H3 Hummers that bathed Jeremy with their halogen gaze from either side. Chained between them, Jeremy began screaming wildly as the vehicles started backing up. ~<>~<>~<>~ Rust? Rust is for bike spokes and shitty metropolitan town cars that get left out in the rain. Trying to keep her award-winning false smile strung tight across her face, the waitress began to reach for the bowl. "AAAAAAHHUUUUUUU!" came the dementia-ridden reply. Trying to work her spoon into a thick bowl of tomato soup, the senior citizen looked at her wrinkled husband helplessly. He stopped, shrugged, and continued to shovel a greasy chicken fried steak into his denture-free mouth. The waitress stopped, beaming another flimsy grin as she took a step back. "Let me get you some slices of bread to help soak some of that up then, okay?" She turned quickly, trying not to act too stiff as she plunged back into the safe haven of the kitchen. Rich Rollins watched it all in silence, trying to chew on the rubbery remains of his catfish filet. Sitting alone, he had nobody to share the special scene with except his own waitress, a chubby thirty-something that looked about ready to pull a gun on the next jackass who decided to stiff her. Rollins thought he might leave her ten percent just to test this theory. "You’re overdoing it, honey. Leave the poor girl alone." Pushing his puddle of meat around in circles, the old husband sighed. His wife’s creaking jaw, which not one moment before had been lolling its purple tongue like a lunatic, closed. The thousand-mile stare left her eyes and her back straightened. "It’s embarrassing." "I hate her youth," she grumbled. Spooning a shaky payload of tomato soup into a mouth twisted with contempt, the old woman turned her gaze to the peach-colored tablecloth. "These kids don’t notice us, anyway. They’re too busy necking on street corners and giving fossils like us the finger." Rich Rollins ignored them. He had his own problems, none of which revolved around some baby boomer’s bitter prejudice. Finally giving up on his catfish, The Renegade turned back to the reason he had sought out this cheap and semi-private establishment in the first place; clearing his head in the middle of a crowd was somehow more manageable at your friendly neighborhood IHOP. He didn’t know why. Maybe it’s the crepes, he thought with a smirk, but he doubted it. He received a letter this morning. It stuck out from the rest of the usual Wednesday pile of billing invoices and hate mail (which were one and the same as far as he was concerned) because it came with a curious name attached to the letterhead: Benjamin Holly. Rod Blagojevich, governor of the great state of Illinois, was looking to get re-elected next month. Benjamin Holly happened to be the only red-hot Republican who stood any real chance of deposing the Serbian select. Rollins didn’t much care for the prick personally (either of them, for that matter), but the name on the stationary certainly caught his attention. The message it contained, however… It wasn’t from Holly. It was from one of his secretaries. Dear Mr. Rollins, My name is Lori Goodwin. I am an administrative assistant for Mr. Benjamin Holly, the future governor of Illinois. I graduated from the University of Chicago with a B.S. in Political Science. I am forty-three years old and have led a life far less public than yours, but it has been a good life nonetheless. I was adopted when I was two years old to a loving family who were close friends of Mr. Holly’s. They did very well by me, as you can plainly see. I tell you these things because of what I read in the Tribune last March. I’m sure you know what I’m talking about; having your mother’s remains defiled in such a way would break many men, but not you. For the record, I don’t believe you killed that girl and her baby. This isn’t an official request by Mr. Holly or anything, so please don’t get the wrong impression. I’ve been meaning to write you this letter for months, and would have done so using my own address, but I’m in the office all the time these days what the election coming up and all. This is me, Lori Goodwin, asking you, Richard Rollins, to meet me in Grant Park on October 11th at three o’clock. Buckingham Fountain should do just fine. I’d ask you to come alone, but I think we both know that wouldn’t be much of a problem for you anyway. -Lori It was almost cute. Some forty-three-year-old orphan had a crush on ‘ol Rich Rollins and wanted to give him the official downtown tour. Maybe coffee at three thirty. Maybe sex in the fountain at three thirty-three. Who knows? Maybe it was time to add "exhibitionist" to his ever-growing list of nasty nicknames. It sure as shit beat baby-butcher. "AAAAAAAHHUUUUUUU!" The woman again. This time she added a pitiful little wheeze for extra flavor. The waitress turned bright red and nearly dropped the hoary old hag’s cold coffee down the front of her apron. "Oh for fuck’s SAKE!" Rollins roared. Kicking himself back from his table, The Renegade turned on his heel and was sticking his finger in the woman’s gummy face before anyone had the chance to blink. Caught with her mouth open, the old woman quite nearly bit off what remained of her rotting tongue as she sucked it back between rotting teeth. "Look here you cunt-dried crone, nobody in this restaurant gives two shits about the fact you lost your will to live around the Taft administration, but you need to scoop your sagging tits off the floor and start acting like a goddamn human being. It’s not enough that you baby booming fucks use up all our social security, but now you want to make us feel awkward just because you can’t take a shit anymore unless you throw some laxative in your applesauce? Christ, lady. Loosen your fuckin’ Huggies so you can yank out that dialysis prod every now and again. Fuck." Sound stopped. Mouths dropped. Eyes blinked. Somewhere Lewis Black was laughing. "Sir," the waitress whispered as she took a mental step backward, "I think it’s time for you to leave." Rollins shook his head and offered the old couple a dismissive wave of his hand as he headed back to his table. A dozen heads followed him in perfect unison. Tossing a wad of cash on top of his catfish, The Renegade began making his way for the door. "You should make the change official," he offered just before slipping outside. You’ll excuse me if I cut the politically correct crap. The truth hurts. ~<>~<>~<>~ A dove bowed its head against the quiet rain, perched under the watchful stone gaze of Abraham Lincoln. Tucking his hands into the pocket of a dirty gray hooded sweatshirt, Rich Rollins observed the avian symbol of peace and prosperity with profound amusement. He wondered which of the after-hours joggers was going to get shit on next. Taking his seat at a lonely park bench, The Renegade watched the slender jets of pond water crisscross one another through the heart of Buckingham Fountain, nestled in the middle of Chicago’s stunning and sprawling Grant Park. Pulling his hood tight around his ears, he waited. Ten minutes later, the woman of the hour arrived. "Richard?" She was a tall thing, wrapped in a tight leather smock that accentuated few flaws and many assets. She was only an older woman by definition; if Rollins didn’t know any better he would have figured she was barely thirty. She moved at a confident gait, radiating poise and professionalism with each subtle sway of her slender hip. Long auburn hair was tied back with a scarf; she looked regal in the way domesticated cats look when they sleep. Rich Rollins noticed these things, but not before he noticed her face. "Oh my god," he breathed. "You… you look just like her." The jaw line. The nose so petite it was damn near breakable. Thin lips that frowned, even when she smiled. Everything about this woman opened up a box in Rollins’ brain, setting loose a flood of memories he had long since stuffed away. "You look just like my mother." He was right; the frown-smile was identical. "Hello Rich," she said. "I’m Lori, your older sister." Doubt and hesitation destroyed one another in a cataclysmic catfight that blinked across Lori’s face like a shadow. It was more than that; looking up, Rollins saw that his dove had just flown overhead. An odd sensation churned Rollins’ stomach, winding it like a Jack-in-the-Box as he waited for the inevitable pop. He simply sat there, dumbfounded, unable to process what his eyes and ears were telling him. It absolutely couldn’t be true. Could it? Immediately offering her a seat next to him, Rich Rollins moved over as Lori Goodwin settled herself. "Of all the things I expected from some stuffy bureaucrat’s secretary, this was definitely not one of them," he said, which made him feel both incredibly vulnerable and extremely stupid. "I mean… oh hell, I don’t know what I mean." Crossing her ankles and placing her hands in her lap, Lori shrugged. "It doesn’t matter. I’m just glad you showed up." Rollins inhaled sharply, trying to break a suddenly awkward silence. "I guess you should start with the obligatory back story, don’t you think? I mean shit… how long have you known? I never even knew I had a sister, but knowing my mother it doesn’t surprise me." "At least you knew her," Lori said. "I didn’t try to find my birth parents until last year, but by then Margaret Rollins was already dead. I started watching you on television for a bit, but I never thought I’d gather the courage to contact you. Or, like I said, that you’d actually show up. You have problems with that from time to time, or so I hear." She grinned again, and in the face of that familiar smile he couldn’t help but laugh. Rich Rollins had never really bought into the whole universal family ties concept that people were always talking about on Oprah, how two siblings separated at birth could just know that they were kin the first time they met, but maybe it wasn’t a bunch of estrogen-centric bullshit after all. He could feel this. And goddamn, he couldn’t deny that it felt good. There weren’t many happy memories hiding in the branches of the Rollins family tree. "Well, I wish I could pull a few pretty pictures from the family photo album, but the truth is you didn’t miss much," Rollins said. "Mom was a hopeless drunk who clung to her welfare checks more tightly than she did to me or Jason, and we were dirt poor until the day I moved out to go and beat people up for a living." She nodded as if she understood, but Rollins got the impression that she wasn’t really listening. She had something else on her mind, and Lori didn’t let the silence linger for long. "What about your… I mean, what about… our father?" A strange look took residence across her brow. It wasn’t quite desperation, but it was something very similar. "I didn’t find much information on him, other than the fact you were named after him." He tried to stop himself, but Rollins’ eyes still made an exasperated loop in their sockets. Richard Rollins Sr. wasn’t exactly a subject he dwelled upon, well… ever. It might not be right to pass judgment on a man you hardly knew, but it sure was easy. "He was gone by the time I learned how to ride a bike," Rollins replied, trying to quell the cacophony of angry expressions that bubbled in his brain every time his father sprang to mind. "I didn’t know him at all, really. He was the perpetual out-of-towner kind of dad, you know? He met mom in the sixties and split by the time I was six. End of story." Lori nodded, biting her lower lip. "I must have been a byproduct of the honeymoon," she offered, finding something interesting on the tips of her pointed shoes and staring a hole right through it. Rollins snorted. "Sweetheart, you were the byproduct of an all-night bender that began in a bar and ended in a bedroom. Considering our mother never mentioned you at all, I’d be thanking my lucky linens that you were fortunate enough to come out here and meet me in the first place. You could have been a fetus in a wastebasket." Lori flinched at that. So did Rollins; he had to remind himself to behave. He had this nasty tendency of turning belligerent and abrasive whenever he felt anxious or uncertain. This whole situation provided plenty of both. "Sorry," he mumbled. "It’s just kinda my way, you know? Some head-peeper once told me it was a defense mechanism. Or something." Turning to face her brother, Lori Goodwin placed a hand on Rollins’ shoulder. It was cold; he tried not to shiver and failed. God, what was wrong with him? "Would you want to meet me again, maybe?" Her voice contained the first glimmer of hope he had witnessed all day. When he saw the genuine desire blossom as her irises inflated, full of that strange need that he still couldn’t place--desolation?--the battle to resist was all up hill. When she added a heartfelt "Please?" it was all he could do not to stumble and roll. Staring off into the misty circus streams of Buckingham Fountain, The Renegade nodded his head. "Okay."
