Whirligig, 01.

4 December 2005

Date: 2005-12-04 # This is what happened. # Catherine Smith ran off with Walter James a few months ago. This would have been fine, certainly—no one would care, no one would interrogate her for it—but six years earlier she walked down the aisle in a white dress and married Django Smith. So, because of that, someone did care, and had Django been able to find her, he would have grilled her ass under the torrid light of an antique noir lamp in a black-and-white room until she sweated out all the answers he wanted, needed. And once those answers were his, once she told him candidly that nothing happened and she was relieved to see him after all this time, once husband and wife reconciled in a tender embrace kindled by an ardent kiss, once he decided to take her back, his life would go as the crow flies: straight and unremitting. All of that, though, was what would have ensued had he found Catherine. Instead, he suspended the search for her after a fortnight, positive she would come home, told his friends and family to take a break, to on no account bring it up in conversation, and tried resuming living his idea of a pastoral life without her. And soon Django quelled that idea, killed it like an unforgiving divinity because some unseen presence whittled into his mind an incorrigible melancholy that estranged him from his loved ones, transmuted him into a sort of derelict incapable of bona fide communication. That pastoral life was nothing more than a veneer to wrap himself in, and was shredded easily with the more imperative things his mind was preoccupied with . . . like wondering if his wife was alive, just what she was doing if she was, what Walter James was doing to her, what they were doing together. There were a lot of things he could have been anxious about, but what tyrannized his thoughts most often was the very cogent likelihood that the two had fucked. And he couldn't take that. The mere thought of Walter stroking in and out of Catherine and having his way with her made Django feel less than a man. Made him outwardly stoic to the point of redefining apathy. So this is where he is now: a thirty-year-old man with his life arrested in a terminal mutation of a disease more apt to vilify high school kids; a thirty-year-old man all right externally, all wrong internally, probably wanting his wife back only to appease the pretense of heroism—intrepidly getting to his feet again in the menacing face of adversity to steal back what was stolen from him through any means necessary. But there was no way he would tell himself that. He would tell himself that he loves her and always has, that he never wanted to be a hero and always felt fine as a normal guy, just as long as his wife was by his side and loved him, too. And he was sure that she loved him, that she only ran away to test the potency of their Herculean relationship. Or someone kidnapped her. Took her right off the streets, came to their home, thumbtacked a note that read I'm leaving you to the door, and overlooked mentioning a ransom just to dilute his mind. He was completely positive. Never a doubt or a suspicion of his own faults. # The fluorescent lights washed the circumscribed bathroom in a surreal white, everything suddenly both pristine and algid. The long mirror on the wall no longer resembled a mirror; it showed now a true doppelganger in place of a reflected image, the doppelganger seeming able to reach out and throttle the staid Django right there. Django looked down at his hand, looked at how it shook impetuously. He tried stabilizing it with his other hand, but it shook, too. A floodgate opened again and let out what was left of his adrenaline, let it race down his veins and put his heart into another gear. His body was on a horrible overdrive, shaking loose any fetters the nervous system had on it. He glanced up at the doppelganger in the mirror and then his vision blurred, eyes shivering too rapidly to allow any semblance of focus. And he couldn't have done it. He kept coming back to that: he couldn't have done it. Hell, his mind was an impregnable stronghold that would have exiled such a thought. And even if a leak had sprung . . . he wouldn't have gone through it. There was no way. He leaned against the bathroom sink, perpendicular to the mirror. He didn't want to see himself. Instead, he wanted to go to bed for the night, wake up, find out that this was all some sort of fever dream. And what he wanted more than that was dreamless sleep because dreams could mutate into nightmares, and he knew what would pollute his unconsciousness. That slick asphalt. That woman who was Catherine one minute and a stranger the next. Then bashing, bashing, and bashing her head into the pavement, whispering to her how much he didn't want her to leave him again the whole time. Her trying screaming and scratching his arms raw, him slamming her head into the pavement again, shushing her gently, watching their blood mix into what he thought of as a misogynistic stew, ending her life with his late father's pocketknife. And what had he done with her body? # Django and Catherine sat together on a blanket before the bay, letting the calm breeze caress them like another lover. She leaned against him, head on his shoulder; he stroked her light hair absently, focused on tracing the bight with his eyes. Noticed how the bay's gentle curves resembled those of his wife. Cherished all of this. "Two years," she said. "Can you believe that?" His lips pulled into a smile, definitely believing it. He always believed they would make it, believed that no number of problems was so heavy to snap their bond in two. And never had the idea of anything but death separating them crept into his mind. No, their vows would hold like a relic. "Is this romantic?" "You're asking me?" "Yeah," she said. "Is this romantic?" "It's the bay," he said. "We've been here before." She wrapped her arms around one of his, looked up to his eyes. "But it's nighttime . . ." He looked at her, smiling. "Night's romantic? But it's daily. Well, you know." "Just agree with me." "Night's so romantic," he said. "Almost dreamy. And goddamn hunky." "You'd never say that if you weren't so sure you'd get laid tonight." Their laughs floated out over the little ripples in the bay. And for the rest of the night they were mostly quiet, secure in the other's presence. # Through fault of design, the Smith living room was the darkest room in the house. Its low ceiling made fans impossible and any bulbed light seemed both maladroit and malapropos. Floor lamps made the family ill at ease, the meager space between the top of the lamp and the ceiling a constant reminder that they were just buoyant enough to keep from drowning in penury. But the television gave the room a thin light now. Its neon glow spread more forward and upward than it did outward, putting dancing shadows across the ceiling, making it look overcast as a metropolitan winter afternoon. Carlie Anne sat in front of the TV in her deviant way, bolt upright on the sofa, regarding the news with a weary consideration. Something concerning a forthcoming storm making holiday travel dangerous—capped off by the depressing death toll of traffic accidents last year—segued into a stolid, almost glacial piece about how a California teen with an internet journal killed himself over unrequited puppy love. "That's just a terrible thing," said a news anchor, straightening his papers in a manner whose impassivity only his line delivery rivaled. Then, after an appropriate pause: "More gang violence erupted today in . . ." Can't look to the news for anything uplifting, she thought. Have to write your own stories for that. "You shouldn't watch the news around Christmastime," Django said quietly, materializing beside the sofa and just out of the lambent glow of the television. "Oh, Jesus," Carlie Anne said. "Try knocking next time, asshole." "Just came by to warn you about the news," he said, sitting down next to Carlie Anne. "You know, it does nothing but depress people, hon, and that makes them less liable to buy me gifts . . . which causes many industries to topple over into a black hole of marketing failure that both misaligns the cosmos and makes Santa Claus weep." "Perfect," she said. "Consider my depression the reason you don't get a present, then." "And I'm the asshole." "Pretty much," she said. "When did you get in?" He took a cigarette from behind his ear and tried lighting it, couldn't, muttered a swear, and hunched down to try again. It caught this time. He took a long drag off of it. "Oh, just a minute ago." Carlie Anne pointed to the cigarette. "Hey, Dave. Uh, you mind?" Django looked up, exhaled. "No, no. Help yourself." She took the cigarette from his fingers, body language so withdrawn it belied her normal attitude; she was certain in her mind someone stood in wait outside the room, prepared to walk in and exhaust this taboo of its on-edge excitement just as she inhaled the smoke. But she expelled the thought and sucked in all the carcinogens she could, flicked the remnant ashes into her other hand. "You mind leaning back?" "What?" "Lean back, please," he said. "You sit too straight and it bothers me." She took another drag. "It's called good posture." "Wreck it, please." Exhaling, she leaned back against the cushions. She thumbed the volume on the remote control as another anchor dragged so-called hazardous toys and violent video games through the mud for sake of some coalition of parents dedicated to ending entertainment. "Can't you buy good cigarettes?" she asked. "Pall Malls are so shit." Django arched an eyebrow. "I hadn't realized you were in a position to be choosy." "I'm not," she said. "I'm just offering constructive criticism, you know? It's something we artists—well, me—use to improve what we do so people don't bitch about it in the future." Django offered her a blank countenance, then turned to the television. "I mean, probably I should just shut the fuck up and be grateful for you, right? But, see, my other pair of lips sometimes needs to voice an opinion. Tragedy of tragedies, it doesn't have a tongue, so the message goes up to my mouth and I bitch." Not looking at her, Django said, "I don't know how we're related." "In other news," an anchor said, "a woman reported missing early today by her husband—is still missing. However, the anxious husband identified his wife's car in a parking lot. Investigators found blood . . ." Django didn't breathe. His ears muted the television and he went back to last night, went back to bashing that woman's head into the parking lot. All of it replayed to him in fast-forward. And he still didn't know where the fuck he put her body. ". . . related," his sister was saying. "Where were you last night, anyway? You missed movie night. A good one, too. I forgot the name, but it had Sean Penn and that Naomi girl. I think the title was about coke. Well, I'm pretty sure it was. The movie had it in it, but not much." "Sorry—" "It's in my room if you want me to get it." "No, no," he said, swallowing hard, trying to seem casual. "I'm good." "So where were you?" "I was out with some friends." "Oh, that restaurant?" she asked. "What was it called?" "No, we hung out at a bar this time." "Oh." She took another drag off the cigarette and put it out on her jeans. "But isn't that sad?" "What?" "That," she said, pointing to the news. "Girl was missing and a whole bunch of blood was splattered all over her car. It's sad, huh? Poor lady. Bet her husband's sad, too." "Oh," he said, uneasy. "Definitely sad. It's a shame when that stuff happens. I couldn't imagine." #
