The Story of My Years (1)

14 April 2005
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2005-04-14 I am Toradio Gonzalez, I am nineteen years old, and I am a professional wrestler. I come from a small village in Mexico named Punta Molas, an ocean town with it’s own poor and impoverished. I know little of my birth parents minus the fact they fell into this category in the town. My childhood seemed good, or at least what I can recall of it, playing football in a small patch of grass in town with the other children, I made my parents happy with my actions. I was always respectful of them; I thought I made them happy. When I was five years old rumors were spread around town that a rich, important American was coming to town. The town was confused, why would an important American come to our small town? Did he want to start a business here with a factory? Did he want to help the town’s largely poor citizens out? As the day drew nearer signs had been posted up around the town celebrating the arrival of this man. It was like the Aztecs celebrating the arrival of Cortez. We believed this man was sent by our Lord and the Virgin Mary to help us, to enrich our lives with what our neighbors to the north in America have. We were wrong. Finally this American came to town, he stood in the village square as an assimilated Mexican American stood beside him to translate. You could almost feel the uneasiness in the crowd as we waited upon this man to begin speaking to us. My parents were extremely excited, a possibility of a place to work, a possibility of a place for me to work when I became older. The possibility of having enough money to send me to college… This man looked like he had the ability to make this happen. Then when he began speaking he spoke of heartache, trust, and becoming famous. He talked about men like Mil Mascars and the Guerreros. He spoke of Andre The Giant, this man spoke of legends of wrestling. Then he smiled. His idea was simple, the parents would bring their young children in front of this man and he would judge them on their future potential of wrestling. The size of their parents and the size they were already at would be factored in. The pay off? Twenty-Five thousand dollars, American. Every person assembled in the village square that day looked at their own children, they would be gone for thirteen years, but it would pay off huge as their child had a chance at becoming rich, famous. It was a simple thing, and my own parents looked at me. At five years old I knew what I had to do. At five years old, living on a dirt floor in a one room shack with two sisters and your parents doing odd jobs to make ends meet, you’re a bit more mature than the average overweight American five year old. It almost seemed as Mr. Thompson stepped down from the stand, the entire village exploded to their own huts. My parents grasped my hands and walked with me down to the ocean, as I bathed in the salt water. Every slight scrape burned like crazy but I didn’t sob, I didn’t make a noise as my mother washed me. A tear trickling down her face. “Por qué, usted grito sirve de madre?” I asked quietly as the calm waves of the Pacific Ocean slapped against my chest. What I had asked was simple: Why do you cry mother? Her coal brown eyes stared into mine, as she swiped the salty wet hair out of my face. Even though she never said a word, now that I think back to that image of my mother I realize now that she worried that she may never see her first born son again. The rest of the process went by slowly as neither of us uttered a phrase. My mother and I came forth from the ocean with smiles on our faces as my father met me with one of his own. All three seemed to be made of plastic, all too fake even for my own young naive mind to understand this. As we entered the hut, my mother slowly made up our usual dinner. Tortillas with beans and corn, it was a simple yet nutritious meal that she labored over every day from the break of dawn. They weren’t the usual quality but everyone ate quickly, the feeling was that we were late. When weren’t we? My mother had decided to stay behind, the next part of the voyage was going to be the roughest on her she thought. My father carried me on his back as he walked to the village square, the line of people seemed huge as my father and I slowly trekked to the back. We could see one family already standing beside the large American, as we continued rapidly through the line, many disgusted faces appearing as people began to walk home. Slowly the line moved closer and closer, I could feel my palms as they began to persperate. I had an idea of what needed to be done, but I also knew I didn’t want to leave my mother and father. Finally the moment came, as I looked at the family that had stood beside Mr. Thompson the entire time. His father was a large man, it was a known fact in the village that he once competed in the Olympics. Part of me was happy, part was sad as I knew I wouldn’t be leaving my parents, but on the other hand I knew that they would still be living this life of hardship… Mr. Thompson walked over, I noticed he was a huge man clearly almost six foot seven. He had an inviting smile on my face as he looked me up and down, he began questioning my parents about sports I was involved in etc… I don’t know what he saw in me that day and I never truly will have a clue why he chose me over the other boy with the wrestling pedigree, but he managed to pick me on that hot Mexican day, I was sad but at the same time I was happy. I wouldn’t be for long though. Enter into my life Mr. Michael Thompson. ----5 Years Later---- A simple smile? Was that too much to ask for an eleven year old boy? I never thought it was, I never good see the harm in staring into the man who promised my family that he’d represent a father figure to me. He’d lead me onto a good life, and promised to make me a star. Instead of the simple smile I felt the whip come crashing down across my back. I could feel the leather tearing into my skin, first came the harsh burning… Then the Icy chill of my own blood. I stifled back my tears. If I cried the whip would only come down harder. I truly didn’t mind lifting the weights. I truly didn’t mind jumping rope. I truly didn’t mind working out on the punching bag. But training to be a warrior in Michael Thompson’s book was much worse. I sat silently on my knee’s in front of him. This I did mind.
