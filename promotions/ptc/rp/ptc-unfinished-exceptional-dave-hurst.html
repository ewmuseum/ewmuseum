Unfinished roleplay - Exceptional Dave Hurst EMERGENCY ROOM RADIOLOGY CAT SCAN It was dark, and that was all I knew for sure. The words flashed by me one after the other. I didn’t know where I was at first. I didn’t know why I was there. I didn’t know how I got there. I just remember the bold red letters of the white hospital signs. I remember corridors, and doors, and vending machines, and doctors, all blurred together and flashing by me in an instant. I remember the concerned stare of the blonde, freckled nurse looking down at me with those bold hazel eyes. I remember her echoed, distorted words, “This… hurt… little…” I couldn’t make out all that was being said. I don’t think I had access to all my faculties. I remember the needle, the sharp pin prick, followed by the blinding glowing light drifting away from me into a yellow haze. -----=====I=====----- The stare hit me first. The vicious, calculated, damning stare of the man about to give his message with the most conviction he could give. I knew that he cared about his job; I knew that he took every aspect of his job seriously. The conviction might have been genuine, and it might have been exaggerated in order to make me understand the severity of what I had done. It didn’t matter – I understood. A chilling wave of guilt hit me. This isn’t your regular guilt. This guilt feels like jumping into the cold sea headfirst, before bringing yourself to the surface as fast as you can, because your body cannot quite take the extreme change in temperature. You cannot take a full breath. You can only struggle as you take in gasps of oxygen; the whole time not wanting to show the obvious signs, so you hide. You duck your head, and swallow the dry, syrup of saliva in your mouth. I don’t think that I was fully conscious when those words were spoken. I don’t remember hearing it clearly. It was more a garbled, muffled distortion of a voice. I was drunk on the sudden realisation of what I had done. It was me. Until now I had been completely disconnected from the events of that night – but not now. “The jury finds David Hurst guilty of vehicular manslaughter.” -----=====I=====----- “I’m sorry David, but I don’t understand what this has to do with your association with The Pirates,” said the first detective, leaning both arms into the table in front of me and staring threateningly into my eyes. I stared back at his pasty, sweating, wrinkled face. I hate it when someone is threatening for the sake of it. It’s not like I’m a suspect. “Now, we’re going to ask you one more time,” said the other detective, standing behind the other with his arms folded, “what do you know about The Pirates?” “I don’t think you understand,” I told the two, “I never stepped foot inside prison. Not a moment after I stepped outside of that courtroom, I was acquitted of all crimes. I was exonerated.” “You were exonerated?” the detective in the background asked, perhaps not quite taking in what I was trying to tell him. “Yes!” I exclaimed, “I was exonerated – by the United States government. Apparently, I didn’t commit any crime at all. I took the fall for someone else, someone who the government didn’t want in the public eye.” “A member of The Pirates…” the first detective mused, leaning back in his chair. Finally, they were getting the idea. There is an emigma surrounding the terrorist organisation known as The Pirates. Those that are aware of them always have their own take on who they really are. Some think that they’re the SLA: The group famous for kidnapping Patty Hearst, and then brainwashing her into robbing a bank after her release. Hearst had her sentence commuted from 35 to just 7 years, and was eventually pardoned. She was exonerated just as I had been, possibly to cover up something bigger than her bank robbery. Some have said that they date back to Privateers: pirates of the 16th to the 19th century, handed letters of marque from the governments of opposing nations to attack their enemies. There’s no doubt that the government are up to some dirty stuff that we don’t know about. A terrorist organisation given under-the-table funding from government sources doesn’t sound all that far-fetched. They did it in the 16th to 19th century, why would they stop now? Sure naval warfare is more sophisticated now, and they most likely do not operate in that fashion anymore, but pirates were all essentially terrorist cells in those days anyway, and they all definitely exist today. The two detectives had gained all the information they needed. The guy in the back left the room, but for some reason the first detective stayed sitting. He chuckled to himself, knowing something that I didn’t. “Why, Hurst? Huh?” the guy asks, “why didn’t you just keep your mouth shut? The pirates…? The pirates are everywhere, son. They’re in every city, they’re in every office, and they’re even in every police station.” The detective, if he even was a detective, got up from his seat and moved over to me. He reached into his pocket and pulled out a black glock from his holster. My eyes were wide open. I could hear my heartbeat pounding through my body. I knew that I had to make a decision – fast. I need to stop this guy, but how? He has a gun in his hand. He could kill me in an instant. “It’s just a shame that you had to-“ I lept from the seat with my arms outstretched and just managed to deflect the weapon as he pulled the trigger. The bullet shot across my hip, tearing the fabric of my jeans and leaving a bloody gash in its wake. I continued to charge into the man, pushing him into the wall. He squeezed the trigger again, another shot ricocheted off of the floor to safety. I slammed my elbow into his jaw once, twice, three times. He slumped into the wall with his arms limp. I yanked the pistol from his fist and slung it across the room, then pushed the doors to the interrogation room open and begun my exit from the building. -----=====I=====----- Sorry, the rest is unfinished. I had other commitments, and in the times that I did have to write, I couldn't figure out where to take the storyline. It was a mess, and I could never finish the story off. Sorry to all involved.
