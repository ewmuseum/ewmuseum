Date: 2009-11-08 The Pawn Advances 1 The light bulb does not know its purpose. It could be in an old garage that hasn't been opened for twenty years, or behind red glass, warning of an imminent disaster. Electronic switches control the bulb's state, and guide whether it illuminates or is at rest. Even were the light were to somehow become self-aware, to see its surroundings and be able to ruminate on them, all it could tell is that it is a part of something larger. So it is with people. So many of them go through their lives unaware of who is manipulating them, forcing them to purchase products or buy in to styles and fashions. Some people might say that it is the marketers who run this world. They are the ones choosing what the populace wears and listens to. Yet the unseen forces that shape this strange little world we live in can come in various forms, and not all of them are well known. Some hide in the shadows, the kind of people who would cause conspiracy theorists to spontaneously combust with delight. They can move the pieces, their pawns, forward, prodding them into the right directions, but they should be careful. They are not alone. And should a pawn make it to the other side... 2 Reykjavik, 1972 – Site of the World Chess Championship match between American Challenger Robert Fischer and Russian Champion Boris Spassky. “He's threatening to leave, and we can't have that happen, not with everything we have at stake. The reputational damage to our country would be too much. We've invested a great deal in this match, in your country, and we don't want it to just be thrown away. For the amount we're paying you, I don't see much action. I'm just saying...” Clifford Walker's voice trailed off as he looked at the stoic demeanour of the Icelandic Council. As someone working for the CIA he was used to being listened to, especially in smaller countries, but there was something about these men and their unofficial organisation that unnerved him. “Do not worry,” replied Gunnar Arnisson, the largest member of the four-man council's arms folded across his barrel chest. “We are in contact with the arbiter for the match, and Fischer will come around.” “How can you know?” Gunnar just smiled, and turned his head to the eldest council member. Halldor Gudjonsson looked every one of his eighty years, but beneath the wrinkled skin and wisps of white hair was an aura of strength. His thin body seemed permanently taut, as if he could leap into action if required. Clifford was just happy he'd never seen him move at more than a slow limp. “We know things, Agent Walker. That is why you came to our country, and why your government has requested our assistance on several occasions.” The elder of the council spoke quietly, and it seemed to the American as if the heating had failed just for a minute. “Lothar Schmid is an acquaintance of ours. He will see that Bobby Fischer returns to the table and resumes his game against the Russian. Do not concern yourself with our methods, only with what you are giving us in return for making sure this proceeds in a fashion amenable to you.” “The...” Clifford paused. “The fiscal arrangements are being done via the central office. If Spassky loses, you'll all get paid however much you agreed in the first instance.” “It is not about the money.” “Well, I'm sure you'll get paid it all the same,” said Clifford. “I stand here with my son, my nephew and my grandson. We do this for the future of Iceland, and for our family. We ask for the money purely because it is the only currency that your government understands.” Halldor paused, but the CIA agent knew that he was not expected to fill the silence. “You will not tell anyone of this. You will report that we merely wanted money and prestige.” Clifford nodded, though he was sure that he'd get a few choice anecdotes to share with his colleagues before the pieces had made their final moves. “You may think otherwise,” said another of the Icelanders, who was approaching middle age, but his body seemed to suit him. His blond hair was tied back into a ponytail, and his striking features gave Walker a good idea of what Halldor had looked like a generation ago. “You may think otherwise,” repeated Lars Halldorsson. “But you will not tell a soul about this conversation. You'll tell them nothing other than what we want them to know about us.” As he spoke, the words became almost musical to Clifford. Some instinct in the back of his brain felt a sudden thrill of terror, and wished it could control his body enough to raise a weapon and fend off the linguistic assault. Bobby Fischer had just become the first person to ever defeat the Russian Champion while playing the Tartakover Variation and had taken a lead in the contest for the first time. He now only needed to match Spassky for the remaining games to become the first American World Champion of the modern era. “Did we really just do that?” asked Magnus. “This one was all his own work,” replied Lars. “Fischer is brilliant, but temperamental. Our main role is to keep him from running away or making the occasional blunder for Spassky to exploit.” “Is that not unfair to the Russian?” “Ah, my son, at times the world can be set against us,” said Lars. “Ultimately our responsibility is to ourselves and to our country. He was the youngest full member in living memory. Halldor himself had picked him out from a young age, saying that he could be the most Gifted member of the family yet. At just sixteen he was granted admission to the Council, but eight years on he had shown no signs of having a Gift, or even the kind of business and political acumen that Gunnar held. Still, sometimes these things blossomed later on in life. It was very rare, but sometimes it took a specific event in life to cause it. “So, will we support Fischer in the future? Do not get too attached to these people. Remember who and what you are. We are the architects of this country. A quarter of a million people, of our people, rely on the work that we do to improve their lives. Do we have no duty to them?” “No,” said Halldor. “No we do not. Magnus, you question well, but your sense of patriotism and history is still lacking. Perhaps with age and with study you will understand our role in life, and what we need to do. Our lineage is unique.” The youngest Council member considered this. He was slowly losing the hatred of being referred to as young or inferior and accepting that those with more experience often knew secrets that were not available to those still in the flush of youth. Magnus loved his father, and the devotion that Lars showed to the patriarch of the family was enough to keep him in line. “I will study further,” he said. “Thank you,” replied Halldor, and nodded in a way that made Magnus know that he had been dismissed. Leaving the room, he walked towards the cafeteria, giving silent greeting to those he recognised on the way there. He saw Fischer's bodyguard, Saemi Palsson, another man who was susceptible to Gunnar's specific charms. Even Bill Lombardy, the American's second, was there. They had not made specific contact with him, preferring to work more on the periphery, but he recognised Magnus as some small cog in the organisational machine. This wasn't some mental invasion such as performed by Gunnar, but the simple reaction of a young man to a girl he finds attractive. “Ummm... sure,” he finally said. “Thanks. Her face was perhaps overly thin, with a nose so small as to be almost classified as AWOL, but her large eyes and mousy hair set something afire in the Icelander. “I'm Magnus. I love to see new places.” Magnus smiled as he listened to her stories, and lamented that he was tied down so heavily to one island. 5 Washington, Present Day “A parking lot is such an undignified place to discuss matters,” said Lars. “Perhaps we can go somewhere more comfortable for you?” “I'm fine right here,” said Suzanne, setting her feet to move quickly if she would need to.
