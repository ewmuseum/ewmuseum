IG 37 - A Tale of Two Lies

2 October 2007
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
“So... why do you wrestle?” I asked. “To cow great men,” the Illusionist replied simply. We met in the smoking section (yes, they still exist) of a small diner off the 65 for the interview. My name is Kevin Johnson and I was working for a little wrestling magazine still trying to get on its feet. Nabbing the Illusionist on the eve of his second PTC Infinite Gauntlet match up was a big score in my book, but when the man arrived at the diner wearing a slick pin-striped suit and his demon-esque wrestling mask, I had to wonder if I’d made a mistake. “You just don’t seem like the type,” I replied, lighting a cigarette. “Did I seem like the type after my first victory?” he asked. He had a voice that you would expect from an on off-duty mime, and it was laced in a faint British accent. “That was surprising,” I told him bluntly. “Everyone had Sonny Silver pegged to bag that match. And your in-ring tactics are nothing if not peculiar.” “What can I say?” he overturned one palm. “I am what I am.” “And what is that, exactly?” I leaned over the table just as our food arrived. A hefty waitress with a sweet southern drawl dropped off a bowl of chicken vegetable for me, while my companion for the evening had decided on a late-night breakfast (“I keep odd hours,” he’d told the waitress while she ogled his mask). When she was gone, he folded his hands behind his plate and leveled his stare on me. “I am an illusionist,” he replied. “Right,” I tapped the head of my cigarette on the ash trey. “But I mean... who are you? What’s your story? Where are you from?” “Does any of that matter?” he asked softly. “If I tell you some grand story of my life, will that aid me in victory? Will it somehow make me a more conventional performer in your eyes? I assure you, Mr. Johnson, I have no interest in the conventional.” I studied him for a while before breaking into a small bit of laughter. “Alright, alright,” I gave in. “So you’re a true man of mystery. An enigma by choice. But you have an accent,” I pointed at him with my smoking fingers. “That tells me something.” “I have many accents,” he said casually. “Would you like to hear a few of them?” I frowned, hesitating with a follow up question. Or perhaps I did just want to hear a few of his accents. “This is just how the wrestling world is now,” I shrugged. “People want know who you are, and not just what you can do in the ring. They want to buy your merchandise. They want to read about you in magazines. Little kids want to emulate you. You might not want that kind of attention, but when you stepped into IG, you got it. Like it or not.” “So it’s a story you want then?” he humored me. “That’s exactly what I want,” I leaned in again and pointed at him. “Your story.” “My story is whatever I choose it to be,” he told me, finally starting on his eggs. “I could tell you that I began my life in World War II, as a Jewish prisoner in a Nazi concentration camp. That would be a good story, hm? I could tell you the tale of a lavish escape, where as a boy, I saved my entire family from the gas houses. It would be an incredible thing, I assure you. You would, however, know that I was lying because age has yet to take the color in my hair, or by the skin on my hand.” “Come on, man...” I leaned back, disappointed that he offered only more games. “Or perhaps you’d be more interested if I told you that I fought for the other side, for the Germans. If I told you that I eagerly stood in line to shovel more Jews into death stoves, as if they were coal. If I told you that by night, I dined with Hitler himself. On eggs.” “That’s not funny,” I said seriously, having a Jewish ancestry on my father’s side. “I’m rarely funny,” came the reply as he sipped his orange juice, poking at his eggs with his fork. “I think that’s the first bit of truth you’ve said all night,” I told the son of a bitch. He paused for a time, sizing me up like some demon measuring my soul for a suit. And then, if I didn’t know better, I’d say he was smiling beneath the shell of his mask. “Very well,” he said, surprising me with a chuckle. “If it’s a story you want, I’ll give it to you.” “The truth?” “That’s a subjective thing - truth. Of all people, an illusionist knows best that what is true for one man is not the same for another. I’ve made a life out of lying to people, you see. Or rather, out of making them see some truth that I, in fact, know is false.” With that, he waved the back of his hand at me, and when he turned it up to show me his palm, there was a small yellow flower in it. “But just this once, Mr. Johnson, I’ll allow you to request a story of me, and I’ll give you the events as I saw them.” “What story will you give me?” “That depends on the question that you ask.” I thought about that for a few moments while he went back to his late-night breakfast. I wondered which area of his life I should prod, and which area would lead to the most information. His childhood perhaps, but as a journalist, I knew that was probably a mistake, since that kind of information was good as a support story for a larger one. I decided, finally, that my question should be all-encompassing, and so I leaned back in our booth and gestured with my hands as I asked for the second time that night: “Who are you?” He paused on his eggs; he paused completely, actually, staring down at his fork or maybe at the table. And then slowly he looked up at me. “I am the Illusionist,” came the answer, and just before I began to complain, he went on. “But that is not my real name, of course. No, I’m afraid that story is a far less intriguing one. In fact, my birth name is an incredibly common one - both first and last - and my childhood was all together uneventful. I had no more problems with schoolyard bullies than any other child, nor was my family any disproportionate sort of dysfunctional. I was never molested by a family member, or anyone else for that matter, and had sex for the first time at the age of seventeen. But that turned out to be an embarrassing event, since the girl was twenty-one and a whore, and my mother found out. “You see, Mr. Johnson, my real story is uneventful. If you want the truth of it, I was born, I grew up, I’m wasting some time with you now, and someday I’m going to die. My story, or rather, my real story, has no more drama than your own, or anyone else’s for that matter. You may believe that you’re unique, but you’re not. Nor am I. Nor are any of your readers. Nor are the men I’ll be competing against tomorrow.” “They’d argue you on that point,” I said. “And they’d lose,” he shrugged. “They come up with eventful stories, I’m sure, and they dream themselves ‘special’ in one way or another. They believe they have stories that are worth telling, and yet I assure you that they do not. Their lives, I’m afraid, are as meaningless and finite as yours and mine. For what’s the result of it Mr. Johnson? Love? Happiness? Deeds that will live on passed you? I’m sorry, but no. In the end, we’re all just worm food. Another part of the eco-system.” “That’s pretty grim.” “I already told you that I’m not funny,” he replied. “But if I might, I will offer you a story. A lie, of course. An illusion, if you will.” “What’s the point then? If I know it’s a lie?” “When I was a child,” he went on anyway, “my body was scarred cruelly in a fire in my bedroom one night. My father set it, or so I’ve believed for all the years since. I woke to the terrible heat of the flames as they licked at my face, burning my bed sheets. I was nine at the time, so I cried and wailed for the world to save me, but no one came. It was the last time I ever cried.” “So the experience hardened you?” I asked. “The experience destroyed my tear ducts,” came the reply. “And so I grew with my scars and I began covering my body to hide them, more comfortable being the masked oddity than the disfigured oddity. You see, there’s a mystery in that, and those who hadn’t seen me might believe, if only for a moment, that beneath my mask I was normal. Perhaps even handsome. As I’ve alluded to tonight, Mr. Johnson, mystery is always better than the truth. “But there was a girl. One miss Angelica Houston, and she was intrigued by me. It was probably the mask, in hindsight, though that may be hard for you to believe. She was a young woman with a love for surprises, you see. She was also rather plain, and perhaps she held out hope that indeed, beneath my mask I was a Prince Charming, and had decided to hide my beauty from the world for whatever reason. Perhaps it was her only chance for a Prince Charming. Her reasons, I can not say.” “She dumped you didn’t she?” I asked him, intrigued now, be his story real or not. He hesitated before saying: “She did, but that’s predictable, is it not? She unmasked me on the night we made love and stared at me naked for a time. I could see in her face that she was battling with herself whether or not to go through with it, seeing finally, that I was no Prince Charming. But rather, I was a heap of shining flesh in unrhythmic patterns on top of regular flesh. She saw my face and the way my nose bubbled and how on of my eyes sagged. “‘I don’t think I can do this,’ she told me, and I assure you it broke my heart, and those words were crueller even than the fire that my father had set. And she turned away from me and wept, either out of pity for me or pity for herself - for the fact that I was what I was, and not what she had hoped. And that, so they say, was that.” “But wait a minute. Wait a minute,” my eyes narrowed on him. “She turned you down, but you told me that you ‘made love.’ Did you... You said that you...” Was he saying what I thought he was saying!? “I’ve told you two stories here tonight, Mr. Johnson,” he said quietly as he stood up from our booth. “And I leave you to ponder which one you prefer; the truth, or the fiction?” “I’m not sure I know which is which,” I told him, hoping now, suddenly, that it was the former, plainer one that was true, as he claimed it was. “Then my work here is done.”
