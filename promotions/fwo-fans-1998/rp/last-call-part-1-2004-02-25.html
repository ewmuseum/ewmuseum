Last Call - Part 1

25 February 2004
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
"Seems I'm not alone at being alone." -Sting Thelma was too much like her father. Everyone said it. Even Mark himself: only out of Windham's weathered red lips there was a touch of distant pride attached. He was alone in thinking similarities did the twelve year-old well. Habitually when disaster struck, the words were brought out of a cage by her mother, tucked away next to the cookie jar, only to be opened when a deeper explanation was to take the longer way around. Blaming Mark Windham was nice and neat. Not to mention, mostly right. Thelma was the sweetest girl in school, on the neighborhood block, in Sweetwater...in all the state. Her mom worked hard raising her right, which was away from her father, from violence, fear, and the Windham family ghosts. Thelma was born running. It was her best line of defense against the man she loved most. Mark never cut the cord. When the temperature in Texas reaches one hundred degrees and Windham's scars slow burn on a summer's night, he's back with his mother, reliving the fire that took her life. Back standing outside the family's house in emotional cement, motionless to his parent's cries, too afraid to rush back in and help. Back in some way letting them die. Everyone has a sob story, a cross to bear, an emotional hill to scale. Windham's disease is he can't climb. What kind of a mind hugs to the tragedies in life as comfort? Windham has: a Lost Soul who knows no better. Everyone carries a burden. Thelma's is she's too much like her father. The jar was last opened Monday when Thelma split an opponent's lip on the court. She's anything but athletically inclined. A year ago Windham's little girl wouldn't have been caught dead on a basketball court. She'd rather ride the horses on the ranch than shoot baskets and struggle playing man-to-man defense. Joined the team this year though, because her best friend Amy plays, and separation to a twelve year-old girl is certain death. The hell of private school is the insecurity in numbers. Thelma has to play, so baldy she does. She runs, looks the part, but underneath her game face, is a tongue-in-check demeanor. In an hour practice is over, two for the games, and court time is nothing if not fuel for the sarcastic wit Thelma and Amy are co-developing. It doesn't matter. Or, it didn't until the last game, when an 8th grader went all Rodman on her. Jessica Presley hounded Thelma all game. Hard reach-ins, low block outs, and accidental trips up court. In Jessica's mind, Pat Summitt could have been watching, and she wasn't making Coach Summitt's notebook as being weak. Late second half, Jess comes down from chasing a rebound without the ball and ends with Thelma in a headlock. The refs don't see, they're letting the girls play tonight, following the ball, too distracted by their own life to give a shit. Jessica tightens her grip for good measure then lets it go and stares. Only losers are weak. Mark knows what his girl does next. Inside of a belly full of sweetness is the rage, and anger that's helped her daddy make millions over the last fifteen years. Without looking, Thelma Louise Windham buries an elbow over Jessica's nose. And she wryly watches the thirteen year-old drop to her knees, white jersey covered in fresh blood, and Thelma knows the girl's in too much shock to cry, but give it a minute, and those damn tears will come. And Thelma wants to be there, hovering over that bitch as they stream down her face, so she can whisper in her ear, "Jessica, only losers are weak." Too much like her father, indeed. The moment's fleeting, and the sweetness soon returns, but Mark is there, hiding forever in his daughter, waiting to be pushed too far. It's the simple reason why his ex-wife won't let him see much of her, and when he does, it's always, by court order, with supervision, because in Jewels' mind, enough time with daddy and Thelma will become a f'king brute. The little woman knows better. She cries when the shock wears off, and asks God why the others can't see. She needs her father; if they had each other, those damn ghosts wouldn't stand a f'king chance. Apart, they ruin the lives of innocent people. Why can't anyone see the cure that Thelma so visibly does? There was one woman who saw, and empathized, one woman who could have healed the wounds of the Windham family, but daddy chased her away. Still, she's in touch with Thelma, because once you've met the sweetest girl in Sweetwater, you never forget. It's Valentine's Day and Thelma writes to her dad in the only form of communication they're able to get by with (emails). The love of Windham's life sent Thelma a pretty funny animated E-Card, one made out of claymation, with the characters from Home Movies (A Thelma favorite). "It's the coolest thing, daddy," Thelma writes, "I told her to send you one, but she hasn't responded yet. I'm trying to help here, okay? (wink) Have to go perform my community service. Hehe. Kidding. More laps to run. Ugh. Bye!" Mark Windham smiles, day made, and carefully tries to shut Thelma's email down. Mark's one of those who thought the world was coming to an end over the Y2K computer bug. To say he's computer illiterate is an understatement, but Thelma's teaching, one step at a time. He can check email now, and hit espn.com. Baby steps. Mark's got it written down, she wants him to buy a computer camera and sign onto an Instant Messenger service so they can video chat. Mom doesn't know of course, and they've agreed secrets between fathers and daughters are the best. He closes the email and instinctively hits the address book Thelma set up. Two email address. Hers, and Ivy's. A subtle hint, one even Windham got. He hasn't emailed Ivy yet. He's not sure if he should. Mark jumps out of the computer chair like a bat out of hell and randomly roams the office in his Sweetwater studio apartment. It's been three months since he's been in touch, six since he dented a chair over Ivy's head in Anaheim at a taping of CSWA Primetime. Six months. She's nearly died since then, but even that event left Mark not knowing what to say. If she ever felt the way he did, it ended that night in Anaheim, when the monster in a cage let itself out. Windham's blood boils, he takes two steps to the nearest wall and launches his closed right-fist clean through. The pain shooting through his nervous system signals a high probability of a broken hand, but Windham pulls it out and immediately makes the first hole a bedfellow. Two eyesores are better than one, anyway: Modern art for those who refuse anger management lessons. Emotionally cornered, Windham unleashes on the lives of those he loves. Personally broke, and professionally aggressively pissed: The Modus Operandi for the Lost Soul. Mark needed to run himself against a wall. Having just returned from GXW's Battleground Britain PPV where he narrowly lost a bloodbath to CSWA World Champion Dan Ryan, Windham was in the mood for another go around. He had Dan finished, but Hornet took his revenge. Hornet. If it weren't for him, Anaheim might not have happened. Paul was a complication Ivy never, and rightly, wanted to cross. If's, and but's...Windham throws himself back at his desk dejectedly. He's to blame. He never had the guts to bare his soul. He's never had the courage to find the strength in the darkness of shadows. It's too late. Potential and opportunity fled the night he choose life over his parents, and the years spent playing dad to his brother Troy when he knew he had no place pretending he was a man, a role model. When Mark had no place pretending he had a heart. Only losers are weak. There in the constant pain in Windham's gut, is a glimmer of what he prays is hope. Hope that it's not too late, because he's tired of losing, so tired of being too weak to put his demons to rest. If he types what he has to say, it'll take four hours. He grabs a loose-leaf notebook from a shelf on his marble desk and with pen in hand, commits to taking the first step. Ivy, I'm---- He's what? Sorry? How many letters have to start that way? How many more times can she spare the energy reading an apology from the man? He's an insincere bastard. If he meant it, he'd be with her today, on his knees, telling her how he loved her in a thousand different ways. But he's not. He's in Sweetwater, crumpling up the same letter she's read a hundred times before. Mark doesn't have the time anymore to play games, or give up. He's felt like shit the past few months, and it's more than just being beat up. Work in this business long enough you get to know your body very well. Windham knew something wasn't right. The first batch of tests were run earlier in the week, and his doctor in Sweetwater broke the news yesterday. There's a seventy-percent chance Windham has cancer. He's supposed to return Monday for more extensive tests, but he's decided he won't. Windham's dying, and he knows it. It's been a process thirty years in the making, only now...there appears to be confirmation the process is being sped up. Truth is: he's ready. But for Thelma, and any hope remaining, Mark will fight. The only disease Windham has to battle are those that have haunted him since childhood, the demons his father created and latched on to Mark as a boy. He's running from himself, from the CSWA, Ryan, his past life, and into Ivy's ground. Even if she's not there anymore, the spirit of her remains, and the time is now for Windham to welcome any life back in his Lost Soul. On Ivy's ground he'll prove himself (if not to her) worthy, and become the Once Great Man he had the potential to be. He hopes it's not cancer. Mark hopes he's not dying, but he's too scared to find out, too smart to waste time worrying about control he's never had. It is, what it is, his body and heart will tell if he's winning, or if he's still lost. Windham picks up his cell phone to return Melton's call. He'll do it, but for his own reasons. Does Ivy care enough to save him? . . . Does he?
