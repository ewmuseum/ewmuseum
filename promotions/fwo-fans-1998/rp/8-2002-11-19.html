previous parts: see-downfall archive "Ticket?" The pale sunlight dipped through the transparent clouds. The sky was blue with white imperfections scattered across. Birds chirped and flew in the midst of the fall morning. Trent stood in front of a man with a suitcase in one had and a ticket in the other. He handed the small piece of paper to the pudgy individual. Awful sadness overtook Trent as he was cleared to board. With a heavy heart, he stepped across the threshold. A new life was beginning. He threw his luggage into an overhead storage compartment and sulked into his seat. Trent's hand wandered across the fresh scar tissue. The wound was still a bit raw, but the memories associated with it brought far more pain. A small knap sack remained tossed over his shoulder. Inside, it contained a pencil and a notebook. The urge to write had disappeared with his first love, but something could always drag him back into the hobby. The train was silent but for the laughter of a few children in the background. All there was for entertainment was the speeding scenery. All this time left alone caused his contemplations to sprint through his mind faster than his visions of the land. Trent reached into the sack and pulled out the blank notebook and mechanical pencil. Fresh lead pressed against the paper, but his mind locked. It was then that the horrid scene replayed itself. The pencil jerked and scratched the once pure pulp until he had conveyed his story in some form. When he was done, he couldn't recollect a single word of text. Everything was written with passion. Grammatical and spelling errors were not worries. For once, it wasn't about the money or the success. It was about telling the story. "Everyone falls. The question isn't when or how, but what their reaction will be. Will you stand up and confront what pushed you down? Will you lie there and die? Or will you struggle in limbo? Me? I don't know where I am yet. Nothing special was to be expected from me at birth. In New Iberia, Louisiana, nothing great was expected of anyone. It was a little, suburban town with a slowly rising population, but a flat-lined economy. Filled with middle class people living in the mid-west, average was perceived as perfection by these people. And, growing up here, I thought average was the best that could be expected. In life, I held no grand expectations, which would make my future endeavors all the more thought provoking. I was born into a family with a mother and father, whom loved each other as much as could be expected, two brothers, one sister, and a dog. An "average" family, at least here in New Iberia. I don't remember my infancy. I don't remember my first years of school. All of this seems vague and unimportant at this time in my life. What I do remember is the horrifying image I watched in fifth grade. Blood covered the white tiling of the brick elementary school as one bigger, fatter kid kicked the grounded body of another child. Groups of other children gathered around to watch in excitement and horror. My mind had gone through a moral tug-of-war at this time. I stepped further into the circle that had been created and pushed the kid, whom was not much bigger than myself, to the ground. No one was there to congratulate me afterward. No one thanked me. Expectations were that I'd be viewed as a hero, but expectations are but nothing more than figments of a diluted imagination in New Iberia. I'd go through school like most children did: making friends, losing friends, doing schoolwork, and generally socializing. Junior high school would be where I met my world, my everything. I didn't know at that time, but I would make her the basis for my existence. She would, years later, become my inspiration and the string that tied together every fiber of my being. The name of this string was Diana Wakesfield. I met her in eighth grade during lunch. She was silent, but the description of her former self is not relevant. The moment we made eye contact, something surged through my veins. Diana shared her deepest feelings with me, and with I, her. There seemed to be some unwritten, unspoken agreement that we could trust each other with everything. Four years of high school would pass. I ended up dedicating my time to football and spending time with Diana. With what little time I had left, I would scribble down little tales of characters that I had created in a large notebook. No matter how indistinct the past may seem sometimes, I can still remember that book and Diana's smile. Before graduation, I submitted a column to the New York Times with an application to join their staff as a mail-in columnist. Surprisingly enough, they accepted my offer. Weekly pay came from each column I wrote, along with my biweekly check from my part-time job at Denny's as a waiter. I ended up finding more time to write during the football off-season. Before I knew it, I had a book sent off to a publishing company. "Bodies lay across a baron wasteland. Black buildings shoot up from the concrete streets, into the night sky. From the skyscraper watched a menacing man with a thick, black beard." The New War. Four months and 158 pages of writing would make me fifty thousand dollars. In a town of average people, I strived to become more than the norm, the expected. The odd part was that where no one else had, I succeeded. In a town filled with average people, I had become the one to make it. I graduated, but my diploma mattered very little. Doing what I had always wanted, Diana and myself lived better lives than we could have ever expected as eighth graders gazing into each other's eyes. With her paycheck from her job as a secretary, we were set. The memories become clearer each time I tell the story. I was sitting at my computer, working on my newest book. Taking a break, I was sitting in the leather recliner to the side of the television watching television. A loud crashing noise came from outside, and the human nature of curiosity told me to take a look. All that was visible, at least in my eyes, was the wreckage I saw before me. The green grass of the parks behind it didn't matter. The cars driving past didn't matter. The bloody, limp body of Diana was all that mattered. I ran forward. As I neared the pile of twisted metal, a large, fiery explosion spewed from the hood of Diana's car. I ducked to the ground, but the damage had been done. The remaining scar on my face would be a reminder at how I failed to save the woman I love. A reminder that, no matter how big a success I became, I would always be a failure. From October 28 to December 10, 2001, I'd spend my days rehabilitating my face, my ability to talk. Some of the muscles had been damaged in the left side of my face. Now, I looked as I did before, but with an ugly, bulging, long scar down the left side of my face. The friends had left me, but that didn't even matter. Diana left me. The story of the accident? Classic drunk-driver case. She was backing into the driveway when someone blindsided her on the driver's side. There was no chance that she could have survived. They never found the truck or the driver. My inspiration. My money. My house. All of it gone, but most importantly, she was gone. There was nothing left for me here, so I packed up and left. The train now barrels down the tracks, headed for Chicago. I don't know what waits for me there, but I'm going to have to forget what I left behind here. Everyone falls. The question isn't how or why... But can I ever pick myself up? All I'll ever be able to do is try. Try to forget Diana Wakefield. Try to forget Trent Harrison. Try to forget New Iberia. Try to be Downfall." The train stopped at some unknown area. A few more people stepped onto the train. Most stood up, but one of the women dared to sit next to the silent author. Trent ignored her presence, but handed her the book. She read the tale and finished, but before she could hand back the scripture, the man was no longer there. Trent walked around the streets of Chicago, homeless and penniless. No job would accept someone like him, but there was no job that would fit his needs. A job where people crowded him. A job where he went from town to town, encountering new places and new people at every bend. A job that would help him forget that poor town of New Iberia. Like a sign from some celestial force, a white flyer fell at his feet. Trent bent over and picked it up from the other random pieces of Chicago litter. An open invitation tournament to decide who'd be on the new World Wrestling Revolution roster. Although the violence aspect did not appeal, he knew this was what he was looking for. Little did he know that the tape would soon be back on rewind.
