Rejection.

16 January 2003
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
There was a small shack on the edge of a busy town. No one ever went there, because it wasn't really an important place. There lived a mother and a son of meager provisions. The only cash they had was the money made from the son's adventures. Months ago, a man in a plain overcoat came to their door and knocked diligently for a good ten minutes before the family awoke to see what was happening. When they answered, they saw nothing but the shadowed figure walking away in a distance. When they opened the manila envelope on their doorway, they saw an invitation to a ball the governor was holding at his mansion. The son did not feel as if he deserved to go. His tattered rags and disheveled hair would not suit well amidst the eloquent gowns and handsome tuxedos. Nevertheless, the mother persisted. "This is the only way any of those people will ever recognize us less fortunate people," mother said with a heavy tone. The son sat down and looked toward his dirtied clothes once more before venturing out the door. If he was going to make himself presentable, there was work to be done. A few houses over lived a retired tailor. The son knocked on the door and awaited a reply, but none was given. Seeing this as a test, he refused to move from the spot. His legs remained crossed for three days. Dehydration was kicking in as the retired tailor stepped out of his house to tend to a small garden he had taken as a hobby. "What are you doing out here, boy?" asked the tailor with a hint of discontent. The boy turned away for a moment, but the tailor invited him in. He fed the boy and offered him housing for the night, but the boy declined. He merely pointed to a suit on the wall that seemed to be going unused. The tailor's face contorted. "You want that old thing? It doesn't look too good, but yes, better than what we are stuck in. I will let you have it, as long as you promise to keep it in tip-top condition." The boy nodded and continued toward a blacksmith's house. For days, he waited outside. When the blacksmith left his house, he saw the boy shivering and gripping his stomach from hunger pains. Upon entry into the small log cabin, the boy pointed to a pair of worn leather shoes hanging on the wall. "I will let you have those as long as you promise to never wear them out in the rain or snow," the blacksmith stated. The boy nodded and walked back to his house with the suit over one arm and the leather shoes in his other hand. The mother went to market, allowing her son to get ready for the ball with the house to himself. The suit hung off of him like it was five sizes too big, and the leather shoes remained in his hand. When his mother arrived home, she looked at her child with tears in her eyes. She wept for a few moments, hugging him close before she released him and stared into his eyes. "You look so handsome. Now, go, and make sure to keep your invitation." The boy walked miles across the snow barefoot, his shoes remaining in his hands. He hopped over branches and paced over broken twigs to arrive at the foot of a mansion. His feet were a pale shade of tan with cuts and bruises scattered about. The bottoms of his pants were soaked in water from melted snow, and his face was bright wed with beads of sweat dripping down. The journey was hard, but he put on his shoes. Mansion doors opened and a beam of light hit the night sky. Glass chandeliers, silk dresses, dark black tuxedos, and silver platters of food made the scent of money fill the room.
