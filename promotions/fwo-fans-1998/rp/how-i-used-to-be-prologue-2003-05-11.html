How I Used To Be (Prologue)

11 May 2003
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
In his dream, Scott Wasner was back in Vancouver. Vancouver-not-Canada, Washington-not-DC, where he had grown up. From his birth until his eighteenth year, Vancouver had been the center of his life. He had watched it grow from a small, almost rural town to a city in its own right, and as it had grown, so had he. He had moved on in his life, but Vancouver was still there. Though he could not see all of it, he knew the town by heart. The fields, now sprouting stores and plazas. The old Freddy's, the Northwest mainstay and site of his first job, stocking shelves. Mill Plain Boulevard running through the heart of it all, and just an apartment complex to the west of it, Mountain View High School, home of the mighty Thunder (Thunder Power!) and the place where a young hotshot shortstop known to his friends as Waz had first drawn the attention of nationwide scouts in the mists of time, or 1989. He was at none of those places now. Park Hill Cemetery was also off of Mill Plain, small, unassuming, and dignified. Scott Wasner stood in front of two gravestones, side by side. ALLEN MICHAEL WASNER August 6, 1949 - December 14, 1979 and JESSICA RUTH WASNER January 14, 1950 - December 14, 1979 He could recall the memory of that December 14 as clearly as yesterday. He was four months into first grade, and finding out that it wasn't as bad as he thought it was. It was a Friday, and the last day before Christmas Vacation. It had snowed that day, the first snow of the year (and a joyous event, snow being so rare in that part of the country), and young Scott's parents had nearly given in to his wheedling that they all take the day off, as a family, and enjoy the snow. School was still on, according to the morning newscasts - as Scott would later come to decide, it would take a Siberian blizzard for Evergreen School District officials to call a day off, even the last day before Christmas Vacation. Scott was sitting in the third chair, middle row of Mr. Freeman's first grade class, listening to the teacher talk about math. He had never liked math much, but he could still do it, a fact he was proud of. As Mr. Freeman spoke, a tall, grave, balding man entered the room. Mr. Freeman stopped talking to the class and went over to talk to the man. After a moment, his face fell. "Scott... could you come here?" Both of Scott's parents worked at the same place, and as they drove there, a man who was speeding lost control of his car and bulleted out into the middle of an intersection. His car slammed into the Wasners' turning car, smashing into it almost head-first at over 50 miles an hour. Scott spent the next eleven years bouncing between foster families, where at best he was a nonentity and at worse a step removed from slave labor, before finally setting out on his own with a baseball scholarship to the University of Washington. Now he was back. He stood at the foot of his parents' graves, looking down at them as the wind blew leaves about his feet. As he watched, the graves started to move - stretching towards each other like taffy, then finally becoming one. He couldn't read the name on the marker, and reached into his pocket, pulling out the glasses he occasionally wore to read. The name was clear. SCOTT ALLEN WASNER November 1, 1972 - December 30, 2002 He took a step back and cleared his throat to scream... ----- (Thursday, May 8, 2003) "Chicken or fish?" Scott Wasner jerked awake in his seat. He looked around wildly, finally recognizing his surroundings. The stewardess in the aisle looked guilty. "I'm so sorry, I had no idea you were asleep... I mean - I, I thought - " Wasner shook his head. "No... no, it's fine. I should thank you, bad dreams." He shook his head again, clearing out the cobwebs instead of negating her words. "What... what was that you said?" She smiled sheepishly. "I asked you, 'chicken or fish?'" He looked at the wrapped meal on the tray on the cart she pulled. There was only one. He cocked an eyebrow. "Uh..." "We're taking a poll. I don't know what it is myself," she said with a grin. He laughed, genuinely. It wasn't the funniest thing ever, but nowadays, he took what he could get. "Put me down for fish. It's got a bit of that smell about it." "Good point. So can I actually get you anything?" He closed his eyes for a moment. "How about a Coke? In the can, please." She smiled. "You got it." She handed him a can. She stood there for another moment, and Wasner looked around the rest of the cabin. He hadn't paid much attention (or much money - he was in coach) when he came in, but the plane was nearly deserted. "So what is there for you in Miami, anyway?" she asked. He looked at her. She was in her late 20s, probably, brown hair and a cute face. His groupie-ing days had left him with a bit of a sixth sense about these things, and he tried to shut the conversation off as politely as he could. Whoever she was, she deserved better. "Visiting family. The wife's, actually. She went down a few days before me." "Uh-huh," the stew said, not sold. He looked at his finger. These things never went smoothly. He put on his best fake aren't-I-an-idiot smile. "Lost it a few days ago. Don't tell the other half." "My lips are sealed. You have fun down there with the, uh, in-laws." "If such a thing is possible." She gave him a polite smile and started down the aisle to the next passenger, a good six rows back. Wasner leaned back in his seat and tried to get a little more sleep before they landed, bracing himself for what dreams would come.
