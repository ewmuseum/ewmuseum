How I Used To Be (4/5)

17 May 2003
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Sunday, April 27 1:47 PM Miami, Florida It was as hot inside the office as out, Scott Wasner thought with disgust. Or damn close. One would think that an investigator with the apparent experience and ability of this one could at least afford AC. He wiped the sweat off his brow. "Anybody here?" he called. Nothing for a moment, then a hideously dressed man emerged from a back room. "Hey!" the man said. "How's it hanging, I'm Larry Zito, that's Larry not Barry, y'know what I mean, nobody ever confused me with a Cy Young winner ater all although I do play some mean softball, so how can I help you, I assume you've come here because you know my reputation, y'know what I mean?" He said it all in one sentence, with more than a touch of carnival huckster (as opposed to your garden-variety rambler), then looked at Wasner appraisingly. Wasner nodded after taking in the eccentric man for a moment. "Yeah, that's right. I've got a job for you." Zito sat down behind a desk. "So how did you hear about me?" "A friend." "A friend, I assume I did some investigating for this friend once?" "Actually I think you investigated him," Wasner said with a small chuckle. "I heard him mention you... hell, years ago. Tony Barrett." Zito sat up in the chair with a look of comprehension and mild surprise. "The pit fighter. He was one of the good ones. Smart too, got out of there before I filed my report. What's he doing nowadays?" "Owns a restaurant, actually." "Huh. Whoda thunk it. So what can I do for you?" Wasner outlined what he wanted in a few efficient sentences, and then added something. "This... this is very personal for me. We've all made mistakes, right? And we want to make them right. Sometimes... hell, you don't care. It's just a case to you, right?" Zito nodded. "Right. Every case is personal to somebody - somebody that's not me. You can't get personal in my business." He leaned back in the chair and ran a hand through his greasy hair. He was silent for a moment. "OK. You realize that in the course of this I might need to go a little... outside the lines... to get everything you need. You're OK with that." Wasner nodded. "And you're not a cop, not in law enforcement or anything." Wasner nodded again. Zito stood up with a smile on his face. "Wonderful, in that case I think we can work something out. I charge a standard, nonrefundable fee at the beginning, and then an hourly fee, waived if I don't find what you're looking for, but I'll tell you right now that's never happened." He named a price, surprisingly underinflated, and Wasner accepted. Zito shook his hand, and Wasner felt like rubbing on his jeans, but refrained. "Wonderful, I'm glad to have you as a client." "Yeah," Wasner said. "I am too." He was - mostly. ----- Thursday, May 8 3:34 PM The door opened on a young woman in her 20s, but it was not Stephanie Griffith as Scott Wasner had hoped. This woman had brown hair and a mousy face. "Hi... can I help you?" He thought for a moment. He had been ready for anything - anything but, well, somebody else. "Um, yeah. Is Stephanie home?" If she even lives here. Thanks, Mr. Master Detective. "No, she's at work right now. I take it you know her?" "Yeah, I'm a..." A what? "An acquaintance of hers. I haven't seen her in quite a while, and I wanted to drop by. I guess I should've called ahead, but I wanted it to be a surprise." The half-truths were coming without thinking about them, and he quickly decided to shut up before he said something really stupid. "Oh, where are my manners?" the woman said. "I'm sorry. I'm Laura... I'm her roommate. Please, come in." The doorway was covered by a small overhang, and the rain was only a minor annoyance, but he was still grateful that the invitation had finally been extended. The house was fairly small, but still a fine enough size for two or so people. It was decorated sparsely but classily. The living room had a couch, a TV, and... a crib. He looked at it, hardly daring to believe what his eyes told him. Laura saw him looking. "That's her son... Danny. He's a great kid. 3 months old next week - " She said more but he didn't hear her. "Can I... ?" he started to ask. "Oh, sure. He likes people, I'm sure he'll like you." They walked over and Scott Wasner got his first good look at his son. Danny had a beautiful face and a dusting of light blond hair. It was overwhelming. He had felt a lot of things in his life, good and bad, but nothing was like this. It pulsed through his veins faster and stronger - and better - than any drug. It was powerful - it was all he could do to keep from being knocked to his knees. Laura reached in and picked him up. His son looked at Scott quizzically, then broke into a smile. He reached out a small hand, and Scott, feeling like he was in a dream, took it. It was so small... "He likes you. Told ya!" He wanted to hold him, but didn't ask - he was still a virtual stranger to this person, and knew he was lucky to even have gotten here. "He's beautiful", was all he could say. She nodded. They stood silently for a moment, then Laura gently set Danny back in his crib. "What did you say your name was?" she asked. "I'm Scott." Even as he said it, alarm bells went off. "Scott," she said flatly. He could have smacked himself. "Yeah." "I should have asked earlier... I'm sorry, but you're going to have to leave. I don't know what you did, but Stephanie did tell me you might come around some day. You should go." She didn't have to add the obvious "or else." He looked at Danny in the crib. "i'm sorry," he said - to Danny, to himself, to Stephanie, to anyone who would listen. He looked up at Laura. "I'll go. Just... give her this." He reached into a pocket and pulled out something he hoped he wouldn't have to take out. He handed the letter to Laura, who took it. "And... tell her I'm sorry. For everything." He turned and left silently, only stopping to take one last look back at his son. His only hope now was that the words he hadn't gotten to say could still save him.
