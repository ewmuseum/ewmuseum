Blessing In Disguise

8 January 2009
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
January 3rd, 2009 Brawn sat in his parked car and observed two girls constructing a snowman. One of the girls leaned down and struggled to push the massive sphere forward in the melting snow. Inside, he sat in frozen vigil, looking down at the mask in his passenger seat. Two excited eyes frozen wide. He had spent 3 years under that green mask, and as he felt the cold air brush across his face-- he wished he was in its comforting warmth. It was a security blanket, a wardrobe item that over repeated wearings turned into an extension of his being. He was a celebrity in his hometown, and when his baseball career floundered it was professional wrestling that turned out to be the unlikely, but promising next step. When FWO floundered, it seemed that Samuel Brawn was decidedly doomed--gifted with the Midas touch in reverse. In 2005, he quietly deposited his FWO checks with the hopes he could spend the money attached to them someday. He moved out of the house occupied by his sister and ever-weakening father. Braun took residence in nearby Choctaw county in a one bedroom apartment. It was there he lived in solitude, closed off from the 712 people in nearby Breaksgood who knew who he was and what he hadn't accomplished: the young man who couldn't take hold of success when it was clearly in his reach. The FWO was back. The news had trickled into the arteries of every major city. In short order his face would be on television every Wednesday evening in Breaksgood. They would hear and see him in in the phosphor of their plasma screens. The local fight club rumors would cease and he would be a professional-- a famous professional. Brawn never understood why a lucha would hide their face. Tradition? Tradition only applies to things that happened yesterday. Brawn needed to be seen. Back in the doldrums of 2005, his mind got the best of him. He was thinking, but he wasn't feeling. He was crippled by depression. Soon he was lying immobile on a stack of mattresses for day-long stretches. A television commercial for a pizza restaurant for kids changed everything. Samuel Braun sought employment at a local mascot service. It was alot like the maid service where his mother once worked--the places where some unwed teenage mother steals your shit and flicks cigarette ash on your carpeting. Brawn was expected to hug sugar-high kids at random for a few hours and mime laughing. A brief, choreographed dance to cassette was extra. When he wasn't working, he spent free time in gyms. He didn't go out. No desire or need. Simple. The new job was a blessing in disguise. The cheerful kids helped with the depression, and he could be close to his friends and loved ones in the anonymity of his emerald costume. When his sister Annie phoned, he was a bank teller. After the requisite teasing about his math skills, no further explanation or evidence was necessary. Annie was a teenager. Teenagers are too busy to care. Annie would have to make time for him now. This was big. Brawn sat in his car, smiling weakly. He had to see her. But he couldn't tell her about the Frog. What would she say? He stared into the goofy, blank stare of the frog. He saw himself. It made sense and it didn't, but either way, he was not pleased.
