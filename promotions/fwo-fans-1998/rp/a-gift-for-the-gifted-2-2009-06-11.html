A Gift For The Gifted (2)

11 June 2009
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
A glob of spit slapped his gloved palm like a snare drum. Samuel Braun clapped his leather paws together, feeling the saliva and dirt combine into a viscous clay between his fingers. He looked nervously down the third base line and saw his dad standing in the coaches box. A small fly tirelessly attacked dad's bald head, which resembled the full moon beyond his shoulder. Sam's heart was a hummingbird and his father's weak smile did little to encourage him to calm down. His fingers gripped the comfortable, soft leather handle of his brand new bat. His stomach tightened and a sour taste occupied his throat. Sam had two doubles and a triple without his new gift, but he knew nothing could be left to chance. It was the ninth inning, no outs, and the score was tied. One swing could cement his destiny. Instead of walking towards the plate, Sam felt his legs guiding him in the opposite direction. As the players on the other team took the field in between innings, Sam pressed his chest against his dad's and clutched him tightly. Sam retreated and saw tears in his dad's eyes, but he could have been mistaken. His own eyes were wet. “Go get 'em, son.” * Doctor's offices were always a peculiar place for Sam, as they always appeared to champion a particular decade. This room had an affinity for the 1980's, the walls' ivory paint matching the dramatic rectangular shapes that comprised the couches. A row of five framed art prints hung on the wall, the third image hung slightly askew. A mélange of concentric circles, triangles, and other spontaneous geometric shapes filled the compositions, rendered in electric fuschia and teal pastels. The apple colored lamp next to him shone light on a collection of tattered magazines and newspapers. He made a cursory glance in their direction before opting to play with the buttons of his clean uniform. “Big game today?” the receptionist asked, her eyes rising over the counter. Her face was lit eerily from underneath, as if she was about to tell a campfire ghost story. “Championship tonight.” Sam replied with finality, looking up and meeting her gaze. She looked at him wordlessly, and after visually measuring his interest in further conversation, returned to the endless marking of documents. Sam crossed his legs, pinching a rogue string in one of his uniform buttons. He sat near the examination room, the voices of dad and the doctor combining into an ambient hum. He made no solid effort to hear what was being said, but suddenly a word jumped out among the aural clutter. Surgery. “Your lungs are filled with all sorts of trash,” the doctor explained. “We can get it out, which is the good news.” “But I don't have insurance.” Dad replied in a futile whine. “And I know I can't can't afford it.” Sam sat and listened to the pause, not lifting his eyes his finger's nervous button-twisting. “Probably not.” The doctor agreed. “But I read about your boy in the paper this morning. Said he's quite a ball player. Said he's gonna be drafted in a couple months. With the money he'll be making, he can afford it. Hell, maybe you'll win the lottery. The bottom line is I don't see you making it to eight months without this surgery.” Sam heard his dad sob for the first time since his mother died five years earlier. The door clicked shut. The sound was muffled but didn't disappear. Sam sensed he was being watched, and looked up to meet the sensitive eyes of the receptionist. He cleared his constricted throat and briskly readjusted his position on the white couch. He resumed fondling the button of his shirt, successfully extracting that rogue string. *
