Final of Jeremy's Song - 14

29 November 2002
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
previous parts: see-downfall archive. Flashes of light hit the bloodied walls. Police investigators held their cameras loosely. A somber feeling overcame each individual in the room. The stench of rotting flesh and unwashed basketball attire infected the clothes and burned the senses of those people. The faux wood walls cried out in pain while the drenched rug remained silent. Italian leather-clad feet cautiously floated over the crime scene in an attempt not to disrupt. A man with a notepad jotted down useless information. Potential motive, details about the crime scene should anything be doctored, and the replies of neighbors and the disoriented father. Something about the man seemed off. A queer sense of dishonesty was to be inferred from his tone. His lacking sadness amidst the tragedy left police with one obvious conclusion. The brown leather trench coat cut through the wind. A detective, brown hair slicked back and dark reflective shades hiding his eyes, turned toward the father. “Where were you the night your son killed himself?” he asked in a sharp tone, fearless toward the father’s potentially hidden sadness. “What’s it matter? Y’God damn idiots failed to notice the note on his desk in the past half hour y’been here,” the father replied. The detective lowered his head and glanced toward the disheveled working area. Officers meandered around but completely ignored what was right before them. The detective spoken of snapped a quick picture of the desk area, focusing a few shots on the note itself before picking it up with his latex glove covered hand. “To Anyone, According to my assumptions, suicide notes are long and drawn out pieces of teenage depression, apologies, or proclamations of hatred. I am here to break the mold. After a careful night of consideration, I have come to a revelation. In death, all of my worries disappear. Basketball is not a considered thought. Alcoholism and family dysfunction are no longer the source of pain and strife. In death, I’ll find peace. I have no one to apologize to except myself. Sincerely, Jeremy.” The detective’s eyes shifted toward the surly, half shirted individual downing a bottle of beer in the corner. Vocabulary and grammar were probably not the father’s best subjects, but this did not put anything past the officer. People pushed themselves to do incredible things after realizing what the recourse to their actions could potentially be. * * * The car ride home was quieter than to be expected. Even the boisterous David Harper left his hands in his lap and his mouth shut. It was an uneasy silence filled with tension and guilt, at least on the passenger’s side. Scenery passed by. The sound of the engine played continually. David burned with the desire to share the information he had received. After holding it in for the entire photo shoot, his mouth opened. “Downfall—“ He stopped for a moment and sighed outwardly. “Trent. A message from your mother was delivered to my cell phone, like I told you. Well, now, there’s something you have to know. ”Apparently, one of your cousins has killed himself. Jeremy was found dead last night. Your mother said she doesn’t expect much from you, but she’d appreciate it if you’d stop by.” The continuing silence shattered something within Harper. The somewhat obvious apathy of his coworker toward such a tragic event caused a near outburst of anger before he recalled Trent’s reaction to every single event. Trent turned the wheel sharply toward a highway exit. David couldn’t catch a full glimpse of where they were headed, but the city of Louisiana had something to do with it. The car drove down the barren road for unnumbered hours. Whereas David’s eyes shifted uneasily in an attempt to keep himself awake, Trent remained with the same unheeding glare. His eyes belonged to the black gravel path. The road bent down cliffs and drove through fertile forests and bluffs. It also shot through miles of corn fields. For some reason, the path seemed familiar to Trent. It was as if he had driven it thousands of times without passing it once. The car stopped thirteen hours later in a car empty aside from their beaten Civic and an unlabeled rustic truck. The house was surrounded by nothing but farmland and a small barn with hills of hay strewn across the land in no particular fashion. The sunlight cast a pink and purple background over the land, amplifying its already present beauty. A small and plain woman peered over the ears of corn. Her walk turned to a quick, delicate sprint toward the two men now standing at her door. The woman found the walkway to the house and stepped into it. “Mother.” With time caught up in a whirlwind of stoppage, the two stared. David to Trent, Trent to Mother, and Mother to Trent. The still-frame photograph-esque moment would remain etched in each of their lives as the moment when the lines of definition and connotation were slightly blurred.
