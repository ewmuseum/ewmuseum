The Poor Girl, 12.20.02

20 December 2002
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
I laughed at the poor girl today. She was sitting in front of the diner, the rain pouring down on her. She didn't bother to try and get under any sort of covering to protect herself, just sat there for the better part of the day. Her clothes were soaked to her skin, the baggy t-shirt she had on pushed against her body, revealing her pregnant stomach. For some strange reason, I laughed harder. For one, she was poor. I could assume that she lived in an alley filled with trash, vermin and other unmentionables. A cardboard box from an expensive refrigerator being her only safety from the elements. Secondly, she was thin, nearly malnourished. Scraping whatever food she could find in the receptacles behind Zom's, and whatever half- eaten pizzas she could scrounge up from her alley. Third, she was probably barely sixteen. Most likely cast aside by drug abusing and uncaring parents who hated their daughter. Or they never planned on having her, she was born though. Beaten, raped, her innocence ripped away from her wretched hands. Finally, she was pregnant. The worst possible thing for her to have to deal with. Her stomach looked like an odd alien protrusion in the rain. Out of place on a body where the skin was wrapped tightly around her bony frame. It was almost guaranteed that the child she would bear was doomed for failure. Nothing to eat, nowhere to live, the worst scenario possible. If she were to have a miscarriage she could be considered lucky. As sad as this would seem to some people, I couldn't help but laugh. Her face was fixed into a look of dismay. Disappointment with herself, her situation in life and what she had been handed. I could look at myself in the mirror and say that I had nearly been in her position once, but I climbed out of the hole that was blacker than an abyss. The frailty of her face, eye sockets sunk back in her head, cheekbones pulled out. It was like a sadistic art form of God. Like a cartoon where the characters are in a car or on a roller coaster going full- tilt, their skin pulled back against their skull. I suppose it is a sight that I should have some remorse in my heart for her. From across the street I looked into her eyes, but I could stare for long. Her misfortune made me want to vomit from where I was standing. When I got closer I saw beautiful emerald green eyes that had a glimmer of lost hope and all that could have been for her. I saw in her eyes something everyone has, but no one ever achieves. Perfection. You can tell a lot from someone's face. Her's immediately told me of her tribulations and everything she never had in life. Sitting in front of Zom's every day was all she had. Perhaps watching the traffic of people walk by the diner brought her some small piece of solace. As I approached her this morning, I threw a dime in her vicinity. I watched with amusement as she scurried in the downpour for ten cents. On her hands and knees like a dog waiting for it's master to throw a bone. She tried to thank me as I walked up the three brick steps into Zom's, but I ignored her. I had already recognized her pitiful being enough for one day. I sat inside of Zom's this morning with a cup of coffee, making small talk with Sloan about the previous day's events. Trite little things to make the morning seem interesting to those around me. But I couldn't help but think about the poor girl who was carrying a child that was already dead. Sure, it may be healthy - to some extent - and it may go on to lead an unproductive life in the streets, but some of us are merely born to die. I thought about this, and quietly laughed to myself.
