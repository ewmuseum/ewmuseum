Sky High #3

19 July 2003
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Once again, our friendly neighborhood blind man addresses whatever the hell comes to mind when he sits down to write. This time he deals with what it means to work for a company. Too often do workers in a company openly criticize the practices that happen within said company. Namely "Don't push this person, push me!" Everyone's entitled to their opinion, but it is a pretty self-defeating purpose when you call the booking staff complete and utter morons for booking someone higher than oneself. First and most obviously, it does not ingratiate oneself with the booking team, as it will be well known that the worker is a team player only as far as the booking directly benefits him or her. Second, you will be denied pushes in the future, as the moronic writing staff will have no loyalties to you or any reason to want to please someone who is only interested in themselves (also they could hold you back just to spite you). Third, you burn bridges ahead of you in a way in that any company that would potentially hire you in the future would know that you're only as faithful and trustworthy of their booking practices as your placement on the card. I won’t pretend to be an expert on the wrestling business, but it seems as though things do end up being easier on the person when they just go with the flow. A company just can’t run properly having a creative team of 45 separate people, 40 of which are only concerned with getting themselves into the franchise position. Things have to go a certain way, and even if someone feels that they are the most talented (and perhaps that may be true), everything needs to run its course. If it were you in the World Title mix, you wouldn’t want everyone and their mom who’d been in the company all but a month challenging you for the World Title even if they had talent. I remember a phrase that I’ve heard the old wrestlers talk a lot about, namely “paying your dues.” It’s almost as if that doesn’t happen anymore. The main federations that are covered in EW-News… the average age is something like 23 or 24, and many times it’ll be a rarity to find someone on a roster over the age of 35. Veteran status in the business is one thing I would say is important. If someone has been wrestling for a decade or more while still putting out consistently high-quality performances, that wrestler deserves to be the champion. If someone is willing to wrestle for pennies in a high school auditorium even though he or she could sell out Madison Square Garden, that wrestler deserves to be the champion. It someone is willing to cut out all the backstage crap and politics and wrestle on an independent show where nudity and blood do not equal high ratings, that wrestler deserves to be the champion. If someone is a team player who is willing to put over the right people at the right time without any questions, that wrestler deserves to be the champion. If someone can laugh and have a beer and shoot the breeze with everyone who doesn’t take their gimmick so seriously that they purposefully isolate themselves from everyone, that wrestler deserves to be champion. Simply put, if the wrestler is a great company man or woman and not someone to consider him or herself to be bigger than the company, that wrestler deserves to be the champion. I’ve only been wrestling for about two years now. Still, it’s astonishing the number of men and women I have personally heard going into a tiff because they hadn’t received a title shot, their entrance music wasn’t done properly, wardrobe didn’t give them exactly what they wanted, or their pyrotechnic display wasn’t as good as they had envisioned. There’s like an entire generation of people who were born to yuppies in the 80’s who may not have necessarily have been born with silver spoons shoved down their throats but nonetheless grew up as spoiled brats to the point that they think that the business owes them something. Maybe it's just me, but I’m willing to bet that any of the big names could retire today and professional wrestling as we know it would survive. We should be especially grateful for having jobs with this down economy, where even the formerly untouchable professions of money managers and lawyers are getting laid off. Numbers are good given the situation, but none of us can deny that there is a general drop in ticket sales all around because people who would come to all the shows just two years ago now can’t afford to spend that income on something else other than groceries. If things get worse, there will be pay cuts and firings even among the good workers, and don’t think that a $1.5 million dollar contract won’t be cut to $500 thousand. As inexpendable as anyone might think they are, it all comes down to the bottom line, and if the cost of keeping you is greater than the money you bring in, you’re going to have to work for less or just leave. What happened to the days of being grateful for being employed in this wonderful sports industry, instead of the “You should be grateful that I’m working for this federation!” attitude that seems to be common now. Some of us are wrestling for a paycheck because we do this very well. Some of us are in it because it’s a form of therapy for us. Some of us are in it because it’s all that we wanted to do since we were little kids. Those are all respectable reasons. But never, under any circumstances, is it respectable to think that you’re doing anyone a favor by being an ass. That’s just silly.
