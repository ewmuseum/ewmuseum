The Ivy League: Obituary

14 October 2003
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
The column I never wanted to write, the column I dreaded coming up with. The column that, on general principle, might be my last. Poison Ivy, signing off. "You know what I've always said, Ivy... this business should never be the most important thing in a person's life. The trick has been finding that piece that's more important." I guess he found that piece. THE ELIMINATOR - TOTAL ELIMINATION ELI FLAIR JULY 16, 1994 - SEPTEMBER 30, 2003 No sooner am I back in the driver's seat and I find out that my best friend, my family... has been struggling with some things and he's decided that the best way to deal is to leave the business. In fairness, he's spent less than thirty days with his daughter since she was born, and he doesn't want to miss a moment. In fairness, he doesn't want her to grow up without a father like so many other children of professional wrestlers do. In fairness... nobody deserves it more than he and Angel. For years now, people have called for the head of Eli Flair to be served to them on a silver platter. In 1995, they said his melodrama was taking away from the exciting product on television... product he was an integral part in providing. In 1996 they said he didn't fit into the whole "America's Team" vs. "GUNS and company" theme that had overtaken the entire BUSINESS, let alone one or two promotions. In 1997, the people got their wish and he was gone for an entire year. 1998 saw a rebirth, of both professional wrestling and Eli Flair. He was back with a vengeance, even though his critics said he was too beaten up to put on a decent match. When he proved them wrong, the people said he was too extreme, too much of an 'antihero' to carry a promotion as World Champion. When he proved them wrong, they said he was too old to be a wrestler and he should hang it up. Too old to be a part of the new CWL. Too old to be a part of the fWo. Too old to go toe to toe with Donaven Winters wrapped in barbed wire, four years after the war with Kevin Powers. Too old to revitalize the fWo's lagging Hardcore division. Too fucking old to be taken seriously as a contender to Creed's fWo World Championship. Too fucking old to turn Creed from a powerful, unstoppable, remorseless Champion into a powerful, unstoppable, remorseless, HATED, REVILED, GROTESQUE Champion. And he did it all on one bad leg. Well, congratulations, because you got your wish. As much as he hated being separated from his wife and daughter, he did it because he loved what he did. He loved the Original Nobody monicker that had been bestowed on him, and the hope it gave the faceless kids in the crowd that saw what he did and knew he could be anyone of them. Congratulations, for turning this from a business he practically gave his life for on numerous occasions into a pain in the ass job. Any obituary would be incomplete without giving credit to Dr. Silver and the fact that Eli never got his hand raised, no matter what the stakes or the situation called for. Credit to 'The Iceman' Steve Radder and the myriad of cage, cell, and high- stakes matches that the two engaged in all over the globe for titles, for money, and for the simple love of working with each other. There's a void in this business now that nobody saw coming, but should've. There's a problem with this business, where every other wrestler can be interchanged with each other, and a precious few athletes and performers dare to be different. Even fewer truly love what they do. Say what you will about how Eli's refusal to take time off for injury affected his actual ability... but he always made up for it. The Hardcore Revolution was a fad. Getting thrown through barbed wire, getting chokeslammed through a table to the floor, falling through the top of a cage to the mat - that's easy. Telling a STORY in the ring without using those cheap props - that's hard. Telling a STORY with nothing but two wrestlers and a World Title belt thrown in the middle... that's even harder. The remarkable thing about Eli Flair... he could do both. Special thanks to the Elimination Squad, without whom Eli and I would've never made our return. The small spark to come back to professional wrestling turned into a raging inferno, the night I turned on U-62 and saw someone named The Eliminator trying to wrestle. An honorable mention should also go out to 'Eradicator' Erik Kelly, for his utter refusal to work with us as he prepared for his Cyberslam showdown with Kodiak Vic Creed. I hope Kelly saw what Eli did for Creed and felt like a complete and utter tool for his ego. He made it okay for people to come through that curtain and be themselves. He made it okay to give an opponent their due. He showed a selfish and self- serving business that it's just as easy to get both himself and his opponent over through a win or a loss. Trading victories with Troy Windham proved that. Or maybe it's because Troy is so damned good. Or maybe they both are. Troy Windham was an enigma in Eli Flair's career. Two men who were so similar, yet so incredibly different. They're both the best at what they do, which is entertain. And when both men were in the ring against each other, they were that much better. When ELVIS LIVES XII was over, when CSWA Anniversary 2001 was over... it wasn't the main event that crowned Steve Radder the CSWA World Champion that everyone talked about. It wasn't the way Chad Merritt got one over on the former ClaimStakers that everyone talked about. It was Eli Flair and Troy Windham. Eli Flair was good. Troy was good. Together, they made history. Look around the world of professional wrestling and fighting, and I defy you to say that Eli Flair didn't have a tremendous impact. The number of wrestlers who come to the ring with that same stoic glare that I know so well. The number of people who cheered for him for no- showing an event to be there for the birth of his daughter. The way in which Team Extreme made the kids without hope feel good about themselves. Hell, Eli Flair might be the first WRESTLER in the history of the Asylum to receive a standing ovation. How many wrestlers go out there, no matter who the opponent, what the size of the crowd is, or how important the match is in the grand scheme of careers, and wrestle with the same unflinching intensity if the crowd is 20, 200, or 103,000? How many wrestlers, when told that their current career path is going to be completely devoted to making someone else look good on what would be their defining moment, would give it everything they've got? How many wrestlers would spend weeks not only doing their job, but going ABOVE AND BEYOND it to sell a Pay Per View? You're losing, you need a month off for general rest, and you still do your damnedest to tell one of the most gripping stories of the year inside the ring. Team EQ owes just as much of their success to Eli Flair as they do their own talents. I can't stress enough how incredibly talented Triple X and Lance Knight are on their own. I can't tell you how incredibly skilled in the ring Triple X is - or how he, Joey Melton, and Eddie Mayfield are the current Holy Grail of trash talking. You can all see that for yourself. I can't stress enough how Lance Knight and Evan Aho are constantly flip flopping back and forth as to who is the single greatest mat wrestler in the world. But the fact remains that Triple X learned his tenacity from nearly a dozen heated matches with Eli Flair. The fact remains that Lance Knight learned a good deal of his confidence and stride from dozens of late night car ride discussions with Eli Flair and myself. The fact that both men will admit to this takes nothing away from their success - they've earned it and they deserve every stitch of it. That's the biggest tragedy of all. I can go on and on about what he did in the ring, but that's only half the story. I firmly believe that any negative stories floating around the business about Eli are rooted in bias or half - truths. The fact remains, no matter how much you might ever personally clash with Eli in the locker room, when it's time to go out and do some business he is a complete professional. With his status as a fan favorite and smark enemy, to have a bad match would only bury himself. For that matter, I defy you to HEAR, let alone confirm, any rumors about an Eli Flair - induced glass ceiling. Remember, the outcome of the match is immaterial, but this is a man who took and sold the Epic Beef Legdrop a year and some change ago. Remember, this is a man who cared enough about the fWo and its Hardcore Championship to literally FIGHT for it, only to turn around and drop it to the man he wanted to work with before the shutdown. Remember, this is a man who has worked with literally everyone in both the CSWA and the fWo, from the top of the card to the bottom, all for the purpose of having a good show. Voice from the grave: "Pot. Kettle. Black." How does it feel to know that one of the last truly honest voices in professional wrestling has been silenced? How does it feel to know that the man who never had a problem talking with and offering advice to anybody who had a question for him has lost his love of the business? The man who truly had so much love and so much concern for all of his "kids" that he showed up for work many times in such a desperate state of missing his Angel that the only thing that took him to work was the prospect of talking with Lance Knight, Brand Frontier, Triple X, Deacon, Aimz, Kevin Masters, Allen Speck... all his "kids" that, whether they knew it or not, he felt responsible for keeping on the right path in one form or another. Say what you will about the ideas he had for Creed, or for Mark Windham, or for his multitude of show - stealing matches with everyone from Steven Shadows to Kevin Powers, but as long as the fans were entertained he was there for his kids first and his own career second. Eli Flair was honored to pass the torch to Deacon at Thanksgiving Weekend Spectacular. Eli Flair was honored to be the transitional man that finally brought Brand Frontier face to face with Victor Creed. Eli Flair was honored to show the world that putting over a woman (Aimz) didn't make him lost a stitch of heat. Most of all, I know that not a day goes by that Eli Flair didn't think about the first, last, and only match he had with the Boston Strangler, whose only comment about the night he put the King of Extreme over was "We're glad we could do that for you." The Strangler, in my opinion, represented everything that was right about professional wrestling. Well, it's over now. Finished. I don't know if he'll ever be back. I don't know if he'll ever appear in front of a television camera again as anyone other than the husband of the lead singer of Valerian's Garden, but if he doesn't, I think we can all agree that he's earned his right to privacy. I think we can all agree that he's earned his right to happiness. And I think we can all agree that for a man who gave nine long years of blood, sweat, and broken bones to the professional wrestling business, he's given all that he can be expected to give. Memorials can be sent to [detail withheld] Donations can be made to help restore Coop's and give Eli his *real* professional dream - to train a bunch of kids to respect the business and to make something of themselves. If there's one lesson that Eli Flair has left us with, it's that anybody - ANYBODY - with a dream and enough hard work can make a difference. If there's one example that Eli Flair has left us with, it's that this business works a lot better when everyone pitches in to MAKE it work better. Nobody is responsible for quality product, but everybody should share in the credit. Jerk the curtain, close the show, it doesn't matter. Roger Waters said it best: "Ashes and diamonds, foe and friend. We were all equal in the end." For perhaps the last time... This is Ivy. Reference material
