The Ivy League: Sex Sells (But Who's Buying?)

24 June 2003
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
You're a woman, how could you understand? I went out to dinner with my brother last week. Good man, my brother - I'm real proud of him. He, like many people in today's world, nailed himself into the All-American Teenaged Coffin, when he got his girlfriend pregnant two months before he graduated highschool. Our parents were angry and rightfully so, I think - Kim was a lovely girl and deserved more than having to deal with the responsibilities of motherhood at eighteen years old. But, to his credit, Brian got himself a job and kept it, and he and Kim are still married today, nearly fifteen years later, with four kids, a house in Concord, MA, and a slew of thriving businesses. He's an old- fashioned kinda guy, he works while his wife stays home with the kids. Fine, that's what she wants too. But he knows better than that with me. Anyways, we're out there having a lovely dinner, catching up, since the nature of my career is such that I can't take many breaks. After I promised him that I'd take a REAL vacation one of these days, the waiter came by with refills for our coffee and the check. I owed him one, so I gestured for him to hand it to me. The waiter chuckled to himself before placing the bill in front of my brother. Yeah, you can imagine how well that went over. Needless to say, I avoided making a scene, but it was a struggle of epic proportions. I was still fuming about it when I got to work - CSWA's Battle of the Belts XVII was a classic - if crowded - salute to old school wrestling, prepared to go off on the first person who treated me differently, just for being a woman. It didn't happen, and I was pleasantly surprised. But it brought up another thought - was that waiter in DC the exception, or is the CSWA locker room, a place I've called home for nearly a decade, the exception? Maybe I've simply worked my ass off for so long in that place between managing, writing, and producing, that I'm like the walls or the ceiling - just EXPECTED? I see other women in this sport, and while there are a healthy number of them like the fWo Hardcore Champion Aimz, or the OSF World Champion Tempest who also don't follow the 'typical' rules for how we're supposed to act, there's also Teri Melton, there's Pamela Dee, there's Desire, there's Sunshine Del Payne... all of whom run the gamut of stereotype from the quiet and helpless to the full- blown skank. Is it any wonder it's so difficult for a woman in this sport to gain respect? What about the industry leaders? What about Vince and his juggernaut of Sports Entertainment? Silicon- enhanced peroxide queens with no unique skills. Substitute any one of them for any other one of them and I doubt the people would even notice the difference. I'm not trying to rag on anyone in particular - if I was Desire and my only marketable skill was showing off my body I'd be doing the exact same thing as her - I don't fault her for having to make a living. What I fault her - and the others - for, is making it difficult for those of us with actual charisma and presence to use them effectively. All the times I've been approached for photo spreads, swimsuit editions of whatever, bla, bla, bla... thanks, but no thanks. Maybe I'm taking all of this just a bit too personally, however. Who am I to try and get things changed? Who am I to take up a cause that I have little to no hope of actually winning? Eli Flair told me a long time ago, you can't *make* anyone care about anything. All you can do is mess with the present to ensure your future. All you can do is fight for personal respect, not for a mass of people who don't necessarily *want* your help. In the course of my day, I hear almost zero sexist comments directed towards me, and I thank you all - you who I deal with on a regular basis - for that. I thank you for seeing me as a person, first and foremost. Thank you for realizing that a brain hides behind these green eyes and glasses. Thank you for believing that I have something to contribute to this sport beyond what other women were contributing when I first showed up in 1995. To the women who fight just as diligently for respect as I do - don't give it up. Never, ever give it up. And to the women who have found contentment in being eye candy and barely- covered pieces of meat... do the rest of us a favor. Don't talk. Ever. This is Ivy.
