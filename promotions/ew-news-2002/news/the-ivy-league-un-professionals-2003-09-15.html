The Ivy League: (Un) Professionals

15 September 2003
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
I can't believe I have to get angry about this stuff. Non- Sequitor life update: I can actually take four steps in a row without falling over in extreme amounts of pain. My doctors are calling my recovery something like a miracle. Not unheard of, but close enough to it that they think I could be back to work by the end of October. As many of you may know, I am more than just an on- air personality in the business of professional wrestling. I have a Masters degree in Business as well as one in Accounting. To that end, I handle the business affairs for both Eli Flair in wrestling and for his wife's very talented band, Valerian's Garden. And I'm quite hands- on with the work I do. I'm proud of the fact that I've been able to negotiate on their behalf. Without a solid reputation, all the talent in the world would be wasted in a string of clubs in Los Angeles. But with the reputation of Ivy McGinnis negotiating for them, a great deal of clout is attached to their name. So you can imagine how happy I was to hear that a certain band of dubious talent that somehow managed to survive the 1980s explosion of hair and spandex decided that they didn't want to open for these up - and - coming musicians with nothing but platinum in their future. It's just another case of a man being pissed off that a woman is kicking his ass in his own chosen profession, but in this particular case, the hair band in question completely destroyed the monitors during their last song. And I didn't really understand the technical explanation but according to Garden keyboardist Rich "Nine" Stewart, they caused the entire sound system to feed back on itself. Garden didn't get to play, which put them in breach of contract, which meant that not only did they not get paid for a gig, but they had to refund the audience's money, and were out the money they spent to GET there. It's not as bad as it could be. I already spoke with the promoter, and he assured me that he would make arrangements with the club owner to reschedule the show for later in the year and trying to find a way to pay the band back for the refunds they shelled out WITHOUT raising ticket prices. But it made me think about how entertainers have a choice - to either help or screw their fellow entertainers. And far too often for my taste, nobody knows what it means to do the right thing. Particularly in the wrestling business. You're the champion, right? You don't want to wrestle the schmoe they stick in the main event with you, so you protest by having the most godawful match in history. No effort towards making a memorable match. No effort towards making your opponent look good. The people boo you outta the arena but you don't care because you've got your nightly payoffs, your guaranteed downside, and whatever else you've got cookin'. You'll do okay. But what about the guys making fifty bucks in the dark match? Screw with the headline match and those guys aren't gonna get paid next time out. He *needs* that money to feed his family, pay his bills, keep his car alive from one week to the next. Who the hell are you to take his money away? The music business is much more civil in this respect, as a band can always make themselves known with a solid set. Obviously, the scenario I presented above is an extreme case, but typically a band's reputation is made or broken on their own laurels. They don't have to put their trust in anyone but each other - the loyalty a musician has to her bandmates is much stronger than the loyalty between wrestlers. That's because there's no bottleneck at the top of the music charts. And the preliminary guy today is the Main Man, the Paragon, the Greatest American Hero, tomorrow. Personally, I love it. I think the idea that someone could come up through the ranks and outclass me at what I do is exciting and keeps me on my toes. Hell, anyone who can outclass Poison Ivy deserves more than just *my* job. It challenges me to stay current, stay topical, and stay on the forefront of everything my career is about. And yet, if the 'next Poison Ivy' comes along, I won't try to sabotage her career. I won't try to make her work even harder than I did to make it. What I'll do for this poor soul is suggest to her that instead of trying to be the next Poison Ivy, she should try to be the first "whoever she is" and make her OWN niche in the business. The talent always rises. You can keep the talent's name off the marquee, but you can't keep 'em from stealing the show. I sound like a broken record, but take a good, close look at the worst instigators of this. Every single main eventer who refuses to work with the lower card wrestlers... are people who don't have the talent to make it on their own two feet. Think about it. This is Ivy.
