A dice-and-paper game invented in a back room in Greensboro in March 1988, carried onto PRODIGY in 1992, onto its own domain in 1998, and run on the board that domain became until January 2014 — twenty-six years of one federation now held across 209 shows, 63 roleplays and sixteen title lineages, told in its own cards, its own newspaper and its own out-of-fiction memoir. Written by AI, 2026

Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

It began as something to do on a cold, wet afternoon. In March 1988, in Greensboro, North Carolina — a city where Crockett's NWA ran the Coliseum about every three weeks — its two founders read one of the play-by-mail advertisements that ran in the back of the wrestling magazines, decided it looked dull, and built their own game instead. Dice came out of an unused Dungeons & Dragons Basic Edition set. A move list ran from five-point armbars to hundred-point brainbusters: you rolled for control, you executed a move, you took the damage off a starting total, and once a wrestler dropped under a hundred points you could go for the pin. The first three characters made under those rules were Hornet, Mark Windham and the CS Express.

The titles arrived in the order the game needed them — a World Championship first, then the United States and Greensboro belts, then tag titles — and by the end of that year five cards had been staged, all of them in a warehouse the fed billed as an auditorium. Then came the writing. “Of course, we'd now all call it roleplay,” its own memoir says, “but it began as 'shows' like 'Buckley's Better Beef' or 'Cornette's Corner'.” The commentary layer that grew up around a dice game is what the CSWA turned out to be for.

In 1992 it went looking for more people and found PRODIGY, where fantasy wrestling was already running under “Other” in the Sports BB. Seventy e-mails went out, at a quarter each past the first thirty. Within weeks it was running a tournament on the board; within four its own champion had taken two other federations' World titles on tour; within a year it had helped assemble the first superleague on the service, and by 1993 that champion's belt had absorbed ten titles into one. What followed is the shape of the PRODIGY years told through one federation's record: superleagues formed and broke apart over politics, the CSWA closed itself in May 1993 with a card called LAST CALL and quietly reopened five weeks later, and the population thinned out for good when the service moved to hourly billing.

The web era is the part that survives best. From March 1998 the fed ran at its own domain, and that domain grew into a hub other federations plugged into. What it carried over from PRODIGY was the touring circuit — PRIMETIME and SHOWTIME through San Diego, Memphis, Baltimore, Las Vegas, Nassau and Trinidad — the annual furniture of ANNIVERSARY, FISH FUND, ELVIS LIVES, BATTLE OF THE BELTS and WINTER'S WARRIORS, and a Greensboro Championship named for the city the whole thing was invented in, still being won on cards thirteen years later. It also ran a newspaper about itself. The CS TRIBUNE reported the fed's own gates and buyrates in kayfabe, and once carried the firing of its own play-by-play announcer as a news item, cancelling his column in the same issue.

Its rules page is unusually frank about how any of it was decided. Matches went on judged roleplay, the front office booked the rest around the arc, and “story takes precedence over winning and losing.” Real wrestlers were not allowed on the roster: “You will not be allowed to join the league with Ric Flair, Benoit, Raven, etc.” The acronym itself is a joke the fed told on itself — the kayfabe expansion is ChampionShip Wrestling Alliance, and the hub's own FAQ, written from the same desk, says the name is really built out of its founders' own: “most of us know better. (Grin)”

How it ended is not in the surviving record as an announcement. The fed kept counting its own anniversaries without ever resetting the number — CSWA15 in 2003, CSWA17: Rebirth in 2005, an eighteenth annual Gold Rush in 2006 — through delays and hiatuses it named itself out loud, and in December 2013 it opened a membership drive under new ownership, back and “looking to move away from its past glory.” It carded CSWA Versus for 11 January 2014 in Chapel Hill and took roleplay for it. The newest post on its own roleplay board is dated the day after that.

Storylines

The CSWA ran an awards ceremony twice in the surviving record and both times said out loud what it thought it was making. The 1993-94 ceremony, staged inside its own ELVIS LIVES card at Graceland, gave Match of the Year to a Hornet/Windham dream match and Wrestler of the Year to the Unified World Champion; the 1998-99 ceremony gave Event of the Year to a pay-per-view that ended with the arena on fire, Surprise of the Year to a hero turning after ten years, Match of the Year to a hardcore feud, and Wrestler of the Year to a man who had held the World Title for three months. The arcs below are led by those categories and by the fed's own feud profiles, and recapped from the cards they ran across.

The back room, the dice, and a champion nobody wanted to be Written by AI, 2026

The CSWA began in March 1988 as a game two teenagers built in a back room in Greensboro out of a move list and an unused Dungeons & Dragons dice set, and the fed later wrote its own first night up as a card. In that telling, a hundred and fifty people turn up to a warehouse off Market Street that has no name until a customer asks for one at the gate, the fireworks fail twice and then fire into the man lighting them, and a four-man tournament crowns the first CSWA World Heavyweight Champion. Joey Melton, who had come expecting a WCW tryout and stayed only because the fee was doubled, made Mark Windham submit to a figure four and then Dark Knight submit in the final, took the belt and told the crowd he had beaten girlfriends with bigger ones. Hornet lost his first match that night. In the parking lot afterwards he and Windham named themselves America's Team.

The 1988 card archive then builds a promotion out of tournaments, in the order the game needed them: Jay Youngblood wins the first United States Championship, the C.S. Express beat the Big Connection for the first World Tag Team Championship, Purple Hayes takes the first CSWA Heavyweight Championship, Strange But True the first Greensboro Tag Team Championship, and the Greensboro Heavyweight Championship goes to Big Wesley Slack over Mark Windham in its own final. The fed's own out-of-fiction memoir remembers that last one the other way round — it has Windham winning the first Greensboro Championship — and both readings stand here, because the archive and the memoir are both the fed's own.

What the archive actually shows across that first year is Melton holding the World Championship and everyone else circling it: Hornet takes the United States belt off Youngblood, loses it to Youngblood's tag partner Monte Farriss, and then — by the rankings not yet having been reissued, a technicality the fed still jokes about — gets the World Title shot anyway. Degadeth had taken the belt off Melton in a cage at the ELVIS LIVES CELEBRATION and had not been beaten since. At the ELVIS LIVES COMEBACK CELEBRATION, Hornet beat him for it, and the CSWA had the champion it would spend the next decade arranging itself around.

Seventy e-mails, and a belt made out of other people's belts Written by AI, 2026

In 1992 the game went looking for more people. Its own memoir, The REAL Story, records the method exactly: seventy e-mails sent to a PRODIGY sports board at a quarter each past the first thirty, an ULTRATITLE tournament run on the board to show what the fed was, and a first member who slipped in at seeds sixty-three and sixty-four. Within weeks Hornet was touring — he took the JWA and WDWF World Championships inside a month — and by May 1992 six leagues had formed the Enterprise superleague and merged their World titles into one belt on him.

The Unified World Heavyweight Championship's own ledger is what survives of the next four years, and it reads as a running argument about whether one man should hold everything. Hornet consolidated the IWCCW, AAWC and ten-title CSWA Unified belts by beating Joey Melton in the first IRONMAN of CHAMPIONS at LAST CALL II in October 1993, then added the UWL half by beating Polaris in Charleston and the AR Superleague's by beating Billy Freedom. In June 1994, in a dispute with member federations about the state of the championship, he handed it to the authorities and it went to a sixteen-man tournament — which a masked man calling himself Mr. Magnificent won, unmasking as Mark Windham with his contract dispute settled minutes earlier.

Hornet took it back nineteen days later at ELVIS LIVES VIII at Graceland, in the third Hornet/Windham dream match, and the way he took it is the hinge of another feud entirely: Windham broke off a cover to drag a commentator to the apron for calling him “the Living Psycho,” the commentator hit him with the microphone, and Hornet rolled him up without ever knowing why he had stopped.

The Corporation, the WHEEL of DEATH, and a favour with a price Written by AI, 2026

Ray S. Cornette bought his way onto the CSWA Board of Directors by swindling two seats out of their holders while disguised as a Japanese businessman, revealed himself at THANKSGIVING WEEKEND SPECTACULAR 1994, and suspended Mark Windham indefinitely. To get his friend reinstated Hornet signed for the WHEEL of DEATH at SUPER BOWL BLAST '95 — a three-tiered cage with timed trapdoors and, on the top level, a wheel with wrist and ankle restraints that reached forty-five miles an hour and shot fire while it turned. The team that strapped more of the other side in won; the Unified and Enterprise World Championships, Cornette's two board seats and Windham's career were all on it.

Hornet and Windham needed a third man and Mike Randalls offered, for a price: two signed matches, the second with the Unified Championship on the line. They won the WHEEL. Then Randalls collected. He beat Hornet in a short match a fortnight later with Hornet's back already reinjured, lost the cage rematch at WINTER'S WARRIORS VI when Hornet had the cage electrified mid-move and hit a shooting star press on a man still shaking, and then produced a return clause from the same contract and took the title three days later in an empty Merritt Auditorium.

Mickey Benedict, and what a nickname cost Written by AI, 2026

The fed's own feud profile dates this one to a chair shot in 1992 delivered for no stated reason — asked why, Windham said “Why not?” — and traces it through every bad decision Windham made afterwards. At the same WINTER'S WARRIORS VI, Benedict put his career against Windham's nickname in an electrified cage, with Windham needing a pin inside ten minutes with one arm tied behind his back. The served copy of that card breaks off with the referee calling thirty seconds left; the finish survives only in a line of commentary later the same night, and in the profile, which agrees: Benedict lasted, took the nickname, and was thrown into the live cage on his way out of the sport.

From a wheelchair he then chose Windham's opponent for a rematch at FISH FUND X and chose his brother. Timmy Windham, coming back from a neck injury, begged to be allowed to lie down. Mark piledrove him onto concrete to get his nickname back. The rest of the arc is the fed spending three years on what that bought: a father who turned out to be alive and then turned out not to be, a divorce, a search Windham conducted on the road instead of wrestling, and a graveside DNA test in late 1998 that sent Timmy off in Mark's car and into an accident. At ELVIS LIVES XI Benedict reappeared at ringside being pushed by Timmy Windham, who was supposed to be dead, threw a fireball into Mark's face and let his brother drop a concrete block on his head.

Hornet and GUNS: six matches, and nothing settled Written by AI, 2026

The CS TRIBUNE's own feud profile calls this the longest-running rivalry in wrestling and lays out its own timeline, and the timeline is the story: Hornet stops GUNS winning a title, five times, and each time it costs him more. It starts in March 1995 with Hornet wearing GUNS' own El Toro mask as a favour, winning the AAWC Unified Championship in it, and being thrown into the third row by the man he was helping. At FISH FUND X, with the Unified World Championship on the line in the second IRONMAN of CHAMPIONS, Hornet threw GUNS over the top rope on purpose to disqualify himself rather than let him have it — and neither man won; the points went to Alexandr Karelin.

They then became partners for a year, which turned out to be a two-year plan: at WINTER'S WARRIORS VII, with GUNS about to retain the Enterprise World Championship, Hornet climbed the cage, taped his hand, punched his own partner in the back of the head and handed the belt to Mark Windham. America's Team was back and GUNS had been the scapegoat of it. Six months later they signed a career match at FISH FUND XI: End of an Era. GUNS finally got his pinfall — with his manager moving him into position while Hornet lay in the ring post — and Hornet walked out through the crowd, and the fed went into a decline its own commissioner would later date to that exact night.

The sixth meeting was the main event of ANNIVERSARY 1998: Titanic — Sink or Swim, two-out-of-three falls for the vacant CSWA World Championship, and it went past an hour before both Windhams came down the aisle from opposite sides and broke it. The third fall was ruled a no-contest, the match a draw at a fall apiece, and the belt stayed vacant. Three years of it, and the fed's own record has them at three wins to two with the sixth thrown out.

The Red Midget, the trial, and a fed that came back on April Fool's Day Written by AI, 2026

A midget called Lyle had been the first paying customer through the gate on the CSWA's own first night. Nine years later he was murdered on live television at ANNIVERSARY 1997, and the fed put the trial on the air. In FROM THE PAST…TO THE PRESENT, Hornet defends the accused commentator as a licensed attorney, cross-examines his own former employer, and breaks him into admitting the order on the stand. Chad Merritt is arrested in the courtroom, gets six months in the Green Valley Home for the Mentally Deranged, and takes a phone call there from the man he had killed — alive, on a cruise liner, paid by somebody else to disappear.

The same document is the fed's own account of why it stopped running. It has Merritt in therapy working backwards through 1997 to find the mistake, landing on the career match at FISH FUND XI, and then on his own scheme with the two World titles; it has ten cards in a year, arenas booked and no-showed, contracts bought out, and an FCC fine for broadcasting a murder. It ends with the two co-owners signing the company back together in an Atlanta bar and holding a press conference on 1 April 1998 to announce a tenth-anniversary card, because if he was going to make a fool of himself he might as well do it on the right day.

Thirty-two men for a vacant belt, and an arena on fire Written by AI, 2026

ANNIVERSARY 1998 had left the World Championship vacant, and so had the four-way after it, so the fed ran a thirty-two-man tournament through the summer: first round at SuperPRIMETIME in Dallas, second and third at SuperPRIMETIME in Chicago, the final four at FISH FUND XII: Fire It Up!. Hornet went out in the semi-final to a newcomer called “Hurricane” Eddy Love, who then beat Billy Starr in the final and became World Champion in his first year in the company. A second eight-man bracket on the same card gave Kevin Powers the United States Championship.

Then FISH FUND Park caught fire. Timmy Windham, who had just cost his brother a match for the second time, ran back into the building looking for him; Hornet went in after Timmy; the stage door blew out and threw Hornet across the parking lot with it. The fed booked Remembering Timmy in Philadelphia three months later, opened it with a ten-bell salute and a silent crowd, and gave the whole card over to the roster talking about him. Its main event put the World Championship on a tag match against Eddy Love and a masked partner; the mask came off as Steve Radder, the partner Mark Vizzack had chosen walked out on him, and Hornet came down the aisle on crutches and hit Love with one. The 1998-99 awards named the night the arena burned Event of the Year.

Eli Flair and Kevin Powers, and the table with the glass on it Written by AI, 2026

The fed voted this its Match of the Year and the two men accepted the award together in a tuxedo and a t-shirt. It ran on tables: at PRIMETIME in El Paso Poison Ivy had two men set one up at ringside and emptied a bag of broken glass and thumbtacks across it, and both men went through it on a clothesline before Flair carried Powers back in for a tombstone. At Remembering Timmy they fought the United States Championship out inside five layers of barbed wire with the ring ropes taken down, the winner being whoever could walk out. At SHOWTIME in Kansas City Flair issued Ivy an ultimatum about choosing between him and Hornet and Powers answered it by coming back with a steel-plated baseball bat.

Hornet's turn Written by AI, 2026

At ELVIS LIVES XI, Mark Vizzack beat Eddy Love in an electrified cage to win the World Championship, and Hornet gave the speech — proud of him, calling him a friend, telling him he would be coming for the belt. Then he DDT'd the referee, locked the Scorpion Deathlock on the new champion, and Poison Ivy locked the cage door from the outside so nobody could stop it. “It's nothing personal,” he said. He took the title off Vizzack at SHOWTIME in San Antonio with a branding iron, and the fed's own newspaper recorded that for the first time in eleven years the crowd threw things.

The Board of Directors stripped him of it three weeks later at BATTLE of the BELTS XVI, and put the vacant championship into a six-man IRONMAN of CHAMPIONS at ANNIVERSARY 1999: Revelation — four matches each for Hornet, Vizzack, Eddy Love, Kevin Powers, Deacon and Eli Flair. Hornet reached the last match still able to win it, hit a shooting star press on Flair, and took the microphone to gloat while the referee tried to tell him Flair's boot had been on the rope. He would not listen. The referee restarted the match; Ivy, who had spent the night walking out on him, caned him in the corner; Flair hit an inverted DDT and became CSWA World Heavyweight Champion. The last shot of the night is Hornet sitting in the corner mouthing “what have I done.” The fed's own awards, five days earlier, had already given his turn Surprise of the Year.

Deacon, and a championship handed back Written by AI, 2026

Deacon came into the CSWA in 1998, seven feet and three hundred and twenty-five pounds, led to the ring by a manager called the Shepherd, and won the 1998-99 award for Most Original Personality without saying a word at the podium. He took the World Championship off Eli Flair at THANKSGIVING WEEKEND SPECTACULAR '99 in December 1999 and held it for a year, which is the longest reign the fed's own Champions and Contenders pages record for that belt in its web era.

It ended on 29 November 2000, at a press conference Merritt called to read out a statement from Deacon and his physician. The champion had been diagnosed with multiple sclerosis and wanted time with his family; the Championship Committee vacated the title. Merritt held the belt up for the cameras, thanked him for taking on everyone thrown at him, and announced a fifth IRONMAN of CHAMPIONS to fill it. The fed's own Champions and Contenders page records the reason in one line under the title history, and it is a line about a character.

The ClaimStakers, and the two men who built the place suing each other Written by AI, 2026

The press conference above is interrupted before the questions start: Mike Randalls, Hornet and Eli Flair walk into the room and Merritt has himself hustled out. The three of them had come together at ANNIVERSARY 2000 with Poison Ivy — two men who had spent five years trying to end each other, and a third who had beaten both — and the fed's own newspaper wrote it up as the night its commissioner lost control of the company on the same evening he took sole ownership of it.

That ownership was itself a court case. The CS TRIBUNE's own archived headlines carry the whole thing: in May 2000 Stephen Thomas filed an injunction against his partner, the case was fast-tracked, the injunction granted, and every CSWA show stopped — television into reruns, house shows cancelled, the newspaper itself going quiet for two months. A judge let the fed reopen under Merritt's purview pending judgment. Thomas's case was that the 1997 murder trial had nearly bankrupted the company; Merritt's answer was that his partner could no longer tell the difference between the feud and the fed, because most of it had been on-air the whole time. By August the newspaper was reporting that Thomas was building a rival league with Hornet as its cornerstone, and its readers' own poll on who should win the court battle came back fifty-eight per cent for “they're both greedy.”

The last champion of the web era Written by AI, 2026

The fifth IRONMAN of CHAMPIONS ran across the “Swimming Upstream” tour into ELVIS LIVES XII in Nashville, six men on a points table the fed printed on the front page of its own newspaper and updated round by round: February has all six level on twenty; April has Steve Radder ahead; July closes it out — “Steve Radder became the CSWA World Champion by virtue of his 55 points and a final round DQ win over Kevin Powers.” The same page has the fed advertising a Presidential title decided by a ten-man Top Dog tournament and a four-way tag match where losing a fall costs Joey Melton his CSWA career. The September 2001 capture is the last front page the record holds, and it is still selling ANNIVERSARY.

The fed that kept counting Written by AI, 2026

After the domain became fwrestling.com the CSWA kept its own numbering and never reset it. Its board carries CSWA15 — an ANNIVERSARY subtitled “It's Time (Better Late than Never)” whose own forum descriptions call the fed a phoenix rising from the ashes — and then CSWA17: Rebirth, which asks in its own subtitle whether the CSWA can survive the party, and carries a PRIMETIME in Oklahoma City labelled “the PRIMETIME that never was.” An eighteenth annual Gold Rush followed in 2006. When the results for that one did not appear, the fed staged the delay: a champion came on camera to explain that the master tapes had fallen into a vat of acid, that he had pinned everyone and left with the belt, and that this was the official update — and another manager, watching on a hotel television with a bowl of popcorn, called it a scam on air.

CSWA: BLUE MOON in 2012 does the same trick without blinking: the card's own announcement says the name is “a reference to the CSWA's slow pace of the past,” restores the UNIFIED Championship, and books the last two men to hold it against each other — a match that had been meant to bring the company back before and was interrupted, in the fed's own words, by a hacker.

CSWA Versus, and the last card Written by AI, 2026

In November 2013 a thread on the board said only that a CSWA return was true, was happening, was going to be epic, and — with the exclamation mark the fed put there itself — was going to be consistent; a man who had sent an application in nine years earlier replied asking whether anyone had read it, and was approved on the spot. The membership drive that followed described a promotion under new ownership, plagued with delays and hiatuses, looking to move away from its past glory with new stories and new characters, and racing toward a twenty-sixth anniversary.

PRIMETIME: Winter Wonderland ran on 22 December 2013: Cameron Cruise beat Mark Windham, Pat Gordon Jr upset Eli Flair, Viktor Reise took Bonecrusher apart, an outside promoter interrupted a match between three second-generation newcomers, and Dan Ryan retained the UNIFIED World Championship over the Deacon. Roleplay for CSWA Versus in Chapel Hill opened eight days later with no word limit at all. Mark Windham posted his on 10 January: “Hello Darkness my old friend. I've come to talk with you again. Dan Ryan. Yes we can come home again, but we're never quite the same are we?” The card was for the following night. The newest post on the fed's own roleplay board is dated the day after that, and it was still the newest one nine years later.

All Action Wrestling Conference (AAWC, All Action, 1993)

Crossover booking with CSWA (1988) (1993–1996)
The two federations booked into each other across the Prodigy years, and CSWA’s own title history dates it. The AAWC World Championship was one of three titles merged at the IRONMAN of CHAMPIONS at CSWA’s LAST CALL II on 24 October 1993; the AAWC’s own World Champion Hot Shot wrestled in that six-man field; Hornet captured the AAWC Universal Championship in a Unified Championship defence at Thanksgiving Weekend 1995; and a full AAWC card of 5 August 1995 was hosted on CSWA’s own Prodigy directory.

New Frontier Wrestling (NFW)

Affiliate of CSWA (1988)
NFW lists the CSWA as a federation affiliate on its own affiliates page and names it in its own retrospective History alongside the UWA and the fWo as a fellow PRODIGY league; NFW's own copyright line states that "The Ultratitle is property under a leasing agreement with the CSWA", the trophy the CSWA's own founding memoir records being created in its first years as the "Ultimate Title".
