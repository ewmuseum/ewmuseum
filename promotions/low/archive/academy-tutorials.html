But, before I begin describing each style, let me tell you that no style is better than the other. Each style has its separate pros and cons… their own good and bad halves. The most traditional role-play style is what many learn as soon as they start… script style. Script style is a great start for rookies to the game, and is what most learn right off of the bat, as I previously stated. Script style is written like… well, a script to a television show, or any other type of shows - be it movies, cartoons, etcetera… Personally, I started off with script style, and it helped me into my role-playing groove much faster than the others styles would have done. I started with a humorous, witty character; and I believe script style is the style-of-choice for these types of characters. When reading and writing a script-styled role-play, the dialogue hits the reader harder than it would in other styles. The reader takes in the meanings and scenario faster with script-style, which can definitely be a good thing. An example of script-style would be something like… Character: “Hey!” [The stubby man crouched down, attempting to regain his breath after the chase.] Character: “Stop that man! He has my money!” [His voice echoed off of the alleyway walls and followed the robber as he fell to the ground in exhaustion.] … But of course, you can go wild with whatever the character is saying. An advantage of script-style writing is that when you write show segments, or “in-cards”, as they are known, you do not have to alter your primary writing style. One common flaw with script-style is that some writers don’t go into much detail with the settings, appearances, and overall description; which can hurt the piece of writing. My advice is to remember to put detail into the scenarios. Detail is a key importance to writing… specifically novel-style writing. Novel-style role-playing is for the more advanced writer, or for the writer with a better understanding of the English language and grammatical rules. Novel-style is not necessarily better than script-style, but it is becoming more and more popular throughout eFedding. Novel-style is written in the same sense that most, if not all, books and novels are written in. While writing in this style, it is a key importance to remember to add emphasis, expression, and detail into everything you write. An example of novel-style writing would be… “Hey!” the stubby man shouted down the alleyway, while crouched down, attempting to regain his breath after the chase. “Stop that man! He has my money!” he barked out once again, as his voice reverberated between the two brick walls that made the alley, tracing the robber as he collapsed to the cement walkway in exhaustion. As you see… novel-style is a bit more complex than script-style, but it is well worth it to give the reader insight and relations to the character. If you have a firm grasp on the English language, than this is the writing style that would be better suited for you… it’s quite easy to use after learning the basics. Another thing to remember while writing in either script-style or novel-style is the tense. I’m sure you know them, but in case they’ve slipped your mind: past, or present. “What about future?” you say? This is for advanced writers only, as it will come off looking sub-par if mistakes appear. It includes things like, ‘He will walk down to the ring, and he will fight for his life.’ This can sometimes be incorporated with present tense. ‘He will walk down to the ring, and he will fight for his life. He knows it.’ Unless you truly know what you are doing, and it is for a very good reason, keep one tense while writing; it throws the reader off if you’re skipping around with the tenses. [Back to top] The Quantity Factor Here’s a topic that a lot of writer’s are unsure with… the size of the role-play. “How long should it be?” “Is this long enough?” I’ve heard those questions a lot during my eFedding tenure. Remember the rule: “quality over quantity”? Good… because that’s an important part of the size issue. Never make your role-play long for length’s sake. If your role-play has quality concreted into it, length won’t matter. Length rarely matters. Never concern yourself with it; if you find yourself counting pages and words while writing, then I advise you to move your role-playing into Notepad, or WordPad. Length is inconsequential. Just tell your story. However long your role-play is when you finish telling your story [not to be mistaken with purposely dragging the role-play on] is how long your role-play should be. If it’s short and packed with quality… good - if it’s long and packed with quality… good. Many people don’t like extremely long role-plays, so, try not to bore them to tears with the length alone. I usually keep my role-plays moderate sized… not too long, and not too short. I tell my story, and however long it is, it’s good. [Back to top] The Quality Factor Quality is a key necessity to role-playing. Quality is what makes the role-play worth writing and worth reading. Never just “slap together” a role-play without thinking on it, and making sure it’s worthwhile. Many people confuse the “quality over quantity” rule, and think length is what makes the role-play… but as stated above, it isn’t. Do not write something that has absolutely no point at all. If I wanted to see a ten-page role-play about how your opponent is less of a man than you are, I’d watch a Triple H vignette. Give different settings to your role-play; in-ring role-plays get very old, very quick. Have your character sitting at a bar, drinking Jack Daniels, or at a rock concert. Just vary your settings to what your character would be doing. Don’t litter the writing with filler, otherwise known as: pointless crap. That doesn’t make for a quality-packing role-play. Another thing that makes for quality: grammar and readability. If your role-play is filled to the brim with mistakes, horrid grammar, and no punctuation and such, then it isn’t quality. It’s a lot of misspelled, disorganised and utter mayhem of a role-play. Something that would help this is Microsoft Word’s Spellchecker and Grammar checker. They are a role-play’s saviours. Remember to proofread your work after you finish it, as well. It will definitely help you out in the long run. Remember… “Quality over quantity.” [Back to top] All About Ambiance What’s a role-play without description? When you write, no matter what style it may be, make sure you describe things to a certain extent. Ambience is what helps the reader see what you’re writing about. The man stands in the hallway, and shakes his head. That doesn’t cut it… see how much you can describe there? How tall is the man? What colour is he? How old does he seem to be? And the hallway… is it crowded? Vacant? Creepy? Well… you need to tell us these things! “… And shakes his head.” What direction did he shake his head? Did his hair move? Does he have hair? If so, what colour and style? How long does he shake his head for? In that simple sentence, the reader may be asking questions like those, and you need to inform them! How much ambience is too much ambience? Ambience is good so long as it doesn't choke the reader. Put enough of it in that people can immerse themselves in the scene, and know what's going on, without drowning them in description and metaphors. There isn’t really a rule of thumb on how much ambience is good and how much is bad. It depends on the individual piece of writing, and the style of the writer. Use everything needed to get your points across and give the story life for the read… but nothing beyond that. [Back to top] From A To B: Planning Your Roleplay A role-play needs MEANING. Without a storyline or a plot to follow, there is nothing about the role-play that separates it from random gibberish. Before I write a role-play, I pre-plan it… I lay it out on a piece of paper for me to look at, and visualise when I’m writing the actual role-play. It helps a lot. There needs to be an introduction, a plot, a climax, and a conclusion while writing. Fair enough, if it’s a series, “to be continued” can be used, but there needs to be some sort of conclusion… some sort of point that is gotten across to the reader. While writing about the plot and pre-planning… remember that you have to get from Point A… to Point B. If you think you started at Point A, and ended in the same damn place… then there is something wrong; and this is where going back and checking on the layouts of the role-play comes in. If the role-play is meant to follow into another one, for a series… do not solve the problems and finish the entire story in one role-play. If it’s a one-role-play deal, then yes… finish off the main highlights within the boundaries. A role-play without a plot is a worthless waste of talent and writing. Remember to use your character’s gimmick, history and cast of characters [be it friends, family, foes, etc.] to your advantage while writing. It seriously helps you out. [Back to top] Character Development Tutorials Choosing The Perfect Finisher This has nothing to do with your actual role-playing ability, but it still says a whole lot about your originality, and your level of seriousness and compromise. Let’s take an example here, say you start off with a 5’8 wrestler, a man that weighs about one hundred and fifty pounds, would it be realistic for him to power bomb guys that are 6’8? No! Your finisher has to adapt with the traits that come with your weight and your size. If you are 5’8, you can rely on quickness, high flying, cheap tricks, or simple moves, that do a lot of damage, and can easily pulled off by a guy that is smallest in a federation. Realism is the key in this game. The main point is realism that I cannot stress enough. With certain class weights and height factors, certain moves can be pulled off, while others are simply impossible. We here at LOW stride on what is possible and what could be done in a wrestling ring. Now, as for choosing your finisher. Originality comes in. Sure, a good old Jackknife Powerbomb, or the dreaded Stone Cold Stunner are moves that can easily be done, and can really knock some sense into your opponent, but … originality! Think of it this way, your wrestler wants to impress the fans out there, so if he could do a 580 Splash and a Stone Cold Stunner, which one do you think he would do? I can’t stress enough how much a finisher is important, because that is the way your wrestler is seen. If your finisher is a very technical type, then you are a king of the mat. If your finisher is one where you use an illegal object, then, we know how to write you in. The writers use the finishers as a special guideline to how your wrestler is. Not every one has time to fill in 200 moves for their wrestler, so in the eventuality (which I strongly discourage) that you do not have a separate move set, then your finisher will be worked on as your in-ring skill. High flying special? High flying tactics. Also, adapt your finisher to the way you want your matches to end. If your finisher can be applied as a counter, you can have more exciting endings that if a proper set-up is absolutely needed to do the whole move. In short, that is what I wanted to get across. To let you know that your finisher is not only something that writers will use to finish a match, it also says a lot about you, and your character. Remember, the greater your imagination is, the greater your RPs become, and the better you are. It all starts at your stats, and your finisher. [Back to top] Segment Writing Tutorials The Basics And Beyond What is an in-card? Let me start off by saying that the in-card is the backbone to every feud and every show. Why’s that? Well you can’t really do too much of a feud inside of a role-play. I mean come on, let’s all be original shall we? Also a show can’t go match after match, that’d be boring, correct? So that’s where in-cards come in to save the day! An in-card is basically described as a role-play in an event or a show. Most of the times an in-card consists of a wrestler arriving to the arena or hyping his of her match up with an interview. Well I think that’s plain overrated. Use your imagination; go beyond the possibilities of an in-card. When you leave the academy, you will notice a lot of effort put into in-cards just like effort put into role-plays. Both are needed for a successful federation, and that’s the only way it’ll work, with your cooperation. Now below, we will go with some detail about an in-card. It might still seem weird and confusing, but down the line you’ll get it slowly. Just keep reading. How do I make an in-card? Do you have Microsoft Word or Notepad? Do you have a keyboard? Do you have a monitor? Do you have a computer? If you’ve answered yes to all those answers, you can make an in-card. But you also need something else in the in-card. Let me explain. Of course you have a wrestler, wouldn’t it be silly if you didn’t have one? No, it’d be stupid. Now you have yourself a wrestler. Now you need a setting. Hmm, a good setting? An arena is a popular one; but lets try to stay away with that. Let’s see, a bar! Yes, there we go. Now we need a gimmick. Hmm, let’s use another popular one. Bad ass. Yeah, you know you like it. You know what I’m talking about? Doesn’t take shit from no one. Thinks he is a bad ass and takes everyone on win or lose. You know? More guts than brains? Alright, we have a gimmick, a wrestler, and a setting. Ok, let’s name our wrestler Pee Wee. Alright? Agreed. Now either Pee Wee can walk into a bar and start shit with someone, or he can walk into a bar and meet someone he despises, LaLa, his arch nemesis. See, you need a plan right. Pee Wee walks into a bar as a bad ass, and runs into LaLa. Now let’s see what you need to finish this. Here are some frequent questions. How should the in-card be formatted? Well first have a look at your weekly shows. Either they are in novel script, which is barely, or just plain old text. What am I talking about? Take a gander below. Novel: “Holy shit,” Brian screams out as he jumps to his feet and looks across from the announcers’ table towards the ring. “He just got dropped right through the ring!” Plain old text: Brian: Holy shit! He just got dropped right through the ring! Brian screams as he jumps to his feet and stares right across the announcers’ table and towards the ring. Ok, there. Those are the two well known ones, but most federations pick Plain old text… So now, we have the format of the in-card. How long should it be? An in-card barely survives on length; it is all in the quality in it. All in-cards MUST have the following. -Great detail -Ambiance -Quality -A reason You need detail. You can’t make an in-card like the following, and get high marks: Jim walks out of his card and into the arena. Joe Shmoe walks up to his with a microphone. Jim: Sup? Joe: Hi If you plan on doing that, I might as well say you’re looking at a 0 instantly. You need detail! What is he wearing? Does he have any hair? Tattoos? I can imagine Jim, an overweight wrestler walking up to a tall interviewer, who thinks he’s all bad. You get the point, you need detail. Your in-cards need a special atmosphere! Something to go down, just not an interview. Maybe a sneak attack or a call out. Something SPECIAL! Oh boy, I have seen enough interviews in my days. It’s just horrid! Now let’s think of something better. An atmosphere of say distinct hatred for another. You need quality! It needs a reason to be put up. Just not a stare down, something more. Words that mean something, just not “shut up you loser”. Something personal or something that will dawn on you. A reason. Of course you need a reason to make an in-card. Start a feud, continue a feud, or what ever. So let’s see the final out come of this in-card we have been brewing up. In-card: The dark and dusky night glooms on. Star after star, they show up in the night sky. Many campers and children might be staring at those stars, but one man in particular lets out a small grin, never seen before. Pee Wee stands tall as he holds onto the edge of the small knob and opens the door to the bar. Instantly tons of eyes trek upon him as his feet creak upon the dusty wooden boards. Pee Wee’s eyes gaze around the bar. Red stools are set against a dusty old counter table; where on the edges a cushion is placed. Small chandeliers hang over two pool table, just before a pair break. His gaze falls upon a man; a man he knows too well, yet not enough. The man is no other then LaLa. Pee Wee pulls puts his hands into his long blue jeans, but not before he straights out his “Security” black t-shirt. He stretches his neck from side to side, having his long silky blonde hair wave. His blue eyes reflect upon everyman in front of him. His brown boots clatter amongst the floor as the walks up the bartender, a man who is wiping a mug clean with a red clean rag. The bartender shows off a gray mustache and an olden face. He wears on top of his head, great white hair; a pair of glasses to go along with it. His shirt is checkered red and black. His blue tight jeans are held up with a long leather belt around his big waist. He chucks out his chest and looks at Pee Wee, whom is now sitting on an empty stool, abandoned from everyone. Pee Wee: How ya’ doing? The bartender gives him a sleek kind of look, as if he suspicious of something. Bartender: Jus’ fine, jus fine. Anythin’ you’d reckon t’ drink? Pee Wee gives himself a sigh and pushes himself straight up and looks at the bartender. Pee Wee: What eve’ you lik’, I lik’. The bartender puts down the mug and cup and gives him a mysterious expression. Without saying a word, the bartender turns around and sets off to do as asked from Pee Wee. Pee Wee settles into this surrounding easily, but not by getting a couple of glances towards him. He glances back at the many of eyes on him and nods his head towards them. Many snicker and turn away and get back to what they were doing earlier. The bartender continues to struggle with the drink and Pee Wee glances towards LaLa, who is staring into space at nothing. His eyes trek along the ceiling to where LaLa is staring, and doesn’t seem to find anything out of the particular. Just then LaLa’s eyes set upon Pee Wee. The look, in Pee Wee’s mind is full of cockiness and brutality; thought LaLa is just giving a friendly nod. Pee Wee emerges from his stool, stricken with anger and hatred right away. Pee Wee walks over, his boots clattering. All eyes on him as LaLa watches him approach him. LaLa looks from his empty chair he sits in, next to a small rounded wooden table with a beer bottle on it. He takes a swig of it and sets it down to a small clink on the table. Pee Wee watches this and towers of LaLa like a grown man over a child. His tongue itches inside his mouth and his ears twitch from side to side. Pee Wee: S’, t’is the bar ya spea’ ov? LaLa gives him a nod of his head, and a small glance into his eyes. Both lock, freezing time for a split second, but part their own ways. Pee Wee: So, thought ya had a match ‘nigh. LaLa nods his head once again, taking another sip of his beer, and setting it down to another clink on the wooden table. LaLa: Yup. Like to get my blood flowing before I step into the ring. Fresh, you know. Pee Wee smiles and scratches his chin, as LaLa pushes off the table and up to his feet, nose to nose with Pee Wee. Pee Wee looks back, hands at his side, forming a small fist. LaLa: Well, I guess it’s time for me to get going and whoop some ass. All of a sudden, an angry Pee Wee throws his hand and grasps the neck of LaLa. LaLa stands there, dumbstruck, looking into Pee Wee’s eyes; the rest of the men in the bar look on as well. Pee Wee: Do ya really t’ink tat ya playin’ mind gam’s with me ‘s goin’ ta make ya ta bette’ man? LaLa smiles as he grasps a hold of Pee Wee’s hands and shoots it away from his neck; the red mark still stands there as he rubs his neck and sizes up Pee Wee. LaLa: I said I got a match to go to, and I think you shouldn’t get in my way, boy. Pee Wee smiles as LaLa pushes himself bye Pee Wee as if he was a door and walks through the bar with every eye on his neck. As soon as he pushes the door and out, all the eyes to back on Pee Wee, who stares back at them. Pee Wee: Whatcha ya starin’ at? End of in-card. Well there you. All to know about an in-card.
