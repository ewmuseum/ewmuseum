A Tokyo-based promotion that staged two Lessons In Bloodshed broadcasts before a third went unfinished and an operator hiatus in July 2010 left its own Tsunawoharu Championship uncrowned. Nineteen wrestlers and five roleplays survive from its six-month run. Written by AI, 2026

Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

Isamu League opened in Tokyo in January 2010 as a direct successor to a defunct promotion, Rising Sun Wrestling, closed since September 2008. Its own promoter, Joey Clerk, reopened under a new name and a new financial backer, Japanese businessman Goro Yamashi, after what he called years of sitting out the business. The promotion's own establishment history recounts Rising Sun Wrestling's turbulent past in full — a hardcore cult following, a riot that killed a promoter, and Clerk's own graphic content getting him banned from the alliance that had once given him back-to-back Promotion of the Year awards.

At launch it had signed one wrestler, “Gentleman” Jonathan Marx, on the strength of his name alone. Its own top prize, the Tsunawoharu Championship, was unveiled at a 31 January 2010 press conference that Yamashi turned into more of a spectacle about himself than about the belt.

Its debut broadcast, Lesson 1, aired 9 March 2010: Randall Beetwix beat goldFISH in the promotion's first-ever match, Naoki Inoue was awarded a forfeit win when AK-47 was unable to compete, Nate Narwin beat Inoue, and Marx closed the show by submitting Riddick with Marxism.

Lesson 2, on 23 March, ran only one match — goldFISH and Riddick fought to a time-limit draw despite a finish that sent both men through a burning barbwire table — before Clerk closed the show announcing the Tsunawoharu Championship's own fate: a one-night tournament open to the whole roster, seeded by ranking points, to crown a first titleholder at the promotion's own first pay-per-view, planned for that June.

Lesson 3 never aired. Its own co-operators posted repeated delays through April 2010, citing real-life work and travel commitments, before a hiatus announcement that July admitted neither of them knew what would become of the promotion, though it “may reopen one day.” No later document survives, and the Tsunawoharu Championship was never awarded.
