Wired to the Moon II...

18 August 2003
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2003-08-18 I can’t help but remember that day. Believe me, it's a day I'd rather forget. It was a couple years ago. Sarah had been bugging me to take her to the carnival. I had a couple days off, so I gave in. I figured it’d be a good father-daughter time. You know, the kind of wholesome family crap they show on TV. I should have known better. Nothing’s wholesome when you’re me. We walked down the midway, passing all the carnival barkers. They shouted out to me, daring me to try knock down all the bottles with one throw, or toss the ring onto the neck of a bottle. I wasn’t buying into their deal, but Sarah was. There’s something about a giant stuffed bear that messes with people’s minds. She pulled on my sleeve and begged me to win one for her. Hey, she’s my little girl. Nothing too good for Daddy’s Little Girl. So I decided I’m going to win her a nice, big prize. I started looking around for a contest suited to my unique skills. Sure enough, I find the old carnival favorite: the Ring the Bell contest. You know, the one where you take a big hammer and smack the plunger, trying to send the gadget up the pole to ring the bell at the top. The Monkey Boy with the hammer saw me coming. “Hey big guy. Looking to win a prize for the little lady?” I nodded. “Damn right. I’m planning on taking that big pink bear off your hands.” “I like your confidence, and I’d hate for you to disappoint your little girl.” He stuck his hand out, and I handed him a couple bills. “You get three swings. If you ring the bell, the pink bear is yours.” The kid was maybe a quarter of my size. He spat in his hands, grabbed the hammer, and slammed it down on the plunger. The ringer shot up the post and rang the bell. He smiled and handed me the hammer. “See? Nothing to it. You ring the bell and I’ll go get that bear for you.” Sarah was glowing. I could see the pride in her eyes. Who knew that, years later, I’d throw all that away. I grabbed the hammer, raised it up, and slammed it down. I paused, waiting for the ‘ding’ of the bell. I waited... and waited. The kid looked at me. “Oh, so close. You’re warmed up now, so let it fly!” Damn right I was ready. I grabbed the hammer and clubbed the plunger again. This time, I watched the plunger shoot up higher, higher… and fall short. I caught the kid smirking a “boy are you stupid” grin. He wiped it off as I looked at him. “One more shot, big guy.” “That’s all I need,” I told him as I grabbed him by the throat. Sarah giggled, the kid shrieked, and I grunted as I chokeslammed him down on the plunger. To this day, I don’t know if I rang the bell. I just walked over, grabbed Sarah’s big bear, and together my daughter and I continued down the boardwalk. In a secluded corner near a big flagpole, I saw the tent. “Fortunes Told - $5” I thought it’d be good for a laugh so I took Sarah and her bear inside. Creepy looking place with candles all over. An old woman, dressed like a gypsy, sat on the opposite side of a round hardwood table. “Please, sit down,” she said. I was hoping she'd have one of those thick, gypsy accents. Guess five bucks doesn’t get you as much as it used to. Sarah looked nervously at the old woman and hugged her bear. I brushed the hair out of her face. “It’s okay. Nothing to worry about.” I turned to the gypsy woman and sat down at the table. I slid five dollars over to her. “Okay,” I dared her, “tell me my future.” She passed a wrinkly hand over the bill, dragging it to the edge of the table and disappearing underneath. She then turned her attention to the crystal ball that sat at the edge of the table. I had thought it was just a prop, and that she’d use tarot cards or tea leaves to do her fortune telling. But I guess she was going all out with her gig. Sarah was taken right in as the old woman peered into the crystal ball. “She’s magic,” Sarah whispered to me. “I see,” the old woman began,” I see you fighting with someone. You're in front of thousands of people, like a modern day gladiator. Are you a boxer?” “A Fighter.” No big surprise there, I told myself. She’d probably seen me on television. “You’ve had much conflict in the past. Your brothers… were wrestlers also?” “Yes.” Lucky guess. “Wrestling has separated you from your family. They see it as the reason one brother is dead and the other is crippled. They blame you for the misfortune, or at least you feel they do.” Unless the old bag was a big fan of mine, there was no way she could have known that. Her next revelation was equally disturbing. “When you were a child, your father used to hit you.” “Look, I didn’t give you five bucks to tell me things I already know,” I snapped at her. “Now tell me my future or you’ll be hobbling around with that glass ball up your two hundred year old sphincter.” She looked deeper into the ball. “Oh dear.” I wasn’t impressed with her theatrics. “Oh dear, what?” “You’re lying in the ring, in a puddle of blood. Your daughter is kneeling beside you, using the blood to write on your chest. There’s a doctor beside you and he’s shaking his head.” She looked away from the ball. “I’m sorry, I can’t see anything beyond that.” I looked over at Sarah. As I thought, she looked scared out of her mind. I stood up. “If my kid has nightmares, I’ll be back to give you some of your own,” I told the old woman. She looked sympathetically at my daughter. “It’s okay Sarah. It won’t happen for a few years yet.” It won’t happen at all, I told myself as we walked out of the tent. I'm Damon O'Halloran. I put people on stretchers, not ride one myself. But out of the blue it hit like a thunderbolt. I walked back into the tent. “How did you know Sarah’s name?” I asked. But the gypsy was gone. All that was left behind were questions... ones that I wasn't ready to answer.
