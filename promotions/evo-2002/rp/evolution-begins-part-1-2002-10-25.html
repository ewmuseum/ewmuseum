eVolution begins part 1

25 October 2002
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 25th October 2002 Kai Kua bowed ceremoniously towards the empty corner of the bedroom in his small but quaint apartment on Manhattan’s east eighty-sixth street. He straightened, focusing on the confluence of the walls, measuring his breathing and gathering his concentration. ‘Haiiii – Ya!’ in a blur of enigma Kai’s compact and toned body snapped into a backwards somersault, the black, white and red belt that held his Shaolin robes closed flapped in the air. He bounded once, then twice. At the apex of the second leap, he shot his feet out, catching the sand stuffed canvas dummy at the point a man’s chin would be. The head was nearly torn from the base as the dummy slammed against the wall and ricocheted to the floor. Kai tucked his legs under him and landed upright. Sweat ran from his freshly shaven head and ran into the face paint that ran faintly from the right corner of his mouth almost to his ear. He wiped at it with the floppy sleeve, then set the dummy upright. Kai had been vigorously working out for almost an hour. He spun once more on the ball of his right foot, striking the dummy in its midsection with and elbow and following with a forearm to the throat. He stepped back and unleashed a ferocious 540º Crescent Kick, ripping the head off of the dummy. The head dropped to the floor and moved slightly, then abruptly stopped. ‘Fuck.’ The solitary word from Kai’s mouth indicated his displeasure at the situation. He stood still, completely and utterly at one with his surroundings. Kai took a deep, cleansing breath, he then stripped off his robes. Kai placed his robes in his wooden box, kept under his bed; the box contained all his Shaolin equipment. Kai strode naked into his bathroom and showered. He dressed in a pair of Nike sweatpants, a Miami University Football Jersey, and a midnight black Nike pullover hooded jumper. His sports shoes were purchased only a few weeks ago, and looked it, sparkling a pearly white. He folded his towel over a nearby radiator and turned the heating up a notch. Kai smiled a wry grin, in Marcus’s death he sure had benefited. Kai walked into his dank kitchen and frowned; he mumbled something in Chinese, he smiled but then remembered why he was angry again. In the kitchen he ate two pieces of tofu – Japanese soybean curd – that he had left for his lunch, and drank a glass of freshly squeezed orange juice. He reached up into the fruit bowl on top of his refrigerator, pulled down his keys, and stuffed them into his pockets. He patted his pocket to check he had the items required for the trip downtown. Kai looked at his forearms, to the tattoo’s covering his Shaolin scars, he shuddered. “…” Kai shook his head and moved towards his front door. Kai opened the door slowly checking all the vital things, windows closed, television off…he nodded and slammed the door shut. He turned, pulled his keys out of his pocket and locked the door. On the street Kai let himself into his old Shelby GT and stretched his legs out as he closed the door. His friend Jin referred to it as the spider’s trap. Kai smiled with satisfaction his right eyebrow raised slightly. He patted his shaven head, feeling the contours and the smoothness of it. For a guy who was Chinese, and a small nose he did his fair share of trapping. Oh to be a bachelor in New York with all those anxious single ladies yearning to be – well, trapped. He drew a deep breath. Even with Chinatown just around the corner, a vigorous hour workout a day plus an extra session at the local dojo every weekend to stay in peak physical condition, this rigorous regime kept Kai’s body taut and agile. Of course, Kai thought to himself as he started the motor, it was the way of the Shaolin as much as the hunting instinct that made him stay in top shape. Somebody once told him to, ‘fight with honour and strength.’ Nevertheless, a much wiser person told him, ‘in combat all you have got going for you is good physical condition and good luck.’ In Chinatown and in wrestling you took your advantages where you found them. Kai drove west on eighty-sixth to third, then uptown to 102nd and east again to the dinghy tenement-filled block where the hundred year old brick pile that hosts the twenty third precinct is located, and parked his car. He looked towards the quay and began to jog; Kai had done this for the past week. It often occurred to Kai Kua that the forms of leisure activity that men and women choose are more reflective of their personalities than their professions. Life may visit a salesman’s role on someone whose inclinations are better revealed by undivided attention to a stamp collection. Kai tugged at his sweatpants to set them more securely around his waist. The wind that blew across the Rockaway Inlet from the Atlantic Ocean gave an extra bite to the chill of the failing day. He checked his watch and increased the length of his stride. His pulse rate picked up a bit, but there was no change in his breathing pattern; mainly due to the Shaolin breath control exercises, he had done. He had run six miles in less than an hour, and had another two miles left to his car in the parking space outside the police precinct, near Chinatown. Kua was a contemplative man. He had chosen to leave the monastery and live for the fight so to speak; he had chosen this path along with his close friend Jamal, who was working the same fight arenas as him. Unlike Jamal, Kai was not married and had no family. He read a lot, and enjoyed working out every da, both for the pleasure of activity as well as to keep his sacred vowels to keep in peak physical condition, he also enjoyed the chance to contend with his thoughts. He smiled to himself as he picked up his pace a bit more. Jamal, on the other hand, hated running. He could not talk and run at the same time. Much too passive, he always said. Instead, he would go to a gym; something Kai could not and would not do. He feared he would become soft if he used machinery to help him keep fit; instead, he spent hours a day kicking the stuffing out of a dummy, practising the taijiquan techniques he knew too well. Of course, Kai thought, he had to live with his rage. His father, he knew nothing of and his mother, well to Kai she might as well be dead. He grimaced and started to run harder almost in an attempt to inflict pain into him for the guilt he was feeling. Kai could see his car across the muddy field and past the built up area of Chinatown. He leaned his lean frame into the wind and began to pump his arms. He was 5’9” smaller then Jamal by a couple of inches, but at 193lbs of muscle he weighed more. The wind burned his high cheekbones set in a narrow yet handsome face, with small lifeless brown eyes, a scrunched up nose, and a placid expression. He sprinted the last quarter mile and came to the old Shelby, pounding his feet now on concrete. He touched the back of the car and smiled. He stood taking small breaths, seeming as if he had not ran anywhere. Kai went to the boot of his car, looking at the now muddy sports shoes. He opened the boot carefully climbing out of each shoe, he placed them neatly by the side of a spare wheel. He slipped on his worn but comfortable Timberland boots. Kai shut the boot and locked the car. As on every Saturday Kai would make his way to the dojo in Chinatown to train under a master of taijiquan, Matsushima. Kai entered a loft building in Chinatown. The old structure had been reinforced on the inside and the brick repainted and sandblasted. A savings bank for Asian citizens occupied the lower floor. One flight up there was the Tokyo and Seoul Sauna and Health Club, an oriental massage parlour. On the third and top floor was the ten-do karate and taijiquan dojo. Kai took a key from his pocket and let himself in. the room was dimly lit. Such light as there was reflected from the thick white mats that covered the floor and padded the walls. In the locker room in the rear Kai stripped and showered, washing himself thoroughly, seated on a small wooden stool. When he was dry, he put on a loincloth tied about his middle with a cotton rope, then the freshly laid judo suit with flapping pants legs and sleeves. He laid his street clothes in a small pile in the corner and bowed to the Buddha on the wall. He knocked indifferently at an adjacent door. ‘Hai?’ ‘Kai Kua, Matsushima-sama.’ Always Sama, this showed Kai’s deeper respect for the Japanese gentlemen who had also left the same Shaolin monastery as him all those years ago. The light in the office had a red cast from two paper lanterns. An inlaid writing table of oriental design stood in a corner beside straight-backed armless chair. Tatami mats of woven grass covered the floor. On a cushion in the midst, Hiroo Matsushima sat cross-legged. He was if indeterminate age by appearance though Kai knew he had left the monastery at eighteen which was in 1948, he must have been around 72 years old. He seemed slight in only a cotton judo suit.
