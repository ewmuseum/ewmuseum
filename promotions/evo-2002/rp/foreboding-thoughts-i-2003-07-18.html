Foreboding Thoughts I...

18 July 2003
Content noticeA period document, served unedited. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
Date: 2003-07-18 Damon O'Halloran stared at the wall as he finished squeezing out another repetition of dumbbell curls. He dropped the weights on the floor and stretched his arm muscles. A thin layer of sweat covered his body; his muscles now warm and stretched. Damon walked over to the mirror and inspected himself one final time: wrestling boots shined and double tied, trunks fastened, wrists taped. Body pumped. Time to wage war. Damon stepped out of the locker room and walked down the dimly lit hallway. At the end, the bright orange lights shone through the curtains, the dull roar of the crowd’s anticipation sending shivers down O'Halloran’s spine. After all these years, Damon still felt the rush when he stepped through the curtains. He took a breath, stepped through the curtain... ... And felt the wind rush by as he began to fall. He twisted his body, trying to grab something, anything, but still he continued down into the darkness. The roaring in his ears increased, and Damon felt that the freefall was coming to an end. He braced himself for impact. He stopped. No crunch, nothing. Just stopped. “Watch that first step,” a voice said from the darkness. Damon scrambled to his feet. “Where am I?” “Damon, right now you’re nowhere.” The oddly familiar voice was getting closer. “But that’ll change soon enough. We’ve got places to go, people to see.” The darkness was beginning to lift, like a mist disappearing in the morning sun. “What do you mean ‘we’, buddy. Who the Hell are you? Im not going anywhere until I start getting some answers.” The features on the person in front of him began to develop. There was something very familiar about this guy. “Do I know you?” The haze lifted. A young man in his early 20’s stood before Damon. His dark hair and dark eyes were a contrast to his pale skin. He wore a ‘O'Halloran = Cell Champion’ t-shirt, his thumbs tucked into the pockets of his jeans. “Of course you know me. I’m your brother Tom. You killed me, remember?” “No...” Damon said in disbelief, his voice barely a whisper. “You’re not him. Tom’s dead. They killed him.” “Tom was killed because all he wanted to do was become a fighter and lead the new revolution. You worshipped him. 'They' took that away from him. That was his dream and they squashed it.“ The man smiled. “Still don’t recognize me? Here, maybe this will refresh your memory.” He raised the shotgun to his lips and slid the barrel into his mouth. “Tom! NO!” Damon cried out and lunged toward him. Everything seemed to happen in slow motion. The flash from the muzzle could be seen though Tom’s cheeks, which billowed out as smoke escaped past his lips and down the barrel. A red haze sprayed out from the back of his head, followed by red chunks and some bloody teeth. His body jerked back and collapsed, the gun falling to the side. Damon fell to the ground beside his brother; a cold empty feeling growing in his chest quickly replaced by sorrowful agony. “Oh God...” he said, his voice hoarse. Tom looked up. “Give us a kiss,” he said, puckering his bloody lips. Damon stood up as Tom got to his feet. “Wait a second. I thought you died when those bastards gunned you down? I saw it with my own eyes... Didnt I?!' Tom shook his head. “They didn‘t find the men who did it? I‘m so disappointed in today‘s law enforcement. No, I made my own death... I killed myself. I wanted the whole country to divide. What are we living in today, Damon? Nothing but a Unionist Stronghold. We are only dogs to them. So I created a wave of hatred, to say the least. It was a little overkill, I admit, but that‘s us O'Halloran boys: go big or go home.” “I’m dead, right? I slipped in the shower and broke my neck?” Damon asked as Tom wiped the blood from his face. Tom was no worse for wear. “No, you’re not dead. Well, not totally dead. I’m not a ghost, if that’s what you’re thinking.” He paused in thought. “Wait. Yeah, I guess you could think of me as a ghost. I’m the Ghost of your Past.” The sun shone down on O'Halloran, and he squinted his eyes against the light. “Tom, you know I can’t handle the light! Where’s my sunglasses?” “Little brother, you’re such a baby.” Tom handed his brother a pair of sunglasses. Damon slid them on and looked around. Fields of wheat lined the road, swaying slightly in the breeze. Damon kicked a pebble off the gravel road, raising a small cloud of dust under his foot. “Where are we?” “More like when are we,” Tom replied. “Remember that summer when Dad took us to visit his Uncle Tim in Lahinch in County Clare? We spent the summer on his farm...” “... And you and Shane were terrified of the cows!” Damon laughed. “I remember that. Every time a cow moo’d at you, you thought it was getting ready to trample you.” Tom smiled and started to walk down the road. Damon followed. The warm summer sun warmed his skin; the dust stuck to his sweaty legs. The fields began to fall away, and the road dwindled into a path. Damon was thankful for the cool shade provided by the trees that surrounded him. He could see a river just beyond the trees as the path opened up into a small clearing in the forest. “You watch your mouth when you talk to me boy!” Damon watched a large boy with a child’s face struggle to fight back tears as a barrel-chested man brought down a thick leather belt across the boy’s outstretched palms. The man’s face was red with anger. “When I tell you to do something, you do it! I don’t care how big you are! I’m your father and you’ll do as your told!” “B-but I don’t like fishing,” the boy sobbed. “So you throw away the fishing rod I gave you? You ungrateful bastard!” The man brought the belt down again across the boy’s red-welted palms. Tom stood back and watched his younger brother as the scene unfolded. He saw Damon involuntarily rub the palms of his hands as he remembered the blows his father dealt him that day, and many other days. “He didn’t like people disagreeing with him,” Tom said. “It was his way or the highway.” “He was never around,” Damon said as he watched the big boy’s tears roll down his cheeks. “He was always out on his damn fishing boat with his crew. He wanted us all to become commercial fishermen like him.” Tom grimaced. “I hate fish. Never did like the smell.” “Mam used to say that he wanted us to become fisherman so we could all be together on the boat,” Damon said. “That was the line, until that night when he got himself drunk and told us that we’d join his crew so he wouldn’t have to pay us. We‘d be cheap help for him. It sealed the deal for me. I hate fishing, I hate boats, and I hate him, a weak man, the antithesis of what Nationalism stands for. Luckily a man is not judged by what his father does rather than his own achievements' Damon spat. He picked up a nearby branch. “That’s it.
