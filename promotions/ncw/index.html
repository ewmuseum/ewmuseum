A play-by-e-mail fed run almost single-handedly by one Iowa-based operator across two runs -- founded 1994, merged away into a rival that January, and relaunched by the same founder weeks later as "NCW, v.2" -- whose own inaugural supercard crowned four intergender champions in one night before a signed, reasoned closure ended it for good that November. Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

Network Championship Wrestling opened in Iowa in October 1994 under the on-air persona of its founder, the Jammer, and ran for two and a half years before merging into a rival promotion that January 1997. Its founder relaunched it himself weeks later, announcing “NCW is back, baby! And better than ever!” and billing the comeback plainly as “the resurgance of an e-wrestling legend” rather than a new venture wearing an old name.

Its original run produced one of its best-known acts, the tag team Fatal Attraction, who left the promotion in a March 1996 contract dispute while holding both this fed's own World Tag Team title and its counterpart from a sister promotion at once — by the promotion's own account, the first tag team or singles wrestler ever to hold both belts simultaneously. A June 1997 community reminiscence from a former booker credited the promotion's own style — its presentation, its humor, its character types — with reshaping how a “good” e-fed was expected to look, tracing a whole family tree of later promotions back to its own influence.

The relaunched promotion crowned its own titles in a single supercard that June, Free-for-All, staged live in Iowa City: four championships — the World Internet, World Women's, World Tag Team and World Heavyweight titles — each decided by an elimination gauntlet run through the promotion's entire active roster, its Tag Team gauntlet open to men and women alike. The card ran alongside a week of tie-in community events in Iowa City, including a beauty pageant styled after the promotion's own name and a charity push for local pet adoption.

The Jammer closed the promotion himself on 25 November 1997, in a public post that named the reasons plainly: the same booker excesses that had defined the original run — “big egos, 'shoot' interviews, and requests for run-ins galore” — returning in the relaunch, and his own free time “dwindled to almost nothing” over the summer with no recovery in the fall. “Thus ends the legacy of NCW,” he wrote, before listing his remaining wrestlers as available for other promotions to sign.
