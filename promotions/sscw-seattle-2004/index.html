A Seattle promotion that launched in the spring of 2000 with a president's open recruiting letter, crowned its first United States Champion on its debut Sunday Night Survival card, and resurfaced under the same ownership and arena four years later with a revised handbook recasting its own on-screen financial backer. Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

Stars and Stripes Championship Wrestling staged its own founding around a wrestler's story rather than a business one: CEO and President Lance Avery, a former tag and mid-card champion for another promotion whose world-title shot was ended by a hip injury off a top-turnbuckle dropkick, was released and told by doctors he could never compete again. Encouraged by his girlfriend, Ashley Parks, out of a depression that followed, he spent ten months tracking down a ring, belts, staff and an arena on backing from a company called the American Sports Network, and opened SSCW to the public on 22 June 2004.

The federation's own handbook revised itself within three days of that first posting: the financial backer behind American Sports Network is billed as Chester Taylor in the original text and as Dave Simmons, described in identical terms, in a Version 1.2 released two days later, with one of the promotion's referees renamed the same way — the same office recast rather than a change of plans. Around Avery and his backer, the handbook staged a full office: Commissioner Orlando Robinson overseeing talent recruiting, announcers Chad Brown and Stevie T. Dee calling the promotion's main weekly show and “Lethal” Larry Cobalt joining Brown on its twice-weekly roleplay bulletin, ring announcer Arnold Myers, and a slate of referees headed by Kevin “Swift” Justice. Three championships were planned from the start — a World Championship, a United States Championship, and a World Tag Team Championship — each deliberately defended only rarely to keep it, in the handbook's own words, a matter of “prestige, honor, and utmost pride.”

Its debut card, Saturday Night SURVIVAL, was billed for 23 June 2004, staged live outside the SSCW Arena itself — a converted glass factory still a week behind its own construction schedule — in downtown Seattle. The card topped out with “Marvelous” Mike Magnum against “The Italian Stallion” Bruno Bandini, carried three further matches and three dark bouts beneath it, and promised an announcement about the United States Championship on the night.

No result for that debut card survives. The record ends two days later with a hostile, content-free reply, and no closing notice or further post survives after it.
