Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

National e-mail Championship Wrestling opened in late March 1997 out of Detroit, Michigan, and its own FAQ names its models directly: “the old NWA and Bill Watts' UWF,” run at the scale of “a good-sized independent regional fed, like USWA or the now-defunct Smoky Mountain.” It let handlers invent their own wrestlers rather than portray real ones, and decided matches on a blend of booking and simulation: a flash-quality score set against a strategy grade, with a simulator called in only when the two were close. Three cards went out each week — Superstars, Friday Fracas, and a weekend wrap-up — alongside three magazine shows built from handlers' own submitted flashes.

Four titles ran under its own roof: a National Heavyweight Championship open only to its top five contenders, a Television Championship open to anyone, a Submission Ring held by whoever last won by submission, and a Unified Tag Team Championship built, its own FAQ says, from a set of Mexican tag belts a promoter donated and had altered for NECW's own use. Its broadcast team ran deep enough to carry its own internal drama: colour man “Living Legend” Al Rich was fired from the booth after returning to the ring himself to feud with a tag team he was supposed to be calling fairly.

It ran a full calendar into the fall: BorderWars '97 in October, further cards and magazine shows into 1998, and Collision Course '98 as late as 1 February 1998. It closed six weeks later, on 15 March 1998, at its own in-fiction press conference: its president, citing a newly diagnosed illness, announced NECW had run for “a year in operation,” put out more than seventy television shows and nine pay-per-views, and launched wrestlers who had gone on to compete elsewhere under their own names. A rival federation's own president offered every NECW wrestler a contract on the spot.
