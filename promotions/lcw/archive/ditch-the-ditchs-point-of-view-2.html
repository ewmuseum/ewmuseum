Comments of any sort are encouraged. I like Cinnamon Toast Crunch. The Ditch, editor. Contact the Ditch at [detail withheld] Point of View 2, May 27, 2002: The US needs a bigger stick. It's Memorial Day, so what better time to discuss the military? It's hard to imagine the size of the US military. Consider that so much raw armament was moved to the Middle East for the Gulf War, that it was the equivalent of moving the city of Seattle. Holy crap! No wonder that war was about as competitive as a HHH vs Spike Dudley match. Today, the US couldn't pull that off. Clinton halved the military's size. There are a number of problems with this. 1. Right now, we can only overwhelm with explosives, as opposed to ground forces. While we can destroy bases of operations, it's difficult to pin down a fighting force with just planes. This results in scattering the enemy, not defeating the enemy for all time. 2. Right now, we can only fight one large theater war, or two smaller theater wars, at the same time. To give you an idea of what that means, World War 2 had two large theaters of operation. It would take years of buildup to reach that strength again. 3. Right now, the US military is the only force that is both willing and able to carry out the US' will in global conflicts. The UN is a joke, the EU has a very limited army and often disagrees, Russia's military is a shell of what it used to be, and the rest of the world either dislikes us or is of no use. At this point, a war on terror will be fought with US forces doing the vast majority of the work. 4. Right now, there are dozens of useful applications of the US military. From resolving conflicts (Israel/Palestine, India/Pakistan) to preventing the accumulation of weapons of mass destruction by madmen (Iraq) to stamping out those who supply terrorists with their wares (Iran, North Korea) to aiding nations struggling for real democracy in the face of brutal oppression (most of the Third World), there's a lot that needs to be done. The US needs a larger military. Here's the 'how': 1. Funding? It looks like there's going to be another wave of welfare reforms. That should do the trick. Plus, if they can afford bloated piles of pork like the recent farm bill, they can afford to boost the military. 2. Soldiers? Increase salaries. Those in the armed forces deserve more money, especially since they're dealing with a lot more sophisticated things than rifles and foxholes. If the Army actually pays a competitive wage, there will be more and better soldiers coming in. 3. Technology? No, we don't need to spend billions on the latest plane just because it makes military contractors happy. Our technology is SO much better than the rest of the world, it isn't funny. Stick with the basics and we can double the military's effectiveness with a lot less than double the price. 4. Streamlining. The military is a bureaucracy just like any other part of the government. Cut the red tape and more money will go where it's needed. In a few years, the military can be up to where it was in 1990, and we'll be able to effectively wage war on the forces of terrorism and oppression. And that, my friends, is a good thing (unless you're Tim Hall, in which case you don't matter.) Point of View 2, April 29, 2002: Education The left wing has a distinct advantage with Americans in certain issues, and perhaps chief among them is education. The left always says that they'll try to increase education spending, and year after year they keep that promise. Yet, in many ways the education system is the worst it has ever been. Huge percentages of those in public schools fail even rather basic standardized tests in a variety of subjects, at a variety of grade levels. These numbers are worst in the places where education is the most vital (poorer and inner city schools). What's the solution? 1. Raise and maintain standards. Often, educators will seek to correct poor performance by rigging the tests. Not that long ago, there was a huge story about how the average SAT score was up, and it was covered as plainly as could be. In reality, the grades were inflated across the board, and minus the inflation, grades went DOWN! The easier the tests are, the less students are taught in order to pass them. 2. Less control of the NEA over schools. As the decades have passed, the NEA has done what it can to get more and more power over schools, from administration to curriculum to budgets. The NEA has always been a leading opponent of measures designed to solve fundamental problems in the system. Can any of you tell me what direction the NEA tilts? Yup, they lean as heavily to the left as possible, and the more influential they've become, the worse schools have become. 3. Cut the red tape. Nearly every single private school (including the high school I went to) is able to provide a better education with a dramatically lower per-student cost. Why? It's simple. Public schools are burdened by wasteful regulations and mandates from the federal government. Streamline the education system and there will be plenty of money to spend on what students need . The main thing would be putting more control in the hands of state and local governments, where individuals have more of a say over what goes on and can make more specific changes as needed. 4. School vouchers. Here's where things get a lot more politically charged. School vouchers would be something to help the poor at the expense of the rich . Elite private schools contain primarily the children whose parents are the wealthiest, and not so much the children who would be the best students. Vouchers would be money sent from the government to every parent of a school-aged child, to be spent only on education. These vouchers would allow millions upon millions of families to afford private schools, which in turn will help millions of children to better themselves socially through education. But, you ask, what of the public schools? Well, the worst schools would wind up shutting down, and as private schools become more prevalent, public schools will be forced to make the changes necessary to compete . Competition is one of the vital parts of something I keep bringing up over and over in these columns, capitalism. 4a. Who opposes school vouchers? The left, because it would take power away from public schools (and thus the NEA) and place it in the hands of the parents. If the left is the better side for education, then why would they oppose something like school vouchers which would be designed to take away an advantage the rich have always had on the poor? Point of View 2, April 16, 2002: Israel and terrorism Not many people fully understand the situation in Israel. I think it's very important that everyone does. We start in 1948. The area in and around Israel was controlled by the British, and it was the home to a pocket of Jews and nomadic Arab tribes. The Jews asked the UN to create a nation, and the UN agreed. The area was divided up rather oddly, but when all was said and done the livable land was split fairly evenly. On the day of Israel's founding, the British troops left, and the Arab League declared war on Israel despite Israel's desire for peaceful coexistence. Armies from five Arab nations invaded, and were soundly defeated. In the process, nearly half a million Arabs living in Israel fled, getting out of the way of the armies with the hope that they would return home to an all-Arab state. This is where much of the current Palestinian population comes from. Meanwhile, 600,000 Jews were forced out of surrounding Arab nations and moved to Israel. In 1950, Jordan annexed what Arab land there was left from the division of British land. This is the 'West Bank', currently known as the 'occupied territories'. There was no call for a Palestinian state, despite the fact that Palestinians were the most populous ethnic group in Jordan and were completely disenfranchised. In 1967, Egypt and Jordan attacked Israel without the slightest provocation, and again Israel won handily. In the process, they took land from both Egypt and Jordan won in the process of the war, which was their right as the defenders in the war. This is where the current Israel borders were established. In 1973, Israel was attacked AGAIN, and AGAIN they won. Afterwards, Egypt and Israel signed a peace deal (which Egypt has kept), and in return Israel gave back some of the land won from Egypt in 1967. The PLO, or Palestine Liberation Organization, is the group headed by Arafat which has become the Palestinian Authority. They formed in 1964, three years before Israel won the West Bank. To repeat, at that time the land which is demanded be given over for a Palestinian state was controlled by Jordan. The PLO did NOT want Jordan to give them land for their own nation, but instead, simply wanted to destroy Israel. The PLO was founded by the Arab League, not the Palestinians, and is run primarily by non-Palestinians; Arafat is an Egyptian by birth. It was not until the 1993 Oslo agreement that the PLO removed the plank in their charter calling for Israel's destruction. Currently, there are 3.7 million Palestinian refugees. Out of all the Arab countries calling for Israel to give back the West Bank, only one- Jordan- is willing to allow these refugees into their country. So, the Arabs invade Israel, lose land because of it, and then ignore the refugees created by their actions while calling for Israel to help found a state which wasn't considered necessary when the same land was controlled- without any political rights given to citizens- by Arabs. Yeah. The Oslo agreement was signed in 1993 by Rabin and Arafat, and was gradually leading towards a Palestinian state. Between 1993 and 1999 there were more Israelis killed by terrorist attacks than from 1967 to 1992. During this time, Arafat only gave lip service to peace while knowingly allowing terrorist groups to operate. In 2000, the Palestinians launched the Intifada (a wave of terror attacks), claiming that it was because current Israeli leader Sharon had dared to visit the mosque on the Temple Mount. However, it was set into motion months earlier, when Arafat showed once and for all that he will not commit to peace. At Camp David, Arafat, Clinton and then Israeli leader Barak met, hoping to sign another peace deal that would ACTUALLY stop the terrorism. Barak offered the Palestinians a fully recognized state in 95-97 percent of the land in dispute, and supplies for a 40,000 man police force to help control it. Arafat rejected the offer and walked away from the table without a counter offer. It was at this point that the Intifada was born. Today, Israeli leader Sharon is attempting to shut down the terror networks in the West Bank, and in the process he's pretty much shut down the Palestinian Authority. Here are several reasons why this is justified. 1. As mentioned above, the notion of the Palestinians having suffered a horrible wrong at the hands of Israel is nonsense, especially since... 2. Arafat refuses to allow a meaningful peace, even when offered virtually everything he could possibly want. 3. Far from being like such organizations like the IRA, or freedom fighters like the founders of America, the Palestinian Authority preaches anti-Semitism and genocide, is a dictatorship, and even during peace talks continues to preach to its people (from the children on up) that the Jews must be 'driven into the sea'. Hatred of the Jewish people is at the forefront, not the government of Israel. 4. The Israelis are not massacring civilians. Reports from the PA not only exaggerate death tolls, but also equate armed combatants with innocents. 5. The heavy level of military control is necessary, since terrorist groups have shown a willingness to use any means at hand to kill Israelis. From children as suicide bombers, to transporting bombers in ambulances, the methods are utterly deplorable and designed in such a way as to make it impossible to halt the terror without infringing on civilians. 6. It's been effective! Hundreds of known terrorists have been arrested or killed, lots of supplies have been taken, and the basic operations which have gone on for decades are now in shambles. At this point, it seems to me that the only solution is a Palestinian state, but NOT one controlled by Arafat, the PLO, the PA, or any other such group which has coddled and created terrorists. Palestinians should have UN monitored (or even UN controled) elections for leaders, and these leaders in turn should negotiate peace with Israel. Israel has shown that it wants peace, just as long as the terrorism stops. Now to why the US has a role to play in this whole deal. President Bush declared war on terrorism, and he was right to do so. Terrorism uses violence against civilians as a means to a political gain, rather than violence against an oppressing military, passive resistance or negotiation. Terrorism can NOT be rewarded, or tolerated, and that is why Israel must be allowed to retaliate against the PA and the terrorist groups. This is exactly what we did in Afghanistan; a dictatorial regime headed by non-natives with an eye towards aiding and abetting terror was flushed out and replaced by a sane government. If we're to be taken seriously, we need to support the eradication or terrorism across the globe, even in places where Europe seems obsessed with tolerating it. Point of View 2, April 7, 2002: Less Government = Good This is a big one, so you might need two or three sittings to digest it all. Trust me, it's all good. America's GDP is 6 trillion dollars a year. The GDP is then turned into money through the economy. One-third of that money goes to the government... 2 trillion dollars a year. Two-thirds go to the American people. I, for one, believe that the first number is too high, and the second number is too small. Let me say that the government has a role. There is anywhere from 25 to 50 percent of government spending which is either absolutely necessary, or generally good for the American public. Things like infrastructure, the military, education, the criminal justice system (including industry regulation), emergency response, and so on, are all vital. When determining the functions of government, we need to ask two things. Number one, is it good that this is being done? Number two, would this be better left to the private sector? If the answers are "yes" and "no", then it's a good thing. Otherwise, it's not. I'll discuss the second question. The private sector will find solutions to problems in the most efficient manner possible, simply because it operates on profit. The government, on the other hand, has no such obligation; it will only rarely take the efficient route in solving a problem. As more and more aspects of life are delegated to the government, the size of the federal bureaucracy increases. These workers are a twofold drain on the economy; they produce nothing, and they draw salary from it. Need an example of how bad things are? Try this. It takes three dollars of taxpayer money for every one which reaches the hands of a welfare recipient. The rest is sucked up in the leviathan that is the government. Many other programs, such as Medicare and Medicaid, are the same way. A belief that the government should run as many aspects of life as possible is socialism. Socialism leads to things like state-run industry, which in turn is the ultimate antithesis of capitalism. Socialism is NOT the Soviet Union or China; those are representations of communism. A good example of what the American left wants can be found in Europe, where most nations are democratic, yet also have governments at levels above 50 percent of GDP. People get lots of goodies, like mandated vacations, high minimum wage, health care, tons of unemployment benefits, huge perks for union workers, fat checks for the elderly, etc. Europe is also about 20 years away from a crippling economic collapse; already they face a massive current debt, and as the population grows older, they will be forced to tax workers to an unbearable degree. Europe also has far more unemployment, and a far smaller GDP, than in the US. Why such a big problem? TOO MUCH GOVERNMENT. So, let me sum up what I've said so far. Governments are inherently wasteful, and as a nation moves towards more and more government action it faces big troubles. If you follow me so far, then it's fairly evident that socialism is simply not the answer. That said, what can we in America do to move away from socialism? Simple. Reject its core mantra: "From each, according to his means. To each, according to his needs." In the rejection of this statement, you reject two things. First, higher taxes for higher income levels. Second, the bloated entitlement system. I'll discuss both of these points. On the first point, there are of course limits. Someone scrubbing toilets or flipping burgers doesn't make enough to be able to afford to pay taxes. At the same time, someone who founds a business and guides said business to success shouldn't have well over half his income taken away simply because of success. That in mind, I think we ought to reduce the "slope" of the income tax curve even further than it is currently. The upper class pays slightly more than the middle class, which only pays slightly more than the lower class. In the process, nobody's taxes ought to be raised. If we do this, every single American will have more money than they could ever imagine, from the top to the bottom, as hundreds of billions are pumped into the economy. The above tax cut could be facilitated by going through with point two and destroying the entitlement system. That means the government moves away from welfare, Medicare, Medicaid, social security, and all variations thereof. These programs all act to redistribute wealth from those who earn to those who do not; socialism at its most basic. I'll cover these programs individually. -Welfare: The reason why we have projected budget surpluses is because Clinton was forced by Republicans to sign welfare reform. Thanks to it, millions of Americans have moved off the government dole and become productive workers, which reduces the debt twofold. We'll probably see a continuation of this in the Bush administration. We shank welfare a lot, and I bet we can shrink it even more. Further, there must be ways to streamline it to the point where it's 50-100% more efficient than today. -Medicare and Medicaid. Many talk about how expensive medical care is, and how few people either can't afford it or don't have health insurance. I say, if we get rid of most of the tax burden imposed by these systems, far more people will be able to afford insurance on their own, and prices will fall. Government payment of most medical care artificially inflates the price; the elderly don't "shop around" if taxpayers are the ones footing the bill. Keep a remnant of the system around for the extremely poor and merge it with welfare. -Social security. Boy, what a monster this is. Here are a couple reasons why we not only don't need to put more money into it, but should in fact kill it altogether. 1. The government has "borrowed" hundreds of billions from the system to cover up the deficit. Said money ought to be returned as soon as possible, and be paid back to those who originally paid it. 2. The system was set up to secure savings in the event that they're wiped out in a banking collapse... which has only EVER happened in 1929. The FDIC is around to prevent that sort of thing today. 3. The system was designed for a period when the average person would only be around 1-5 years to collect it. Now, it's more like 12-20 years. This needs to be altered if we're going to at least be realistic in keeping it in any form. 4. The system isn't nearly as good a savings mechanism as even the most risk-free market investments. That is to say, the public could do better if they controlled the funds on their own. 5. Social security is the biggest tax burden faced by the poor; even if it's refunded later, the money is held by the government without collecting interest until April 15. Social security is the most prominent legacy of FDR's New Deal. It, along with several other "entitlement" programs, were supposedly a major factor in the US' economic recovery. This is simply false. The only reason why the economy pulled up is World War 2; production and army jobs killed the unemployment rate. The government went into MASSIVE debt in the process, but it got the job done. Sadly, once the war machine was turned down, the entitlements were not. There's a reason for that. The Democratic party uses entitlement politics in an attempt to sway many voters their way. When you see statements to the effect of "Republicans want to cut (insert entitlement program here). (Insert group of people here) will starve/ not get medicine/ spontaneously combust, and give the money to the rich. They're evil," you can sense that something odd is going on. By pandering to those who receive federal funds by claiming that they can't survive without the programs, Democrats in essence blackmail many into voting for them. This is why welfare reform didn't go through before 1994. So, there you have it. Reasons why less government action than we have today is better, along with specific examples of how moving to streamline the government will help fight the vestiges of socialism which tug at our collective purse strings. Gooooo capitalism! Point of View 2, March 29, 2002: Right is right "What, ANOTHER column? He barely updates the ones he has now." Yeah, I know that's what some of you are thinking. Well, for my money, more Ditch is better than less Ditch, so more Ditch it is! This column will be about politics, and nothing but. So, first, a little background. 1. Democracy and capitalism are good. Socialism cannot work with either of them. Conservatism, on face, rejects socialism and embraces capitalism more often than liberalism. That's why I identify myself as a conservative. 2. The two-party system, as it stands, is not the best possible representation of democracy. Too many politicians vote for what's popular, not what they believe; too often we are forced to choose between men and/or women who don't actually stand for what we believe in. That's bad, and there must be ways of solving this. 3. I might be right-wing, but I'm not a Republican shill (or even a Republican). I'll call it like I see it. If Booker T has a bad match, he has a bad match; if George Bush makes a bad call, he makes a bad call. My personal preferences won't get in the way of what makes sense to me. 4. Not enough people vote, and not enough people are informed on the issues. Both lead to the wrong people getting elected. It is my goal to inform you, the reader, about what I consider to be correct, and to get you to vote for people who best respresent said opinions. If what I say makes you think the opposite, then I still want you to vote... just as long as you know WHY. 5. If you don't agree with me, call me on it, but be sure to use important things like 'fact' and 'reason'. I won't hesitate to reprint opposing viewpoints in this column if they make sense. The same goes for questions; if you don't understand, don't hesitate to ask. 6. Don't skim. Sometimes, the smaller points on an issue can be crucial; read it all, even if you have a 6-second attention span. So, now that all that's established, time for the first issue, and the one where I think most of you will agree with me... TAX CUTS = GOOD First and foremost, tax cuts give money back to the people, who are then able to spend more. Public spending aids the economy, allowing for the investment which is so crucial for growth and development. The bigger the economy, the more revenue is taken in long-term by taxes. This works to a point, of course; we can't have a 1% tax and expect to have a surplus. At the same time, the Reagan tax cuts helped expand government income while also giving the economy a shot in the arm. They helped so much that both Bush (senior) and Clinton were able to do small tax raises without serious harm to the economy. The Reagan cuts were a good thing, but you won't always hear that. There are two major criticisms to it. 1. The rich got richer and the poor got poorer. 2. It created a huge deficit. Wrong on both counts! 1. The rich got richer, and the poor also got richer. The rich simply got richer RELATIVE to the poor. Everyone has more, so everyone wins; I don't see the problem with that. 2. The deficit was caused by a glut of pork and social program spending. This was Reagan's mistake; he was supposed to cut taxes and cut the budget, but he allowed congress to inflate government spending. Same with Bush (senior), who not only let government get bigger, but also RAISED taxes. If the spending had been kept to inflation-level increases, then the deficit wouldn't have happened. It should also be noted that the Bush (junior) tax cuts haven't gone into effect yet for the most part, and are not the cause of this year's shortfall; that blame lies on 9/11. So, to sum up, tax cuts boost the economy, which create long-term revenue growth. While they favor the wealthy, they help the poor and middle-class as well. Reagan's cuts were a very good thing, and the deficit wasn't caused by them, but by excess spending. Next column: Why the government's role should be as limited as possible.
