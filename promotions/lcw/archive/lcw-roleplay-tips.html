Roleplay Tips

January 2002
Content noticeA period document, served as recovered; withheld details are marked. It carries the humor, language, and content typical of its era and community — some of it crude, dated, or offensive, including material that wouldn't be written today. See Curation.
LCW Central:: Where It All Comes Together LCW's Official Roleplay Tips Welcome to LCW's Official Roleplay Tips Section. After putting my character Lex Laww on the shelf, I sat down and devised a set of simple steps that made Lex a main eventer in three different feds. Not content to base it solely on what I know (cause Lord knows I could be a lot better), I sent it along to some of the consensus top roleplayers in this fed: (in no particular order) Archangel, Blayd, Gol, Harbinger, Hector Carreras, Mike Static, New School, Rook Black, and Used. This is what we've come up with… Tip One: This might sound nitpicky, but professionalism is a must, so correct spelling and grammar are right up there with entertaining content on what we rate to determine your push. The number one thing to any roleplay is not grammar and spelling but it definitely helps. Nothing bothers us as bookers more than to have to sit down and read a roleplay that confuses us. This also includes using appropriate spacing in your roleplay. Too often does a relatively high quality roleplay get squashed because of poor usage of spacing. If all your dialogue and sentences run together, legibility is nil. Bad Example: Lex Laww: I am the best ever! Hector Carreras: No you're not, I'm better than you! Lex Laww: JUST BRING IT! (Both man glance at each other with anger in the eyes.) Hector: Your days are numbered! See, that is just too confusing to follow. With proper spacing, however: Good Example: Lex Laww: Mr. Carreras, I'm growing tired of your incessant whining. (Lex stares down at Carreras in a condescending manner, and Hector cannot hold himself back any longer, as he lashes out verbally at the former lane-crossing guard.) Hector: And I'M growing tired of your bull$*@^! Shut up, or put up. While proper grammar is a very important part of a roleplay, don't be afraid to use slang or certain types of dialect if your character or an extra calls for it IN THEIR DIALOGUE. An example is if your character is from the streets of a city and uses current urban slang, such as referring to shoes as "kicks", and other such slang. While using slang and accents is good in small doses, it can be over done. We'll figure out that your character is from New York or Taiwan, you don't necessarily have to ram it down our throats. However, this is no excuse to make the rest of your roleplay (character actions, setting description, etc), poorly worded or spelled. Use the proper homophones as well, as these commonly made mistakes can really dull down the quality of a roleplay. Here are some of the more commonly misused homophones and their real meanings: Hear: To listen to one speaking or the sounds of a surrounding area. "He could hear them discussing his contract in the next room, and it infuriated him." Here: A location usually in reference to one's current location. "The match will take place right here , in this very ring." Their: Possessive pronoun for multiple people. " Their title belts were thrown on top of the heap that used to be the Tag Team Champions." They're: Contraction of "they" and "are". " They're scheduled to be in action here tonight on Tempest." There: One's location, usually in reference to a destination rather than a current area. "Who the hell is that, over there in the shadows of the parking garage." Threw: To have thrown. "He threw the football with all the precision of a quarterback, nailing the Hardcore Champion right in the nose." Through: to go from one end to another. "While traveling through the arena, they were attacked by their rivals." Too: linking word means "also". "Where he went, his faithful dog was sure to go, too ." Two: numerical adjective, meaning one more than one and one less than three. "One plus one equals two ." To: helping verb. "He wants to go." Of course, there are literally hundreds more; however, going into them all would take longer than I've intended this section to be. Because the spellchecker on our Roleplay Board can be a huge hassle, you may want to write your roleplays in a program such as MS Word, MS Works, or WordPerfect, or any program that has a spellchecker. After you're done with the spellchecker, you can cut and paste your roleplay onto the Add New Topic page on the roleplay board, making sure that you have the correct spacing when you do so. Tip Two: Develop your character. Get a gimmick in mind, which by no means am I telling you to choose something as awful as TL Hopper or Duke the Dumpster Droese. Just get a rough idea of what your character should be. (NOTE: This is where originality really, REALLY makes a huge impact. Sure, the Stone Cold gimmick works on television, and sure, the Undertaker gimmick works on television, but they've both grown somewhat stale, hence Austin actually wrestling lately and the BikerTaker gimmick. Be original, or else you'll end up jobbing to nearly everyone in the fed. Face it friends, as much as we all miss the days of indestructible good guys and spooky evil bad guys, that time has passed. Come with something hokey like that, and suffer the consequences. It has to have some basis in reality. Undertaker worked because he stopped being supernatural and started being a devil-worshipper. This is a small difference in terms, but a big difference in plausibility. Once you have the gimmick, it's time for the fun part - developing your character physically, mentally, and psychologically. Personally, I started with the physical aspect, since that's what first sprang to mind. Get a height and weight that sound reasonable (not 5'5", 700 pounds or 7'5", 215 pounds), and then flesh out your character's appearance - things to include would be hair color and style, eye color, skin tone/nationality (not a must, but it helps us), build (skinny, athletic, muscular), attire (ring, entrance, and interview), and the type of wrestler your character is. The key is to make everything mesh together well. An evil, sadistic wrestler isn't going to come out with an N'Sync t-shirt on or enter to the sounds of Hanson's "Mmmbop". Beyond that, start developing your character in your own mind - what makes him/her tick? Create a background that is fairly original (not everyone has trained at the Dungeon, especially a lot of the younger guys) and think of some things about your character that set him or her apart from the rest of the wrestling world (both the real world and the e-wrestling world.) Example: Name: Lex Laww Height: 6'3" Weight: 245 lbs. Description: Lex is muscular, with a very well defined musculature. His short, black hair is razor-cut and spiked on the top, and his dark brown eyes and olive skin mark him as being Italian. Lex wears black or Navy blue police-style fatigues with a Kevlar vest to the ring, along with silver Oakley X-Metal sunglasses. Lex wrestles in these fatigues with black wrestling boots and Navy blue elbow pads. For interviews, he generally wears custom-tailored designer suits, along with black dress shoes and wired-rimmed Armani eyeglasses. Here is an example of a background for a character that is realistic and thought out: Name: Alexis Fairchild Background: Alexis is the fourth child of five, and the only daughter of "Jumping" Jack Fairchild, a former wrestler who now runs a wrestling school in the Fairchilds' hometown of Lincoln, Nebraska. She was always a tomboy and hung out at the school after getting done with school. She took up wrestling as a way to keep fit during her off seasons from gymnastics and volleyball, and she took to it very quickly. During college (she has a degree in sports therapy), she wrestled in the local Indy feds to make some extra money, and prove that women can wrestle just as good as men. When EHW opened a Women's division, her father urged her to try out, and she was accepted. Do the same thing with their personality; only your best bet is to develop that in vignettes, which we will get to later. Perhaps the most important part in developing your character is choosing a frame of mind, or mentality that is realistic for their gimmick. Great roleplays are much more than just attacks on individuals...they are thought out and acted out in the mind frame of your character. Consistency is important; as one does not go about changing the way he acts and speaks day to day. Also, make your character human, not a superhero. For example, don't have a 7 foot 5 inch wrestler with the best technical skills in the world. It's also crucial to try to give him/her some flaws. Not everyone is perfect, and people will relate to you more if your character seems more human. Tip Three: When roleplaying against your opponent, MAKE SURE YOU READ THEIR ROLEPLAY (S). This is crucial, as it will make it much easier to pick them apart in your attacks and thus give the bookers the idea that you've thought out your attack. While this won't guarantee a win, it will most likely prevent your wrestler from being squashed in a match because you did a "You suck" promo (basically just saying to your opponent, "You suck, and I'm better.") For this example, I've chosen to use Big Pee-On, the first title contender Lex Laww went after when he entered EHW, with whom I had a great set of back-and-forth roleplays. The other guys have chosen memorable opponents and put the gist of their attacks down here. Bad Example: "Pee-On, you suck! I'm better than you because I'm Lex Laww, and I will kick your ass tonight for that title!" Good Examples: "Pee-On, I've got a ten-inch height advantage on you, I outweigh you by eighty pounds, and my reach is a good 8 inches longer than yours. I've excelled at amateur wrestling, boxing, and been successful as a technician in Japan. What can you say? You're a barroom brawler? Face it, boy, you're outclassed, and tomorrow night, at Fury, I'm going to take you out, because while I've been conditioning, you've been drinking beer. While I've been weightlifting, you've been drinking beer. While I've been eating healthy…well, you know the rest…so show up tomorrow night with my belt polished and get ready to hand it over." On the other hand, if your character is well established and you are known for your well-done roleplays, you can sometimes use a simple "You suck" promo to disrespect your opponent if he is a newcomer or not very skilled by giving the effect that you are simply brushing them off. Not all attacks have to deal with an opponent's wrestling ability... one moment of this type of roleplay was when Hector Carreras (formerly La Mosca) came back to the fed after being such a big "heel" character, and trying to become a "face" after he had done so many despicable things in his previous run, and the character of Pheonyx challenged Hector's true integrity: "Hector, you need to prove that you are no longer the monster that you were, the man hiding behind the mask and politics to get your one and only title reign... prove to me that you can earn any title shots that you deserve by your wrestling talent and not by underhanded tactics... prove to me that you deserve to even get a title shot after all that you have done to so many people... prove to me that you are no longer the little man with the big mouth. Another thing that we frown upon is profanity. Unless it is in your character to do so, keep profane words to a minimum and make sure that you would be able to hear them on either of the "Big 2's" network programs. We're going for realism here, and HBO doesn't have its own wrestling federation. In other words, if every other word your wrestler says is profanity, it may be time to sit back and develop your character further. Once again, this is an issue of maturity, as cursing is either funny or intimidating when you are ten, but it shows no real skill to type one four letter word after another. A gentleman that I once worked with at McDonald's (rather intelligent, did a roleplay fed through snail mail, actually) had this to say - "If you are filling up your roleplays (or even your speech here in the real world) with profanity, that simply means you have nothing better to say." An EXTREMELY important note about the Roleplay board - don't take it personally. If someone insults your character, they are doing just that - insulting your character and not you as a person. On the flip side, before you fire off those insults on the OOC board, treat your fellow members with respect, or else we will suspend you. Not everyone will be a main eventer after their first roleplay, especially in such a talented and established fed such as ours; however, that doesn't mean that they aren't trying or even trying to improve, so cut them some slack. One possibility to ensure decreased friction in a heated verbal battle is to contact the other roleplayer via email or Instant Messenger and tell them that's it's been good to work with them. Don't take roleplays as an attack on you, take them as a chess match - try to out-think each other and enjoy the struggle and the art form. Tip Four: Roleplaying is NOT all about attacks. In fact, a good majority of the time you won't even mention your opponent in your roleplays. These are the almighty vignettes, which are a big part of character development and are especially important in this fed, where it's key that you show us that you have a realistic character. Don't limit yourself to an in-ring or in-arena attack. Go to your character's hometown or old stomping grounds. Show how the people they grew up with now react to them in their celebrity (or near-celebrity) status. You could even show how the general public reacts to your wrestler when they do something like go out for takeout or hit the Laundromat. It doesn't have to be a ground-shaking event, but it often helps if it is in character. What I'm saying is this - I wouldn't write about how people react to me or how I would react to them in turn, I would write about how the interaction between Lex Laww and people in his local gym. One of the most memorable vignettes was one from the stable Pawn Shop where they went to a small video store and two of the members started fighting in the store over their differing opinions of the ending of "Fight Club". A word of warning, though - get too involved with vignettes and you leave your opponent an easy opening for a simple attack on you: "You're too caught up in your own little world to see the beating I've got in store for you." Vignettes are cool, but keep your opponent in mind. However, for the most part, this is the best way to get your character over. Your character will become very stale, very fast if all he does is come out and talk trash. Use vignettes to show the human side of your wrestler off. Tip Five: If you know HTML, you can use it for style and flair in your roleplays (but only if you use it correctly.) If you do decide to use HTML, but you don't know that much about it, ask me (Wyatt), and I can refer you to several good introductory sites that will help you to get enough of an understanding that you can spice up your roleplays. The most important note about HTML: DON'T GO OVERBOARD! Make sure you keep your roleplays within a general color scheme - keep all your dialogue from one character one color, another character another color, and so on. Also, you can set the actions that occur in the roleplay apart in several different ways: 1) Use parenthesis or brackets, 2) Use a different color than the dialogue (this can get confusing if you have too many characters though), or 3) Use either bold or Italics to set the actions apart. For your benefit, I've included some simple tags to help you get started: Font Alterations: Bold: Bold Italic: Italic Underline: Underline Font colors: Blue: BLAH Green: BLAH You can also use hexadecimal values in your roleplays for coloration as well. If you want a much larger variety of colors, this is the way to go. Email or IM me and I will send you the address of an on-line resource that I have for the various different colors you can use. Tip Six: Get to know us, the staff here at LCW. We all use AOL Instant Messenger in some form or another, and you can find our IM names on the staff page. Contact one of us and get to know us…sure we're busy, but we'll be straightforward with you if we have something else that we need to work on. Chances are, though, that we'll help you out a lot more if you talk to us than if you don't. We do our best to listen to your input on angles and ideas (and sometimes the goings-on within a match, provided they aren't the determining factor in the outcome). Also, we love to hear CONSTRUCTIVE criticism - if you have a legitimate complaint, contact us and make sure we know about it, or else we won't be able to fix it. Also, if we have some idea of what you are planning for your roleplays, we can get a better idea of the psychology involved in the match and devote more time to it. Don't take this to mean that becoming best friends with us will allow you to make your way to the top, because we don't run LCW that way; however, a good working relationship is always a plus in any arena, and an e-federation is no exception. Camaraderie is what it all boils down to. Have a good time with it, and you'll go far. Treat it like work, and flounder about in the mid-card. You'll find that a lot of the people in LCW are cool, even the booking staff. We've put a lot of combined effort into these tips, and we're glad that we are able to share our knowledge with you in the hopes that we can help you improve your standings here in LCW, as our goal is to make this the hands-down best e-federation on the 'net. I'd like to give a special thanks to all the contributors: Dave, Tim, Ben, KC, Ryan, Joel, Mike, Gavin, and Tom. I couldn't have done it without you, guys! (Actually, I could've, but it wouldn't have been nearly as good!) To all of you reading this, we appreciate your drive to succeed, and good luck! [name withheld], Co-Head Booker and creator of Lex Laww
