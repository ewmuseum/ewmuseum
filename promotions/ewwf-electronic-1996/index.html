A single-post 'Electronic World Wrestling Federation' pitching a WWF-roster business simulation with its own currency, match-fee economy and pay-per-view calendar -- a different, unrelated 'EWWF' from either of the project's two held Extreme World Wrestling Federation wings. Written by AI, 2026

Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

The Electronic World Wrestling Federation opened on 16 December 1996 with a single founding post pitching itself as a business game built on top of wrestling: “we (for the time being) only use WWF wrestlers,” run on a TNM engine, “but this is a fed with a twist! It also involves a bit of business!” Owning wrestlers was only step one — the stated goal was to build a coalition big enough to take over the federation itself, which the post likened, half-joking, to building your own New World Order.

Starting capital was $1,100 in EWWF bucks against a $250 price per wrestler; a new owner picked four WWF wrestlers to control and sent in promos for each, since a promo that caught the president's eye earned more matches.

Its economy ran on entry fees and side bets. A standard match cost $15 into a winner-take-all pot, with $5 add-ons for a named stipulation — an Iron Man Match, a Death Match, best 2-out-of-3 falls, a Triangle Match (one-fall or elimination), a Ladder Match reserved for title bouts, a Steel Cage, an I Quit Match, a Towel Match, or Falls-Count-Anywhere — stacked with No DQ, No Count-outs, and No Time Limit for the same $5 each, plus $5 for a henchman to interfere. An owner could also fix a match outright by paying the president the full pot, forfeiting the money but buying a ranking boost toward a title shot; facing one of your own wrestlers against another was expressly barred as too easy a way to get rich.

Money came from ads ($25 the first airing, $10 every repeat, three per show), interviews ($10, published “uncut, uncensored”), and jobs assigned by the president, who took $1,000 up front for the role; a wrestler could be sold to another owner or back to the federation for $100. Its calendar ran Wednesday Night War as the weekly show and a monthly pay-per-view modeled on the WWF's own real one — Royal Rumble, WrestleMania, King of the Ring, SummerSlam, and Survivor Series kept their real names, the other months left as placeholders (“Season's Beatings” for December, a blank for May) with a $25 bounty offered to whoever named them better.

No card, roster listing, or result of any kind survives beyond this one document, and how far the promotion ran, or how it ended, is not in the surviving record.
