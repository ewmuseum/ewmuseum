A submission-form league that opened two championship tournaments in November 1992 and drew the newsgroup's own longest argument about whether a federation belonged there at all. Written by AI, 2026

Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

The Simulated Wrestling League opened with a joke about its own subject line. “Thought that unusual subject would get your attention...Now then, on to the business at hand, namely my new wrestling federation.” That was 4 November 1992. Two days later the same post went out again under the name Simulated Wrestling Federation, which is what most of its own entrants called it afterwards.

The form was the point. An entrant sent in an appearance, entrance music, height and weight, a style chosen from brawling, scientific, underhanded and high-flyer, thirty moves that matched the style, and a finisher invented and named and described; a tag partner, a valet, five double-team moves and a double finisher were optional. Matches would be e-mailed to the two people in them, and ratings posted to the newsgroup every fortnight so that managers could sign the fights they wanted.

Entries closed on Friday 13 November and the brackets went up on the 17th: seven singles pairings for the Heavyweight Championship tournament, three tag pairings and one bye for the tag tournament. Entrants were invited to mail threats at each other in good fun, carbon-copied to the office, and two did it publicly instead — a manager in a white linen suit and spats promising his man would leave The Tick unable to keep ticking, and a tag team called Rhythm Nation announcing itself with a manageress, a finisher called the Rhythmplex and the fans as a secret weapon.

That second promo is what started the argument. On 22 November someone addressed the league directly — “To whomever is running the SWF: I don't intend to speak for everyone on the net, but I think the vast majority of us don't particularly care what's going on in your PBeM league” — and the thread ran for two days, thirteen posts, taking in the FWF, Dreamcard, a count of nine active federations, an argument about who had the standing to complain, and one reader's flat defence: “all wrestling is fake anyway so what is wrong with a fake federation with its own fake matches.”

The tournaments were late. On 5 January 1993 its operator posted that he had returned from a long absence, that his system of role-playing worked, and that a complete rundown of the matches would start going out on the 11th. No result of any of them survives.
