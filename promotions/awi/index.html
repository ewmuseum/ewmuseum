Epitaph Written by AI, 2026

Allied Wrestling International tells three different stories about its own birth, none of them reconciled by anything that survives: its own December 1997 repost of its first pay-per-view says it was born “in early 1994 when the AWE and the UWA merged”; a held 1995 broadcast dates that same three-city Union supercard to 18 July 1995 and has it billing itself “the birth of the A.W.I.”; and a third-party e-fed directory of the day lists its opening as February 1996. The contradiction is internal to the promotion's own telling and is left exactly as it stands.

It ran on a dice-based role-playing system its own staff called BODYSLAM!, built, in a co-founder's own words, “heavily upon M. David Clark's WWF RPG and the Coliseum RPG,” and it barred real-wrestler impressions outright: “Copycats of real wrestlers are not allowed. In fact, they aren't even looked at twice.” Its roster ran almost entirely on invented gimmicks instead — martial artists out of the Hamakushi Dojo, a masked executioner called the Hangman working out of the Sixgun Stable, an archer entering under the name Truth Williams who put a blindfolded arrow through a target on camera to make his point.

Its tag team scene turned on Megaton, Heavy Duty and Mass Master, who won the World Tag Team titles at that same Union card by beating the reigning champions into unconsciousness and then had their manager, Vinnie “the Mouth,” send in a victory-tour videotape of the pair touring Hiroshima, Pompeii and Chernobyl for on-camera lessons in destruction, one of them tearing up at the memory of a childhood spent near a nuclear reactor. War Machine held the top singles title through the same stretch, fending off serial challenges from “the Olympian” Gary White, a self-declared amateur-and-Olympic champion who wore his gold medal to the ring as proof he was the promotion's real world's champion, and from a stable calling itself Perfection, which announced a three-step plan to take the tag titles by way of a hired manager it refused to name on camera.

Its recurring newsdesk segment, Miranda Wright's Courtroom, staged its scandals as mock legal proceedings — putting a manager on the witness stand to answer accusations of favoring one client over another, adjourning with a threatened title match rather than a verdict. By June 1997 the promotion had joined six other federations in a standing seven-fed alliance, and its own August 1997 Armageddon pay-per-view was still running a North American Tag Team Title tournament and drawing fan support the same year.

Its surviving record runs through December 1997 with no closing statement of any kind; the promotion is still active, still booking, at the edge of what survives.
